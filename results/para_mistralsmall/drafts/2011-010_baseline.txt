**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that § 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage to injuries occurring on land where there is a "substantial nexus" to extractive operations on the shelf. *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1134, 1143 (9th Cir. 2010). The plain text and structure of the OCSLA, as well as controlling Supreme Court precedent, compel the conclusion that coverage under § 1333(b) is limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

Section 1333(b) provides that LHWCA coverage extends to "the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" modifies "injury," not "operations." *See* *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 424 (1985) ("The statute’s coverage is tied to the place of the injury, not the place of the employer’s business."). Thus, the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf for § 1333(b) to apply.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), reinforces this interpretation. In *Tallentire*, the Court noted that § 1333(b) "makes compensation for the death or injury of an 'employee' resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act." *Id.* at 219 n.2. Although the Court did not explicitly address the situs requirement in *Tallentire*, its observation that § 1333(b) applies to injuries "on the Outer Continental Shelf" supports the conclusion that the injury must occur on the shelf. *See id.*

The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test conflicts with the statutory text and the Supreme Court’s interpretation of the OCSLA. The court below relied on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), which applied a "but for" test to extend coverage to an injury occurring during an employee’s commute to an offshore platform. *Id.* at 811. However, the Fifth Circuit has rejected this approach, holding that § 1333(b) requires both a "but for" causal connection *and* that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. *See Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). This Court should follow the Fifth Circuit’s reasoning, as it is more consistent with the statutory text and Supreme Court precedent.

**II. THE LHWCA DOES NOT COVER INJURIES OCCURRING AT LAS CONCHITA BECAUSE IT IS NOT A MARITIME SITUS.**

Even if § 1333(b) were construed to extend coverage to land-based injuries with a substantial nexus to shelf operations, the LHWCA does not cover Mr. Valladolid’s injury because La Conchita is not a maritime situs under § 903(a) of the LHWCA. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit has consistently held that a facility is a maritime situs only if it is "an adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading [or] unloading . . . a vessel." *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). The court below correctly applied this test and concluded that La Conchita does not qualify.

The functional relationship test set forth in *Herron* requires consideration of several factors, including "the particular suitability of the site for the maritime uses referred to in the statute; whether adjoining properties are devoted primarily to uses in maritime commerce; the proximity of the site to the waterway; and whether the site is as close to the waterway as is feasible given all of the circumstances in the case." *Id.* Applying these factors, the Benefits Review Board ("BRB") and the Ninth Circuit correctly determined that La Conchita is not a maritime situs. La Conchita is separated from the ocean by a highway and railroad tracks, has no direct access to any pier or dock, and is three miles from the nearest loading facility. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1159-60. These facts distinguish this case from *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), where the Supreme Court held that workers handling cargo within a port were engaged in maritime employment. *Id.* at 82-83. Unlike the facilities in *Pfeiffer*, La Conchita is not an integral part of the loading or unloading process.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Supreme Court’s admonition that the LHWCA’s coverage is tied to the place of the injury, not the place of the employer’s business. *Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 424. The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s holding that La Conchita is a maritime situs and affirm the BRB’s denial of LHWCA benefits.
