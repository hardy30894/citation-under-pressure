## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in dismissing Beg's complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA). The commercial activity exception to the FSIA, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), applies to Beg's claims because Pakistan's expropriation of his property and subsequent actions constitute a commercial activity that had a direct effect in the United States.

The FSIA provides that a foreign state is not immune from the jurisdiction of United States courts if the action is "based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). To determine whether an activity is commercial, courts look to the nature of the activity rather than its purpose. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). In this case, Pakistan's expropriation of Beg's property and subsequent transfer of the property to the military or other individuals constitutes a commercial activity.

The Supreme Court has held that a foreign state's actions are commercial in nature if they are "the type of actions by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce." Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614 (quoting Alfred Dunhill of London, Inc. v. Republic of Cuba, 425 U.S. 682, 704 (1976) (plurality opinion)). Expropriation of property can be considered a commercial activity if it is done for a commercial purpose, such as to sell or use the property for a profit. See de Sanchez v. Banco Central de Nicaragua, 770 F.2d 1385, 1393 (5th Cir. 1985). Here, Pakistan's expropriation of Beg's property and subsequent transfer of the property to the military or other individuals can be seen as a commercial activity, as it involves the taking and disposition of property, a type of action typically engaged in by private parties.

Furthermore, the fact that the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States to offer Beg an alternative parcel of land demonstrates that Pakistan's actions had a direct effect in the United States. See Callejo v. Bancomer, S.A., 764 F.2d 1101, 1111 (5th Cir. 1985) (holding that a foreign state's actions had a direct effect in the United States where the state made representations to a plaintiff in the United States). The direct effect requirement is met when the foreign state's actions have a "legally significant" effect in the United States. See Adler v. Federal Republic of Nigeria, 219 F.3d 869, 876 (9th Cir. 2000). In this case, the Government of Punjab's offer of alternative land to Beg in the United States had a legally significant effect, as it was an attempt to resolve the dispute and compensate Beg for his expropriated property.

Other courts have also found that a foreign state's actions can have a direct effect in the United States even if the initial act was taken outside the United States. See, e.g., Texas Trading & Milling Corp. v. Federal Republic of Nigeria, 647 F.2d 300, 312 (2d Cir. 1981) (holding that Nigeria's breach of a contract to purchase cement had a direct effect in the United States because the breach caused financial losses to American suppliers). Similarly, in this case, Pakistan's expropriation of Beg's property and subsequent actions had a direct effect in the United States, as they led to Beg's financial losses and attempts to seek compensation in the United States.

The district court's dismissal of Beg's complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction was therefore in error. The commercial activity exception to the FSIA applies to Beg's claims, and the court should have exercised jurisdiction over the case. See, e.g., Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina, 965 F.2d 699, 709 (9th Cir. 1992) (holding that Argentina's expropriation of property and subsequent actions constituted a commercial activity that had a direct effect in the United States).

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Beg's complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The commercial activity exception to the FSIA applies to Beg's claims, and the court should have exercised jurisdiction over the case. This Court should reverse the district court's dismissal and remand the case for further proceedings.
