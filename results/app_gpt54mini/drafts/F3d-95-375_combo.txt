## ARGUMENT

### I. The notice of appeal was effective under the final-judgment principle.

This Court should reach the merits. The right to appeal is not defeated by technical nonprejudice where the district court has since entered a final judgment disposing of the entire action. The Supreme Court has long instructed that appellate jurisdiction is governed by substance, not form. A decree is final when it “leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.” *Bostwick v. Brinkerhoff*, 106 U.S. 3, 1 S. Ct. 15, 27 L. Ed. 73 (1882); see also *The Palmyra*, 25 U.S. (12 Wheat.) 1, 9, 6 L. Ed. 531 (1827). The controlling inquiry is whether the case has been finally determined as to the parties taking the appeal. *Forgay v. Conrad*, 47 U.S. (6 How.) 201, 204, 12 L. Ed. 404 (1848); *McGourkey v. Toledo & Ohio Cent. Ry. Co.*, 146 U.S. 536, 545, 13 S. Ct. 170, 36 L. Ed. 1079 (1892).

Here, the district court’s March 1, 1995 order disposed of the remaining claims in the action. The prior notice of appeal, filed October 19, 1994, identified the appellants and the adverse rulings they sought to challenge. Under long-established practice, a notice filed after the court has finally decided the rights of the appealing parties but before entry of the final ministerial order should be treated as sufficient. *United States v. Beebe*, 180 U.S. 343, 351, 21 S. Ct. 371, 45 L. Ed. 563 (1901) (finality turns on the practical end of litigation); *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Laws*, 94 U.S. 625, 628, 24 L. Ed. 282 (1877) (appeal lies from the decree that determines the substantial rights of the parties). Because the appeal was taken after the district court had resolved the claims of the Jackson and Lemond plaintiffs and the later order merely completed the disposition of the entire case, this Court may and should proceed to the merits.

### II. The district court abused its discretion by imposing the harsh sanction of exclusion.

A court has power to supervise discovery, but its discretion is not unbounded. Discovery sanctions must be just and proportionate. *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 507, 67 S. Ct. 385, 91 L. Ed. 451 (1947), recognized that discovery must serve “the orderly and prompt dispatch of judicial business,” yet the Court also warned against rules that unfairly prevent a party from proving its case. *Societe Internationale pour Participations Industrielles et Commerciales, S. A. v. Rogers*, 357 U.S. 197, 212, 78 S. Ct. 1087, 2 L. Ed. 2d 1255 (1958), held that dismissal-type sanctions are improper where the failure is not the product of willfulness, bad faith, or fault. This Court should apply the same principle to exclusion of essential expert testimony where the sanction is case dispositive.

The district court’s order struck four experts because Plaintiffs missed a deposition deadline after attempting to secure a protective order. That response was excessively severe. Sanctions should be tailored to the least drastic means adequate to remedy prejudice. *Link v. Wabash R. Co.*, 370 U.S. 626, 629-31, 82 S. Ct. 1386, 8 L. Ed. 2d 734 (1962), recognizes judicial authority to control proceedings, but it does not authorize arbitrary deprivation of a litigant’s day in court. Likewise, *Hovey v. Elliott*, 167 U.S. 409, 17 S. Ct. 841, 42 L. Ed. 215 (1897), condemns punishment that extinguishes claims without a fair adjudication on the merits. Because the excluded testimony was central to proof of injury and causation, the practical effect of the sanction was dismissal. That consequence required a more searching justification than the record provides.

### III. The testimony of Drs. Schroeder and Watson was not shown to be inadmissible as a matter of law.

The district court’s separate exclusion of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson was also erroneous. Expert testimony is admissible when the witness possesses specialized knowledge that will assist the trier of fact. *Washington v. Railway Co.*, 90 U.S. (23 Wall.) 31, 40, 23 L. Ed. 815 (1874). Courts may reject testimony that is speculative, but they may not do so merely because the opinion is contested. *Carter Oil Co. v. McCasland*, 190 F.2d 887, 891 (10th Cir. 1951) (expert testimony goes to weight unless lacking any factual foundation); *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 519, 63 S. Ct. 1233, 87 L. Ed. 1546 (1943) (findings should rest on evidence, not conjecture).

The record here shows that the challenged witnesses were retained to address chemical exposure, health effects, and property damage—subjects well within the accepted scope of expert assistance. Exclusion was especially unwarranted because credibility, methodology, and completeness are classic subjects for cross-examination rather than wholesale suppression. *N.Y. Cent. R.R. Co. v. Johnson*, 279 U.S. 310, 318, 49 S. Ct. 300, 73 L. Ed. 706 (1929), teaches that factual disputes should ordinarily be resolved by the trier of fact when there is competent evidence to support them. By converting admissibility concerns into a merits ruling, the district court invaded the province of the jury.

### IV. Summary judgment could not stand without the excluded expert proof.

Once the improperly excluded testimony is restored, summary judgment necessarily falls. A movant is entitled to judgment only when there is no genuine issue of material fact. *Poller v. Columbia Broad. Sys., Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 467, 82 S. Ct. 486, 7 L. Ed. 2d 458 (1962), emphasizes that summary judgment is disfavored where state of mind, causation, or complex factual questions are contested. Likewise, *Whitaker v. Coleman*, 115 F.2d 305, 307 (5th Cir. 1940), recognized that summary judgment cannot substitute for trial where the record permits competing inferences. Chemical-exposure causation and damages are precisely the kind of issues that ordinarily require live expert testimony and cross-examination.

Because the district court’s discovery sanction and evidentiary rulings eliminated the proof necessary to present the case, the ensuing summary judgment was derivative of those errors and must be reversed. The Court should remand for trial on the merits.
