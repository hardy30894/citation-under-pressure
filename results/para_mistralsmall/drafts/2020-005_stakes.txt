**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s extensive in-state activities provided sufficient contacts to support specific jurisdiction.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the defendant’s forum-related activities give rise to the plaintiff’s claims. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985). Here, Ford’s nationwide system of sales, service, and marketing—including its distribution of replacement parts and advertising in Montana—established the necessary connection between its activities and Gullett’s claims. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 779 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where defendant’s forum-related activities were part of a regular, systematic, and continuous course of conduct).

Ford’s own internal documents confirm that it purposefully availed itself of Montana’s market by selling replacement parts and providing authorized service through Montana dealerships. *See Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where defendant’s forum-directed activities were “purposefully directed” at the forum state). The fact that the vehicle at issue entered Montana through the stream of commerce does not defeat jurisdiction, as Ford’s ongoing in-state activities—distinct from the mere sale of the vehicle—provided the requisite minimum contacts. *See J. McIntyre Mach., Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 886 (2011) (plurality opinion) (recognizing jurisdiction where defendant’s forum-directed conduct is “purposefully availed”).

**II. The “arising out of or relating to” requirement is satisfied where Ford’s in-state activities contributed to the plaintiff’s injuries.**

Specific jurisdiction exists where the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 141 S. Ct. 1017, 1025 (2021) (citing *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984)). Here, Ford’s design, manufacture, and distribution of the Explorer—including its tire selection and warnings—were integral to the product that caused Gullett’s fatal accident. *See Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1781 (2017) (finding a sufficient relationship where the defendant’s forum conduct contributed to the plaintiff’s claims).

Ford’s argument that the vehicle’s presence in Montana was fortuitous ignores the reality of its nationwide business model. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (rejecting the argument that foreseeability alone suffices; instead, jurisdiction requires purposeful availment). Ford’s extensive in-state activities—including parts distribution and service networks—demonstrate that it purposefully structured its business to benefit from Montana’s market. *See Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987) (plurality opinion) (finding jurisdiction where defendant’s forum-directed conduct was part of a “regular course of dealing”).

**III. The exercise of jurisdiction does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.**

Even if Ford’s contacts were minimal, the exercise of jurisdiction here comports with due process because Montana’s interests in adjudicating the dispute are substantial. *See Int’l Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945) (balancing the defendant’s contacts with the forum’s interests). Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents who are injured by defective products, and the litigation of Ford’s design and warning claims will not impose an undue burden on the company. *See Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 920 (2011) (recognizing that jurisdiction is permissible where the forum has a “legitimate interest” in the dispute).

Ford’s reliance on *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014), is misplaced. That case involved general jurisdiction, not specific jurisdiction, and addressed the limits of a state’s authority to exercise jurisdiction over a defendant based on its global contacts. *Id.* at 127. Here, the exercise of specific jurisdiction is narrowly tailored to Ford’s forum-related activities and the plaintiff’s claims. *See Ford Motor Co.*, 141 S. Ct. at 1025 (upholding jurisdiction where the defendant’s in-state conduct was “regular and ongoing”).

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s extensive in-state activities provided sufficient contacts to support specific jurisdiction. The exercise of jurisdiction here does not offend due process, as Ford purposefully availed itself of Montana’s market and its forum-related conduct contributed to the plaintiff’s injuries. The judgment below should be affirmed.
