**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” and thus excluded from the ADEA’s definition of “employee.” *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603-04 (8th Cir. 1990), *aff’d*, 499 U.S. 916 (1991). This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the ADEA, as well as the weight of authority interpreting the statute.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Supreme Court has instructed that statutory exemptions must be construed narrowly, *see, e.g., Public Employees Ret. Sys. of Ohio v. Betts*, 492 U.S. 158, 173 (1989), but this principle does not alter the plain meaning of the statute. The legislative history confirms that Congress intended the exception to apply to high-level advisors and policymakers in state and local government, including those in the judiciary. *See* Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179, 2180.

The petitioners’ contention that judges are not policymakers because their primary function is to “decide cases” misapprehends the nature of judicial power. *See Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604. As the First Circuit recognized, judging necessarily involves policymaking—particularly in resolving unsettled legal questions, interpreting statutes, and developing common law. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). The Supreme Court has long acknowledged that judges exercise discretion and judgment akin to policymakers in other branches. *See, e.g., United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 703 (1974) (noting the judiciary’s “constitutional responsibility to define and protect fundamental values”).

Moreover, Missouri’s judges exercise significant policymaking authority in administering the state’s judicial system. *See* Mo. Const. art. V, § 4 (vesting the Supreme Court with rulemaking and supervisory powers). This includes setting ethical standards for attorneys, resolving intra-court disputes, and shaping the development of state law. *See Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604-05. Such responsibilities place judges squarely within the ADEA’s policymaking exception.

**II. THE ADEA DOES NOT PREEMPT MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR JUDGES.**

Even if judges were deemed “employees” under the ADEA, the statute does not preempt Missouri’s constitutional retirement provision. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that federal statutes do not preempt state laws unless Congress has clearly manifested its intent to do so. *See, e.g., Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 499 U.S. 916, 917-18 (1991) (quoting *Atascadero State Hosp. v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985)).

The ADEA contains no express preemption provision, and its structure and legislative history demonstrate that Congress did not intend to interfere with state judicial retirement laws. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 623(b) (excluding from preemption state laws “relating to age discrimination”); *EEOC v. Illinois*, 721 F.Supp. 156, 160 (N.D. Ill. 1989) (finding no preemption where ADEA’s text and legislative history are silent on the issue). The Court should therefore defer to Missouri’s sovereign authority to regulate its judiciary. *See Gregory*, 499 U.S. at 918.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Eighth Circuit correctly upheld Missouri’s retirement provision as rationally related to legitimate state interests. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606-07. The Supreme Court has long deferred to state classifications affecting age, recognizing that such distinctions need only a rational basis. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam).

Missouri’s retirement provision advances several rational interests:
1. **Judicial Competence**: Mandatory retirement ensures that judges do not serve beyond the age at which cognitive or physical decline may impair performance. *See Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979).
2. **Public Confidence**: An age limit promotes public trust in the judiciary by ensuring that judges share the life experiences of the majority of citizens. *See Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988).
3. **Administrative Efficiency**: The rule prevents the logistical burdens of case-by-case evaluations of judicial fitness. *See id.*

The petitioners’ arguments to the contrary are policy-based and do not overcome the presumption of rationality. *See Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314. The Court should affirm the Eighth Circuit’s holding that Missouri’s retirement provision does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment below. The ADEA does not apply to Missouri’s judges, and even if it did, it does not preempt the state’s mandatory retirement requirement. Moreover, the requirement does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.
