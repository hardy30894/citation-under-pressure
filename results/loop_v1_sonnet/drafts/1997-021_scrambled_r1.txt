# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Application of a Unitary "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard to Polling Is Not Rationally Related to the Purposes of the Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not speak to employer polling; the practice is a creature of Board policy. This Court has long recognized that an employer confronted with objective evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support faces a genuine dilemma: it must choose between continuing to bargain with a union that may no longer represent its employees and risking liability for withdrawing recognition improvidently. See *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969) (discussing the tension between an employer's duty to bargain and its right to test a union's continuing majority status). Polling, no less than an RM election, serves the Act's core purpose of ensuring that bargaining representatives actually represent the employees for whom they purport to speak. *Cf. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (recognizing that a bargaining order is justified only where it reflects the wishes of employees).

The Board's decision to impose upon polling the identical evidentiary threshold required for outright withdrawal of recognition—rather than a lesser showing calibrated to the far less drastic character of a poll—cannot be reconciled with the Act's design. An administrative agency's order can be sustained only on the grounds actually invoked by the agency, and those grounds must be adequate to support the result reached. *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943) ("For the courts cannot exercise their duty of review unless they are advised of the considerations underlying the action under review."). Here, the Board's own stated rationale—that RM elections are the "preferred" method of testing employee sentiment because they carry procedural safeguards absent from polling—points toward a *lower*, not identical, threshold for the less consequential act of polling. Collapsing three distinct employer options—withdrawal, RM election, and polling—into one undifferentiated standard, without accounting for their obviously different risks to employees and to the collective-bargaining relationship, is the sort of unreasoned equivalence that *Chenery* forbids.

This Court has likewise cautioned that the Board's freedom to fashion policy is not boundless where it collides with employers' legitimate interests recognized elsewhere in the Act's structure. *See Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945) (Board must accommodate competing statutory interests rather than mechanically apply a single rule). The Board's insistence on a single "reasonable doubt" threshold for polling, withdrawal, and RM elections alike—while simultaneously declaring elections preferable—reflects exactly the kind of unreasoned, one-size-fits-all policy this Court has refused to credit. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941) (Board's remedial discretion must be exercised consistently with statutory purposes, not administrative convenience alone).

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt about the Union's majority status cannot stand. Review of Board factfinding is not toothless. "Substantial evidence is more than a scintilla, and must do more than create a suspicion of the existence of the fact to be established. It means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). The Board's factual determinations must be tested against this standard on the record as a whole, not merely against the fragments of evidence the Board finds convenient. *NLRB v. Walton Manufacturing Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 407–08 (1962) (Board's credibility-based findings must still rest on evidence sufficient to support the conclusion drawn).

The Board's treatment of the record here reflects a one-sided approach to factfinding. The ALJ and the Board discounted, one by one, every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown—discrediting Ridgick's and Marsh's statements as equivocal, refusing to credit Bloch's and Mohr's reports of coworker sentiment because the affected employees did not personally testify, and excluding employees no longer on the payroll from the calculus entirely—while crediting nothing that corroborated the Union's continuing support. This selective winnowing of the evidence mirrors the error this Court has condemned when the Board's inference is not a reasonable one drawn from the record as a whole, but rather a predetermined conclusion imposed upon ambiguous facts. *Cf. NLRB v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co.*, 337 U.S. 656, 659–60 (1949) (Board's inference from circumstantial evidence must be a reasonable one, not merely a possible one, when tested against the whole record).

Nor can the Board's approach be justified as ordinary credibility assessment entitled to deference. Where, as here, the Board applies a categorical rule—discounting entire categories of evidence (hearsay reports of coworkers' views, statements made during hiring interviews) without individualized assessment—it has not performed the evidentiary weighing that substantial-evidence review demands; it has instead erected an evidentiary standard so exacting that no employer's evidence could ever satisfy it. The cumulative, uncontradicted testimony of numerous employees—far more than the Board's crabbed count of seven—demonstrates that Allentown's doubt was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
