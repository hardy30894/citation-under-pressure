The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power, and the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA was authorized.

First, Congress supplied an intelligible principle. Under the familiar standard, Congress may confer discretion so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the delegate. J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Court has repeatedly upheld broad delegations where Congress identified the policy, the factors to be considered, and the boundaries of the authority. Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372-79 (1989); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). That is exactly what Congress did here. Section 811(h) authorizes temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and it requires consideration of specified statutory factors, notice, and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3), (4). The statute therefore does not leave the Attorney General to legislate at large; it cabins temporary scheduling with a concrete substantive standard and procedural constraints. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 374-75; Yakus, 321 U.S. at 427-28.

The Court has never required heightened scrutiny merely because delegated authority can affect criminal liability. In Yakus, the Court upheld a wartime price-control scheme even though violations were criminally punishable, emphasizing that Congress may delegate the task of applying broad standards in complex, rapidly changing fields. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 427-28, 432. Likewise, in United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-22 (1911), the Court sustained criminal sanctions for violation of regulations issued under congressional standards. The Controlled Substances Act fits comfortably within that line. Congress confronted a technical, evolving drug market in which prompt action is necessary to protect the public; that practical reality is precisely why the Court has recognized broad room for delegation. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372-74; Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250.

Nor does Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), or A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), compel a different result. Those cases invalidated statutes that gave the Executive essentially unfettered power, without meaningful policy or standards to guide action. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 415; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h) is the opposite: it identifies the public-safety objective, limits the factors the Attorney General may consider, requires consultation, and imposes a short temporary duration. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3), (4), (2). As the Court explained in Mistretta, the nondelegation doctrine is satisfied where Congress provides an intelligible principle and relevant limiting criteria. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372-79.

Second, the Attorney General validly subdelegated temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the drug laws. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Court has long recognized that, absent a contrary congressional command, an agency head may delegate assigned functions to subordinate officers. United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512-17 (1974); Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co., 331 U.S. 111, 122-24 (1947); Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 315-17 (1903). Here Congress did the opposite of forbidding subdelegation: it affirmatively authorized it. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General’s regulation assigning the CSA’s functions to the DEA Administrator therefore rested on direct statutory authority. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). And because the DEA Administrator was acting as the Attorney General’s delegate, the temporary scheduling order was validly issued.

The respondents’ contrary position would require this Court to treat every criminal statute with delegated administrative implementation as suspect. But the Court has consistently rejected such a cramped view of Congress’s power to legislate through standards and executive administration. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372-79; Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-28; Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 517-22; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 249-50. The judgment should be affirmed.
