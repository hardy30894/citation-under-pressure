# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 1231(a)(6) DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN WHOSE DEPORTATION IS NOT REASONABLY FORESEEABLE

### A. The Doctrine of Constitutional Avoidance Requires Construing the Statute to Impose a Reasonable-Time Limitation

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien ordered removed "may be detained beyond the removal period." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). The statute is silent on the duration of such detention. Where a statute is susceptible of a construction that avoids serious constitutional doubt, this Court has consistently adopted that construction. As the Court explained in *United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366 (1909), "where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter." *Id.* at 408.

Indefinite civil detention of a removable alien raises exactly such doubts. This Court has long recognized that "freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty" protected by the Due Process Clause. *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Civil confinement, to satisfy due process, must bear a "reasonable relation to the purpose for which the individual [was] committed." *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972). The purpose of post-removal detention under § 1231(a)(6) is to "assur[e] the alien's presence at the moment of removal." Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention bears no reasonable relation to that purpose, and detention becomes potentially permanent—civil confinement with no termination point in sight.

This constitutional concern is heightened because § 1231(a)(6) "does not apply narrowly to a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals," but sweeps broadly to cover aliens removed "for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations." A statute of such breadth cannot be read to authorize indefinite detention without triggering the gravest due process concerns.

### B. The Statute Must Be Construed to Contain an Implicit "Reasonable Time" Limitation

Applying these principles, § 1231(a)(6), "read in light of the Constitution's demands, limits an alien's post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien's removal from the United States." The statute "does not permit indefinite detention." Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.

This construction does not strip the Government of its authority to protect the public. The choice is not between imprisonment and an alien "living at large"; rather, it is between imprisonment and supervised release under conditions that may be enforced, with return to custody upon violation. See 8 U.S.C. §§ 1231(a)(3), 1253. Congress's authority "to remove aliens, to subject them to supervision with conditions when released from detention, or to incarcerate them where appropriate for violation of those conditions" remains fully intact.

### C. The Habeas Court Must Determine Whether Removal Is Reasonably Foreseeable

The proper inquiry for a habeas court is "whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal." Reasonableness must be measured "primarily in terms of the statute's basic purpose"—ensuring presence at removal. If removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is unreasonable and unauthorized. If removal is reasonably foreseeable, courts may consider the risk of further crimes as a factor justifying confinement within that period.

For guidance, a presumptively reasonable period of six months should apply. After that period, once the alien "provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing." As detention lengthens, "what counts as the 'reasonably foreseeable future' conversely would have to shrink."

### D. This Case Illustrates Why the Fifth Circuit's "Impossibility" Standard Cannot Stand

The Fifth Circuit held that Zadvydas's detention was lawful so long as "good faith efforts to effectuate deportation continue" and he failed to show that deportation "will prove impossible." *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). That standard demands more than the statute, properly construed, can bear—it would "require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable."

Petitioner, born stateless in a displaced-persons camp, has been rejected by Germany, Lithuania, and the Dominican Republic. He has remained in INS custody years beyond the ninety-day removal period with no realistic prospect of removal. Under the correct standard, he has provided good reason to believe there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future—a showing the Government has not rebutted, particularly given the length of his post-removal confinement.

### E. Excludable-Alien Precedent Does Not Control

*Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is not to the contrary. Mezei concerned an alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States. Petitioner, by contrast, was lawfully admitted as a resident alien and had lived in this country since childhood. Aliens who have not yet gained initial admission "would present a very different question." The constitutional calculus for admitted aliens facing indefinite detention is not diminished by precedent addressing exclusion at the threshold of entry.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
