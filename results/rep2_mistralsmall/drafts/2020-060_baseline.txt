**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT GUAM’S CERCLA § 107(a) CLAIM IS TIMELY BECAUSE THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT TRIGGER § 113(f)(3)(B).**

CERCLA § 107(a) authorizes “any person” to seek recovery of “all costs of removal or remedial action” incurred in response to a hazardous substance release. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(A), (B). By contrast, § 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only where a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” Id. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The D.C. Circuit’s decision below erroneously conflates these distinct remedies. As the Supreme Court has recognized, § 107 and § 113 serve different purposes: § 107 allows a party that has incurred response costs to seek full recovery from other responsible parties, while § 113 permits contribution only after a party has resolved its liability through settlement or judgment. *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 61–62 (1998); *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167–68 (2004).

The 2004 Consent Decree did not “resolve” Guam’s liability under § 113(f)(3)(B). The decree explicitly disclaimed any admission of liability and was entered without adjudication of facts or law. *See* Consent Decree, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam) (stating that the agreement was “without any finding or admission of liability”). Courts interpreting similar disclaimers have held that they do not preclude § 107 claims where the settlement does not resolve liability on the merits. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding no § 113(f)(3)(B) resolution despite liability disclaimer); *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (same). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion misreads the decree’s terms and misapplies CERCLA’s statutory framework.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY REJECTED THE UNITED STATES’ STATUTE-OF-LIMITATIONS DEFENSE BECAUSE GUAM’S § 107 CLAIM ACCRUED WHEN IT INCURRED RESPONSE COSTS, NOT WHEN THE CONSENT DECREE WAS ENTERED.**

CERCLA § 113(g)(2)(B) imposes a six-year limitations period for § 107 claims, running from the date “the liability or response action accrues.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). Liability accrues when a party incurs response costs, not when a settlement is reached. *See United States v. Colorado & E. R.R. Co.*, 50 F.3d 1530, 1535 (10th Cir. 1995) (“The plain language of § 9613(g)(2)(B) makes clear that the limitations period begins to run when the plaintiff incurs response costs.”). Guam incurred substantial costs in closing the Ordot Dump and constructing the required cover system pursuant to the 2004 decree. These costs were incurred between 2004 and 2011, well within the six-year limitations period. The D.C. Circuit’s holding that the limitations period began to run in 2004—when the decree was entered—is inconsistent with CERCLA’s text and precedent.

**III. THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S DECISION THREATENS TO UNFAIRLY EXTINGUISH GUAM’S RIGHTS UNDER CERCLA.**

The D.C. Circuit’s rule—under which a party’s § 107 claim is time-barred if it enters any settlement with the government—creates perverse incentives. As the district court noted, this interpretation would allow the government to force settlements that inadvertently bar future cost-recovery claims, leaving innocent parties like Guam to bear the financial burden of cleanup. *See Guam v. United States*, 341 F. Supp. 3d 74, 89 (D.D.C. 2018) (warning that the government’s position would “silently extinguish” rights without due process). This result contravenes CERCLA’s remedial purpose: to ensure that responsible parties, not taxpayers, pay for environmental harm. *See General Elec. Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010).

**CONCLUSION**

The district court correctly held that Guam’s § 107(a) claim is timely and that the 2004 Consent Decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and reinstate the district court’s judgment.
