# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1333(b)'s Text Extends Coverage to Any Injury "Resulting From" Outer Continental Shelf Operations, Without Regard to Where the Injury Occurs

The statute Congress wrote asks a single causal question: did the disability or death "result[] from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf"? 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The operative geographic limitation attaches to the *operations*—they must be "conducted on the outer Continental Shelf"—not to the situs of the injury itself. As the Third Circuit correctly recognized in the only appellate decision to examine the text closely without importing an extraneous limitation, a claimant satisfies § 1333(b) by showing that the injury would not have occurred "but for" operations on the Shelf. *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 809–11 (3d Cir. 1988). Under that causal test, an employee injured in a car accident en route to a helicopter that would carry him to an offshore platform was eligible for benefits, because his injury was linked to Shelf operations even though it occurred far from the Shelf itself. *Id.* at 806, 811.

Nothing in § 1333(b)'s language superimposes a second, independent situs-of-injury requirement. Congress knew how to write a locale-based limitation when it wanted one; it did so elsewhere in OCSLA. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) expressly applies adjacent-state law "to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That provision, construed in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), turns on "locale, not . . . the status of the individual injured or killed." *Id.* at 219. Section 1333(b) contains no comparable locale-defining clause; it is, as this Court itself observed, the "one provision of OCSLA" that "superimposes a status requirement" keyed to the employee, rather than a pure situs test keyed to a place. *Id.* at 219 n.2. Petitioner's reliance on that same footnote to import a situs-of-injury rule inverts the Court's own description of the provision.

## II. The *Tallentire* Footnote Is Unconsidered Dictum and Does Not Control This Case

The *Tallentire* Court expressly disclaimed any need to construe § 1333(b), stating that "because this case does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b), this provision has no bearing on this case." 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. A statement offered in a footnote, unbriefed by the parties and unnecessary to the holding, is "textbook dictum" and is entitled to no controlling weight. This Court has long taught that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," because "their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821); *accord Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens*). Dicta of that unconsidered variety carries no predictive value where, as here, "the issue was not before the Court, was not briefed by the parties, and had no relevance to the case before it." *See District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570, 625 n.25 (2008) (declining to treat unreasoned dictum as controlling). The *Tallentire* footnote cannot bear the weight Petitioner places on it.

## III. A Situs-of-Injury Test Would Defeat the Remedial Purpose of the Compensation Scheme

The Longshore Act, which § 1333(b) incorporates, is a remedial statute that this Court has consistently instructed must "be liberally construed in conformance with its purpose, and in a way which avoids harsh and incongruous results." *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953). A rigid situs-of-injury rule produces exactly the kind of incongruity *Voris* warned against: it would deny compensation to a worker whose death is inextricably tied to Shelf extraction operations merely because the fatal accident occurred a few hundred feet inland, at a facility built solely to process the very oil the offshore platforms produced. Coverage under a remedial compensation scheme should not turn on such fortuities of geography when the causal link to covered operations is undisputed. *Cf. P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 82–83 (1979) (recognizing that employees performing "intermediate steps" functionally connected to covered maritime activity are covered employees, without rigid formalism as to precise location).

## IV. The Fifth Circuit's Contrary Rule in *Mills* Rests on Inapposite Authority

The Fifth Circuit's situs-of-injury rule, adopted in *Mills v. Director, OWCP*, 877 F.2d 356, 361–62 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), leans on *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), a decision addressing whether a platform worker in *state* waters satisfied the Longshore Act's own maritime-employment "status" test—a wholly different statutory question from the causal inquiry Congress wrote into § 1333(b). *Herb's Welding* does not purport to graft a situs-of-injury requirement onto § 1333(b), and *Mills*'s extension of it does violence to the statute's actual text, which conditions coverage on the situs of *operations*, not of injury.

## Conclusion

Because § 1333(b) asks only whether an injury resulted from operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf, and because Juan Valladolid's death bore the requisite causal connection to Pacific Operations's Shelf extraction activities, the judgment below should be affirmed.
