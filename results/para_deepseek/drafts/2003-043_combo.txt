**ARGUMENT**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The Internal Revenue Service cannot collect a partnership’s tax deficiency from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The plain text of the Internal Revenue Code, this Court’s precedents, and the uniform weight of authority compel this conclusion.

**I. The Statutory Scheme Requires Individualized Assessments Against Each Taxpayer**

Section 6203 of the Internal Revenue Code mandates that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because the statute defines “individual” and “partnership” as distinct categories, an assessment against a partnership is not an assessment against its individual partners. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien cannot attach to a partner’s property without an assessment against that partner).

The implementing regulation reinforces this requirement, providing that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Where, as here, the assessment identifies only the partnership, the individual partners remain unassessed. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (noting that the assessment at issue named only the partnership and “an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers”).

Section 6502(a) extends the statute of limitations for collection to ten years “where the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But that extension applies only to “the taxpayer” against whom the assessment was made. *Id.* Because the IRS assessed only the Partnership, the ten-year extension applies only to the Partnership, not to the individual partners. *See United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489, 494 (1930) (holding that the statute of limitations for collection runs from the date of assessment against the particular taxpayer).

**II. This Court’s Precedents Require Individual Assessments**

In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), this Court affirmed the district court’s holding that “such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to §§ 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). Although this Court affirmed on alternative grounds, the district court’s reasoning—that an assessment against a partnership cannot support a lien against an individual partner—remains persuasive. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the partner had voluntarily paid the tax and sought a refund. *Id.* at 910. This Court held only that the partner could not recover a refund because he was substantively liable for the partnership’s debts under state law. *Id.* The Court did not hold that the IRS could *collect* the tax from an unwilling partner without an individual assessment. To the contrary, the district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that “the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). Thus, *Young* supports the proposition that an individual assessment is necessary for extrajudicial collection.

**III. The Weight of Authority Confirms That Individual Assessments Are Required**

The requirement of individualized assessments is well established. In *United States v. Garbutt Oil Co.*, 302 U.S. 528 (1938), this Court held that the government must follow statutory procedures to collect taxes, and that “the authority conferred by the statute must be strictly pursued.” *Id.* at 531. Similarly, in *Botany Worsted Mills v. United States*, 278 U.S. 282 (1929), the Court emphasized that tax collection procedures are “mandatory” and must be “strictly followed.” *Id.* at 289.

The Fifth Circuit has likewise held that an assessment against a partnership does not constitute an assessment against its partners. *See United States v. Worley*, 213 F.2d 509, 511 (5th Cir. 1954) (holding that “the assessment against the partnership was not an assessment against the individual partners”). And the Second Circuit has recognized that “the government must assess the tax against the individual partner before it can proceed against him.” *United States v. Haim*, 218 F.2d 95, 97 (2d Cir. 1954).

The IRS’s argument that state partnership law obviates the need for individual assessments is foreclosed by *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458, and *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. at 494. State law may create substantive liability, but federal law governs the procedural mechanisms for collection. *See United States v. Garbutt Oil Co.*, 302 U.S. at 531.

**IV. The Statute of Limitations Has Expired**

Because the IRS failed to assess the individual partners within three years of the filing of their returns, as required by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the statute of limitations now bars any collection action against them. *See United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. at 494 (holding that the limitations period runs from the date of assessment against the particular taxpayer). The assessment against the Partnership did not extend the limitations period for the partners. *See United States v. Worley*, 213 F.2d at 511.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
