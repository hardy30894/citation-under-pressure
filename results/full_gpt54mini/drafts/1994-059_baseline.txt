**ARGUMENT**

**I. States may not add to the Constitution’s exclusive qualifications for Members of Congress.**

The Constitution sets forth the qualifications for the National Legislature. Article I requires that a Representative be at least twenty-five years old, a seven-year citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected; a Senator must be at least thirty, a nine-year citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, cl. 2; 3, cl. 3. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), this Court held that those qualifications are “fixed” by the Constitution and that neither Congress nor the States may add to them. Id. at 522-48. The Court explained that the Framers “intended the qualifications in the Constitution to be exclusive.” Id. at 548.

That principle controls here. Amendment 73 does not merely regulate the manner of election; it disqualifies otherwise eligible candidates from appearing on the ballot once they have served the specified number of terms. The practical and legal effect is to add new qualifications for federal office. As this Court has recognized in the related context of ballot-access restrictions, a State may not evade constitutional limits by relabeling a qualification as an election procedure. See *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788-89 (1983); *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 523-24 (2001). Under *Powell*, a State may not do indirectly—by ballot exclusion—what it cannot do directly.

The Constitution also demonstrates that when the Framers wished to permit States a role in congressional qualifications, they said so expressly. Article I allows States to prescribe the “Times, Places and Manner” of holding elections, but not the qualifications of candidates. U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1. And where the Constitution contemplates alteration of qualifications, it does so only through constitutional amendment. See U.S. Const. art. V. The omission of any state power to add qualifications is decisive. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540-41. Amendment 73 therefore conflicts with the federal Constitution and cannot stand.

**II. The Tenth Amendment and the reserved powers of the people of Arkansas do not authorize restrictions on federal qualifications.**

Arkansas argues that Amendment 73 is a valid exercise of reserved power. But the Tenth Amendment reserves only those powers “not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States.” U.S. Const. amend. X. The power to alter qualifications for federal office is not reserved, because the Constitution itself has already addressed that subject. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 530-31, 546-47. Once the Constitution enumerates qualifications for Members of Congress, state initiative power cannot supplement them. See *Thornton v. U.S. Term Limits, Inc.*, 514 U.S. 779, 827-28 (1995) (holding that the States lack authority to impose congressional term limits through their own constitutions).

Nor can Amendment 73 be sustained as a ballot-access regulation. This Court has repeatedly held that ballot-access rules may not “freeze the political status quo” or impose undue burdens on voters and candidates. *Anderson*, 460 U.S. at 787-88; *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428, 433-34 (1992). But Amendment 73 is not a neutral regulation of election administration. It is a substantive disqualification tied to prior service. That is a qualification, not a manner rule. See *Cook*, 531 U.S. at 523-24.

**III. The unconstitutional congressional term-limit provisions are severable from the provisions governing state offices.**

The judgment below correctly preserved the State-office provisions. Severability turns on whether the valid portions can stand independently and whether the legislature or electorate would have enacted them without the invalid part. *Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock*, 480 U.S. 678, 684-86 (1987); *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 186 (1992). A severability clause is strong evidence of that intent. *Alaska Airlines*, 480 U.S. at 686. Here, Amendment 73 contains an express severability clause. Ark. Const. amend. 73, § 4.

The State-office term limits are fully operative on their own. They regulate only Arkansas officers, over whom the State possesses undisputed authority. See *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 501 U.S. 452, 463-64 (1991). Removing the congressional provisions does not impair the operation of the state provisions. A court should therefore sever only the unconstitutional portions and leave the remainder intact. See *Alaska Airlines*, 480 U.S. at 684-88.

**CONCLUSION**

Because Amendment 73 attempts to add to the Constitution’s exclusive qualifications for Members of Congress, the congressional term-limit provisions are invalid. But the State-office provisions are independently valid and severable. The judgment should be affirmed in part and reversed in part consistent with that conclusion.
