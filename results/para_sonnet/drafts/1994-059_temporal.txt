**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I Establish an Exclusive and Uniform Standard That the States May Not Supplement.**

Article I, § 2, cl. 2 and § 3, cl. 3 prescribe, in precise and exhaustive terms, the age, citizenship, and residency qualifications for Representatives and Senators. This Court has already recognized that such enumerated qualifications are fixed and may not be enlarged even by the body directly affected—the House of Representatives itself. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected member for reasons beyond those listed in the Constitution, explaining that "the Convention intended that the qualifications for membership in Congress be fixed" and that to permit additional qualifications would produce "the possibility of an irresponsible body" and would frustrate "the fundamental principle of our representative democracy . . . that the people should choose whom they please to govern them." *Id.* at 540, 547 (internal quotation marks omitted). If Congress itself cannot add to these enumerated standards, a single State plainly cannot do so unilaterally, thereby imposing upon the national legislature a qualification that thirty-seven other States have never adopted and that the People of the entire Union never ratified.

The historical materials canvassed in *Powell* confirm this uniformity principle. The Framers rejected proposals that would have allowed the several States to fix additional qualifications, precisely because they feared the "abuses" that would follow if "exclusion... could be error or fraud [used] to defeat the will of the people." *Id.* at 533. The Federalist itself described the qualifications as "defined and fixed in the Constitution" and "unalterable by the legislature." The Federalist No. 60. A regime in which each State could impose its own supplemental bar to congressional service—whether by age, wealth, or, as here, incumbency—would recreate exactly the patchwork the Framers sought to avoid.

**II. The National Character of the Congress Forecloses State Authority to Add Qualifications.**

This Court has long recognized that Members of Congress are federal officers occupying offices "created by the Constitution of the United States," and not offices held at the pleasure or control of the several States. *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 316 (1941). The government of the Union, though "limited in its powers, is supreme within its sphere of action," *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819), and the structure of that government—including who may sit in its legislative branch—is not a matter reserved to the States under the Tenth Amendment, because the States never possessed antecedent authority over offices that did not exist until the Constitution created them. As *McCulloch* teaches, the States retain no power "to retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control" the operations of the constitutional organs of the federal government. *Id.* at 436. Arkansas's Amendment 73 does precisely that: it burdens and impedes the operation of the House and Senate by excluding otherwise eligible citizens from federal office based on a criterion the Constitution does not list.

**III. Amendment 73's Ballot-Exclusion Mechanism Is a Qualification in Substance, Whatever Its Form.**

Appellants' attempt to recast Section 3 as a mere "ballot access" regulation cannot survive scrutiny of its actual operation. The Elections Clause, Article I, § 4, permits States to regulate only the "Times, Places and Manner" of elections—procedural matters such as districting, registration, and voting mechanics. This Court's precedent confines that authority to procedural regulation and does not extend it to substantive eligibility. *See Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932) (Elections Clause authority is to regulate the "procedure and safeguards" of elections). A rule that permanently forecloses an entire class of citizens from having their names printed on the ballot for federal office—regardless of how popular they may be with the electorate—does not regulate the "manner" of holding elections; it determines who may hold the office at all. That is a qualification, not a procedure, and it lies outside the Elections Clause altogether.

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly protected the People's right to an unencumbered choice of their representatives. In *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1, 17 (1964), the Court explained that Article I "means that as nearly as is practicable one man's vote in a congressional election is to be worth as much as another's," a principle rooted in "the fundamental idea of a House of Representatives elected 'by the People.'" And in *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23, 31 (1968), the Court struck down state ballot restrictions that burdened voters' ability to cast an effective vote for the candidate of their choice, holding that the right to vote is "heavily burdened" if States may exclude candidates without a compelling justification. Amendment 73 does exactly what *Williams* condemned: it forecloses the electorate's ability to vote for an eligible, willing candidate based on a criterion having nothing to do with the enumerated constitutional qualifications.

**IV. Uniformity of Congressional Qualifications Is Essential to National Government.**

Finally, as *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 657–58 (1884), recognized, "the government of the United States . . . must have the power to protect the elections on which its existence depends," free from the risk that individual States might, through varying qualifications, distort the composition of the national legislature. A term-limits qualification adopted by Arkansas alone would produce precisely the "hodge-podge" of local rules for a national body that the constitutional design forbids.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court invalidating Section 3 of Amendment 73 should be affirmed.
