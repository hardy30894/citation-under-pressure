ARGUMENT

I. The BOP’s Program Statement Improperly Redefines “Crime of Violence” by Looking to a Sentencing Enhancement Rather Than to the Elements of the Offense of Conviction.

Congress directed the Bureau of Prisons to reward inmates who complete drug treatment with early release unless the inmate’s “current offense” is a “crime of violence.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3) defines that term by reference to the elements of the offense — whether it “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force,” or “by its nature, involves a substantial risk” that force will be used “in the course of committing the offense.” Conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846 has no such element. The statutory text must control. As this Court has long recognized, resort to a statute’s purpose is warranted only where the words themselves leave genuine doubt; where Congress has spoken through the elements of an offense, an agency may not substitute a different measure. United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc., 310 U.S. 534, 543-44 (1940) (courts look to the words Congress used, guided by purpose only where the text is unclear).

Program Statement 5162.02 abandons the statutory, elements-based test and instead asks whether the sentencing court applied a Guidelines weapon enhancement — a question about facts found for sentencing, not about the elements of the crime of conviction. That is precisely the kind of agency overreach condemned in Manhattan General Equipment Co. v. Commissioner, 297 U.S. 129, 134 (1936), where the Court held that an administrative regulation may implement a statute but may not, through interpretation, “add to” or extend the statute’s reach beyond what Congress prescribed. The Program Statement does exactly that: it converts a nonviolent narcotics conspiracy into a “violent” offense based on a factor — a gun found in a vacation home, unconnected to the arrest — that has nothing to do with any element of § 846. Similarly, in Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc., 322 U.S. 607 (1944), the Court refused to accept an administrative gloss that swept within a statutory category conduct the statute’s own terms did not cover. The same result should follow here.

II. The Program Statement Is Not Entitled to Deference Because It Conflicts with the BOP’s Own Regulation and Is Neither Consistent Nor Persuasive.

Even administrative interpretations of an agency’s own regulations command deference only when they do not conflict with the regulation’s plain terms. Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co., 325 U.S. 410, 413-14 (1945) (agency interpretation of its own regulation is “of controlling weight unless it is plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation”). Here, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 defines “crime of violence” by cross-reference to § 924(c)(3) — an elements-based test. The Program Statement is “plainly erroneous or inconsistent” with that regulation because it substitutes a sentencing-enhancement test for the elements test the regulation itself adopted. Bowles, 325 U.S. at 414.

Nor does the Program Statement merit the lesser deference described in Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134 (1944). Under Skidmore, the weight owed an agency’s interpretive pronouncement “will depend upon the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all those factors which give it power to persuade, if lacking power to control.” Id. at 140. The Program Statement offers no reasoned explanation why a Guidelines enhancement — imposed here although guns played no part in Roussos’ arrest or plea — transforms the “nature” of the underlying conspiracy offense into one that, in the statutory phrase, “involves a substantial risk” of force. Nor is the interpretation the kind of longstanding, consistently applied construction that courts have historically credited. Cf. Norwegian Nitrogen Products Co. v. United States, 288 U.S. 294, 315 (1933) (weight accorded to a settled, contemporaneous administrative construction). An agency cannot exceed the bounds of its delegated authority merely by labeling its action a “Program Statement.” Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 249-50 (1947) (administrative action must remain within the confines of the authority Congress has delegated).

III. Any Doubt About the Statute’s Reach Must Be Resolved in Roussos’ Favor.

Because § 3621(e)(2)(B) and its implementing regulation bear directly on the duration of Roussos’ confinement, the rule of lenity applies with full force. That rule, older than the Republic’s administrative state, teaches that “penal laws are to be construed strictly” and that it is “the legislature, not the Court,” which defines an offense and its consequences. United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95-96 (1820). Where, as here, an ambiguity exists over whether a nonviolent drug conspiracy becomes “violent” by virtue of an unrelated sentencing factor, that ambiguity should be resolved “in favor of lenity.” Bell v. United States, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955). The Court should not adopt a construction that lengthens a prisoner’s time in custody on “no more than a guess as to what Congress intended.” Ladner v. United States, 358 U.S. 169, 178 (1958).

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Program Statement’s classification of Roussos’ offense as a crime of violence conflicts with the statute, the regulation, and settled principles of administrative and criminal-law interpretation. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the writ should issue.
