**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Post-Removal-Period Statute Contains an Implicit “Reasonable Time” Limitation**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), contains an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 2495 (2001). The Supreme Court held that indefinite detention of aliens in the former category would raise serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 2495. This interpretation aligns with the traditional understanding of “reasonable time” in statutory construction, as seen in *Klein v. United States*, 104 U.S. 200, 204 (1881), which held that a statute authorizing a “reasonable time” for performance is not indefinite. The purpose of the statute is to assure the alien’s presence at removal. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2504. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized. *Id.* at 2503.

**II. The “Reasonably Foreseeable Future” Standard Requires Release**

The Court established that the habeas court must ask whether detention exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2504. The Court noted that “as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink.” *Id.* at 2505. Zadvydas has been detained for over three years. The alien must provide “good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future.” *Id.* at 2505. The government must rebut this showing. *Id.* at 2505. The government has failed to identify a specific country to accept Zadvydas, demonstrating that removal is not reasonably foreseeable.

**III. The Constitution Prohibits Indefinite Detention**

The Constitution prohibits indefinite detention. *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 211 (1953). The Court in *Zadvydas* emphasized that the statute applies broadly to aliens ordered removed for many reasons, including visa violations, and that indefinite detention would be unconstitutional. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2499. The Due Process Clause requires a balancing of interests. *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976). The private interest in liberty is significant. The risk of erroneous deprivation is high because the government has not identified a specific country to accept Zadvydas. The government’s interest in removal is outweighed by the lack of a realistic path to removal. *Johnson v. Eisentrager*, 339 U.S. 763, 785 (1950) (holding that indefinite detention of enemy aliens is suspect).

**IV. The Government Has Not Met Its Burden to Show Removal is Foreseeable**

The Court rejected the standard that the alien must show deportation is impossible. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 2503. The Court stated that “judicial intrusion should not be considered... until there is a more definitive showing that deportation is impossible, not merely problematical, difficult and distant.” *Id.* (citing *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 294 (5th Cir. 1999)). However, the Court also noted that the alien must show that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 2505. The government has not met its burden to show that removal is reasonably foreseeable. *Kleindienst v. Mandel*, 408 U.S. 753, 762 (1972) (recognizing the executive’s broad discretion in removal matters, but noting that discretion is not unlimited). The government’s failure to identify a country to accept Zadvydas demonstrates that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. *Fiallo v. Bell*, 430 U.S. 787, 797 (1977) (noting that the government’s interest in excluding aliens is not absolute).
