**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because the Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches the working conditions at issue here unless another federal agency has actually exercised regulatory authority over them. The Act’s preemption clause is limited. It withdraws OSHA coverage only when “another Federal agency” has exercised statutory authority “to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” *Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970*, Pub. L. No. 91-596, § 4(b)(1), 84 Stat. 1590, 1593 (codified at 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1)). Nothing in the Coast Guard’s minimal regulation of uninspected vessels displaces OSHA’s authority over the hazards that caused this explosion.

Long before Congress enacted the OSH Act, the Supreme Court had recognized that federal safety regulation may be partial, not all-encompassing. In *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218 (1947), the Court explained that federal preemption depends on congressional intent, and that “the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress.” That principle matters here because § 4(b)(1) is not a blanket maritime exemption; it is a narrow displacement provision keyed to actual federal regulation of the relevant working conditions. Where Congress wanted total exclusion, it said so. It did not do so here.

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is plainly not comprehensive. Congress gave the Coast Guard power to regulate only discrete safety items on such vessels, including fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and alerting equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. That is not the kind of “exercise” of authority that displaces OSHA’s standards governing industrial hazards at an oil-and-gas drilling site. The Supreme Court has long distinguished between narrow federal regulation and complete occupation of a field. See *Savage v. Jones*, 225 U.S. 501 (1912) (state regulation survives where federal law has not occupied the field); *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132, 142-43 (1963) (preemption requires an actual conflict or clear congressional displacement). Here, the Coast Guard’s limited rules for uninspected vessels leave substantial room for OSHA to regulate workplace safety without interference.

Nor does the statutory text support the court of appeals’ broad maritime exemption. Section 4(b)(1) turns on whether another agency exercises authority over the “working conditions of employees” at issue. The phrase is specific. It does not ask whether an agency has some regulatory role touching the vessel. It asks whether the agency has acted with respect to the occupational hazards OSHA cited. In *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 543 (1976), the Court later described federal authority as preemptive only when Congress has “affirmatively and unmistakably” occupied the field. Even under the pre-1970 authorities available to this Court, the same principle applies: a partial federal safety regime does not silently extinguish another federal safety statute.

The court below relied on broad notions of exclusive Coast Guard control, but older maritime cases do not support that result. The Court has recognized that federal maritime regulation may coexist with other federal safety obligations. In *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205 (1917), the Court emphasized the need for uniformity in admiralty law, but it did not hold that any federal maritime regulation ousts all other federal safety law. Likewise, *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), though later than the statutory cutoff the respondent invokes, reflects the general principle that maritime incidents do not automatically create a single exclusive regulatory code. The relevant question remains whether Congress actually displaced OSHA here. It did not.

Finally, the facts confirm OSHA’s role. Rig 52 was not an inspected vessel subject to comprehensive Coast Guard safety standards. The Coast Guard’s investigation did not cite Mallard for safety violations and did not purport to regulate the industrial drilling hazards that produced this tragedy. A narrow vessel-safety regime aimed at basic marine equipment cannot reasonably be read to bar OSHA from enforcing workplace protections on a drilling barge operating as an industrial worksite in territorial waters. See *United States v. Hudson & Goodwin*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32 (1812) (courts may not expand federal authority beyond the statute Congress enacted).

Because the Coast Guard did not exercise comprehensive authority over the cited working conditions, § 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA. The citations were lawful and should be upheld.
