### ARGUMENT

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that it lacked jurisdiction. A Board refusal to reopen a prior benefits claim is a “final decision” within the meaning of 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), because it is the Board’s consummated disposition of the reopening request and leaves nothing further to do within the agency. This Court has repeatedly treated agency actions denying reopening as reviewable final determinations unless Congress clearly forecloses review. See Lindahl v. Office of Personnel Management, 470 U.S. 768, 791–99 (1985); Robertson v. Chambers, 341 U.S. 37, 40–41 (1951).

The starting point is the statute. Section 355(f) authorizes judicial review of “final decision[s]” of the Board. Unlike provisions that expressly confine review to “final decision[s] . . . made after a hearing,” Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99, 108 (1977), the Railroad Retirement Act incorporates no such narrowing language in the relevant review provision. That textual difference matters. In Sanders, the Court held that the Social Security Act’s review provision “clearly limits judicial review to a particular type of agency action, a ‘final decision of the Secretary made after a hearing.’” Id. at 108. The Court then explained that allowing review merely because a claimant filed an unsuccessful reopening petition would “frustrate the congressional purpose” of limiting review of the original benefits denial. Id. at 108–09. Section 355(f) is not drafted in the same way, and Congress did not replicate Sanders’s explicit “made after a hearing” limitation.

This Court’s decision in Lindahl confirms that Congress sometimes uses “final decision” language to permit review of agency benefit determinations while excluding only narrow categories of factual or discretionary matters. 470 U.S. at 791–99. There, the Court construed the Civil Service Retirement Act and held that judicial review extends to statutory entitlement questions even though certain determinations are committed to agency discretion. Id. at 791–99. The Court emphasized that Congress’s chosen review language must be given effect, not displaced by generalized concerns about agency administration. Id. at 780–81, 797–99.

Here, the Board’s refusal to reopen was not an antecedent procedural ruling; it was the agency’s final word on whether Salinas could revive the 2006 claim. The Board conducted proceedings, applied its reopening regulations, and definitively rejected reopening because the request fell outside the regulatory period. That is the quintessential “final decision” of an agency on the reopening question. See Your Home Visiting Nurse Services, Inc. v. Shalala, 525 U.S. 449, 453–56 (1999) (describing reopening denial as a conclusive agency action under Medicare regulations).

The Fifth Circuit’s rule rests on Roberts v. U.S. Railroad Retirement Board, 346 F.3d 139, 140–41 (5th Cir. 2003), but Roberts should not control this Court’s interpretation of the statute. Roberts relied heavily on Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. at 108–09, but Sanders interpreted a materially different statute with an express “made after a hearing” limitation. Id. at 108. Roberts also treated reopening denials as beyond review because reopening is created by regulation, not statute. 346 F.3d at 140–41. But agency regulations can define the manner in which a claim is finally resolved, and when the Board exercises that regulatory authority and issues a conclusive denial, the decision is final in the ordinary judicial-review sense. See Lindahl, 470 U.S. at 791–99.

The government may argue that review of reopening denials would undermine finality. But finality is preserved because reopening is available only within the Board’s regulatory limits, and judicial review is available only after the Board has finally acted on the reopening request. See Your Home Visiting Nurse Services, 525 U.S. at 454–56. Moreover, this Court has recognized that the existence of agency reconsideration mechanisms does not itself bar review of the agency’s final refusal to grant relief. See Dada v. Mukasey, 554 U.S. 1, 18–21 (2008) (treating agency reopening-type mechanisms as part of the final administrative process). What matters is whether the agency has reached a definitive disposition. Here, it has.

The better reading of § 355(f) is the one adopted by the D.C. Circuit in Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board, 826 F.3d 500, 502–05 (D.C. Cir. 2016): a Board denial of reopening is itself a final decision subject to judicial review. That interpretation gives effect to the statute’s text and this Court’s administrative-finality precedents. See Lindahl, 470 U.S. at 791–99; Robertson, 341 U.S. at 40–41. The petition should therefore be granted, and the Fifth Circuit’s jurisdictional dismissal reversed.
