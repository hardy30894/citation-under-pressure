# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA BECAUSE CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY MANIFEST AN INTENT TO INTRUDE UPON THE STATE'S AUTHORITY TO DEFINE THE QUALIFICATIONS OF ITS OWN JUDGES

### A. The Plain Statement Rule Requires a Clear Expression of Congressional Intent Before Federal Law Is Read to Override a State's Constitutional Authority to Establish Qualifications for Its Highest Officers

This Court has long recognized that "the States retain substantial sovereign authority under our constitutional system." *Gregory* implicates precisely the kind of decision this Court has held to be at the core of state sovereignty: the power to prescribe qualifications for those who exercise the state's own governmental powers. As Judge Bowman correctly observed below, "the tenure of state judges is a matter of considerable importance to a state, and one that traditionally has been left to each state to regulate." 898 F.2d at 601.

This Court's decision in *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336 (1971), establishes the governing interpretive principle: "In traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision." *Id.* at 349. This clear-statement rule is not a mere canon of construction but a structural safeguard rooted in the Tenth Amendment's guarantee that powers not delegated to the federal government "are reserved to the States." The authority to set qualifications for state judges—including age limits—is a decision "of the most fundamental sort for a sovereign entity." *FERC v. Mississippi*, 456 U.S. 742, 761 (1982) (recognizing the "power to make decisions and to set policy" as fundamental to sovereignty).

Where Congress legislates in an area of traditional state sovereignty, this Court demands that Congress "unambiguously" express its intent to alter the federal balance. *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985) (requiring Congress to make its intention "unmistakably clear in the language of the statute"). Nothing in the ADEA's text unambiguously subjects state judges selected under a merit-retention system to federal age-discrimination scrutiny.

### B. Missouri's Appointed Judges Are "Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level" Exempt from ADEA Coverage

The ADEA's definition of "employee" excludes "any person elected to public office . . . or an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri's judges fall within this exemption.

Judging is not merely mechanical application of settled rules. As the First Circuit recognized in language the Eighth Circuit adopted, "'[p]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Missouri's appellate judges do not merely resolve discrete disputes; through binding precedent they "establish common law for the entire state," 898 F.2d at 604 n.4, deciding questions with sweeping "ethical, moral, and public policy implications," *id.* (citing *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988)). Missouri's Chief Justice and presiding judges further exercise direct administrative and rulemaking authority over the judiciary itself. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4.

Reading the "policymaking" exception to exclude judges, as petitioners urge, would produce an implausible result: because judges are neither "elected officials," "personal staff," nor "immediate advisors" in the conventional sense, no exemption in § 630(f) would ever apply to *appointed* judges, even though *elected* judges are categorically exempt. Congress cannot be presumed to have intended so anomalous a distinction between two methods of selecting state judges who exercise identical constitutional powers. *See* 898 F.2d at 603.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT AGE FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE EQUAL PROTECTION

### A. Rational Basis Review Governs Because Age Is Not a Suspect Classification and There Is No Fundamental Right to Judicial Office

This Court held definitively in *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307 (1976), that mandatory retirement provisions for public officials are subject only to rational basis review. Age is not a suspect classification, and, as this Court stated, "[t]his Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental." *Id.* at 313. Petitioners possess no fundamental right to remain on the bench past age seventy. *See also San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973).

Under rational basis review, Missouri's provision "need not be tied to empirical data" and will be upheld if there is "any reasonably conceivable state of facts that could provide a rational basis." *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 314-15.

### B. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Age Rationally Serves Legitimate State Interests

Missouri's rule satisfies this deferential standard many times over. As the courts have repeatedly recognized, mandatory retirement for judges rationally addresses the reality that "the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode." *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979). It further "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges," a risk that "increases as a judge's age increases." *Id.* at 629.

Mandatory retirement also spares the state and its judiciary the institutional harm of individualized competency proceedings against aging jurists. As the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court explained in language the Eighth Circuit found persuasive, "[a] line drawn at age seventy eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence." *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver General*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). The rule likewise "makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary, thereby making the judiciary more representative of society." *Id.*

Petitioners may disagree with these judgments, but disagreement is not disproof of rationality. As the Eighth Circuit correctly concluded, "[o]ne might disagree with some or all of these rationales on either empirical or philosophical grounds and yet be unable properly to conclude that a state which thinks otherwise lacks a rational basis for its mandatory retirement rule for judges." 898 F.2d at 606.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
