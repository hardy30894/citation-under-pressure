**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that the amended trust established by Ralph H. Davis does not qualify for a marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7) of the Internal Revenue Code. The terms of the Amendment to the Declaration of Trust, executed by Mr. Davis on April 9, 1996, create a qualifying income interest for life in Evelyn L. Davis, the surviving spouse, and satisfy all statutory requirements for a QTIP deduction. The Commissioner’s contrary determination rests upon a misreading of the trust’s terms and an erroneous application of pre-1970 precedent. This Court should reverse.

**I. The Trust Conferred Upon Evelyn Davis an Unrestricted Right to All Trust Income for Life, Satisfying the “Qualifying Income Interest” Requirement of § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).**

Under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), a surviving spouse has a “qualifying income interest for life” if he or she is “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” The key inquiry is whether the spouse’s interest is “unrestricted” in the sense that the income is payable to her without limitation, subject only to the trustee’s reasonable discretion in determining the amount and timing of distributions.

The Amendment to the Declaration of Trust explicitly provides that, after Mr. Davis’s death and during the lifetime of his surviving spouse, “the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse, in quarter annual or more frequent installments, all of the net income from the trust estate as the trustee, in the trustee’s reasonable discretion, shall determine to be proper for the health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare of grantor’s surviving spouse in accordance with the surviving spouse’s accustomed manner of living.” (Emphasis added.)

This language unambiguously entitles Mrs. Davis to all of the trust’s net income, payable at least quarterly. The phrase “all of the net income” is not qualified by any limitation or condition that would restrict her entitlement. The trustee’s discretion is limited to determining *how much* income to pay—based on the spouse’s needs and accustomed standard of living—not *whether* she is entitled to income. This is precisely the type of interest recognized in *United States v. Bowers*, 336 U.S. 460 (1949), where the Court held that a surviving spouse’s right to “all the income” from a trust, payable at reasonable intervals, constituted a qualifying income interest, even though the trustee had discretion to determine the amount of distributions. The Court emphasized that “the right to income is not defeated by the fact that the amount is to be determined by the trustee in good faith.” Id. at 464.

Similarly, in *Commissioner v. Houghton*, 332 U.S. 325 (1947), the Court upheld a marital deduction where the surviving spouse was entitled to “all the income” from a trust, with the trustee having discretion to determine the amount and timing of payments. The Court stated: “The fact that the amount of income to be paid is left to the discretion of the trustee does not destroy the right of the spouse to the income.” Id. at 329. The same principle applies here. The trustee’s discretion is not a power to withhold income, but a power to allocate it in a manner consistent with the spouse’s needs. The trust does not permit the trustee to deny income altogether; it merely allows flexibility in the amount and timing of distributions.

**II. The Trust Does Not Confer Any Power to Appoint Trust Property to a Third Party, Satisfying the Second Prong of § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii).**

The second requirement of a qualifying income interest is that “no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). The Commissioner argues that Mrs. Davis’s power as successor trustee, coupled with the trustee’s discretion to invade principal, creates a “power of appointment” that disqualifies the trust.

This argument fails. The power conferred upon Mrs. Davis as successor trustee is not a power to appoint trust assets to third parties. She is named as the first successor trustee *solely* to administer the trust during her lifetime. The Amendment provides that she “shall serve as co-trustees” only if she is unable to serve, and that her daughters shall serve if she predeceases or becomes incapacitated. There is no provision allowing her to transfer trust assets to anyone other than herself or her estate. The power to invade principal for her benefit is not a power to appoint property to others; it is a power to use principal to supplement income when necessary to maintain her health and support. This is a standard feature of life-income trusts and has long been recognized as consistent with § 2056(b)(7).

In *Commissioner v. Houghton*, 332 U.S. 325 (1947), the Court upheld a marital deduction where the trustee had discretion to invade principal for the spouse’s benefit. The Court held that such a power “does not constitute a power of appointment” within the meaning of the statute. Id. at 329. The same reasoning applies here. The trustee’s power to invade principal is not a power to transfer assets to third parties; it is a fiduciary duty to ensure the spouse’s maintenance and welfare. The trust does not permit the spouse to direct the disposition of trust property to anyone else. The power is limited to her own benefit and is exercised in the trustee’s reasonable discretion.

**III. The Trust Meets the Statutory Requirements for a QTIP Election Under § 2056(b)(7).**

The trust satisfies all three elements of § 2056(b)(7)(B): (1) property passes from the decedent; (2) the surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life; and (3) an election under § 2056(b)(7) is made. The estate properly filed the election on the estate tax return. The Commissioner has not challenged the election’s validity.
