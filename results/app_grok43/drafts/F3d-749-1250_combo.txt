I. The District Court Plainly Erred by Admitting Expert Testimony That Directly Assessed Credibility and Usurped the Jury’s Function.

Agent Jones’s testimony—that Stanley’s statements “did not make sense,” displayed “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element,” had “never” been seen “with an innocent person,” and were “deceptive” when invoking faith—invaded the exclusive province of the jury to determine credibility. Quercia v. United States, 289 U.S. 466, 468 (1933) (jury alone weighs truthfulness). Such opinions are inadmissible because they substitute the expert’s judgment for the jurors’. United States v. Johnson, 319 U.S. 503, 519 (1943) (expert testimony must not “invade the province of the jury”).

Expert evidence is confined to matters beyond ordinary knowledge and must rest on a demonstrable scientific foundation. Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). Testimony diagnosing deception from demeanor or religious references satisfies neither requirement. See Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948) (character and credibility rest on community reputation, not individualized expert diagnosis).

Because Stanley did not object, review is for plain error. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). The error was plain: longstanding precedent forbids witnesses from vouching for or against a defendant’s veracity. Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 82 (1942) (verdict must rest on evidence, not improper opinion).

II. The Error Affected Substantial Rights and Seriously Undermined the Fairness of the Proceedings.

The testimony struck at the heart of Stanley’s defense by branding his exculpatory statements inherently false. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (error affects substantial rights when it “had substantial influence” on the verdict). No curative instruction was given, and the government relied on the agent’s conclusions to rebut Stanley’s account. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88-89 (1935) (prosecutorial presentation of improper evidence requires reversal when it prejudices the accused).

The resulting prejudice is not cured by other evidence. The jury at the first trial deadlocked; the second verdict rested in material part on the expert’s forbidden credibility assessment. Because the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” relief is required. Atkinson, 297 U.S. at 160.

The conviction must be reversed.
