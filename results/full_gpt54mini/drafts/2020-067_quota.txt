**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals correctly refused to confine plain-error review to the trial record alone. Plain-error doctrine exists to determine whether a forfeited error “affected substantial rights” and whether correction is necessary to protect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 734-36 (1993). That inquiry is not artificially cabined to the evidence that happened to be presented to the jury when the question is whether the defendant can show a reasonable probability of a different outcome and whether the appellate court should exercise its discretion to notice the error. Id. at 734; United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 81-83 (2004).

1. **Plain-error review permits consideration of the whole appellate record.**  
Rule 52(b) “authorizes the Courts of Appeals to correct only particularly egregious errors,” but it does not prescribe a wooden evidentiary partition between the jury record and other reliable materials bearing on prejudice. Olano, 507 U.S. at 736. In assessing prejudice, courts ask whether the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” Id. at 734. That assessment necessarily turns on the record as a whole, not solely the evidence submitted to the jury on an omitted element. See Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 469-70 (1997) (considering the entire record in rejecting plain-error relief); United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 632-33 (2002) (same).

This Court has repeatedly looked beyond the specific omission at trial to the larger record when deciding whether an unpreserved error warrants relief. In Johnson, the Court held that where evidence of the omitted element was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted,” reversal was unwarranted. 520 U.S. at 470. In Cotton, the Court held that even a structural defect in the indictment did not satisfy plain error because the evidence of the omitted fact was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted.” 535 U.S. at 633. Those decisions would make little sense if appellate courts were forbidden to examine the remainder of the record for prejudice.

2. **Rehaif errors are not exempt from ordinary plain-error principles.**  
In Rehaif v. United States, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2200 (2019), the Court held that the Government must prove both possession and knowledge of status under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g). But Rehaif did not alter the plain-error framework. To the contrary, in Greer v. United States, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2096-97 (2021), the Court explained that a defendant raising an unpreserved Rehaif claim “has the burden” to show a “reasonable probability” that, but for the error, the outcome would have been different. The Court specifically held that appellate courts may consider “the entire record” when evaluating substantial rights and the fairness inquiry. Id. at 2098-2100. And the Court rejected the notion that review must be limited to the trial record alone, because defendants often can show no prejudice where the record reveals prior felony convictions and no basis to think the defendant would have contested status. Id. at 2097-98.

That is exactly this case. Greer pleaded guilty to being a felon in possession and had multiple prior felony convictions. Greer, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-98. On remand, the Eleventh Circuit properly consulted Greer’s prior convictions and the full record in determining that he could not show a reasonable probability of a different result. See id. at 2098-2100. Nothing in Rehaif or Rule 52(b) requires blinders that would prevent a court from considering facts already established in the criminal history record and elsewhere in the case file.

3. **The contrary rule would distort plain-error review and produce windfalls.**  
Plain-error review is meant to prevent miscarriages of justice, not to create them. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). The Court has therefore cautioned that appellate discretion under Rule 52(b) should be exercised only when an error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985). That standard asks whether correction is truly necessary in light of the whole case. Johnson, 520 U.S. at 469-70; Cotton, 535 U.S. at 632-33.

A rule restricting review to trial evidence alone would be especially unwarranted in Rehaif cases, where the defendant’s status as a felon is often undisputed and reflected in undisputed court records. See Greer, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-98. It would let defendants obtain reversal despite proof elsewhere in the record that they knew of their status, contrary to the logic of Olano, Johnson, Cotton, and Greer. Plain error does not require appellate courts to close their eyes to reality.

4. **The Eleventh Circuit’s approach was correct.**  
The Eleventh Circuit applied the proper plain-error standard and looked to the record as a whole to determine prejudice and the fairness inquiry. That approach accords with this Court’s cases and with the very nature of Rule 52(b). See Olano, 507 U.S. at 734-37; Johnson, 520 U.S. at 469-70; Cotton, 535 U.S. at 632-33; Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. at 81-83; Young, 470 U.S. at 15; Rehaif, 139 S. Ct. at 2200; Greer, 141 S. Ct. at 2096-2100.

The judgment should be affirmed.
