ARGUMENT

A federal appellate court conducting plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may examine the entire record to determine whether an error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). The inquiry is not confined to evidence presented to the jury.

This Court has repeatedly held that the prejudice analysis requires consideration of “the record as a whole.” United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002). In Vonn the Court looked beyond the plea colloquy to the full proceedings, explaining that the defendant bears the burden of showing “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, he would not have entered the plea.” Id. at 62. The same principle governs Rehaif errors. See United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (requiring “a showing of ‘a reasonable probability that, but for [the error claimed], the result of the proceeding would have been different’”).

The fourth prong likewise demands a “case-specific and fact-intensive” examination of the entire record. Puckett v. United States, 556 U.S. 129, 142 (2009). Courts must assess whether the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 467 (1997) (quoting Olano, 507 U.S. at 736). That assessment routinely includes uncontroverted facts outside the trial evidence, such as prior convictions. Henderson v. United States, 568 U.S. 266, 276 (2013).

Limiting review to the trial transcript would contradict the Court’s instruction that plain-error analysis is “flexible” and sensitive to the “particular facts of each case.” Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 138 S. Ct. 1897, 1906 (2018). In Molina-Martinez v. United States, 136 S. Ct. 1338 (2016), the Court examined the presentence report and sentencing transcript—materials never shown to the jury—to evaluate whether a Guidelines error affected substantial rights. Id. at 1346-47. The same materials are properly considered here.

The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted consistently with precedent when it reviewed Greer’s prior convictions and the full record. Because that review revealed overwhelming evidence that Greer knew his felon status, he cannot satisfy either the third or fourth prong of plain-error review. Olano, 507 U.S. at 734-37. The judgment should be affirmed.
