ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred by Dismissing the Hostile Work Environment Claim on the Ground That No Sexually Offensive Acts Occurred Within the Limitations Period.

Title VII prohibits a hostile work environment on the basis of sex. Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 66 (1986). To prevail, a plaintiff must show that the workplace was permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment. Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). Conduct need not be overtly sexual; facially neutral acts may support the claim when they occur in a context of sex-based animus. Alfano v. Costello, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002).

The district court confined its analysis to the three notes and hotel-room calls from 1998–2001, concluding that no “sexually offensive acts” fell within the 300-day window preceding the September 2003 EEOC charge. This approach contravenes the requirement that incidents be examined “in their totality.” Alfano, 294 F.3d at 375. The record contains repeated sex-based differential treatment after March 2002: Irving’s refusal to permit email or telephone communication, his denial of promotion assessment while male colleagues advanced, the counseling plan imposed only on Moll, the prohibition on working from home while male Sales Engineers continued the practice, the denial of a July 5, 2002 vacation request granted to less-tenured males, and the exclusion of women from client entertainment. These acts, though not sexually explicit, are properly considered together with the earlier incidents because they bear on whether the environment was hostile on the basis of sex. See id.

The district court’s truncation of the record also conflicts with the principle that a hostile-work-environment claim is timely if any act contributing to the hostile environment occurred within the filing period. National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan, 536 U.S. 101, 117 (2002). Because the later, facially neutral incidents may be viewed as part of the same unlawful practice, summary judgment on timeliness grounds was improper.

II. The District Court’s Alternative Reliance on the Sham-Issue Doctrine Does Not Justify Dismissal.

A non-party witness’s later testimony that conflicts with an earlier statement does not trigger the sham-issue rule applicable to party affidavits that contradict prior deposition testimony. See Perma Research & Development Co. v. Singer Co., 410 F.2d 572, 578 (2d Cir. 1969). The district court therefore could not disregard the corroborative testimony of other employees on that basis.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be vacated insofar as it dismissed the hostile-work-environment claim.
