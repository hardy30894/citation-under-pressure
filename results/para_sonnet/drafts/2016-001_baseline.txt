# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 289'S PLAIN TEXT LIMITS DAMAGES TO PROFITS ATTRIBUTABLE TO THE INFRINGING "ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE," NOT THE ENTIRE END PRODUCT

Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that a design patent infringer "shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit" for applying a patented design "to any article of manufacture." 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute's operative language draws a critical distinction between the "article of manufacture" to which the design is applied and the infringer's end product sold to consumers. Congress used the term "article of manufacture," not "product as sold" or "end product." Where Congress employs a particular term in a statute, courts must give effect to that specific choice. See Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) ("[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.").

The "article of manufacture" to which a design patent applies is not necessarily coextensive with the entire commercial product. This Court has long recognized that a single product may comprise multiple "articles of manufacture" for patent purposes. In the design patent context specifically, an article of manufacture can be merely a *component* of a larger, multicomponent product. Apple's own design patents illustrate this point: the patents at issue claim ornamental designs for a rounded rectangular front face, a grid of icons, and a bezel—discrete components of the smartphone, not the smartphone as a whole with its internal circuitry, processor, memory, and thousands of other component technologies that Samsung independently engineered and that bear no relationship whatsoever to the patented ornamental designs.

## II. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT'S CATEGORICAL RULE THAT § 289 DAMAGES MUST ALWAYS BE CALCULATED ON THE ENTIRE PRODUCT CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH THE STATUTE OR THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT

The Federal Circuit below did not merely affirm a jury verdict supported by substantial evidence—it adopted, in its underlying panel and en banc proceedings on other issues, a categorical rule foreclosing any factual inquiry into whether the relevant "article of manufacture" was the entire smartphone or merely a component thereof. That approach conflicts with the statutory text and with basic principles of remedial proportionality that this Court has applied even under statutes awarding "total profit."

This Court's decision in *Root v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co.*, 105 U.S. 189 (1882), construing a predecessor design patent damages provision, cautioned against damages awards that bear no relationship to the value the infringer actually derived from copying the patented design. Although the current statutory text employs "total profit" language deliberately chosen to overrule the apportionment requirement this Court had imposed for utility patents in cases like *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), and *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), Congress's 1887 amendment addressed a specific problem: the difficulty design patent owners faced in proving which portion of profits stemmed from the design as opposed to other features of an article. That amendment did not purport to redefine what constitutes the relevant "article of manufacture" in the first instance—it merely eliminated apportionment *within* that article once properly identified.

The court below effectively read the "article of manufacture" inquiry out of the statute entirely, treating it as automatically synonymous with the end product as sold to consumers regardless of the scope of the patented design. Such a reading renders the words "article of manufacture" surplusage, contrary to the settled principle that courts must, "if possible," give effect to "every clause and word of a statute." *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (internal quotation marks omitted).

## III. A PROPER FRAMEWORK REQUIRES A TWO-STEP INQUIRY: IDENTIFY THE RELEVANT ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE, THEN CALCULATE TOTAL PROFIT ON THAT ARTICLE

Petitioner urges adoption of a two-step framework faithful to the statutory text. First, the finder of fact must identify the "article of manufacture" to which the infringed design has been applied, considering factors including the scope of the design claimed in the patent, the degree to which the component is viewed as a separate unit from the whole, whether the component is a separately manufactured or purchased item, and the conceptual distinctiveness of the component from the whole product. Second, once that article is identified, § 289's "total profit" measure applies to the profits attributable to *that* article, without further apportionment—consistent with Congress's evident intent to spare patentees the evidentiary burden condemned in the *Dobson* cases.

This framework respects both halves of the statutory text. It honors "total profit" by refusing to require patentees to apportion profits attributable to the design *within* the relevant article. But it equally honors "article of manufacture" by requiring the fact-finder to first determine what that article actually is, rather than assuming as a matter of law that it is always the entire multicomponent commercial product.

## IV. THE JUDGMENT BELOW SHOULD BE VACATED AND REMANDED

Because the jury here was instructed, consistent with the Federal Circuit's categorical approach, that it could award Samsung's entire smartphone profits without ever considering whether the smartphone itself—or only a component such as its screen or housing—constituted the relevant "article of manufacture," the damages award cannot stand. The case should be remanded for a proper damages determination under the framework set forth above.
