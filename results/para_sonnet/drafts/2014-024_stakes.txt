# ARGUMENT

## SECTION 875(c) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENTS TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS THREATS

### A. The Presumption of Scienter That Applies to Criminal Statutes Requires Reading a Subjective-Intent Element Into §875(c)

This Court has long recognized "the background presumption . . . that a defendant must know the facts that make his conduct fit the definition of the offense." *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 619 (1994). Congress is presumed to have "intend[ed] to require a defendant to possess a culpable mental state" with regard to each element that "criminalizes otherwise innocent conduct." *Id.* at 605, 618–19. The mere transmission of words describing violence is not "innocent" in the way that, for example, receiving a package is innocent absent knowledge of its contents—but words are different from other conduct because the First Amendment protects the vast majority of violent, angry, or disturbing speech. Given that §875(c) reaches directly into the heart of protected expression, this Court should presume that Congress meant to require proof that the speaker himself intended his words to be understood as a threat, not merely that a reasonable listener might so understand them.

This Court applied that presumption in *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), holding that criminal statutes silent as to mental state should ordinarily be read to require *mens rea*, because "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention . . . is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." *Id.* at 250. Section 875(c) is silent as to intent, but that silence cannot be read to eliminate the requirement altogether—particularly where the conduct being regulated is speech.

### B. This Court's True-Threats Doctrine Demands Proof of the Speaker's Subjective Intent

The Third Circuit's objective-intent, "reasonable speaker" standard cannot be reconciled with this Court's decision in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003). In *Black*, this Court explained that "'[t]rue threats' encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The word "means"—not "should have known" or "would reasonably be understood"—locates the constitutional inquiry in the speaker's own mental state. *Black* further explained that the rationale for stripping true threats of First Amendment protection is that "the speaker" intends to place the victim "in fear of bodily harm or death." *Id.* This formulation is inescapably speaker-focused.

The Third Circuit acknowledged that *Black* announced this standard, framing the question below as "whether the Supreme Court decision in *Virginia v. Black* . . . overturns" the Third Circuit's pre-existing objective standard from *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which held a statement is a true threat when "a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat." The Third Circuit answered that question incorrectly. *Black*'s holding that a speaker "means to communicate" a serious intent to commit violence, 538 U.S. at 359, is not compatible with a rule under which a defendant can be convicted based solely on how a reasonable *listener* might have construed ambiguous, fragmented statements—including the rap-lyric-style, stream-of-consciousness Facebook posts at issue here.

### C. An Objective Standard Impermissibly Chills Protected Speech

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that the First Amendment requires "breathing space" for protected expression, and that statutes regulating speech must be construed to avoid punishing individuals for negligent misjudgments about how their words will be received by others. In *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988), this Court reaffirmed that speech on matters of public concern—even speech that is "vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp,"—receives robust protection, quoting *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 270 (1964). An objective standard for §875(c) permits a jury to convict based purely on a negligence theory: the defendant "should have known" his words would be taken as threatening, even though he did not intend them that way and did not even recognize the risk. That is precisely the kind of strict or negligence-based liability this Court has refused to impose in the speech context, because such liability inevitably chills protected expression by punishing speakers for the unforeseen reactions of others.

The facts here illustrate the danger. Elonis posted material styled explicitly as rap lyrics, invoking a well-known genre in which violent, exaggerated language is common artistic expression. He posted a link to the Wikipedia entry on "Freedom of Speech" alongside one of the posts at issue, and he directly asserted that his words did not constitute punishable threats. A jury applying a purely objective standard was never asked to determine whether Elonis *intended* these statements as threats or merely as therapeutic, cathartic, or artistic expression that he did not intend anyone to take literally. The Third Circuit's standard permitted the jury to convict him without ever deciding that question.

### D. The Jury Instruction Was Reversible Error

The district court instructed the jury that "[a] statement is a true threat when a defendant intentionally makes a statement in a context or under such circumstances wherein a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted . . . as a serious expression of an intention to inflict bodily injury." This instruction required only that Elonis intentionally make the statement—not that he intend it to be understood as threatening. Under *Black*, the jury was required to find that Elonis himself meant to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit violence. 538 U.S. at 359. Because the jury instead was permitted to convict based on what a "reasonable person would foresee," the instruction failed to require the jury to find the essential element that the First Amendment demands.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
