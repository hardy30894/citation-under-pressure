Respondent’s position is correct: the navigability-for-title inquiry turns on whether the relevant river segment was navigable at the time of statehood, because state title under the equal-footing doctrine is fixed at the moment of admission to the Union. The Supreme Court has repeatedly said so.

In United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court held that the test for title is whether the river was “navigable in fact” at the time of statehood, and it distinguished that title inquiry from later commercial use. Id. at 75-76. The Court explained that title depends on the condition of the waterway “at the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” Id. at 76. That rule follows from the equal-footing doctrine, under which new states enter the Union with the same sovereign rights as the original states. Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 228-29 (1845); Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894).

The Court has consistently reaffirmed that navigability for title is measured at statehood. In United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), the Court stated that title to the beds of navigable waters passed to the state “at the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” Id. at 55-56. In United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), the Court again held that the decisive question is navigability “at the time of statehood.” Id. at 14. And in Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), the Court explained that “title to the beds of navigable waters passed to the States upon their admission to the Union.” Id. at 551. That rule is not merely a background principle; it is the operative constitutional test.

Petitioner’s contrary theory would collapse the title inquiry into a modern, use-based assessment detached from the moment title vested. That is not the law. The Court has made clear that later improvements, dams, changing channels, or subsequent commercial conditions do not determine whether the State acquired title at statehood. United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940), recognized that navigability for regulatory purposes may account for later artificial or natural improvements, but it did not alter the statehood rule for title. The Court specifically distinguished regulatory navigability from title navigability. Id. at 407; see also PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana, 565 U.S. 576, 592-93 (2012) (explaining that title navigability asks whether the river segment was navigable “at the time of statehood,” while later events may inform, but do not replace, that inquiry). The Court there held that “the relevant time for determining navigability for title purposes is the time of statehood.” Id. at 592.

The statehood rule also follows from the nature of the property right at issue. Equal-footing title is a sovereign prerogative, and it cannot depend on post-admission conditions unrelated to what the new State received upon entry. Shively, 152 U.S. at 26-27; Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922). If a river was not navigable in fact at statehood, the bed did not pass to the State by operation of the equal-footing doctrine. Utah, 283 U.S. at 75-76.

Accordingly, the district court correctly treated statehood navigability as the controlling constitutional test. The inquiry is historical, segment-specific, and fixed at admission to the Union. That rule governs here, and petitioner’s attempt to substitute a modern or retrospective benchmark should be rejected.
