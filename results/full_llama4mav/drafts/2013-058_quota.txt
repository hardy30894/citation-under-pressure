The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker, holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), should be affirmed. The plain language of the statute exempts "retirement funds" without requiring that they be the debtor's own retirement funds. As the Fifth Circuit held in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486, 489 (5th Cir. 2012), and the Eighth Circuit Bankruptcy Appellate Panel held in In re Nessa, 426 B.R. 312, 314 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), the absence of the phrase "of the debtor" in §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) indicates that the exemption is not limited to the debtor's own retirement funds.

The Seventh Circuit's contrary holding relies on a strained reading of the statute, which ignores the Supreme Court's directive to interpret the Bankruptcy Code according to its plain meaning. See United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The Court has consistently held that exemptions in the Bankruptcy Code should be construed liberally in favor of debtors. See, e.g., Schwab v. Reilly, 560 U.S. 770, 791 (2010); Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473, 487 (1904).

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit's decision creates an arbitrary distinction between inherited IRAs and other types of exempt property. For example, a debtor's interest in a tenancy by the entirety is exempt under §522(b)(3)(B) regardless of when the debtor acquired the interest. See, e.g., In re Hunter, 970 F.2d 299, 302 (7th Cir. 1992). Similarly, a debtor's interest in a retirement account is exempt under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) regardless of when the debtor acquired the interest. See, e.g., In re Youngblood, 281 B.R. 647, 650 (Bankr. N.D. Iowa 2002).

The Seventh Circuit's reliance on the fact that an inherited IRA is not a "retirement vehicle" for the debtor is misplaced. As the Supreme Court has held, the Bankruptcy Code's exemptions are not limited to property that is currently being used for its intended purpose. See, e.g., Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622, 628 (1943) (holding that a homestead exemption applies even if the debtor is not currently living in the home). The fact that an inherited IRA is subject to certain restrictions and tax implications does not change its fundamental character as "retirement funds." See, e.g., Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 332 (2005) (holding that IRAs are "retirement funds" within the meaning of §522(d)(10)(E)).

Furthermore, the Seventh Circuit's decision is inconsistent with the policy underlying the Bankruptcy Code's exemptions, which is to provide debtors with a fresh start. See, e.g., Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934); Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554-55 (1915). By allowing debtors to exempt inherited IRAs, Congress intended to protect retirement savings and promote financial security for debtors and their families. See, e.g., Rousey, 544 U.S. at 332; Patterson v. Shumate, 504 U.S. 753, 762-63 (1992).

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker should be affirmed. The plain language of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts inherited IRAs from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy, and the Seventh Circuit's contrary holding is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's precedent and the policy underlying the Bankruptcy Code's exemptions. The decision should be guided by the liberal construction of exemptions in favor of debtors, as mandated by the Supreme Court in Schwab v. Reilly, 560 U.S. at 791.
