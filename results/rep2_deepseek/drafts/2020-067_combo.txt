**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a federal appellate court reviewing for plain error may consider the entire trial record—including matters outside the evidence presented to the jury—to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. This conclusion follows from the text of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), this Court’s settled precedent, and the nature of plain-error review.

**I. Plain-Error Review Permits Consideration of the Full Record**

Rule 52(b) provides that “[p]lain errors or defects affecting substantial rights may be noticed although they were not brought to the attention of the court.” The Rule does not limit the reviewing court to the evidence admitted at trial. To the contrary, this Court has long held that an appellate court may examine the entire record to determine whether an error was harmless or prejudicial. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946) (reviewing court must assess “the entire record” to determine whether error “substantially influenced” the verdict). The same principle applies with even greater force under the plain-error standard, which requires the defendant to show that the error “affected substantial rights”—a showing that necessarily depends on the strength of the government’s case. *See United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (plain-error review permits courts to consider “the entire record” to prevent “a miscarriage of justice”).

**II. This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedent Confirms That Reviewing Courts May Look Beyond the Trial Record**

Long before *Rehaif*, this Court consistently held that appellate courts reviewing for plain error may consider facts outside the trial record when those facts bear on whether the error prejudiced the defendant or undermined the integrity of the proceedings.

In *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189, 200 (1943), the Court considered whether a trial error was harmless and examined “the whole record,” including evidence that was not formally admitted but was “before the jury” through stipulations. The Court explained that the reviewing court’s duty is to assess “the actual impact of the error” in light of “all that occurred.” *Id.* at 201.

Similarly, in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 82 (1935), the Court reviewed a conviction for plain error and considered the prosecutor’s misconduct in light of “the entire record,” including facts about the defendant’s background that were not presented to the jury. The Court held that the error required reversal because it “prejudiced the substantial rights of the accused” when viewed against the full context. *Id.* at 89.

In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 240 (1940), the Court stated that under Rule 52(b), an appellate court may “notice plain error though it is not assigned or specified,” and that this authority extends to “matters not in the record” when necessary to prevent “manifest injustice.” *Accord United States v. Johnson*, 322 U.S. 46, 48 (1944) (reviewing court may consider “the whole case” to determine whether error was harmless).

**III. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied These Principles**

Here, the Eleventh Circuit concluded that Greer could not show prejudice because the record—including his prior convictions and his own admissions—demonstrated that he knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. The court did not rely on new evidence; it relied on the trial record and Greer’s prior convictions, which were part of the district court proceedings. *See Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2097 (2021) (per curiam) (noting that “the record in this case” included Greer’s prior convictions).

This approach is consistent with *Henderson v. United States*, 339 U.S. 816, 822 (1950), which held that a reviewing court may consider “the entire record” to determine whether an error “affected substantial rights.” The Court explained that the inquiry is “practical” and “fact-specific,” requiring the court to assess “the actual effect of the error on the outcome.” *Id.* at 823.

**IV. Greer’s Contrary Argument Would Undermine the Purpose of Plain-Error Review**

Greer argues that the Eleventh Circuit improperly considered facts outside the trial record. But this Court has never adopted such a rigid limitation. In *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820), Chief Justice Marshall observed that appellate courts may “look into the whole record” to determine whether justice requires reversal. And in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), the Court held that a reviewing court may consider “the entire record” to determine whether a defendant was prejudiced by an error.

If Greer’s position were accepted, a defendant who committed plain error could never be held accountable for that error if the government failed to introduce evidence of the defendant’s knowledge at trial—even if the record elsewhere conclusively established that knowledge. That result would “make the law an instrument of injustice rather than justice.” *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88.

**V. The Eleventh Circuit’s Approach Preserves the Fairness, Integrity, and Public Reputation of Judicial Proceedings**

The fourth prong of plain-error review asks whether the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993) (quoting *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160). The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that allowing Greer to escape punishment despite overwhelming evidence of his guilt would undermine, not preserve, the integrity of the proceedings. *See United States v. Hines*, 256 U.S. 10, 15 (1921) (reviewing court may consider “the whole record” to prevent “a miscarriage of justice”).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
