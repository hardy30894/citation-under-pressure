**ARGUMENT**

I. **The tribal court denied Selam compulsory process by failing to secure the attendance of defense witnesses necessary to a fair trial.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act guarantees that “[n]o Indian tribe in exercising powers of self-government shall . . . deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right . . . to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This Court has recognized that ICRA is construed to protect fundamental fairness in tribal criminal proceedings. See *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 56 (1978); *Poodry v. Tonawanda Band of Seneca Indians*, 85 F.3d 874, 889-90 (2d Cir. 1996).

Selam did all that the record fairly required. He supplied counsel with a list of five potential character witnesses before trial. Although counsel sought his assistance, the record shows only that Selam did not help locate them; it does not show that he knowingly relinquished the right to subpoena witnesses. To the contrary, the tribal judge and counsel advised him of the right to compel witnesses, but no subpoena process was ever meaningfully invoked on his behalf. A criminal defendant’s compulsory-process right is violated when the defendant is deprived of the opportunity to present witnesses material to the defense. See *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19-23 (1967); *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 408-09 (1988).

The district court appears to have treated Selam’s failure to request subpoenas as dispositive. But waiver of a fundamental trial right is not lightly inferred. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). Nothing in this record shows an intentional relinquishment of the right to present witnesses. Instead, the record shows a lay advocate, not trained counsel, attempting to proceed in tribal court, with at least two witnesses later determined to be harmful and the remaining witnesses never secured. The resulting absence of defense witnesses deprived Selam of the ability to develop a defense and to test the prosecution’s proof through live testimony. See *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973); *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690-91 (1986).

The denial was prejudicial. In the first case, the prosecution relied on the child’s in-court testimony and a police investigator’s repetition of her prior statement. In the second, the child did not testify at all; the prosecution instead used multiple hearsay witnesses to present her accusation. Where the prosecution’s proof turns on credibility, the defendant’s inability to present witnesses in his favor is especially serious. See *Washington*, 388 U.S. at 19; *Crane*, 476 U.S. at 690. The habeas court therefore erred in concluding that compulsory process was not denied.

II. **Selam did not waive his confrontation claim, and the conviction in the second case cannot stand because hearsay testimony was admitted without the constitutionally required opportunity for confrontation.**

The ICRA separately guarantees “the right . . . to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). The right of confrontation is a basic component of a fair criminal trial. See *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 403-06 (1965); *Dutton v. Evans*, 400 U.S. 74, 89 (1970). It ordinarily requires that testimonial accusers appear for cross-examination unless unavailability and prior opportunity to examine are shown. See *Ohio v. Roberts*, 448 U.S. 56, 63-66 (1980); *Barber v. Page*, 390 U.S. 719, 722-25 (1968).

The district court held that Selam waived this claim by not presenting it in the tribal court of appeals. But federal habeas courts may not impose a waiver where the record does not show a knowing and intelligent relinquishment of a constitutional claim. *Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464; *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963). Selam litigated his compulsory-process claim in the tribal appellate court; his confrontation challenge arose from the same trials and the same hearsay proof. Nothing in the record indicates that he intentionally abandoned the claim. At most, he failed to label it separately. That is not enough to forfeit review of a fundamental trial right. See *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 485-88 (1986).

On the merits, the confrontation violation is clear. In the second trial, the five-year-old victim did not testify. The prosecution instead introduced her accusations through Dr. Beamer, her parents, and Investigator Clements. That is precisely the sort of substitute proof the Confrontation Clause disfavors absent a showing that the declarant is unavailable and the statement bears adequate indicia of reliability. See *Roberts*, 448 U.S. at 65-66; *Barber*, 390 U.S. at 724-25. No such showing was made here. Selam was never afforded an opportunity to cross-examine the child whose out-of-court statements formed the core of the prosecution’s proof.

Because the second conviction rests on uncross-examined testimonial hearsay, and because Selam did not knowingly waive his confrontation right, the judgment should be reversed and the petition granted. At minimum, the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with ICRA and the Sixth Amendment principles incorporated in it. See *Dutton*, 400 U.S. at 89; *Roberts*, 448 U.S. at 63-66.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court should reverse the judgment denying habeas relief and remand with instructions to grant the petition.
