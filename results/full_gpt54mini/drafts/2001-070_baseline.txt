## Argument

### I. The Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of persons with mental retardation.

The Court’s Eighth Amendment precedents ask whether a punishment is inconsistent with “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). The answer here is yes: the national consensus has changed since Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), and capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders is now barred by the Constitution.

In Penry, the Court held only that, on the record then before it, there was not yet a sufficient national consensus to conclude that the Eighth Amendment forbade execution of the mentally retarded. 492 U.S. at 340. But Penry expressly recognized that such a result could follow when “objective indicia” of legislative and sentencing judgment demonstrated a settled national judgment. Id. at 330-35. Since Penry, that judgment has emerged.

The most important objective evidence is legislative enactment. A substantial number of States have now prohibited the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and the movement has been one-directional: toward exemption, not retention. That pattern is precisely the sort of “objective indicia” the Court relied on in earlier Eighth Amendment cases. See Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584, 593-97 (1977); Enmund v. Florida, 458 U.S. 782, 789-93 (1982); Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 408-10 (1986). When the States act in a sustained and consistent way, their judgments illuminate the “evolving standards” inquiry. Trop, 356 U.S. at 101.

The same conclusion follows from the Court’s treatment of mentally impaired defendants in the capital context. In Penry, the Court acknowledged that mental retardation is relevant both to culpability and to punishment. 492 U.S. at 322-28. That reasoning remains sound and now supports a categorical bar. Persons with mental retardation have diminished capacities to understand and process information, to learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, to control impulses, and to understand the reactions of others. Those characteristics directly undermine the retributive and deterrent justifications for the death penalty. See Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 183-87 (1976). If the punishment must be reserved for the most blameworthy offenders, execution of the mentally retarded fails that test. See Atkins v. Commonwealth, 260 Va. 375, 533 S.E.2d 349 (2000), rev’d, 536 U.S. 304 (2002).

The Commonwealth’s reliance on Penry is misplaced because Penry itself was not a final constitutional endorsement of executions in this setting. It was a snapshot of the law and the national consensus at that time. 492 U.S. at 340. This Court has not hesitated to revisit prior Eighth Amendment judgments when societal consensus and understanding changed. See Ford, 477 U.S. at 408-10; Coker, 433 U.S. at 593-97. That is exactly what should occur here.

Nor is this case controlled by the rule that the Court generally defers to democratic judgment in punishment policy. The Eighth Amendment is not frozen in 1791, nor even in 1989. Trop, 356 U.S. at 101. Where the States have converged on a categorical exemption and where the penological justifications for death are materially weakened, the Constitution requires the same result nationwide.

### II. Atkins’s execution would not advance the penological goals of capital punishment and would create an unacceptable risk of arbitrary punishment.

The death penalty is justified only insofar as it serves retribution and deterrence. Gregg, 428 U.S. at 183-87. Those rationales have diminished force with respect to offenders with mental retardation. Because of their impairments, such defendants are less able to understand the consequences of conduct, less able to assist counsel effectively, and more vulnerable to false or exaggerated confessions and to the influence of others. Those realities increase the risk that capital sentencing will be unreliable and arbitrary, concerns at the core of the Eighth Amendment. See Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280, 304-05 (1976); Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604-05 (1978).

The Court has long insisted that capital punishment must rest on individualized sentencing. See Zant v. Stephens, 462 U.S. 862, 879-80 (1983). But individualized consideration does not mean the State may execute offenders whose diminished culpability removes them from the class of the death-eligible. As in Ford, the relevant question is whether the Constitution tolerates a punishment that offends basic standards of fairness and decency. 477 U.S. at 409-10. It does not.

### III. The judgment below should be reversed.

Because the Eighth Amendment bars the execution of mentally retarded persons, Atkins’s death sentence cannot stand. The Court should reverse the Virginia Supreme Court and remand for entry of a sentence other than death. See Penry, 492 U.S. at 340; Trop, 356 U.S. at 101; Gregg, 428 U.S. at 183-87.
