# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ABUSED ITS DISCRETION BY REFUSING TO RESUBMIT THE AMBIGUOUS VERDICT TO THE JURY FOR CLARIFICATION

When a jury returns a verdict that is ambiguous or internally inconsistent, the trial court has a duty to resolve the ambiguity by resubmitting the matter to the jury before it is discharged, rather than guessing at the jury's intent after the fact. See *Domeracki v. Humble Oil & Refining Co.*, 443 F.2d 1245, 1251 (3d Cir. 1971) (holding that where a verdict was ambiguous, "the trial judge should have submitted the matter back to the jury for clarification" while the jury was still available to correct its own error); *Global Van Lines, Inc. v. Kechijian*, 622 F.2d 1032, 1039 (9th Cir. 1980) (same). This rule exists precisely because the jury, not the court, is the body charged with finding the facts, and only the jury can definitively say what it meant by its own verdict.

Here, the verdict was unmistakably ambiguous. The jury found on Form 1 that Enron breached the contract and awarded Unit $70,768.02—a figure that is, to the penny, approximately equal to the unpaid invoice amount of $157,262.36 minus the set-off figure of $86,494.30 that the jury separately entered on Form 3. That arithmetic coincidence is not accidental; it demonstrates that the jury had already performed the net-damages calculation the instructions permitted it to make if it found only substantial performance, and had already accounted for the cost of correcting the pipe separations within its Form 1 award. The jury's subsequent entry of the identical amount on Form 3 is reasonably read as a duplicate notation of the very same deduction, not as evidence that the jury intended Enron to receive an *additional* $86,494.30 on top of what it had already subtracted.

Faced with this genuine ambiguity, Unit specifically asked the trial court to question the jury before it was discharged. The district court's refusal to do so was error. As the Fifth Circuit has explained, when counsel identifies a plausible ambiguity in the verdict and the jury remains available, "the trial court should resubmit the issue to the jury for clarification" rather than resolve the ambiguity unilaterally. *Bonura v. Sea Land Serv., Inc.*, 505 F.2d 665, 669 (5th Cir. 1974). By declining Unit's request, the district court forfeited the one opportunity to learn, from the only source competent to say so, what the jury actually intended—and instead substituted its own speculative arithmetic for the jury's judgment.

## II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE VERDICT TO DENY UNIT ANY RECOVERY WHERE A FAIR READING OF THE VERDICT SUPPORTS AN AWARD TO UNIT

Even apart from the court's refusal to seek clarification, the interpretation the district court ultimately adopted cannot stand because it is not the only, or even the most natural, reading of the jury's verdict. It is a "duty of the courts to attempt to harmonize the answers" returned by a jury "if it is possible under a fair reading of them." *Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963). Courts must exhaust every reasonable construction that reconciles a jury's findings before concluding that the jury's work is contradictory or must be read against the prevailing party. See *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd.*, 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962) ("[W]here there is a view of the case that makes the jury's answers ... consistent, they must be resolved that way."). This principle applies with equal force to general verdicts returned on multiple forms as it does to special interrogatories, because in both settings the court's task is to effectuate the jury's actual findings, not to impose a construction the jury never intended.

The district court did not attempt to harmonize the two verdict amounts; it simply subtracted the Form 3 figure from the Form 1 figure and declared that Unit takes nothing. That approach ignores the far more plausible explanation for the numbers the jury chose: that the jury, following the instruction that it should award Unit "the amount of the unpaid invoices, less the cost to Enron of correcting any defects," had already performed that subtraction when it wrote $70,768.02 on Form 1, and that the identical figure on Form 3 reflected the same finding on damages caused by the pipe separations, recorded on the form the jury was told to use to record any set-off finding—not a second, independent deduction to be layered on top of the first. Where, as here, a fair and eminently reasonable reading of the verdict forms produces a coherent result that gives the prevailing party the benefit of the jury's breach finding, that reading must control over one that renders the jury's principal finding meaningless. See *Gallick*, 372 U.S. at 119.

The consequence of the district court's contrary interpretation is particularly troubling: it converts a jury verdict expressly finding that Enron breached the contract and caused Unit compensable damages into a judgment of zero for Unit, effectively nullifying the jury's central finding on liability. Verdicts should not be construed, where any reasonable alternative exists, to defeat the very findings the jury made. The district court's judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded either for entry of judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02, or, in the alternative, for a new trial so that a properly instructed jury may clearly express its intent.
