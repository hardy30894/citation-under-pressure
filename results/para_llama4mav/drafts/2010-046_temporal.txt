The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not bar the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population. The PLRA limits the authority of federal courts to grant prospective relief in prison conditions cases, but it does not prohibit courts from ordering remedies that are narrowly drawn to correct constitutional violations. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1); see Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908).

In Ex parte Young, the Supreme Court established that federal courts have the authority to enjoin state officials from violating federal law, including constitutional provisions. The Court held that a federal court may issue an injunction against state officials to prevent them from enforcing a state law that is unconstitutional. Id. at 159-60. Similarly, in this case, the district court's order is aimed at remedying unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

The district court's order is also consistent with the principles enunciated in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), where the Court held that the Due Process Clause prohibits the use of coercive measures to extract confessions from prisoners. The Court emphasized that the Constitution requires that prisoners be treated in a manner that respects their fundamental rights. Id. at 285-86. The district court's order is designed to ensure that California's prisons provide constitutionally adequate medical care to prisoners, which is a fundamental right protected by the Eighth Amendment.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have the authority to fashion remedies to correct constitutional violations. In United States v. Morgan, 346 U.S. 502 (1954), the Court held that a federal court may issue a writ of mandamus to compel a state official to perform a ministerial duty required by federal law. The Court emphasized that federal courts have a duty to ensure that state officials comply with federal law. Id. at 505. The district court's order in this case is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

The PLRA does not alter the fundamental principles established by these cases. Rather, it requires that prospective relief be narrowly drawn and the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1). The district court's order meets these requirements, as it is designed to remedy specific constitutional violations and is narrowly tailored to achieve that goal.

In Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Court held that a court's order should be given a reasonable construction to achieve its intended purpose. The district court's order in this case should be construed in light of its purpose to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The order is a necessary measure to ensure that the state's prisons provide constitutionally adequate medical care to prisoners.

In conclusion, the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in the state's prisons. The order is consistent with the principles established by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Young, Brown v. Mississippi, and United States v. Morgan, and is narrowly drawn to correct constitutional violations. The PLRA does not bar the order, and it should be upheld.
