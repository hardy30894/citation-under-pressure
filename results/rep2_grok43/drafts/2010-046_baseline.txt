**ARGUMENT**

The three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5 percent of design capacity satisfies every prerequisite of the Prison Litigation Reform Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3626. Section 3626(a)(3)(A) authorizes a prisoner-release order only when “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and “no other relief will remedy the violation.” The district court made both findings after an extensive evidentiary hearing. Those findings are reviewable only for clear error. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985).

The record establishes that severe overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations. *See Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (conditions may violate the Eighth Amendment when they “deprive inmates of the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities”). Medical and mental-health care had collapsed under the weight of nearly twice the designed capacity, producing preventable deaths and suffering on a systemic scale. The court credited extensive expert testimony that no other remedy—additional hiring, new facilities without population reduction, or further monitoring—could achieve constitutional compliance within a reasonable time.

Section 3626(a)(3)(E) further requires that the order “extend no further than necessary” and be “the least intrusive means” to correct the violation. The 137.5 percent cap meets this standard. It leaves California free to choose among sentencing reforms, good-time credits, parole adjustments, and transfers, thereby preserving state sovereignty over criminal-justice policy. *See Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000) (PLRA respects “the principles of federalism and separation of powers”). The order also incorporates a two-year implementation period and ongoing monitoring, satisfying the statutory command that relief be “narrowly drawn” and that the court “give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), (a)(3)(B).

Petitioners’ contrary arguments rest on an unduly expansive reading of the PLRA that would effectively nullify the statute’s explicit authorization of population caps. Nothing in the Act or in this Court’s precedents supports that result. The order therefore comports with the PLRA in every respect.
