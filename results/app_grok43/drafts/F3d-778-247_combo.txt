ARGUMENT

I. The warrant failed to establish probable cause or a nexus to the searched premises. The Fourth Amendment demands an affidavit showing facts from which a neutral magistrate can find probable cause. Boyd v. United States, 116 U.S. 616 (1886). An officer’s conclusions cannot substitute for facts. Johnson v. United States, 333 U.S. 10 (1948). The affidavit here rested on stale reports and uncorroborated assertions, violating the two-pronged test requiring both veracity and basis of knowledge. Aguilar v. Texas, 378 U.S. 108 (1964); Spinelli v. United States, 393 U.S. 410 (1969). Evidence seized must be suppressed. Weeks v. United States, 232 U.S. 383 (1914).

II. Admission of uncharged molestation testimony violated settled limits on character evidence. Such proof is inadmissible to show propensity. Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469 (1948). The testimony served only to inflame the jury and cannot be deemed harmless.

III. Congress lacked authority under the Commerce Clause to criminalize the charged conduct. The Clause empowers regulation of interstate commerce, not purely local acts lacking a substantial effect on commerce. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100 (1941); Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111 (1942). Application of 18 U.S.C. §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert exceeds this power.

IV. The 480-month sentence constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. A sentence grossly disproportionate to the offense violates the Eighth Amendment. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910). The term imposed here bears no reasonable relation to the conduct and must be vacated.

These errors require reversal of the convictions and sentence.
