**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in concluding that the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the '566 application under 35 U.S.C. § 120. That conclusion rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the statutory requirements for continuity and the legal standard governing incorporation by reference in a chain of continuation applications. The Board’s determination fails to account for the critical distinction between *independent* and *dependent* claims, the requirement that each application in a chain must *independently* satisfy the continuity requirements of § 120, and the principle that an applicant cannot rely on a reference incorporated by reference in a prior application unless that reference was *properly identified and incorporated* at the time of filing.

Under 35 U.S.C. § 120, a continuation application is entitled to the benefit of the filing date of the prior application *only if* the prior application “contains a disclosure of the subject matter claimed in the continuation.” This requirement is not satisfied merely by the existence of a chain of applications, nor by the mere presence of a reference incorporated by reference in an earlier application. Rather, each application in the chain must independently disclose the claimed subject matter, and any incorporation by reference must be *effective at the time of filing* of the application seeking the benefit.

Here, the '880 application seeks the benefit of the April 13, 1989 filing date of the '566 application. To establish that right, Harari must show that the '566 application, as filed, contained a disclosure of the subject matter claimed in the '880 application. The Board found that the '566 application incorporated the '579 application by reference, and that the '579 application disclosed the claimed subject matter. But this conclusion ignores the fact that the '579 application was *not* filed on the same day as the '566 application—indeed, it was filed on the same day as the '566 application, but *not* on the same day as the '880 application. The Board’s reliance on the incorporation statement in the '566 application is misplaced because that statement, by its own terms, incorporated two applications: one filed June 8, 1988, and the other filed on the same day as the '566 application—i.e., April 13, 1989. The '579 application was that second application.

The critical flaw in the Board’s reasoning is its failure to recognize that the '566 application’s incorporation statement, while accurate in identifying the '579 application as one of the two copending applications, did not *effectively* incorporate the '579 application into the '566 application’s disclosure *for the purpose of satisfying § 120* unless the '579 application was *already in existence and publicly available* at the time of the '566 application’s filing. The '579 application was filed on April 13, 1989—*the same day* as the '566 application. But under the rules of patent law, an application is not considered “filed” until it is *received* by the Office. See 37 C.F.R. § 1.53(b). Thus, the '579 application could not have been *in existence* at the time the '566 application was filed unless it was filed *before* the '566 application. The record does not show that the '579 application was filed before the '566 application. Indeed, the incorporation statement itself refers to the '579 application as “copending,” which implies that it was filed *on the same day*—not before.

This timing issue is fatal to Harari’s claim of priority. As this court held in *Harari I*, the identity of an incorporated reference must be clear to a reasonable examiner at the time of filing. 602 F.3d at 1352–53. Here, the incorporation statement in the '566 application referred to the '579 application as “copending,” but the '579 application was not *filed* until the same day. The Board’s conclusion that the '566 application incorporated the '579 application by reference *for the purpose of § 120* is therefore unsupported by the record and contrary to established law.

Moreover, the Board’s analysis fails to account for the intervening applications in the chain: the '838, '768, and '708 applications. Each of these applications must independently satisfy the continuity requirements of § 120. The '838 application is a divisional of the '566 application, but it does not contain any incorporation statement referencing the '579 application. The '768 application, a continuation of the '838, likewise contains no such reference. The '708 application, a continuation of the '768, *does* contain a reference to the '579 application, but it was filed on December 20, 1996—*after* the '880 application was filed on May 14, 1999. Thus, the '708 application cannot serve as a basis for claiming priority to the '566 application.

The Board’s conclusion that the '880 application is entitled to the benefit of the '566 application’s filing date is therefore legally unsound. The chain of applications fails to establish that the '579 application was *properly incorporated* into the '566 application at the time of filing, and no subsequent application in the chain contains a valid incorporation statement that would allow the '880 application to claim priority to the '566 application. The only valid basis for priority is the December 20, 1996 filing date of the '708 application, which is *after* the date of conception of the '601 patent.

The Board’s decision, therefore, is contrary to the law and the facts. It permits Harari to claim priority to an earlier filing date based on an incorporation statement that was not effective at the time of filing and that was not properly carried forward through the chain of applications. This undermines the fundamental principle that priority must be based on a clear, effective disclosure in the prior application at the time of filing.

For these reasons, the Board erred in granting Harari the benefit of the '566 application’s filing date.
