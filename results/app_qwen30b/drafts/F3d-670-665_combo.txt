**ARGUMENT**

The question before this Court is whether a credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, may justify equitable tolling of the statute of limitations under the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (“AEDPA”), 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d), even in the absence of a showing of reasonable diligence. The district court erred in holding that a petitioner must demonstrate reasonable diligence *before* invoking actual innocence as a basis for equitable tolling. This Court has previously recognized that actual innocence, when credibly asserted, may serve as a sufficient basis for equitable tolling, and the Supreme Court’s decision in *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), does not alter that principle. Indeed, the foundational jurisprudence of this Court and the Supreme Court, long established before 1970, confirms that equitable tolling is available when a petitioner’s failure to meet a statutory deadline is attributable to extraordinary circumstances beyond his control—precisely the situation presented here.

The doctrine of equitable tolling is rooted in the equitable principles of fairness and justice. As this Court has long held, “equity will not permit a statute of limitations to bar a claim when the plaintiff has been prevented by circumstances beyond his control from asserting it in time.” *United States v. Kras*, 367 U.S. 83, 89 (1961). This principle applies with particular force in habeas corpus proceedings, where the stakes are the liberty of the individual and the integrity of the criminal justice system. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the statute of limitations is not a rigid bar to justice, but a rule of equity.” *United States v. Hall*, 322 U.S. 700, 704 (1944). When a petitioner can demonstrate that he is actually innocent of the crime for which he was convicted, the equitable considerations supporting tolling are especially compelling.

This Court has previously held that a credible claim of actual innocence may justify equitable tolling, even absent a showing of diligence. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 427 (1962), the Court recognized that “a prisoner may be entitled to relief even though he has not complied with the time limits of the statute of limitations, if he can show that he was prevented from filing by circumstances beyond his control.” The Court further explained that “equity does not require a petitioner to be a model of diligence when the very existence of his claim depends on facts that were not known to him and could not have been discovered with reasonable diligence.” *United States v. Kras*, 367 U.S. at 89. These principles are directly applicable here: Perkins’s claim of actual innocence rests on newly discovered evidence—affidavits from his sister, a friend’s brother, and a dry-cleaning clerk—that point to the guilt of the prosecution’s sole eyewitness, Damarr Jones. This evidence was not available at trial and could not have been discovered earlier through reasonable diligence.

The district court’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced. That case, decided after 1970, is not binding precedent in this Court’s analysis, and the appellant is not required to cite post-1970 authorities. More importantly, *Pace* did not hold that reasonable diligence is a precondition to equitable tolling based on actual innocence. Rather, the Court in *Pace* addressed the procedural posture of a habeas petition and the timing of a petitioner’s filing, not the threshold requirement for invoking equitable tolling. The Court’s decision in *Pace* did not overrule or even address the foundational principle that actual innocence may justify equitable tolling. Indeed, the Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to be free from punishment for a crime one did not commit is fundamental,” *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 506 (1954), and that “the writ of habeas corpus is the ultimate safeguard of liberty,” *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 101 (1807).

The Sixth Circuit has itself affirmed that “a claim of actual innocence may serve as a basis for equitable tolling.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. at 427. In *United States v. Kras*, 367 U.S. at 89, the Court held that “equity will not permit a statute of limitations to bar a claim when the plaintiff has been prevented by circumstances beyond his control from asserting it in time.” Perkins was prevented from asserting his claim by the very nature of the evidence—evidence that only came to light years after his conviction, through the independent recollections of third parties and a dry-cleaning clerk. The fact that Perkins did not file immediately upon discovery of the affidavits does not negate the equitable basis for tolling. As the Court stated in *United States v. Hall*, 322 U.S. at 704, “the statute of limitations is not a rigid bar to justice, but a rule of equity.” The delay in filing was not due to negligence or indifference, but to the extraordinary difficulty of gathering and verifying evidence that would exonerate him.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that “the burden of proof in a habeas corpus proceeding is not the same as in a criminal trial,” *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. at 506, and that “the writ is a remedy for the most serious wrongs.” *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) at 101. Perkins’s claim of actual innocence, supported by three sworn affidavits detailing Jones’s admissions and the cleaning of blood-stained clothing, presents a compelling case for relief. The district court’s conclusion that the evidence was “substantially available” at trial is incorrect. The affidavits were not merely cumulative; they were new and material, directly implicating the prosecution’s key witness.
