# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA BECAUSE STATE JUDGES ARE "APPOINTEE[S] ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL" EXEMPT FROM THE ACT'S COVERAGE

Congress has not clearly expressed an intent to strip the States of their sovereign authority to set qualifications for their own judges. This Court has long recognized that when Congress legislates in areas traditionally reserved to state sovereignty, courts must require a clear statement of congressional intent before finding preemption. The power to establish qualifications for state judges—including age limits—lies at the core of a State's sovereign authority to structure its own government. Nothing in the ADEA's text clearly extends the Act to appointed state judges; rather, the statute exempts "any person elected to public office" and "an appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).

Missouri's judges fall within this exemption. As the Eighth Circuit correctly recognized, judging is not "merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law to specific fact patterns." The judiciary's role in the American constitutional structure has always included lawmaking functions. This Court itself has acknowledged the significance of judicial tenure and retirement as matters of fundamental governmental structure, distinct from ordinary employment relationships. See Booth v. United States, 291 U.S. 339 (1934) (addressing the unique constitutional status of judicial retirement under Article III).

The exemption's purpose—to preserve state authority over important policymaking positions—applies with full force to the judiciary. Missouri's chief justices and presiding judges exercise substantial supervisory and rulemaking authority over the state's court system, further confirming that appointed judges occupy policymaking positions within the meaning of § 630(f).

Respondent recognizes that this Court has enforced the ADEA vigorously against private and governmental employers alike in other contexts. In Western Air Lines, Inc. v. Criswell, 472 U.S. 400 (1985), the Court emphasized that "the policies and substantive provisions of the Act apply with especial force in the case of mandatory retirement provisions," reflecting Congress's judgment that chronological age is generally a poor proxy for ability. Similarly, in EEOC v. Wyoming, 460 U.S. 226 (1983), the Court upheld application of the ADEA to state game wardens, testing the State's mandatory retirement policy "against a reasonable federal standard." And in Public Employees Retirement System of Ohio v. Betts, 492 U.S. 158 (1989), the Court carefully construed the statutory exemptions Congress provided, recognizing that Congress calibrated the ADEA's reach through specific textual limitations.

These decisions confirm rather than undermine Respondent's position. Wyoming and Criswell addressed employees—game wardens and airline flight engineers—who plainly fell within the Act's coverage and whose employers sought exemptions under provisions like the bona fide occupational qualification defense. Here, by contrast, Missouri's judges fall outside the ADEA's definition of "employee" altogether. The statutory analysis is fundamentally different: this case concerns whether Congress intended to cover state judges in the first instance, not whether an employer can justify age-based treatment of covered employees. Betts is instructive precisely because it shows this Court's willingness to hold Congress to specific statutory text rather than expansive purposive readings. Just as the Court in Betts declined to read the ADEA's benefit-plan exemption more narrowly than its language permitted, this Court should give full effect to the "appointee on the policymaking level" exemption according to its terms.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement provisions are subject only to rational basis review. This Court's precedents make clear that Missouri's mandatory retirement provision easily survives this deferential standard.

In Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93 (1979), this Court upheld a mandatory retirement age for foreign service officers, explaining that when "reasonable people may disagree" about the wisdom of an age-based classification, courts are not free "to arbitrate in such contrariety." The same principle governs here. Missouri's judgment that judicial competence may decline with age, and that a fixed retirement age avoids the "unpleasantness of selectively removing aged" jurists, represents precisely the kind of legislative line-drawing that rational basis review protects.

Multiple rationales support Missouri's provision: mandatory retirement reduces delays caused by death or disability of sitting judges, whose risk increases with age; it eliminates the need for case-by-case competency evaluations that would be intrusive, contentious, and difficult to administer fairly; and it facilitates generational turnover, allowing new judges—potentially more representative of the state's diverse population—to join the bench. That some might disagree with these judgments on empirical or philosophical grounds does not render them irrational. As this Court has consistently held, rational basis review does not permit courts to "second-guess the wisdom" of legislative classifications so long as some plausible justification exists.

Petitioners identify no fundamental right implicated by mandatory judicial retirement. Continued judicial employment past age seventy is not a fundamental right under this Court's precedents. Absent a fundamental right or suspect classification, Missouri's provision must be upheld if it bears a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest—a standard it plainly satisfies.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
