The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf to the extent that it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to mean that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969).

However, the Court has also emphasized that the adoption of state law is not a blanket incorporation of all state laws, but rather a limited adoption of state law to fill gaps in federal law. As the Fifth Circuit held in Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), state law is only adopted when there is a "necessity to fill a significant void or gap" in federal law.

In this case, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's provisions are not incomplete or lacking in any significant way, and therefore, there is no gap for state law to fill. The Ninth Circuit's holding that California's wage and hour laws can apply on the outer Continental Shelf despite the FLSA's comprehensive coverage is inconsistent with the OCSLA's limited adoption of state law.

The Rodrigue decision supports this interpretation, as it held that state law is adopted as surrogate federal law on the outer Continental Shelf, but only to the extent that it is necessary to fill gaps in federal law. 395 U.S. at 355-56. The Court's emphasis on the limited adoption of state law is consistent with the Fifth Circuit's holding in Continental Oil, which requires a "significant void or gap" in federal law before state law can be applied.

Therefore, the Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of Newton's claims should be reinstated. The FLSA's comprehensive coverage of wage and hour claims leaves no room for the application of California's wage and hour laws on the outer Continental Shelf.
