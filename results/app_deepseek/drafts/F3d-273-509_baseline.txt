I. The Release Does Not Bar Bancorp’s Claims Because Its Plain Language Covers Only the OCC’s Closure Decision, Not the FDIC’s Subsequent Administration of the Receivership

The release confines Bancorp’s relinquishment to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language is narrow. None of Bancorp’s four claims—unjust enrichment from the FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest, breach of fiduciary duty in the sale of assets, corporate waste in charging legal and office expenses, and failure to account—challenges the OCC’s decision to close. Each challenges the FDIC’s independent, post-closure management of the receivership estate.

A release is a contract, and its scope is determined by the language of the instrument. *See Lee v. Bankers Trust Co.*, 166 F.3d 540, 544 (2d Cir. 1999). Where, as here, the release is unambiguous, it must be enforced according to its terms, and it may not be extended to claims not clearly covered. *See Empire State Sav. Bank v. Lewis*, 285 A.D. 743, 744 (1st Dep’t 1955). A release is also construed strictly against the drafting party—here, the FDIC. *See Town of Amherst v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 251 A.D.2d 1062, 1063 (4th Dep’t 1998).

The FDIC and the district court read “with respect to” so broadly that the release would encompass any claim that would not exist but for the closure. That reading inverts the release’s plain meaning. The phrase “with respect to the decision . . . to close” directs attention to the act of deciding to close, not to everything that happened afterward. The FDIC’s alleged self-dealing—paying itself interest, charging expenses to the estate, selling assets below franchise value, and failing to account—comprises discretionary acts taken during the receivership, years after the closure. Those acts are distinct from the decision that triggered the receivership. The decision to close and the manner of administering the estate are separate subjects; a release covering the former does not immunize the latter.

Nor does the settlement context support the district court’s view. The release was executed to settle combined litigation between the FDIC and Joseph Chuang—litigation to which Bancorp was not a party. The release’s narrow clause reflects the parties’ focus on the closure decision, not on the FDIC’s ongoing management. Had the FDIC sought a release of all receivership-related claims, it could have said so. It did not. Summary judgment on the release was therefore error.

II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Begin to Run Until the Receivership Terminated on November 1, 1995

The district court held, in the alternative, that Bancorp’s claims accrued as early as 1985–1991, when the FDIC charged expenses, sold assets, and paid itself interest, and that the six-year limitations period of N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) had expired by October 31, 1995. That holding ignores the continuing fiduciary character of the FDIC’s role as receiver.

New York law has long recognized that claims for breach of fiduciary duty in the administration of a trust or estate do not accrue until the fiduciary’s duties are concluded. “[T]he statute of limitations does not begin to run against a beneficiary in favor of the trustee until the trust is terminated or the trustee has repudiated the trust.” *Matter of Cornell*, 269 A.D. 624, 625 (1st Dep’t 1945); *see also Matter of Ruskay’s Estate*, 5 A.D.2d 720, 721 (1st Dep’t 1958) (claim against estate for improper self-dealing accrues upon final accounting). The FDIC, as receiver, is a statutory fiduciary charged with administering the Bank’s assets for the benefit of creditors and shareholders. Its duties are continuous and do not end with the payment of depositors. Indeed, the FDIC remained in receivership until November 1, 1995, and, as Bancorp alleged, never provided an adequate accounting.

The district court segmented the receivership into discrete events—each expense charge, each asset sale, each interest payment—and held that the limitations period ran separately from each. That approach is inconsistent with the law of trusts. A beneficiary cannot evaluate the propriety of a fiduciary’s self-dealing or expenditures until the fiduciary has rendered a final account. Until then, the wrongful acts are part of an ongoing fiduciary relationship, not completed and cognizable wrongs. The FDIC neither repudiated its trust nor terminated the receivership until November 1, 1995. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995—one day before termination. The claims were timely.

Even if certain individual acts could have been discovered earlier, the FDIC’s failure to account means that Bancorp did not know, and could not reasonably have known, the full extent of the alleged waste and misappropriation. Under New York law, the limitations period is tolled while a fiduciary conceals its breach by failing to disclose material facts. *See* C.P.L.R. § 213(8); *see also* *Matter of Nevelson*, 176 A.D.2d 502, 503 (1st Dep’t 1991). The district court therefore erred in awarding summary judgment on limitations grounds.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
