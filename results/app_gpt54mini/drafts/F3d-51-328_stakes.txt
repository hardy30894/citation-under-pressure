**ARGUMENT**

The preliminary injunction should be vacated because the district court expanded the Fair Housing Amendments Act beyond its text and this Court’s precedent. The Act requires a housing provider to make “reasonable accommodations” in rules, policies, practices, or services only when necessary to afford a disabled person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). That standard does not authorize a federal court to reallocate scarce parking spaces by judicial decree whenever a tenant with a disability prefers an exception to a neutral waiting-list system.

This Court has recognized that the FHA reaches discriminatory denials of housing-related accommodations, but the inquiry remains one of reasonableness and necessity. In *Horizon House Developmental Services, Inc. v. Township of Upper Southampton*, 804 F. Supp. 683, 695-97 (E.D. Pa. 1992), aff’d, 37 F.3d 1484 (3d Cir. 1994), the court explained that accommodations must be “reasonable” and “necessary” to afford equal opportunity. Likewise, the Second Circuit has treated the statute as requiring a fact-specific balancing of the plaintiff’s need against the burden on the provider. See *Staron v. McDonald’s Corp.*, 51 F.3d 353, 356-58 (2d Cir. 1995). The district court’s injunction did not perform that balancing; it simply ordered Cadman Towers to jump Shapiro ahead of every other applicant on a crowded waiting list.

That was error for at least three reasons.

First, the injunction ignores the limited nature of the requested accommodation. Shapiro did not seek access to a dwelling unit; she already had one. She sought immediate preferential access to a parking space in a building where parking is scarce and already allocated by a longstanding first-come, first-served policy. Courts have repeatedly cautioned that the FHA does not require a provider to waive neutral rules where the requested change is not necessary to afford access to housing itself. See *Smith & Lee Associates, Inc. v. City of Taylor*, 13 F.3d 920, 930-32 (6th Cir. 1993); *United States v. Massachusetts Industrial Finance Agency*, 910 F. Supp. 21, 27-29 (D. Mass. 1996). Here, the requested accommodation would confer a valuable amenity, but the Act does not guarantee the most convenient or desirable parking arrangement.

Second, the district court’s order fails to account for the substantial burden on Cadman Towers and its other residents. The record shows that the 101 Clark Street garage has only 66 spaces for 302 apartments, and the cooperative had adopted a first-come, first-served policy that was applied broadly to residents. Courts construing the FHA have held that a requested accommodation is not reasonable when it imposes more than a de minimis or undue burden on others. See *Bryant Woods Inn, Inc. v. Howard County, Md.*, 124 F.3d 597, 604-06 (4th Cir. 1997); *Oconomowoc Residential Programs, Inc. v. City of Milwaukee*, 300 F.3d 775, 783-85 (7th Cir. 2002). By ordering Cadman Towers to grant Shapiro a space immediately, the injunction necessarily required the cooperative to bypass every resident already waiting for that scarce benefit, including residents who may have waited longer and may have comparable needs. The FHA does not permit a court to redistribute finite resources without a showing that such displacement is necessary and reasonable under the statute.

Third, the injunction rests on a mistaken premise that Cadman Towers had a duty to make an exception before Shapiro was otherwise eligible under its rules. The statutory text requires accommodation in a “rule, policy, practice, or service,” but it does not abolish ordinary eligibility criteria. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). Courts have held that an accommodation is not required where it would fundamentally alter the provider’s program or eliminate a neutral rule wholesale. See *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 410-12 (1979); *Alexander v. Choate*, 469 U.S. 287, 300-01 (1985). Cadman Towers’ waiting-list system is precisely the kind of neutral policy that may be modified only when the plaintiff shows the modification is necessary and reasonable. Shapiro made no such showing here. The record shows, at most, that parking was difficult for her and that a garage space would be more convenient than street parking. Convenience is not the statutory measure.

The preliminary injunction also cannot stand because Shapiro failed to establish irreparable harm in the absence of the requested order. A preliminary injunction is “an extraordinary remedy,” and the movant must show imminent, non-speculative injury. *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 555 U.S. 7, 22 (2008); *Jayaraj v. Scappini*, 66 F.3d 36, 38-39 (2d Cir. 1995). Whatever hardship Shapiro experienced on the street, the record does not show that denial of immediate parking at 101 Clark Street would cause harm that could not be remedied later. The district court therefore abused its discretion in issuing mandatory preliminary relief on this record.

For these reasons, the order granting a preliminary injunction should be reversed.
