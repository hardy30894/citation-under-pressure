The Federal Railroad Safety Act prohibits a railroad carrier from discharging or otherwise discriminating against an employee for reporting, in good faith, a hazardous safety or security condition. 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). To survive summary judgment on a retaliation claim under this provision, a plaintiff must show that (1) the plaintiff engaged in protected activity, (2) the employer took an adverse action, and (3) the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse action. Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co., 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014).

The district court erred in concluding that no genuine dispute exists on these elements. Plaintiffs’ written statements to Trainmaster Dixon and recorded statements to Claims Representative Murphy expressly identified the absence of side rails and lighting on the bridge as unsafe conditions. These communications constitute protected activity under the statute. See id. at 788 (recognizing that reports of hazardous conditions fall within the provision’s protection).

The subsequent investigative hearing, formal discipline, one-year review periods, and termination of Foster likewise qualify as adverse actions. See id. at 789 (noting that discipline and discharge satisfy the adverse-action element). The undisputed increase in unannounced operations testing, which plaintiffs reasonably viewed as heightened scrutiny that altered their work assignments and compensation, supplies an additional adverse action. See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006) (defining adverse actions to include conduct that “well might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge”).

A reasonable jury could also find that the protected activity contributed to these actions. The temporal proximity between the safety reports in early April 2012 and the initiation of the investigation days later, combined with the testimony of Trainmaster Dixon and Road Foreman Knutstrom that they reviewed the very statements containing the safety complaints, permits an inference of contribution. See Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 791 (holding that temporal proximity and knowledge may support a contributing-factor finding). The Public Law Board’s subsequent reversal of the discipline further undermines BNSF’s asserted non-retaliatory rationale and supports an inference that the safety reports played a role. See id. at 792 (observing that an arbitration decision favorable to the employee may bear on the contributing-factor analysis).

Because genuine issues of material fact exist on each element, summary judgment was improper. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).
