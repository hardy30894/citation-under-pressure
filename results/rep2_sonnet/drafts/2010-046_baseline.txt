**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT'S ORDER SATISFIES THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT BECAUSE OVERCROWDING IS THE PRIMARY CAUSE OF ONGOING EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS AND NO LESSER REMEDY WILL SUFFICE.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3), permits a population cap only where a three-judge court finds "that crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right" and that "no other relief will remedy the violation." Both findings are supported by the extensive record compiled below and are subject to clear-error review. *See* Fed. R. Civ. P. 52(a)(6); *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985) ("If the district court's account of the evidence is plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety, the court of appeals may not reverse it.").

This Court has long recognized that severe overcrowding can itself violate the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment when it produces conditions incompatible with "the minimal civilized measure of life's necessities." *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981). Prison officials violate the Constitution when they act with "deliberate indifference" to a substantial risk of serious harm to inmates, including the risk of inadequate medical and mental health care. *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 828 (1994). The three-judge court found, after extensive trial testimony, that California's severe overcrowding—prisons operating at nearly 200% of design capacity—was the primary cause of the medical and mental-health-care failures that this Court's decisions in *Rhodes* and *Farmer* condemn. That finding, reached after years of litigation and repeated remedial efforts that failed for want of capacity, is not clearly erroneous.

Nor was population relief a remedy of first resort. The district court and receivership spent years attempting narrower fixes—stipulated remedial orders in 2002 and 2004, and ultimately the appointment of a Receiver to administer medical care directly. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088 (9th Cir. 2009) (recounting this history). Those measures failed not from lack of effort but because the sheer number of prisoners overwhelmed any system of care the State could build. The PLRA does not require courts to repeat remedies already proven futile; it requires only that the court find, based on the evidence, that no other relief will remedy the violation. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii).

**II. THE ORDER IS NARROWLY DRAWN, EXTENDS NO FURTHER THAN NECESSARY TO CORRECT THE VIOLATION, AND IS THE LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS TO DO SO, CONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT'S INSTITUTIONAL REFORM PRECEDENTS.**

The PLRA's requirement that relief "extend no further than necessary to correct the violation," "be narrowly drawn," and constitute "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation," § 3626(a)(1)(A), tracks principles this Court articulated well before the statute's enactment. In *Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367 (1992), the Court held that institutional reform decrees must remain responsive to law and fact but rejected any suggestion that federal courts are powerless to order structural relief, including population limits, when necessary to cure constitutional violations in overcrowded jails. *Id.* at 383. And in *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), this Court upheld a district court's order limiting the length of confinement in punitive isolation because of overcrowding, explaining that "the ongoing history of litigation" justified a detailed remedial decree, and that a court confronted with a proven constitutional violation "has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past." *Id.* at 687 n.9 (quoting *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145, 154 (1965)).

The population cap here—requiring California to reduce its prisons to 137.5% of design capacity, still far above capacity itself—is calibrated to the level the State's own experts and Receiver identified as necessary to permit constitutionally adequate care, not an arbitrary or punitive figure. The three-judge court considered numerous alternatives, including new construction, out-of-state transfers, and parole reform, and explained why population reduction achieved through means left largely to the State's own discretion—good-time credits, diversion of low-risk offenders, and similar measures—represented the least intrusive path. That approach honors the deference this Court demanded in *Rufo*, which cautioned courts to give the State "significant latitude" in choosing among means to satisfy a remedial decree. *Rufo*, 502 U.S. at 393.

**III. THE ORDER APPROPRIATELY ACCORDS SUBSTANTIAL WEIGHT TO PUBLIC SAFETY, AS THE PLRA REQUIRES.**

Section 3626(a)(1)(A) directs courts to give "substantial weight" to any adverse impact on public safety. The three-judge court did so, crediting expert testimony that a measured reduction achieved over a period of years, through mechanisms such as credit-earning and diversion of technical parole violators, would not increase—and could decrease—recidivism by allowing correctional resources to be redirected toward rehabilitation. This is consistent with *Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520, 562 (1979), where the Court recognized that courts must remain sensitive to legitimate penological objectives but need not defer to unsupported assertions that constitutional compliance is incompatible with public safety.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the three-judge district court should be affirmed.
