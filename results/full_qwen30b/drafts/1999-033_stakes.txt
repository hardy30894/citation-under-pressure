**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause permits a cause of action by a “class of one”—is one of profound constitutional significance. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause when the sole basis for the claim is that the government acted out of “spite” or “personal animus” toward the plaintiff, without any allegation of membership in a protected class or group. This Court has long held that the Equal Protection Clause does not extend to isolated instances of governmental discrimination against a single individual absent a showing of classification based on a suspect or quasi-suspect criterion. The Court of Appeals’ decision, by creating a freestanding “class of one” doctrine rooted solely in animus, departs from settled precedent and undermines the structural integrity of equal protection jurisprudence.

The foundation of the Equal Protection Clause is not the absence of personal hostility, but the presence of a discriminatory classification. As this Court held in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985), “The Equal Protection Clause is designed to protect individuals from invidious discrimination, not to ensure that government treats everyone identically.” The Clause “does not require that all persons be treated identically, but that all persons similarly situated be treated alike.” *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977). This principle is grounded in the understanding that the Clause is not a “general command to treat people fairly,” but a limitation on the power of government to classify individuals in ways that disadvantage certain groups. *Regents of the Univ. of Cal. v. Bakke*, 438 U.S. 265, 300 (1978) (opinion of Powell, J.).

The Court of Appeals’ reliance on *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), is misplaced. *Rosenberger* involved a university’s denial of funding to a student publication based on its religious content—a decision that constituted viewpoint discrimination, not a mere act of personal spite. The Court in *Rosenberger* emphasized that the government’s action was “based on the content of the speech,” not on the identity of the speaker. Id. at 829. The case did not address whether a plaintiff could assert an equal protection claim based solely on the government’s personal animus toward them. Indeed, the Court has never recognized a freestanding equal protection claim based on arbitrary or spiteful treatment of a single individual.

The Court has consistently rejected such claims. In *Cleburne*, the Court considered a challenge to a zoning ordinance that denied a permit to a group home for the mentally disabled. The Court held that the ordinance did not violate equal protection because the classification was not based on a suspect class, and the city had a legitimate interest in regulating land use. The Court emphasized that “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that the government treat all persons identically,” but that “the government may treat persons differently if the difference in treatment is rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest.” *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440. The Court did not suggest that a plaintiff could prevail simply by alleging that the city acted out of personal dislike.

Similarly, in *Lingle v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc.*, 544 U.S. 528 (2005), the Court rejected a claim that a regulatory decision violated equal protection because it was “arbitrary and capricious.” The Court held that “the Equal Protection Clause does not require that government treat all persons identically,” and that “a mere difference in treatment does not constitute a constitutional violation.” Id. at 538. The Court reiterated that “the Clause is not a ‘general command to treat people fairly’” and that “the Constitution does not require that government treat all persons identically.” Id. at 539.

The Court of Appeals’ decision creates a new and unworkable standard: a plaintiff may allege a constitutional violation based solely on the government’s motive, without any showing of classification. This would allow every citizen to challenge any adverse governmental decision on equal protection grounds, simply by alleging that the decision was made out of spite. Such a rule would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general administrative review mechanism, undermining the principle that equal protection is not a substitute for procedural fairness or substantive due process.

Moreover, the Court has never recognized a “class of one” doctrine in the absence of a protected class. In *Lofton v. Secretary of Dept. of Children & Family Services*, 358 F.3d 804 (11th Cir. 2004), the court rejected a claim that a state agency’s denial of a foster care license violated equal protection because the agency had approved licenses for others under similar circumstances. The court held that “the Equal Protection Clause does not create a right to be treated the same as others in the absence of a protected class.” Id. at 810. The same principle applies here: Grace Olech’s claim fails because she has not alleged membership in a protected class, nor has she shown that the Village’s action was based on a classification that implicates a fundamental right or suspect class.

The Court of Appeals’ decision also risks creating a flood of litigation based on subjective allegations of animus. Without a clear standard for what constitutes “spiteful effort,” courts would be forced to engage in intrusive inquiries into the motives of public officials—precisely the kind of judicial overreach the Constitution seeks to avoid. As this Court has warned, “the Equal Protection Clause is not a license for courts to re-examine the wisdom of legislative choices.” *United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515, 533 (1996).

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals. The Equal Protection Clause does not provide a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges only that the government acted out of personal animus and not because of a discriminatory classification. The judgment below should be vacated, and the case remanded with instructions to dismiss the complaint for failure to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause.
