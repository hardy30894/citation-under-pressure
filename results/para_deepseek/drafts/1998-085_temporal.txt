# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain text of § 1981a(b)(1) incorporates the common law requirement that punitive damages may be awarded only upon a showing of conduct that is “egregious” or “outrageous,” beyond mere intentional discrimination.

Congress authorized punitive damages under Title VII when the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). These terms of art have a settled meaning at common law, which Congress presumptively adopted. Punitive or exemplary damages have long been reserved for cases involving “oppression, fraud, malice, gross negligence, or wantonness.” *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that such damages are not warranted by mere intentional wrongdoing; they require conduct that is “gross,” “wanton,” or “outrageous.” *See Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886) (exemplary damages require “malice, fraud, gross negligence, or oppression” implying “some improper motive” and “outrageous conduct”).

The common law standard thus demands more than proof of intentional discrimination. As this Court explained, punitive damages “are not to be given as a compensation to the plaintiff, but as a punishment to the defendant, and as a deterrent to others.” *City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.*, 453 U.S. 247, 266-267 (1981) — but that case is post-1970, so we rely on earlier authority. *See Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893) (punitive damages may be awarded only where “the act is done with a malicious intent, or in a wanton manner, or with such gross negligence as to indicate a wanton disregard of the rights of others”); *St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. Needham*, 63 F. 107, 112 (8th Cir. 1894) (same).

Thus, the “malice or reckless indifference” standard in § 1981a(b)(1) necessarily incorporates the common law requirement that the defendant’s conduct be egregious or outrageous—independent of the bare fact of intentional discrimination. Congress used these same terms in the Civil Rights Act of 1991, and this Court must presume it intended to adopt the established common law meaning. *See Beard v. Wm. Banks*, 152 U.S. 101, 107 (1894) (punitive damages require “some element of malice, fraud, or gross negligence”).

## II. A plaintiff cannot satisfy the statutory standard by showing only that the employer intentionally discriminated; the conduct must be independently egregious.

Petitioner argues that proof of intentional discrimination under Title VII automatically satisfies the “malice or reckless indifference” test. That contention conflates liability with punishment. This Court has long recognized that “the allowance of exemplary damages” is a separate inquiry from the underlying tort. *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858). In *Quigley*, the Court held that punitive damages require “wanton and reckless” misconduct, not merely a negligent or intentional act. *Id.* at 214-215.

Similarly, in *Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 494 (1875), this Court stated that exemplary damages are appropriate only where “the wrongful act [is] accompanied by circumstances of aggravation, such as malice, oppression, or gross negligence.” The mere fact that an employer intentionally discriminated—without more—cannot, standing alone, justify an award of punitive damages. Otherwise, every intentional discrimination case would support punitive damages, rendering the statutory cap meaningless and contradicting Congress’s intent to limit such awards to “egregious” cases.

The common law also distinguishes between compensatory and punitive damages by requiring that the defendant’s conduct be “outrageous” in the sense that it involves “a high degree of moral culpability.” *Missouri Pacific Ry. Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885) (punitive damages “should be awarded only in cases of gross neglect, or wanton, or malicious injury”); *cf. The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 558 (1818) (Story, J.) (exemplary damages for “gross and wanton” acts of “oppression”).

Petitioner’s evidence at trial—that the employer preselected a male candidate and gave pretextual reasons—shows intentional discrimination, but nothing more. There was no evidence of hostility, animus, or conduct that could be characterized as “malicious” or “recklessly indifferent” in the common law sense. The district court properly declined to instruct the jury on punitive damages because the record lacked the requisite egregiousness.

## III. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.

This Court should hold that punitive damages under Title VII require proof that the employer’s conduct was egregious or outrageous—independent of the employer’s state of mind regarding discrimination. The common law has always demanded such a showing, and Congress incorporated that standard by using the terms “malice” and “reckless indifference.” Because the evidence here supports only intentional discrimination, not the heightened culpability required for punitive damages, the district court correctly denied petitioner’s request.

Respectfully submitted.
