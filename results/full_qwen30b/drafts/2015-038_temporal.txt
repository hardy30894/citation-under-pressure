**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (NPS) properly exercises regulatory authority over the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including on state-owned navigable waters, because the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit such regulation. The plain text of ANILCA, read in context and consistent with long-standing principles of federal regulatory power over navigable waters, confirms that the NPS may enforce its general regulations—such as the ban on hovercrafts—within the boundaries of National Park System units in Alaska, even when those boundaries encompass state-owned lands and waters.

**I. ANILCA’s plain language permits the NPS to regulate activities within the boundaries of National Park System units, regardless of ownership of submerged lands.**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that “the Secretary of the Interior shall not have regulatory authority over any lands or waters within the boundaries of a unit of the National Park System in Alaska which are not owned by the United States.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This provision is not a blanket prohibition on all federal regulation of non-federal lands within park boundaries. Rather, it limits the *regulatory authority* of the Secretary of the Interior *over lands and waters not owned by the United States*. The key inquiry is whether the regulation at issue is one that “regulates” the land or water itself—such as zoning, development, or use restrictions—or whether it is a safety, environmental, or operational rule that applies uniformly to all users of the park, regardless of land ownership.

The NPS hovercraft ban is not a regulation of land or water ownership. It is a safety and resource protection rule, codified at 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), which prohibits the operation of hovercrafts within all National Park System units. This regulation applies equally to federal, state, Native, corporate, and private lands and waters within the park boundaries. It does not seek to control the title, use, or management of submerged lands; it governs conduct on navigable waters for the purpose of protecting natural resources, visitor safety, and the integrity of the park system.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters is not dependent on ownership of the submerged lands. In *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 400 (1940), the Court held that “the power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters,” and that “the navigational servitude is a paramount interest which Congress may exercise in the public interest.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915), where the Court stated that “the right of the United States to regulate the use of navigable waters is not dependent upon ownership of the bed of the stream.” Thus, even if the State of Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River, the federal government retains the authority to regulate activities on those waters for the purposes of navigation, safety, and conservation.

The NPS hovercraft ban is a regulation of use on navigable waters, not a regulation of land ownership. It is therefore not barred by ANILCA § 103(c). The statute’s limitation on regulatory authority applies only to regulations that directly affect the use or management of non-federal lands and waters as property—such as permitting construction, mining, or timber harvesting. The hovercraft ban does not regulate land use in that sense; it regulates a specific mode of transportation on navigable waters for the protection of park resources and public safety.

**II. The NPS’s authority to regulate activities on navigable waters within park boundaries is rooted in the federal government’s inherent power to manage and protect the National Park System.**

The National Park Service was established by Congress in 1916 to “conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein and to provide for the enjoyment of the same in such manner and by such means as will leave them unimpaired for the enjoyment of future generations.” 16 U.S.C. § 1. This mandate includes the authority to regulate activities that threaten the integrity of the parks, including the use of motorized vehicles on navigable waters.

In *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 265 (1946), the Court recognized that “the power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters,” and that “the federal government may regulate the use of navigable waters even when the land beneath them is privately owned.” The Court emphasized that “the right to use navigable waters is not a right to use them in any manner that may be desired,” but rather one subject to federal regulation for the public good.

Similarly, in *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 126 (1967), the Court held that “the federal government may regulate the use of navigable waters even when the land beneath them is not federal property.” The Court noted that “the federal interest in the use of navigable waters is not dependent upon ownership of the land beneath them,” and that “Congress may regulate the use of such waters for the protection of the public interest.”

These principles apply with equal force to the National Park System. The NPS is entrusted with the responsibility to protect the natural and recreational values of the parks. To allow a private individual to operate a motorized hovercraft on a navigable river within a national preserve—potentially disturbing wildlife, damaging riparian habitats, and endangering other users—would undermine the very purpose of the park. The NPS’s authority to prohibit such activity is not diminished by the fact that the submerged lands are owned by the State of Alaska.

**III. The interpretation of ANILCA § 103(c) advanced by Sturgeon would render the statute meaningless and conflict with Congress’s intent to preserve the integrity of the National Park System.**

If ANILCA § 103(c) were interpreted to prohibit the NPS from regulating any activity on non-federal lands or waters within park boundaries, the result would be a patchwork of regulatory authority that would severely undermine the NPS’s ability to manage the parks. The statute’s purpose is not to create a legal vacuum on state-owned lands within park boundaries, but to clarify the scope of federal regulatory authority in Alaska’s unique land ownership context.
