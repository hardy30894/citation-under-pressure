**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eighth Amendment's Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment Does Not Categorically Bar Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders.**

This Court has long recognized that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). But that inquiry is objective, not subjective: it asks whether society, through its legislatures and juries, has in fact repudiated a punishment—not whether this Court believes the punishment unwise. Nothing in the historical understanding of the Clause supports a categorical exemption from capital punishment based on a defendant's mental capacity. As early as *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878), and *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), this Court understood the Clause to prohibit torturous *methods* of punishment, not to impose substantive limits on which offenders may receive an otherwise lawful sentence. The death penalty itself has never been thought "cruel" within the constitutional sense when imposed for the crime of capital murder by a properly instructed jury.

Nor can Atkins point to the kind of national consensus that would justify judicial abolition of a punishment. In *Trop*, the Court found expatriation cruel and unusual only after canvassing legislative and international practice and finding the punishment "very rarely" employed and "abandoned by the vast majority of the nations of the world." 356 U.S. at 102-03. No comparable rejection of capital punishment for mentally retarded offenders exists here. Virginia, like many States, permits its juries to weigh mental capacity as mitigation but has declined to erect a categorical bar. That legislative choice—entrusting the question to the sentencer rather than removing it from the sentencer's hands—reflects precisely the kind of considered judgment that *Trop*'s standard was designed to respect, not override.

**II. The Constitution Commits the Weighing of Diminished Mental Capacity to the Individualized Judgment of the Capital Sentencing Jury.**

This Court's jurisprudence has consistently entrusted questions of an individual defendant's moral culpability, including reduced mental capacity, to the fact-finder rather than converting them into per se constitutional rules. In *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510, 519 (1968), the Court preserved the jury's role as the body vested with "the awesome responsibility of decreeing death," recognizing that capital sentencing determinations depend on the jury's assessment of the individual defendant and the individual crime. That structure—individualized assessment by the jury, rather than categorical exclusion by this Court—is the mechanism the Constitution supplies for taking account of factors such as Atkins's intellectual functioning.

This principle finds further support in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 800-01 (1952), where the Court upheld a State's authority to define and allocate the burden of proving insanity, explaining that the formulation of rules governing criminal responsibility for persons with mental impairment is a matter "of legislative policy" for the States, not a question fixed by the Fourteenth Amendment. If the States retain constitutional latitude to decide how mental impairment bears on responsibility for the underlying offense, they retain at least as much latitude to decide how mental impairment bears on the appropriate punishment once guilt has been fairly adjudicated.

*Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), reinforces the point. There, the Court declined to constitutionalize a diminished-capacity defense for chronic alcoholism, explaining that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability and essential fairness . . . depend on age-old ideas of the appropriate relationship between personal accountability and volitional acts," *id.* at 535-36 (plurality opinion), and that such judgments about responsibility belong to the legislative process, informed by "the general expression of the moral sense of the community." *Id.* at 536. The plurality warned against converting disputed medical and moral questions about mental capacity into fixed constitutional doctrine imposed uniformly on the States. That admonition applies with full force here: whether and how mental retardation should affect eligibility for capital punishment is a question for legislatures and sentencing juries, informed by expert testimony—exactly the process Virginia employed at Atkins's resentencing, where the defense's forensic psychologist testified to Atkins's intellectual limitations and the jury, weighing that evidence against the State's rebuttal and the aggravating circumstances of the crime, returned a sentence of death.

**III. Atkins's Sentence Is Not Disproportionate to His Offense.**

Finally, the proportionality principle recognized in *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910), asks whether the *punishment* is graduated and proportioned to the *offense*—there, the Court condemned a punishment "cruel in its excess of imprisonment" relative to the crime of falsifying a public document. Capital murder, abduction, and armed robbery are offenses for which death has always been considered a proportionate societal response. Atkins's jury, having heard the very evidence of intellectual limitation he now asks this Court to treat as constitutionally dispositive, nevertheless concluded that the aggravating circumstances warranted death. That individualized judgment—not a categorical rule fashioned by this Court—is the proper repository of the Eighth Amendment's proportionality concern.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
