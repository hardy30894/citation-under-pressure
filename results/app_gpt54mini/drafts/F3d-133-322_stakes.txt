## Argument

### I. The indictment and proof were insufficient because Mrs. Parker was not a “public official” performing an “official act” within the meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201.

Section 201(b)(2)(C) reaches only a “public official” who “corruptly demands, seeks, receives, accepts, or agrees to receive or accept anything of value personally or for any other person or entity, in return for being influenced in the performance of any official act.” 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). Congress defined “official act” to mean “any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” that may be pending before a public official in an official capacity. 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). But the Supreme Court has made clear that the statute does not extend to every act connected with government employment; the act must be one taken in the exercise of official duties, not merely an incidental or ministerial task. United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231-32 (1914).

Mrs. Parker’s role at the SSA Office of Hearings and Appeals was clerical. She assisted ALJ Aronson by recording and taking notes at hearings and by typing and mailing his decisions. She was not authorized to approve benefits, deny benefits, or sign Aronson’s name. The government’s theory therefore collapses the statutory distinction between a public employee’s access to the workplace and the exercise of official power. That is not enough under Birdsall, which grounded bribery liability on duties that are part of “settled practice” in the performance of an office, not on mere contact with the machinery of government. Id. at 231.

The Fifth Circuit has likewise recognized that § 201 does not turn on formal title alone, but on whether the defendant’s conduct bears the requisite nexus to an official act. In United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983), cited by the government below, the court emphasized that bribery liability may reach conduct outside a narrow written job description only where the employee in fact participates in the governmental decision-making process. Mrs. Parker did not. Her work typing and mailing decisions did not give her authority to decide SSI claims, and the government cannot transform forgery and after-the-fact deception into an “official act” simply because the documents were mailed on SSA letterhead.

### II. The evidence showed, at most, fraud and forgery, not bribery of an official act within the statute.

The record shows that Mrs. Parker took Simmons’s money after falsely claiming she could secure benefits, then later forged documents and altered letters to simulate favorable SSA action. That conduct may be criminal, but it is not the offense charged unless it was tied to a true “official act.” See United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers of California, 526 U.S. 398, 404-08 (1999) (holding that § 201 requires a link to a specific official act and rejecting a broad gratuity theory untethered to such an act). The government’s proof here showed a scheme to exploit access and impersonate authority, not a lawful decision or action by Mrs. Parker in her capacity as an officer authorized to act.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned against reading bribery statutes so broadly that they become all-purpose corruption provisions untethered to the words Congress used. McDonnell v. United States, 579 U.S. 550, 574-75 (2016), held that “official act” requires a “question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy” that is “specific and focused” and that a public official must make a decision or take an action on that matter. Id. at 569-73. Mrs. Parker’s conduct was not a decision or action on a pending claim in the McDonnell sense; it was a fraudulent manipulation of paperwork after the fact. Even assuming she used her access to influence claim processing, that is not the same as performing an official act herself.

### III. Because the public-bribery convictions fail, the conspiracy convictions must also fall.

A conspiracy conviction cannot stand where the object offense is legally insufficient. See United States v. Feola, 420 U.S. 671, 694 (1975). The indictment charged a conspiracy to commit public bribery under § 201(b)(2)(C); if Mrs. Parker was not capable of committing the substantive offense as charged, then the conspiracy count fails as well. The same principle applies to Mr. Parker’s derivative convictions for the same scheme. See Gebardi v. United States, 287 U.S. 112, 123 (1932).

### Conclusion

Because Mrs. Parker was a clerical employee who lacked authority to make or influence an “official act” within the meaning of § 201, the indictment and proof were insufficient. The public-bribery convictions cannot stand, and the conspiracy convictions dependent on them must be reversed.
