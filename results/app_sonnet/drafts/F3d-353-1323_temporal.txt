**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT PAKISTAN’S CONDUCT WAS NOT “COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY”**

The district court's jurisdictional ruling rests on an outdated conception of sovereign immunity that this Court's predecessor case law has long since abandoned. Although the classical doctrine of absolute immunity traces to *The Schooner Exchange v. McFaddon*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116 (1812), which held that a sovereign's public vessel could not be subjected to the jurisdiction of another nation's courts, that doctrine was never understood to immunize a foreign state's private, commercial dealings. Chief Justice Marshall's opinion was carefully limited to acts of a public, non-commercial character — the movement of a nation's armed vessels — and did not purport to shield a sovereign's proprietary transactions in land or goods. *Id.* at 145–46.

By the mid-twentieth century, American courts recognized that a foreign sovereign's participation in ordinary commercial dealings could not be cloaked in the same immunity afforded to its governmental acts. In *Victory Transport Inc. v. Comisaria General de Abastecimientos y Transportes*, 336 F.2d 354 (2d Cir. 1964), the Second Circuit explained that the restrictive theory of sovereign immunity draws a distinction between "public or sovereign acts (jure imperii) of a state" and "private or commercial acts (jure gestionis)," and that only the former warrant immunity. *Id.* at 359. The court further observed that immunity should be denied unless the act in question is one that is "clearly" governmental in character. *Id.* at 360. This restrictive approach superseded the earlier, broader immunity recognized in *Berizzi Bros. Co. v. S.S. Pesaro*, 271 U.S. 562 (1926), which had treated a state-owned merchant vessel as immune simply because it was state property — a rule *Victory Transport* recognized had been effectively repudiated by the State Department's adoption of the restrictive theory. 336 F.2d at 360–61.

Pakistan's conduct here falls squarely on the commercial side of that line. Pakistan did not merely regulate land use pursuant to a sovereign function; it took Beg's eleven and one-half acres and transferred that land to individuals — members of its military — for private residential use, and subsequently attempted to negotiate a substitute parcel through a representative dispatched to the United States. These are the hallmarks of a proprietary land transaction, not an exercise of governmental police power. As this Court's predecessors recognized in the sovereign-immunity context generally, courts must look past the label a state affixes to its own conduct and examine the "nature" of the act itself. *Republic of Mexico v. Hoffman*, 324 U.S. 30, 35 (1945) (immunity determinations depend on "the nature of the ship's use"). Here, the transfer of expropriated land into private military housing, and the subsequent offer of substitute property through a commercial negotiation, is the sort of proprietary dealing that a restrictive theory of immunity does not shield.

**II. THE EXPROPRIATION AND FAILURE TO COMPENSATE PRODUCED A DIRECT EFFECT IN THE UNITED STATES**

Even under a narrower view of "commercial activity," Pakistan's conduct produced a direct effect within the United States sufficient to sustain jurisdiction. Beg, a resident of the United States, suffered the financial consequence of Pakistan's expropriation and non-payment squarely within this country. The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a foreign sovereign's conduct touches the property or financial interests of persons within the United States, American courts retain a legitimate interest in adjudicating the resulting dispute. *Ex parte Republic of Peru*, 318 U.S. 578, 588 (1943) (recognizing that immunity questions must be weighed against the interests of private litigants affected by a foreign state's conduct). The failure to compensate Beg is not an abstract foreign-law dispute; it is a pecuniary loss suffered by an individual residing in, and pursuing his remedy from, the United States.

**III. THE ACT OF STATE DOCTRINE DOES NOT BAR ADJUDICATION**

To the extent Pakistan suggests that its expropriation is shielded from judicial inquiry as a sovereign act, that doctrine addresses the *validity* of a foreign sovereign's acts within its own territory — it does not bar an American court from exercising jurisdiction over a claim for compensation. *Underhill v. Hernandez*, 168 U.S. 250, 252 (1897) ("Redress of grievances by reason of such acts must be obtained through the means open to be availed of by sovereign powers as between themselves."). Even the modern articulation of the act of state doctrine in *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 428 (1964), recognized that the doctrine is a prudential rule concerning the *merits* of a taking claim, not a jurisdictional bar to suit. Beg does not ask this Court to declare Pakistan's expropriation unlawful under Pakistani law; he seeks compensation for a commercial taking that has already been litigated, and rejected on title grounds, in Pakistan's own courts.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on the merits of Beg's claim.
