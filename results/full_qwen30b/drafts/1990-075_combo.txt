**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it permit an impermissible delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA). The delegation at issue is fully consistent with the constitutional principle that Congress may delegate authority to administrative agencies so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of that power. This Court has long recognized that Congress may empower executive officials to act in the public interest under broad statutory mandates, provided the standards are not so vague or amorphous as to render the delegation a surrender of legislative authority. The Act before us satisfies this standard in every respect.

**I. Congress Has Provided an Intelligible Principle for the Temporary Scheduling of Controlled Substances**

The Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress, but it does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to other branches when it does so with clear and meaningful standards. In *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394 (1928), the Court upheld a delegation of authority to the President to fix rates for transportation of goods, rejecting the argument that such delegation violated Article I, § 1. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not forbid the delegation of legislative power, so long as the legislative authority is not surrendered.” Id. at 409. The Court found that the statute provided “an intelligible principle” by requiring the President to act in accordance with “the public interest” and “the general welfare,” which were sufficient to guide the exercise of discretion. Id.

The same principle applies here. Section 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if it poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard is not vague or unbounded. It is anchored in specific, objective criteria: the substance’s potential for abuse, its pharmacological effects, its history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of such abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. See id. § 811(c). These factors are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory considerations. The Attorney General must evaluate each of them before acting. This is not a delegation of unfettered discretion; it is a delegation of authority to act in response to a defined emergency, guided by a clear statutory framework.

The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations that are similarly broad in scope but grounded in specific standards. In *United States v. Rock Royal Co-op*, 307 U.S. 533 (1939), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to regulate the marketing of agricultural products under the Agricultural Marketing Agreement Act. The Court rejected the argument that the delegation was invalid because the Secretary was required to act “in the public interest.” Id. at 545. The Court held that “the public interest” was not an empty phrase but a standard that could be interpreted in light of the statute’s purpose and the facts of each case. Id. Similarly, in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court upheld a delegation to the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate motor carriers, noting that the Commission’s authority was “not unlimited” but was “subject to the standards and limitations” set forth in the statute. Id. at 543.

The temporary scheduling provision in the Controlled Substances Act is no different. It is not a blank check. It is a targeted delegation to respond to a specific threat—new, dangerous substances that are being manufactured and distributed without prior notice or regulation. The statute’s requirement that the Attorney General consider the eight factors listed in § 811(c), and that he obtain a medical and scientific evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, ensures that the decision is not arbitrary. These are not mere formalities; they are substantive checks on the exercise of power.

**II. The Attorney General May Lawfully Subdelegate Authority to the DEA**

The Attorney General’s authority to delegate his responsibilities under the CSA to the DEA Administrator is well established. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court held that Congress may authorize the delegation of administrative functions to subordinate officials, so long as the delegation is consistent with the statute’s purpose and the delegatee acts within the bounds of the law. Id. at 543. The Court emphasized that “the power to administer a statute is not necessarily a legislative power,” and that “the executive may delegate administrative duties to subordinates.” Id.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co.*, 312 U.S. 595 (1941), where the Court upheld the delegation of authority to railroad commissioners to enforce rate regulations. The Court noted that “the delegation of administrative functions to officers of the executive department is not forbidden by the Constitution.” Id. at 602. The same is true here. The Attorney General, as head of the executive branch, may assign responsibilities to the DEA Administrator, who is a subordinate official within the Department of Justice. The regulation at issue, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely implements the Attorney General’s statutory authority and does not expand it. It is a routine administrative arrangement, not a constitutional violation.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the executive branch may delegate authority to agencies within its control. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506 (1911), the Court upheld the delegation of authority to the Secretary of the Interior to enforce the Lacey Act, noting that “the President and the heads of departments may delegate to subordinates the performance of duties which are administrative and not legislative.” Id. at 525. The temporary scheduling power is administrative in nature—it involves the application of existing standards to new facts, not the creation of new law. The DEA Administrator does not make policy; he applies the criteria set by Congress.
