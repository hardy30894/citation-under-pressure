ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Appellants’ Experts for Alleged Noncompliance with Discovery Orders.

A district court’s exclusion of expert testimony as a discovery sanction is reviewed for abuse of discretion. See National Hockey League v. Metropolitan Hockey Club, Inc., 427 U.S. 639, 642 (1976) (per curiam). Sanctions must be “just” and “specifically related to the particular ‘claim’ which was at issue in the order to provide discovery.” Insurance Corp. of Ireland, Ltd. v. Compagnie des Bauxites de Guinee, 456 U.S. 694, 707 (1982). The June 3, 1993 order required completion of depositions by July 30; the record contains no finding that Appellants willfully refused to produce experts who had formulated opinions. Absent such a predicate, striking the testimony of Drs. Watson, Connor, Schroeder, and Glasser, and Messrs. Cooper and Stock, exceeded the court’s discretion. See id.

II. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Drs. Schroeder and Watson Under Daubert.

Admissibility of expert testimony turns on whether the reasoning or methodology underlying the testimony is scientifically valid. Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 592–93 (1993). The district court’s order contains no analysis of the factors identified in Daubert—testability, peer review, error rate, or general acceptance—and instead rests solely on the experts’ inability to complete review by an arbitrary deadline. That procedural default does not demonstrate that the methodologies themselves are unreliable. See id. at 595 (“The focus . . . must be solely on principles and methodology, not on the conclusions that they generate.”).

III. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Remained Once the Experts’ Testimony Is Considered.

Because the exclusions were erroneous, the record contains admissible expert evidence creating triable issues on causation and damages. See Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986) (summary judgment appropriate only when “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact”). The district court’s grant of summary judgment therefore cannot stand.

IV. This Court Has Jurisdiction Because the Premature Notice of Appeal Ripened Upon Entry of Final Judgment.

A notice of appeal filed after announcement of a decision but before entry of the final judgment is treated as filed on the date of entry. See Fed. R. App. P. 4(a)(2); Firstier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co., 498 U.S. 269, 273–74 (1991). The October 19, 1994 notice, though filed before the March 1, 1995 order disposing of all claims, became effective upon entry of that final judgment. Jurisdiction is therefore secure.
