The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals correctly held that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows plaintiffs to recover punitive damages if they demonstrate that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). While the statute's language focuses on the defendant's state of mind, the legislative history and relevant case law suggest that the defendant's conduct must also be sufficiently egregious to warrant punitive damages.

The legislative history of § 1981a indicates that Congress intended to permit punitive damages only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al). This interpretation is consistent with the common law trend, which requires something substantially more blameworthy than the intention required for liability to justify punitive damages for intentional torts.

In Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), this Court held that punitive damages are available under § 1983 when the defendant's conduct is "shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others." Id. at 56. The Court emphasized that punitive damages are never awarded as of right, no matter how egregious the defendant's conduct, and that the decision to award punitive damages is left to the discretion of the factfinder. Id. at 52.

Similarly, in Kolstad's case, the jury's finding of intentional discrimination is not sufficient, on its own, to support an award of punitive damages. The Court of Appeals correctly held that the district court erred in refusing to instruct the jury on punitive damages, but this does not necessarily mean that Kolstad is entitled to punitive damages. On remand, the jury should consider whether ADA's conduct was sufficiently egregious to warrant punitive damages, in addition to whether ADA acted with "malice" or "reckless indifference" to Kolstad's federally protected rights.

Requiring egregious conduct, in addition to a culpable state of mind, is consistent with the purposes of punitive damages, which are to punish and deter particularly egregious conduct. See Dun & Bradstreet, Inc. v. Greenmoss Builders, Inc., 472 U.S. 749, 760-61 (1985). By limiting punitive damages to cases involving egregious conduct, courts can ensure that employers are held accountable for their actions, while also avoiding excessive or disproportionate punishment.

In conclusion, the ADA respectfully submits that the Court should affirm the Court of Appeals' holding that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. This interpretation is supported by the legislative history, relevant case law, and the purposes of punitive damages.
