ARGUMENT

I. The District Court's Order Reducing California's Prison Population Does Not Violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) requires that any prospective relief in a prison conditions case be "narrowly drawn, extend[s] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and [is] the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The district court's order reducing California's prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions satisfies this requirement.

The Supreme Court has held that the PLRA does not prohibit courts from ordering prospective relief that is necessary to remedy a constitutional violation. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511 (2011) (holding that the PLRA "does not authorize courts to order relief that is not 'narrowly drawn' or that is not 'the least intrusive means' of correcting a violation"). In this case, the district court's order is narrowly drawn to address the specific constitutional violation found by the court, namely, the unconstitutional conditions caused by severe overcrowding in California's prisons.

The court's order is also consistent with the PLRA's requirement that prospective relief be "the least intrusive means necessary to correct" the constitutional violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The court considered various alternatives to reducing the prison population, but found that they were insufficient to remedy the constitutional violation. See Plata v. Brown, 754 F.3d 1070, 1083 (9th Cir. 2014) (noting that the district court considered "a range of alternatives" before ordering the reduction in prison population).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has recognized that reducing prison populations can be an effective means of addressing unconstitutional conditions in prisons. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 502 (2011) (noting that "overcrowding is the primary cause of the violation" and that reducing the prison population is "necessary to remedy the constitutional violation"). The district court's order is therefore consistent with the PLRA and the Supreme Court's interpretation of it.

II. The District Court's Order Is a Proper Exercise of Its Authority to Remedy Unconstitutional Conditions

The district court's order is also a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons. The court has a "broad duty to protect constitutional rights" and may "order the release of prisoners" to remedy unconstitutional conditions. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978) (noting that courts have "broad" authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in prisons).

The Ninth Circuit has also recognized that district courts have the authority to order reductions in prison populations to remedy unconstitutional conditions. See Plata v. Brown, 754 F.3d 1070, 1083 (9th Cir. 2014) (upholding the district court's order reducing California's prison population). The district court's order is therefore a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions in California's prisons.

In sum, the district court's order reducing California's prison population does not violate the PLRA and is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy unconstitutional conditions. The order is narrowly drawn to address the specific constitutional violation found by the court and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation.
