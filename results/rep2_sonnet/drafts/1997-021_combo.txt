# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Rule Equating the Standard for Polling with the Standard for Withdrawing Recognition Is Not a Rational Application of the National Labor Relations Act.

The Board's insistence that an employer may poll its employees only upon the same showing of "objective reasonable doubt" required to withdraw recognition outright cannot be reconciled with the structure and purposes of the Act. An incumbent union's majority status is protected by a presumption, but this Court has always treated that presumption as rebuttable, not conclusive. *Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (recognizing that a bargaining order rests on a presumption of majority support that may be overcome by a showing "that the union no longer" enjoys that support). A rule that treats polling—a mechanism designed precisely to test the continued vitality of that presumption—as requiring the same proof needed to dispense with the presumption altogether drains the polling device of any independent function.

This Court has instructed that an administrative order must rest on a reasoned and rational basis, and that an agency's chosen means must bear some sensible relationship to the end it purports to serve. *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 332 U.S. 194, 196 (1947) ("an administrative order cannot be upheld unless the grounds upon which the agency acted . . . were valid and adequate"). Likewise, this Court has required the Board to explain why it departs from ordinary principles when its rule produces an anomalous result. *NLRB v. Metropolitan Life Insurance Co.*, 380 U.S. 438, 443–44 (1965) (setting aside a Board rule where the Board failed to articulate a rational connection between its chosen standard and the statutory scheme). The Board's own decision in *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967), recognized that polling, conducted with careful procedural safeguards, serves the legitimate function of resolving uncertainty about employee sentiment—a function distinct from, and less drastic than, unilateral withdrawal of recognition. Yet the Board's evidentiary standard makes the safeguarded poll no more available to an employer than the unsupervised, unilateral act of withdrawal. That result is exactly the kind of internal inconsistency this Court has refused to bless. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963) (Board's remedial choices must reflect "reasoned analysis" reconcilable with the policies of the Act).

This Court has also recognized that an employer confronted with a genuine doubt as to a union's continuing majority is entitled to seek resolution of that doubt through proper channels rather than being forced to choose between blind recognition and unlawful refusal to bargain. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 596–97 (1969) (discussing an employer's right to test disputed claims of majority support through an election or other appropriate means when it has "reasonable grounds for believing" the union lacks majority support). While deference to the Board's expertise in administering the Act is due, that deference is not unlimited; it obtains only where the Board's construction has "warrant in the record" and a "reasonable basis in law." *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944). Because the Board's polling standard serves no purpose independent of the withdrawal standard, and because it frustrates rather than furthers the Act's goal of accurately ascertaining employee sentiment, it lacks the rational basis this Court's precedents require.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt of the Union's Majority Status Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding cannot stand, because a reviewing court must examine "the record in its entirety, including the body of evidence opposed to the Board's view." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial evidence review is not a rubber stamp; the courts must set aside Board findings where the record as a whole fails to support them, even accounting for deference to credibility determinations made by the trier of fact. *NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 405 (1962) (per curiam) (remanding where the reviewing court failed to assess whether the Board's credibility-based findings were adequately supported).

Here, the ALJ and the Board disregarded multiple, independent statements from employees—made both before and contemporaneously with the poll—indicating that the union had lost the support of a majority of the unit. The Board's mechanical exclusion of employees hired shortly before the poll, and its discounting of statements by union stewards and rank-and-file employees alike as "equivocal" or "off-the-cuff," reflects precisely the kind of selective, result-driven weighing of evidence this Court condemned when it insisted that reviewing courts examine the whole record rather than only the evidence supporting the agency's conclusion. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 487–88. The persuasiveness of an agency's fact-finding depends upon "the thoroughness evident in its consideration" and the validity of its reasoning. *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). Because the Board's decision reflects neither, and because it disregards objective, corroborated indicia of the union's loss of majority support, its order should not be enforced.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
