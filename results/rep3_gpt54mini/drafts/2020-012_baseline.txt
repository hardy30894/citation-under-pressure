**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Act authorizes judicial review of “any final decision” of the Railroad Retirement Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A Board refusal to reopen a prior benefits claim is a final decision within that statutory text because it is the Board’s definitive disposition of the reopening request and leaves the claimant with no further administrative remedy. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule should be rejected.

In **Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), the D.C. Circuit correctly held that the Act “grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations.” *Id.* at 502. That reading follows the statute’s ordinary command to review final Board action. A reopening denial is final as to the very issue presented: whether the agency will disturb its prior judgment. When the Board says no, there is nothing left for the agency to do on that request. See *id.* at 504.

The opposite conclusion rests on an unduly narrow view of finality. In **Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), the Fifth Circuit held that it lacked jurisdiction to review a refusal to reopen a prior claim, reasoning that review under § 355(f) extends only to merits decisions after administrative appeals. *Id.* at 140–41. But Roberts treated a reopening denial as though it were merely an internal procedural ruling, not the culmination of a distinct administrative proceeding. That approach conflicts with the statute’s text and with the practical reality that reopening is itself the only avenue for relief from a final benefits denial long after ordinary administrative review has closed.

The Supreme Court’s decision in **Califano v. Sanders**, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), does not compel the Fifth Circuit’s rule. Sanders construed the Social Security Act and held that the Administrative Procedure Act does not itself supply jurisdiction to review an agency’s refusal to reopen a claim. *Id.* at 107–09. But the Court also emphasized that jurisdiction depends on the governing statute. *Id.* at 108. Here, Congress enacted a specific review provision for Railroad Retirement Board decisions, 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), and that provision is broader than the Social Security review scheme considered in Sanders. As the D.C. Circuit recognized, the Railroad Retirement Act’s text authorizes review of the Board’s final action on reopening requests. *Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 502.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on Roberts also creates unnecessary inconsistency among the circuits. The Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits have held that refusals to reopen are not immune from judicial review in this context. See **Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); **Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983); **Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995). The Eighth Circuit likewise recognized reviewability. See **Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board**, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991). Those decisions better reflect the statutory command that federal courts review final agency action, not just initial merits determinations.

The agency’s refusal to reopen is especially final here. Salinas asked the Board to revisit his 2006 application, and the hearing officer conclusively denied reopening because the request fell outside the regulatory time limits. That was the Board’s last word on the matter. A final agency decision does not cease to be final simply because it arises in a reopening posture. Under the ordinary meaning of finality, and under § 355(f), the courts of appeals have jurisdiction to review it.

This Court should therefore hold that a Board decision denying reopening is a “final decision” subject to judicial review, vacate the dismissal, and reach the merits of Salinas’s reopening request.
