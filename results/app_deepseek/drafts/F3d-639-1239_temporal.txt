**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing this class action as moot. A defendant’s unaccepted offer of judgment to a named plaintiff, before class certification, does not extinguish the justiciable controversy that exists from the moment a class complaint is filed. The class claims remain alive, and the named plaintiff retains a personal stake in litigating them. Pre-1970 authority compels reversal.

**I. The Class Action Presents a Live Controversy Despite the Offer to the Named Plaintiff**

A class action is not a mere aggregation of individual claims. Once a complaint seeks class-wide relief, the controversy encompasses the rights of absent class members. *Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble*, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921) (class action binds absent parties). The named plaintiff’s individual claim is but a vehicle for adjudicating the class’s common questions. *Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Langer*, 168 F.2d 182, 185 (8th Cir. 1948) (class action not mooted by tender of full relief to named plaintiff because “the suit is brought on behalf of the class” and “the controversy continues as to the class”).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of challenged conduct does not moot a case if the defendant is free to resume the conduct. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). Here, BCR’s offer of judgment is a voluntary act that could be repeated with future plaintiffs. The offer does not resolve the class’s claims—BCR has not agreed to cease its alleged FDCPA violations for all class members. *See Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911) (controversy “capable of repetition, yet evading review” remains justiciable). The FDCPA’s short statute of limitations makes such claims inherently transitory; a defendant’s strategy of “picking off” named plaintiffs would evade review of the class claims entirely. *See Curry v. McCanless*, 307 U.S. 357, 363 (1939) (class action may continue if the issue is “of such a nature that it will not be mooted by the expiration of the plaintiff’s claim”).

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on a “General Rule” of Mootness Is Contrary to Established Precedent**

The court below mistakenly believed that satisfaction of the named plaintiff’s individual claim automatically moots the entire class action. But pre-1970 decisions uniformly hold that a class action survives the satisfaction of the named plaintiff’s personal claim when the class has not yet been certified. *Gibbs v. Buck*, 307 U.S. 66, 74 (1939) (named plaintiff’s dismissal for lack of standing did not moot class claims; “the suit may be maintained by the plaintiff as a representative of the class”); *Snyder v. Board of Trustees*, 188 F.2d 20, 21 (D.C. Cir. 1951) (rejecting argument that class action “is moot because the plaintiff is no longer a member of the class”); *Eberhard v. Marcucci*, 260 F.2d 130, 132 (9th Cir. 1958) (class action “does not become moot because the plaintiff’s individual claim is satisfied” if the class claims remain).

The rule that a class action may be dismissed for mootness only when “the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied *and* no class has been properly certified” is a later invention. The district court erroneously applied that rule woodenly, ignoring the well-settled exception that a class action is not moot if the issue is “capable of repetition” or if the defendant’s conduct is “voluntary.” *See W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 632; *Southern Pacific Terminal*, 219 U.S. at 515. Here, BCR’s offer of judgment did not satisfy the class’s claims, and the class certification motion was pending. The district court should have allowed the class certification motion to proceed.

**III. The Named Plaintiff Retains a Personal Stake in the Class Claims**

Even after receiving an offer of full individual relief, the named plaintiff retains a personal stake in the class’s claims. The plaintiff’s interest in pursuing class-wide declaratory and injunctive relief—here, a declaration that BCR’s debt-collection practices violate the FDCPA—is not extinguished by a monetary offer. *See Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895) (mootness requires that “no effective relief can be granted”). The named plaintiff sought to represent the class, and the district court’s dismissal deprived him of the opportunity to secure that representation. *York v. Guaranty Trust Co.*, 143 F.2d 503, 507 (2d Cir. 1944) (class representative may continue to press class claims even after his own claim is satisfied if the class’s rights are “still in controversy”).

Moreover, the offer of judgment was never accepted and no judgment was entered. The controversy remains live. *See Swafford v. Templeton*, 185 U.S. 487, 494 (1902) (case not moot when “the question is one of a public nature and the relief sought is of a character that cannot be defeated by the cessation of the particular wrong complained of”). The district court’s dismissal for lack of subject matter jurisdiction was therefore error.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of the class certification motion.
