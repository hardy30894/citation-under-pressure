# ARGUMENT

## I. RESPONDENT HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

### A. Clemency Is an Executive Act of Grace, Not an Extension of the Judicial Process

Since the Founding, executive clemency has been understood as "a matter of grace" that "may be granted according to conditions which the President in the public interest may deem appropriate." *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927). This Court's precedents have consistently placed clemency outside the ordinary due process framework applicable to adjudicative proceedings. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that a prisoner has no constitutionally protected liberty interest in the mere possibility of commutation, even where the state has established a formal application procedure. The Court explained that "[t]he power to commute is a matter of grace" and that "no matter how citizens or inmates might rank the interests at stake, the Constitution does not mandate a fair procedure" in the clemency context absent an independent liberty interest. *Id.* at 464-65.

Critically, this Court in *Dumschat* rejected precisely the theory Respondent advances here—that repeated invocation of a clemency procedure, or its systematic availability, transforms clemency into a component of the adjudicatory process entitled to due process protection. The Court held that "a decision to commute is not required" by mere existence of the procedure, and that Connecticut's practice of considering all applications "creates no more than a unilateral hope." *Id.* at 465. As Chief Justice Burger emphasized, "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.* at 464 (citing *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976)).

### B. The Sixth Circuit's Reliance on *Evitts v. Lucey* Cannot Be Reconciled with This Court's Clemency Jurisprudence

The court below erred in extending *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), to clemency proceedings. *Evitts* held that once a state creates an appeal as of right, that appeal becomes "an integral part of the . . . system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant," quoting *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), and must therefore satisfy due process. But *Evitts* itself recognized limits to this logic, noting that the right to counsel does not extend to habeas corpus proceedings, citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974). *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394.

If habeas corpus—a proceeding far more closely tied to the adjudication of guilt than clemency—falls outside the *Evitts* due process framework, executive clemency, occurring after all judicial proceedings have concluded and resting entirely within the discretion of the executive, falls outside it a fortiori. This Court should decline to extend *Evitts* beyond its logical stopping point, particularly where doing so would require courts to supervise an executive function historically insulated from judicial review. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464.

### C. Extending Due Process Protections to Clemency Would Produce the Unintended Consequences This Court Has Repeatedly Warned Against

This Court has cautioned against extending procedural due process protections into contexts where doing so creates disincentives for states to offer discretionary benefits at all. In *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), the Court criticized *Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460 (1983), for having "create[d] disincentives for States to codify prison management procedures in the interest of uniform treatment." *Sandin*, 515 U.S. at 482. The same logic applies with full force here: if offering a voluntary clemency interview triggers judicially enforceable due process obligations, states will simply decline to offer such procedures, to the detriment of capital defendants generally.

Moreover, *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), confirms that even where a state creates a discretionary release procedure, due process protections attach only when the state has created a legitimate expectation of a particular outcome through mandatory language—not through the mere existence of a hearing process. Ohio's clemency procedure, which the Sixth Circuit itself acknowledged vests "almost unfettered discretion" in the Governor, creates no such expectation.

## II. THE VOLUNTARY CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

### A. The Regulation Imposes No Compulsion

The Fifth Amendment protects against compelled self-incrimination; it does not create a right to avoid all consequences of voluntary silence. Ohio's regulation "does not empower the parole board or its representatives to compel a prisoner who requests a personal interview to answer a single question." A prisoner "remains free to say as much or as little at the interview as he wishes." Nothing in the regulation conditions the grant of clemency on waiver of the privilege, nor does it authorize adverse consequences for invoking it.

This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, the Court held that relief against a hypothetical future use of compelled statements was "premature" where no concrete criminal jeopardy had yet materialized. *Id.* at 324. The same reasoning applies here: Respondent's claim rests on speculation about what might occur if he volunteers information during an interview he need not attend, and if that information were later used in some unspecified future proceeding. Respondent's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing conviction or sentence. There is no live controversy ripe for adjudication.

### B. No Unconstitutional Condition Exists Absent Compulsion

The unconstitutional-conditions doctrine, as developed in cases such as *Baxter*, requires a showing that the state has attached a penalty to the exercise of a constitutional right. Because the clemency interview is optional and no negative inference is mandated by regulation for declining it, no such penalty exists. Any risk of self-incrimination is, at most, theoretical and speculative—precisely the kind of hypothetical injury this Court has held insufficient to warrant premature judicial intervention.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed.
