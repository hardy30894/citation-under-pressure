## ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding Exemption 2 of the Freedom of Information Act to cover documents that bear no relation to “internal personnel rules and practices.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The court’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard has no basis in the statute and conflicts with this Court’s precedent narrowly construing FOIA exemptions.

### I. Exemption 2’s Text and This Court’s Precedent Require a Tight Nexus to Personnel Matters

Exemption 2 protects only matters that are “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” This Court has consistently interpreted that language to cover two categories: (1) routine administrative matters of no genuine public interest, such as parking or lunch-hour rules, and (2) internal rules whose disclosure would risk circumvention of agency regulation, but only if they are *personnel* rules. *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 363–64, 369 (1976). The critical limitation is that the information must pertain to *personnel*—the agency’s own employees and their internal operations. *Id.* at 363.

In *Rose*, this Court explained that Exemption 2 was designed for “minor employment matters” and “housekeeping” information. 425 U.S. at 366. The Court expressly rejected the notion that Exemption 2 could be used to withhold “disclosure of matters of genuine public interest” simply because they had some internal dimension. *Id.* at 369. The touchstone is whether the information “relates to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the agency—not whether it is “for internal use” or “predominantly internal.”

### II. The “Predominantly Internal” Test Ignores the Statutory Requirement That the Information Must Relate to *Personnel* Rules

The Ninth Circuit adopted a standard that asks only whether documents are “predominantly internal” and would present a risk of circumvention if disclosed. App. 15–16. That standard eliminates the statutory phrase “personnel rules and practices” and substitutes a vague “internality” test. But FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” *United States Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). The Ninth Circuit’s approach reads the word “personnel” out of the statute.

This Court has never approved a “predominantly internal” test. To the contrary, *Rose* treated Exemption 2 as applying only to “rules and practices *governing personnel*,” not to any internal document. 425 U.S. at 362 (emphasis added). The ESQD arc maps are technical calculations about explosive blast ranges—they describe physical phenomena, not employee conduct. They do not instruct Navy personnel how to perform their duties or govern their behavior. They are simply data about the effects of explosions.

### III. The ESQD Maps Do Not Qualify as “Internal Personnel Rules or Practices”

The Navy’s own affidavits confirm that the ESQD maps are “long term planning tool[s]” used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” App. 53. They are not rules or practices that guide employee behavior. Unlike the law enforcement manuals at issue in *Rose* and *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), the ESQD maps contain no instructions, procedures, or standards for personnel to follow. They are akin to engineering blueprints—scientific data that could be used by anyone, not just agency employees.

The D.C. Circuit’s own cases recognize that Exemption 2 does not reach “purely factual, scientific, or technical data.” *See, e.g., Schwaner v. Department of the Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793, 796 (D.C. Cir. 1990). The ESQD maps fall squarely within that category.

### IV. The Risk of Circumvention Cannot Expand Exemption 2 Beyond Its Plain Meaning

The Ninth Circuit reasoned that disclosure of the ESQD maps could enable someone to plan an attack or circumvent safety regulations. App. 16. But the risk of circumvention is not a free-floating justification for withholding documents. Under *Rose*, the risk of circumvention test applies *only* to materials that already fall within the scope of “internal personnel rules and practices.” 425 U.S. at 369. It does not permit agencies to exempt any internal document that might be misused. If that were the rule, Exemption 2 would swallow FOIA’s presumption of disclosure.

Congress provided separate exemptions for national security and law enforcement information—Exemptions 1 and 7—which are specifically tailored to address the Navy’s stated concerns. *See* 5 U.S.C. §§ 552(b)(1), (b)(7)(F). The Navy chose not to classify the ESQD maps under Exemption 1 and could not meet Exemption 7’s requirement that records be “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” App. 6–7 (dissent). It may not use Exemption 2 as a catch-all.

### Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard violates the plain language of Exemption 2 and this Court’s controlling precedent. The judgment should be reversed.
