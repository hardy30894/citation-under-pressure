**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit properly consulted the full record in determining that Greer failed to satisfy the third and fourth prongs of plain-error review. That approach is compelled by this Court’s precedents.

Plain-error review requires the defendant to show that the error affected his substantial rights and seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993). In making those determinations, “a reviewing court must consider the error in the context of the entire record.” Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 469 (1997). The Court has repeatedly confirmed that the relevant inquiry is not limited to evidence presented to the jury. See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002) (holding that, under Rule 52(b), “the court of appeals may consult the whole record” when assessing whether a Rule 11 error affected substantial rights); United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (same).

This principle applies with particular force to the Rehaif knowledge-of-status element. A defendant who seeks to show that the omission of that element affected his substantial rights must demonstrate “a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” Molina-Martinez v. United States, 578 U.S. 189, 194 (2016). Because the defendant’s prior felony conviction is itself the fact that supplies the prohibited status, the existence and nature of that conviction are indispensable to the prejudice inquiry. The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted consistently with Vonn and Johnson when it examined Greer’s undisputed criminal history rather than confining itself to the trial transcript.

Greer’s contrary rule—that an appellate court may look only at evidence admitted before the jury—would sever the prejudice analysis from the very facts that determine whether the defendant was in fact a prohibited person. Such a limitation finds no support in Olano or its progeny and would produce the anomalous result that a defendant with multiple felony convictions could obtain reversal even though the record conclusively shows he knew of his status. The Court has rejected comparable efforts to restrict plain-error review to an artificially truncated record. See Vonn, 535 U.S. at 59.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
