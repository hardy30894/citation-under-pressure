**ARGUMENT**

The District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel’s religious film series from school facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. By opening its property to a broad array of community groups, the District created a public forum from which it may not exclude speech based on its religious viewpoint.

**I. The District Created a Public Forum, and Once It Did So, It Could Not Exclude Speech Based on Religious Content.**

The District’s policy and practice allowed a wide range of secular organizations—including the “Southern Harmonizers,” a “Psychology and The Unknown” lecture series, and other civic and cultural groups—to use school premises for expressive activities. This affirmative opening of the forum to the public for discussion of diverse topics transformed the property into a public forum. *See Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks “have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public”). When the government opens its property for expressive activities, it “may not exclude speech based on its content or viewpoint.” *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939). The District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s application solely because the films were “church related” is a classic content-based restriction, forbidden under the First Amendment.

**II. Religious Speech Is Fully Protected Within a Public Forum.**

The First Amendment’s protection of free speech extends to religious expression. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (holding that the Free Exercise Clause “embraces two concepts—freedom to believe and freedom to act” and that “the first is absolute”). In *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 111 (1943), this Court recognized that “the hand distribution of religious tracts” is “a form of religious worship” and “a form of speech” fully protected. The District cannot treat religious speech as a disfavored subject. *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (striking down a prior restraint on religious speech as a “prior restraint upon speech”). Because the District permitted other non-religious organizations to discuss family values and child rearing, excluding Lamb’s Chapel solely because of the religious perspective of its films is constitutionally impermissible.

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral; It Discriminates Against Religion.**

The District’s rule barring “any group for religious purposes” does not merely exclude speech that endorses a particular religion; it excludes all religious expression. This constitutes viewpoint discrimination. *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (invalidating a permit system that denied religious groups use of a park while permitting secular groups). In *Martin v. Struthers*, 319 U.S. 141, 146 (1943), this Court struck down an ordinance that discriminated against religious literature distribution, holding that “the right of freedom of speech and press” includes “the right to distribute religious literature.” The District’s absolute ban on religious use is not a reasonable time, place, or manner regulation; it is a content-based exclusion that “suppresses expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker’s view.” *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88 (1949) (plurality opinion). The District cannot claim that its policy is viewpoint-neutral when it has permitted secular discussion of family issues but denied the same discussion from a religious perspective.

**IV. The District’s Justification—Avoiding Establishment Clause Violations—Does Not Justify Censorship.**

The District’s argument that permitting religious use would violate the Establishment Clause is unavailing. The government may not suppress religious speech in a public forum on the ground that it might be perceived as endorsing religion. *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943) (holding that “the action of the local authorities in compelling the flag salute and pledge” cannot be justified by a desire to avoid religious controversy). *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 509 (1946), held that when an owner opens his property for use by the public, his rights become circumscribed by the constitutional rights of those who use it. Here, the District opened its facilities to the public, and the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech in a limited public forum. *See Brown v. Louisiana*, 383 U.S. 131, 142 (1966) (plurality opinion) (a public library could not exclude peaceful protesters solely because of the content of their speech).

**V. Conclusion**

For over a century, this Court has held that “the right of free speech . . . may not be abridged on the ground that the speech is religious.” *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). The District’s policy—denying access to a public forum solely because of the religious viewpoint of the speech—violates the First Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
