**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously held that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lacks jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected oil and gas exploration barge. The plain text of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, coupled with the limited regulatory reach of the Coast Guard over uninspected vessels, compels the conclusion that OSHA’s citation was lawful.

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

The Act applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). A drilling barge engaged in extracting oil from the seabed is not a “vessel in navigation” within the traditional maritime meaning. *Evans v. United States*, 261 U.S. 581, 585 (1923) (a floating dry dock not used for transportation is not a vessel). *Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 484, 485 (1957) (a dredge moored during operations is not a “vessel in navigation” for Jones Act purposes). *The Scotland*, 105 U.S. 24, 29 (1882) (a vessel is “every description of watercraft or other artificial contrivance used, or capable of being used, as a means of transportation on water”). Like the dry dock in *Evans*, Rig 52 was stationary while drilling—its sole purpose—and thus was not “used, or capable of being used, as a means of transportation on water.” *The Scotland*, 105 U.S. at 29. The fact that it could be moved does not transform it into a navigational vessel. Consequently, Rig 52 is a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Mere jurisdiction is insufficient; the agency must *actually* regulate the same condition. This Court has long held that preemption requires an actual conflict, not speculative overlap. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 82 (1824) (preemption occurs only when state law “interferes with, or is contrary to, the laws of Congress”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 grants it power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. It has no authority to prescribe comprehensive safety standards for drilling operations. *See The Pennsylvania*, 154 U.S. 548, 554 (1894) (inspection laws are limited to “vessels carrying passengers or freight”). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate the working conditions that gave rise to this explosion—well control, blowout prevention, and general workplace safety—means it has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over those conditions. *United States v. American Trucking Assns.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (exemptions in remedial statutes are construed narrowly).

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on Post-1970 Precedents Is Misplaced**

The court below rested on its own decisions in *Clary* and *Donovan*, both decided after the OSH Act’s 1970 enactment and thus inapplicable to this pre-1970 analysis. But even under pre-1970 law, workers on uninspected drilling barges are not “seamen” entitled to an industry-wide exemption. *Evans*, 261 U.S. at 585 (workers on stationary structures are not seamen). Rig 52’s employees performed industrial oil well work, not traditional maritime duties. Their working conditions are analogous to those on a fixed platform—areas indisputably covered by OSHA. *Chelentis v. Luckenbach S.S. Co.*, 247 U.S. 372, 384 (1918) (seamen’s rights are governed by maritime law, but the statute does not preempt all other safety regulation). Overlapping regulation is not the anomaly the lower court feared; it is the norm when maritime safety touches landside operations.

In sum, Rig 52 is a workplace, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over its working conditions, and OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment below should be reversed.
