ARGUMENT

I. Bryant has standing to enforce the arbitration award.

An individual employee may sue to enforce rights under a collective bargaining agreement when the union has breached its duty of fair representation. Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 186 (1967). The district court’s contrary holding conflicts with this principle. Bryant’s grievance concerning the Red Letter Day policy was processed through arbitration, and the resulting award confers enforceable benefits upon him as the aggrieved employee. See United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593, 597 (1960) (arbitration awards “must draw [their] essence from the collective bargaining agreement”).

II. The district court misapplied the burden-shifting framework clarified in Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.

In Title VII and § 1981 cases, once the plaintiff establishes a prima facie case, the burden shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason; the plaintiff may then show that reason is pretextual. McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802–04 (1973); Patterson v. McLean Credit Union, 491 U.S. 164, 186 (1989) (§ 1981). Reeves holds that “a plaintiff’s prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer’s asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated.” 530 U.S. 133, 148 (2000). The district court required Bryant to produce direct evidence of discriminatory animus beyond pretext, contrary to Reeves.

III. Genuine issues of material fact preclude summary judgment on the discrimination and retaliation claims.

Summary judgment is improper when the record contains evidence from which a reasonable jury could find for the nonmoving party. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). Bryant, an African-American single parent, received escalating discipline after protesting the Red Letter Day policy on child-care grounds, while the company refused a union offer to cover his assignments and terminated him after he accepted one of four proffered accommodations. These facts create triable issues whether Bell Atlantic’s overtime policy was applied in a discriminatory manner and whether the stated reason for discharge—insubordination—was pretext for retaliation. See Tex. Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981). The district court’s grant of summary judgment therefore cannot stand.
