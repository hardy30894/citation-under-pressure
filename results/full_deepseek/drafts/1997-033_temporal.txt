**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s invocation of §1404(a) to retain a case for trial after the conclusion of §1407(a) pretrial proceedings violates the plain text of the Multidistrict Litigation statute. Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” remand each transferred action to its originating district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. §1407(a). The word “shall” is mandatory, leaving no discretion to the transferee court. *See Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 493 (1935) (statutory “shall” imposes a duty). A transferee court’s use of §1404(a) to reassign the case to itself for trial directly contravenes that command.

When a specific statute—here, §1407(a)—governs a particular subject, it controls over a general grant of authority like §1404(a). *See Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin*, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932) (specific statute prevails over general one). Section 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision, but §1407(a) is a specific, later-enacted statute that carefully limits the Panel’s transfer power to pretrial proceedings only. Congress created a comprehensive scheme: the Panel may transfer cases for coordinated pretrial proceedings, but after those proceedings conclude, the Panel “shall” remand. The Panel’s own Rule 14(b), which purports to authorize transferee courts to invoke §1404(a) after remand, cannot override the statute. *See Standard Oil Co. v. United States*, 283 U.S. 163, 169 (1931) (agency rules inconsistent with statute are void).

No pre-1970 authority supports the district court’s action. The only cases cited by the court below—*Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982)—are post-1970 decisions that are not binding on this Court and that conflict with the plain statutory language. Indeed, the *Pfizer* court acknowledged that its holding was “not compelled by the language of the statute” and rested instead on policy considerations. 447 F.2d at 124-25. Policy cannot override a clear congressional mandate. *See United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, 307 (1947) (courts must enforce the statute as written, even if it leads to inconvenient results).

The multidistrict statute reflects a deliberate choice: plaintiffs retain their right to trial in the original forum after pretrial consolidation ends. *See Pfizer*, 447 F.2d at 124 (noting that Congress intended “to preserve the plaintiff’s original venue choice”). To allow a transferee court to short-circuit that choice by applying §1404(a) strips the remand requirement of all meaning. The district court’s order is therefore clearly erroneous and must be reversed.
