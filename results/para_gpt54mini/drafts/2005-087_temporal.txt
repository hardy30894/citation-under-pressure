The Due Process Clause does not confer the broad right petitioner claims. This Court has repeatedly held that States retain substantial authority to define criminal responsibility and to regulate the evidence admitted on questions of intent and insanity. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968).

First, the Constitution permits States to allocate and limit the use of mental-health evidence in criminal trials. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. The Court rejected the contention that due process compelled a more favorable insanity regime, explaining that the State was free to define the defense and to place the burden of persuasion on the accused. Id. at 798-99. If a State may constitutionally require a defendant to prove insanity, it follows a fortiori that it may confine expert testimony to that defense and prevent the same evidence from being used to negate the separate element of intent. Due process does not freeze one evidentiary theory of mental illness into constitutional law.

Second, the cases on which petitioner relies recognize that the State may define the crime and the defenses to it. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held, in a federal prosecution, that insanity evidence could rebut guilt once raised. But Davis rested on the federal common law of criminal procedure, not on a constitutional command binding the States. Id. at 484-85. The distinction matters. The question here is not what rule might be preferable, but what the Fourteenth Amendment requires. Nothing in Davis suggests that state law must permit psychiatric evidence to be used both as insanity evidence and as a separate means of disproving mens rea.

Third, due process protects the requirement that the State prove the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, but it does not prescribe the exact evidentiary route by which a defendant may contest intent. In Leland, the Court acknowledged that “the burden of proving insanity” may constitutionally rest on the defendant, even though insanity may bear on criminal responsibility. 343 U.S. at 798. And in Davis, the Court described insanity as an affirmative matter distinct from the prosecution’s proof of the act and intent charged. 160 U.S. at 484-85. Arizona’s rule respects that distinction: the State must still prove the charged mental element, but it may require psychiatric evidence to be channeled through the insanity defense rather than used to create a quasi-diminished-capacity defense the State has chosen not to recognize.

Fourth, petitioner’s theory would constitutionalize a particular approach to mental-disease evidence and sharply limit the States’ historic power over criminal defenses. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the States may decide how far to extend exculpation for mental illness. In Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), the Court declined to constitutionalize a broader insanity-based excuse for conduct and cautioned against converting “constitutional law” into “scientific” or policy judgments about criminal responsibility. Id. at 536-37. The plurality and concurrences alike reflect the same principle: questions about the line between legal insanity, mens rea, and evidentiary admissibility are matters largely for state law, not federal due process. Id. at 535-37, 547-51, 552-67.

Fifth, petitioner cannot transform a state evidentiary limitation into a due process violation merely by relabeling the excluded evidence as “intent” evidence. This Court has long recognized that the Constitution does not require States to accept every defense theory a defendant proposes. See Leland, 343 U.S. at 799; Powell, 392 U.S. at 536-37. Arizona was entitled to conclude that psychiatric testimony about a defendant’s mental disease is relevant to insanity, but too indeterminate and potentially confusing when offered to negate intent independent of the insanity defense. The Due Process Clause does not strip the State of that judgment.

Finally, the result petitioner seeks would undercut the very structure of the trial below. Arizona allowed petitioner to present expert evidence on insanity; it did not prevent him from contesting the State’s proof of the act itself or of the objective circumstances surrounding the killing. Due process requires a fair opportunity to defend, not a constitutional entitlement to present the same mental-health evidence under multiple doctrinal labels. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968).

Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed. Arizona’s rule is fully consistent with pre-1970 due process precedent, which leaves to the States the authority to define insanity defenses and to regulate the use of expert mental-state evidence.
