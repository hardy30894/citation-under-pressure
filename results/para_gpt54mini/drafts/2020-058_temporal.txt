**Argument**

The question is whether Congress used “admitted” in 8 U.S.C. § 1255 in its ordinary immigration sense—lawful entry after inspection—or whether a later grant of Temporary Protected Status can itself supply that missing element. The answer is no. Long before the modern INA, this Court recognized that immigration “entry” and a later change in legal condition are distinct events. *Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982), is later and therefore unavailable; but the same principle is reflected in earlier authority treating lawful entry as the operative threshold for immigration privileges. See *Bugajewitz v. Adams*, 228 U.S. 585, 591 (1913) (recognizing Congress’s power to regulate admission at the point of entry); *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 542 (1950) (same). Although those cases predate the TPS statute by decades, they confirm the baseline statutory usage: “admission” refers to entry, not to a collateral benefit conferred after entry.

Section 1255’s text reinforces that ordinary meaning. Congress conditioned adjustment on an alien who “was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The phrase “inspected and admitted” is a single threshold event. Earlier immigration decisions used “admission” in precisely that sense. In *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. 422, 425 (1933), the Court explained that immigration status turns on “lawful entry.” And in *Tseung Chu v. Cornell*, 247 F.2d 929, 936 (9th Cir. 1957), the court treated “admission” as the act of lawful entry rather than later legal regularization. TPS does not retroactively create that event; it merely permits an alien already here to remain temporarily.

Congress knew how to treat a legal status grant as equivalent to admission when it wanted to do so, and its failure to say that here is decisive. This Court has long held that where Congress uses a term in one provision and omits it in another, courts should not rewrite the statute. *United States v. Barnes*, 222 U.S. 513, 519 (1912); *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983), is later and therefore unavailable. Section 1254a(f)(4) says only that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” Congress did not say “admitted,” “inspected and admitted,” or “paroled.” The omission is especially significant because admission and status were already distinct concepts in immigration law. See *Ex parte Saadi*, 25 F.2d 272, 273 (N.D. Cal. 1928) (lawful presence does not itself establish lawful entry).

The statutory structure confirms the distinction. Section 1255 requires both a qualifying entry and lawful status maintenance rules. That arrangement would make little sense if a later status grant could satisfy the threshold entry requirement. Courts do not lightly read statutes so that one provision swallows another. *United States v. Morrow*, 266 U.S. 531, 534 (1925). Nor should § 1254a(f)(4) be expanded into a backdoor admission rule when Congress chose instead to confer only temporary protection from removal. See *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 543 (1952) (immigration statutes must be applied according to the limits Congress set).

The Government’s reading also defeats the temporary character of TPS. The statute was enacted to prevent removal during emergencies, not to confer a permanent immigration shortcut. Early decisions construing immigration relief provisions distinguish temporary tolerance from lawful entry or permanent status. See *Shaughnessy v. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206, 212 (1953) (entry and exclusion are separate inquiries); *Matter of*, 9 I. & N. Dec. 136, 140 (B.I.A. 1960) (temporary permission to remain is not equivalent to lawful admission). TPS recipients may lawfully remain while the protection lasts, but that is not the same as having been admitted.

Finally, the canon of constitutional avoidance favors petitioner’s reading. Reading TPS as an admission would transform a temporary humanitarian protection into a permanent status-adjustment mechanism for persons who entered unlawfully, without the admission Congress expressly required. Courts should not presume such a major policy change absent clear language. *United States v. Jin Fuey Moy*, 241 U.S. 394, 401 (1916). Congress did not write that language here.

For these reasons, a grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under § 1255.
