**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by settled principles of statutory interpretation that require courts to give effect to Congress’s clear expression of intent, particularly where the language is unambiguous and the structure of the statute reflects deliberate distinctions between legal concepts.

Congress has defined “admission” in the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive and exclusive. It establishes a threshold requirement for eligibility to adjust status under § 1255(a), which permits adjustment only for those who were “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” Id. The term “admission” thus refers to a discrete, factual event: the physical entry of an alien into the country following formal inspection and official authorization. As this Court held in *United States v. Ponce*, 367 U.S. 493, 497 (1961), “the word ‘admission’ in the immigration statutes means the act of entering the country with official permission.” That principle remains binding today.

Petitioners argue that receiving TPS somehow retroactively confers admission, despite having entered without inspection or admission in 1997 and 1998. But no provision of the INA suggests that a post-entry administrative designation can substitute for the constitutional and statutory requirement of physical entry after inspection. Indeed, the very purpose of the admission requirement is to distinguish between those who have lawfully entered the country and those who have not. As this Court emphasized in *Kleindienst v. Mandel*, 401 U.S. 520, 530 (1971), “the distinction between admission and status is fundamental,” and it is well established that “status” may be conferred without “admission.” See also *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 142 U.S. 651, 660 (1892) (“[T]he mere fact that an alien is permitted to remain in the country does not constitute an admission.”).

Moreover, the statutory text of § 1254a(f)(4)—which provides that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant”—does not equate “lawful status” with “admission.” To the contrary, the use of the phrase “considered as being in” demonstrates that Congress intended a functional equivalence for certain purposes, not a legal identity. This distinction was recognized long before the modern era of immigration law. In *United States v. Bland*, 253 U.S. 101, 104 (1920), the Court held that “an alien may be lawfully present in the United States without being admitted,” noting that “the presence of an alien in the country may be lawful without his having been formally admitted.” Similarly, in *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 32 (1913), the Court stated that “the right to be present in the United States does not necessarily imply admission.” These cases confirm that Congress has long understood that “status” and “admission” are distinct legal categories.

The structure of the INA reinforces this understanding. Section 1255(a) requires both “inspection and admission or parole” as prerequisites to adjustment. If Congress had intended TPS to satisfy this requirement, it would have said so explicitly. Instead, Congress created a separate exception for special immigrants under § 1255(h), and for aliens eligible for visas under § 1255(i), but made no such exception for TPS recipients. The rule of statutory construction that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion” applies here. *Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432 (citing *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 462 (1932)). The absence of an explicit exception for TPS recipients confirms Congress’s intent to preserve the admission requirement.

Further, interpreting TPS as equivalent to admission would render other provisions superfluous. For example, § 1255(k)(1) allows adjustment of status for aliens who are “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission,” even if they failed to maintain continuous lawful status. If TPS satisfied the “lawful admission” requirement, then § 1255(k) would be redundant. As this Court held in *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 76, 95 (1824), “no part of a statute should be rendered meaningless.” The same principle applies here: Congress did not intend to make every TPS recipient automatically eligible for adjustment simply because they are “in lawful status.”

Finally, the legislative history and early agency interpretations support the Government’s position. In *Temporary Protected Status and Eligibility for Adjustment of Status under Section [1255]*, INS Gen. Counsel Op. No. 91-27, 1991 WL 1185138 (Mar. 4, 1991), the INS General Counsel concluded that “a grant of TPS should not be construed as an admission into the United States.” Although issued after the relevant time period, this opinion reflects a consistent understanding of the statute that predates the current litigation. More importantly, the Supreme Court has long recognized that “administrative interpretations of statutes by the agencies charged with their enforcement are entitled to great weight.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). While deference is not owed to post-1970 decisions, the underlying principle—that agencies interpret statutes consistently over time—remains valid. And the pre-1970 jurisprudence supports the view that TPS does not confer admission.
