ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) because the trial court's jury instruction on "true threats" was flawed. The instruction allowed the jury to convict Elonis based on an objective standard, rather than requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten.

The Supreme Court's decision in Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), controls this case. In Black, the Court held that a "true threat" is a statement that a speaker "means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." Id. at 359. This definition implies that a speaker's subjective intent is a crucial element of a "true threat." The Third Circuit's contrary holding in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which applied an objective standard, is no longer good law in light of Black.

The objective standard applied by the trial court and affirmed by the Third Circuit is inconsistent with the First Amendment's protection of speech. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the First Amendment protects speech that is "unpopular, controversial, or even outrageous." Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). An objective standard for "true threats" would chill protected speech, as speakers would be uncertain about how their words might be interpreted by others. See id. (reversing conviction under a statute prohibiting threats to the President because the defendant's statement was "a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition to the President").

In contrast, a subjective standard for "true threats" ensures that only those who intentionally threaten others are punished, while protecting those who engage in hyperbolic or artistic speech. Elonis' Facebook posts, which were the basis for his conviction, were clearly artistic expressions and not intended to be taken literally. His conviction under an objective standard thus raises serious First Amendment concerns.

The government may argue that a subjective standard would allow individuals to escape liability for threatening speech simply by claiming they did not intend to threaten. However, this concern is overstated. Juries can infer a defendant's subjective intent from the context and circumstances surrounding the speech. See, e.g., United States v. Stewart, 420 F.3d 1007, 1015-16 (9th Cir. 2005) (considering the context and circumstances of the defendant's statement in determining whether it was a "true threat").

In conclusion, the Third Circuit's decision affirming Elonis' conviction should be reversed because the trial court's jury instruction on "true threats" was flawed. A subjective standard for "true threats" is required by the First Amendment and the Supreme Court's decision in Virginia v. Black.
