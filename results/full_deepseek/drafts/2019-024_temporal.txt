**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a plaintiff’s subjective unawareness—even when all relevant information was disclosed—creates a genuine dispute of material fact under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That provision bars suit three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” Actual knowledge means knowledge of the underlying facts constituting the breach, not knowledge of its legal characterization. A plaintiff cannot avoid the limitations period by choosing not to read or failing to recall information that was fully disclosed.

**I. “Actual Knowledge” Requires Knowledge of the Facts, Not Knowledge of the Law**

The plain meaning of “actual knowledge” is knowledge of the factual transaction or event, not awareness that the transaction violates ERISA. This Court has long held that statutes of limitations are triggered by discovery of the facts giving rise to the claim, not by discovery of the legal theory. *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 140 (1879) (limitations period in fraud case runs from “the discovery of the facts constituting the fraud,” not from “the time when the plaintiff discovered the legal effect of those facts”). Similarly, in *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 348 (1874), the Court explained that a plaintiff must have “knowledge of the facts” to start the limitations clock; ignorance of the law does not delay it.

Congress enacted § 1113(2) against this backdrop. The term “actual knowledge” was well understood in 1974 to mean knowledge of the factual occurrence, not of its legal significance. Indeed, the original ERISA included a separate “constructive knowledge” provision based on filed reports, which Congress repealed in 1987. That repeal underscores that only *actual* knowledge—not what a plaintiff *should* have known—triggers the three-year bar. But actual knowledge does not require subjective awareness of illegality. *See, e.g., United States v. Oregon Lumber Co.*, 260 U.S. 290, 300 (1922) (construing “actual knowledge” in a timber statute as knowledge of the physical facts, not of legal rights).

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a plaintiff must know both “that those investments occurred” and “that they were imprudent” conflates knowledge of facts with a legal conclusion. Imprudence is a legal standard, not a fact. A plaintiff who knows that his retirement funds were invested in hedge funds and private equity—as Sulyma did from the disclosed Fund Fact Sheets—has actual knowledge of the “breach or violation” because he knows the factual basis for the claim. He need not also know that the investment was imprudent under ERISA. *Cf. Texas & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Potter*, 291 U.S. 502, 506 (1934) (limitations period begins when plaintiff “knows the facts” that would put a reasonable person on inquiry, not when he appreciates their legal significance).

**II. A Plaintiff’s Failure to Read or Recall Disclosed Information Does Not Negate Actual Knowledge**

Intel disclosed all relevant information to Sulyma before October 29, 2012: Fund Fact Sheets, annual disclosures, and website materials explaining the alternative investments, their strategy, and risks. Sulyma admitted he accessed some of these documents. He testified only that he did not *recall* reading them or was not *aware* of the investments. That is insufficient to create a factual dispute.

This Court has long recognized that a party cannot avoid knowledge by willful blindness or failure to examine available information. *See, e.g., Pocahontas Coal Co. v. United States*, 67 F.2d 226, 229 (4th Cir. 1933) (actual knowledge includes “knowledge of such facts as would lead a reasonably prudent man to make inquiry”); *cf. Wood*, 101 U.S. at 143 (plaintiff “cannot shut his eyes to the facts” and claim ignorance). While § 1113(2) requires “actual” rather than constructive knowledge, it does not require that a plaintiff actively read or remember every document. Actual knowledge can be established by showing that the plaintiff was presented with the information and had the means to know it. *See Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 349 (knowledge of facts that would “excite inquiry” is sufficient to start limitations period when plaintiff fails to investigate).

Here, Sulyma had access to the disclosures and used the websites. His testimony that he did not “recall” seeing the documents is not evidence that he lacked actual knowledge; it is evidence of faulty memory. A statute of limitations cannot be defeated by a plaintiff’s self-serving assertion of non-recollection when the record shows he was exposed to the information. *See Oregon Lumber Co.*, 260 U.S. at 300 (actual knowledge may be inferred from circumstances, even if plaintiff denies awareness).

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Would Eviscerate the Three-Year Limitations Period**

Under the Ninth Circuit’s rule, a plaintiff could always defeat summary judgment by claiming he did not read or remember disclosures, no matter how clear or numerous. That would render § 1113(2) a dead letter for any claim involving complex investments, because plaintiffs will invariably testify to subjective unawareness. Congress intended the three-year period to provide certainty and finality for fiduciaries who fully disclose their actions. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 93-533, at 17 (1973) (statute of limitations “should be sufficient to permit beneficiaries to bring actions, but not so long as to permit stale claims”). The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation undermines that purpose.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
