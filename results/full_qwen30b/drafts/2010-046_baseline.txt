**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s population reduction order, issued in the context of a long-standing, constitutionally mandated remedial effort, is not only consistent with the PLRA but is compelled by the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment. The Ninth Circuit correctly upheld the order as a necessary and narrowly tailored remedy to a pervasive, ongoing constitutional violation. This Court should affirm.

### I. THE PLRA DOES NOT PROHIBIT A COURT FROM ORDERING POPULATION REDUCTION TO REMEDY EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS

The PLRA’s central provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), prohibits federal courts from entering “any order that affects the operation of a prison” unless the order is “narrowly drawn,” “no more extensive than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right,” and “the least intrusive means necessary.” But the PLRA does not create a per se bar on population reduction orders. Rather, it requires courts to apply a balancing test that weighs the severity of the constitutional violation against the burden on prison operations. *See* Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 515 (2011) (“The PLRA does not prohibit a court from ordering a reduction in prison population when such a remedy is necessary to correct a constitutional violation.”).

Here, the district court’s order was precisely such a remedy. The court found that “serious overcrowding” was the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations—specifically, the denial of adequate medical and mental health care. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 559 F.3d 916, 925 (9th Cir. 2009). The court concluded that “the only way to ensure that the constitutional right to adequate medical care is protected” was to reduce the population to 137.5% of design capacity. *Id.* at 926. This was not a sweeping or arbitrary mandate; it was a carefully calibrated, fact-based remedy grounded in expert testimony and years of litigation. The order was “narrowly drawn” and “no more extensive than necessary” to correct the constitutional violation. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that population reduction is a permissible remedy under the PLRA when necessary to correct a constitutional violation. In *Brown v. Plata*, the Court upheld a district court’s order to reduce California’s prison population by approximately 46,000 inmates to remedy the unconstitutional conditions of confinement. *563 U.S. at 515*. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not permit the State to maintain a prison system that is so overcrowded that it cannot provide constitutionally adequate medical care.” *Id.* at 516. The Court further held that “the PLRA does not bar a court from ordering a reduction in prison population when such a remedy is necessary to correct a constitutional violation.” *Id.* at 515. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case was consistent with *Plata* and correctly applied its holding.

### II. THE ORDER WAS NECESSARY TO CORRECT A PROVEN, ONGOING EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATION

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishment,” which includes the “deliberate indifference” to serious medical needs of prisoners. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976). The district court found that California’s prisons were so overcrowded that medical care was “unavailable, delayed, or inadequate” for thousands of inmates. *Plata*, 559 F.3d at 925. The court cited overwhelming evidence: medical staff were overburdened, mental health services were nonexistent, and inmates died due to delayed treatment. *Id.* at 924–25.

The court concluded that “overcrowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violations.” *Id.* at 925. This finding was not speculative—it was supported by expert testimony, including from the State’s own medical consultants. The court’s order to reduce the population to 137.5% of design capacity was a direct response to this finding. It was not a punishment or a policy preference; it was a remedial measure to restore constitutional rights.

The State’s argument that the order violates the PLRA because it “affects the operation of a prison” misreads the statute. The PLRA does not prohibit all orders affecting prison operations; it prohibits only those that are “not narrowly drawn” or “more extensive than necessary.” *18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)*. The order here was neither. It was based on a detailed, evidence-based assessment of the conditions and the only feasible remedy to correct them. The court did not mandate the closure of any prison or the release of any inmate without due process. It simply required the State to reduce its population to a level at which constitutionally adequate care could be provided.

### III. THE ORDER WAS THE LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS NECESSARY TO CORRECT THE VIOLATION

The PLRA requires that any injunctive relief be “the least intrusive means necessary” to correct the violation. *18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)*. The district court considered multiple alternatives—increased funding, staffing, and facility upgrades—but found them insufficient to remedy the crisis. *Plata*, 559 F.3d at 926. The court concluded that “no amount of funding or staffing could overcome the fundamental problem of overcrowding.” *Id.* at 927.

The court’s order was therefore the least intrusive means available. It did not require the State to build new prisons or spend billions on infrastructure. It simply required the State to reduce its population to a level at which existing resources could be effectively deployed. The court even allowed the State to implement the reduction through alternative sentencing, parole, and other lawful means. *Id.* at 928. The order was not a mandate to release dangerous inmates; it was a directive to ensure that the State’s prison system could meet its constitutional obligations.

### IV. THE STATE’S ELEVENTH AMENDMENT AND PLRA CLAIMS ARE BARRED BY CONSENT AND LACHES
