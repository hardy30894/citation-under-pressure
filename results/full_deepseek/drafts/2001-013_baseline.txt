## ARGUMENT

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s limited regulatory authority over uninspected vessels preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act. That provision states that the Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). By its plain terms, preemption requires that another agency actually “exercise” authority over the same working conditions. The Coast Guard does not exercise such authority for uninspected vessels like Rig 52.

### I. The Coast Guard Does Not Exercise Authority Over Occupational Safety and Health for Uninspected Vessels

The Coast Guard’s statutory authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific safety equipment and navigation-related matters. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (authorizing regulations for fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency equipment). The Coast Guard has promulgated regulations for uninspected vessels only on those narrow subjects. See 46 C.F.R. Parts 25–26. These regulations address equipment specification and basic safety of life at sea, not the comprehensive occupational safety and health standards that OSHA enforces—such as the well-control procedures, hazard communication, and confined-space safety at issue here.

This Court has made clear that “pre-emption” under Section 4(b)(1) “requires a showing that the other federal agency has exercised its authority to regulate the particular working conditions involved.” *Ray v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 435 U.S. 151, 171 (1978) (interpreting analogous provision). The Coast Guard has not exercised any authority over the blowout-prevention and well-control procedures that caused the explosion on Rig 52. Indeed, the Coast Guard’s own investigation did not result in any citation or regulation addressing those conditions. When an agency has not “actually exercised its regulatory authority over the working conditions in question,” OSHA retains jurisdiction. *See Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (preemption requires “clear and manifest purpose”). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding improperly expands Section 4(b)(1) beyond its text.

### II. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on *Clary* and *Donovan* Is Misplaced

Both *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), involved vessels that were “in navigation” and whose workers were “seamen” under the Jones Act. In *Clary*, the drilling barge was a “vessel in navigation” and the injured worker was a seaman. 609 F.2d at 1121. In *Donovan*, the court explicitly limited its holding to “seamen on vessels in navigation.” 720 F.2d at 826. Here, Rig 52 was a stationary drilling barge used as a fixed work platform, not a vessel in navigation. The workers were not seamen; they were land-based drilling crew engaged in oil and gas extraction. *See Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. 347, 368 (1995) (seaman status requires connection to a vessel in navigation). The Coast Guard itself has recognized that drilling barges like Rig 52 are “uninspected” and subject to only minimal regulation. The Fifth Circuit’s extension of *Clary* and *Donovan* to such vessels ignores the factual distinctions that drove those decisions.

### III. The OSH Act’s Broad Coverage Supports OSHA’s Jurisdiction

Section 4(a) of the Act provides that the OSH Act applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Congress intended the Act to reach “every workplace in the United States” not covered by another federal agency’s occupational safety and health standards. *See* S. Rep. No. 91-1282, at 10 (1970). The statute’s “remedial nature” requires a “broad interpretation” of its coverage. *See U.S. v. Klinger*, 21 F.3d 567, 573 (5th Cir. 1994). Rig 52 was a workplace in Louisiana’s territorial waters, and no other federal agency had prescribed or enforced occupational safety and health standards for its drilling operations. The Fifth Circuit’s holding creates a regulatory gap that Congress could not have intended.

### IV. The Coast Guard’s Minimal Regulation Does Not Preempt OSHA

Even if the Coast Guard had some authority over uninspected vessels, that authority is not exclusive. The Court has held that “mere existence of a federal regulatory scheme” does not trigger preemption under Section 4(b)(1). *English v. General Electric Co.*, 496 U.S. 72, 87 (1990). The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are “minimal” and do not address the “working conditions” that OSHA regulates. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102. The Fifth Circuit’s reasoning—that any Coast Guard regulation, no matter how limited, preempts all OSHA regulation—would create an absurd result: uninspected vessels would be subject to *no* occupational safety and health oversight, even though Congress intended OSHA to fill such gaps. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 651(b) (purpose to “assure” safe working conditions for every working man and woman).

### Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit’s decision conflicts with the text of Section 4(b)(1), the narrow scope of Coast Guard authority over uninspected vessels, and this Court’s preemption precedents. OSHA has jurisdiction to cite the working conditions on Rig 52. The judgment should be reversed.
