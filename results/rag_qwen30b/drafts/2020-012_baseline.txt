**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a decision by the Railroad Retirement Board denying a request to reopen a prior benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)—is one of first impression before this Court in the context of the Railroad Retirement Act. The Fifth Circuit’s refusal to review the Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application rests on a rigid, outdated interpretation of jurisdictional limits that contradicts both the statutory text and the Supreme Court’s repeated emphasis on the importance of meaningful judicial review in administrative benefit programs.

The Railroad Retirement Act, like the Social Security Act before it, establishes a comprehensive system for the adjudication of benefits claims. Section 355(f) of the Act provides that “any individual, after any final decision of the Board made after a hearing to which he was a party, may obtain a review of such decision by a civil action commenced within sixty days.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The critical inquiry is whether the Board’s decision denying Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 claim qualifies as a “final decision” within the meaning of this provision.

The Fifth Circuit, relying on *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), concluded that such decisions are not reviewable. But *Roberts* misapplied the Supreme Court’s precedent in *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), which held that judicial review under § 205(g) of the Social Security Act is limited to “final decision[s] of the Secretary made after a hearing.” Id. at 100. The Court in *Califano* emphasized that a petition to reopen a prior final decision may be denied without a hearing, and that such denials are not themselves “final decisions” under the statute because they are not made “after a hearing.” Id. at 101–102. The Court further noted that the opportunity to reopen and any hearing on that request are governed by agency regulations, not by the statute itself. Id.

This reasoning, however, does not compel the conclusion that a denial of a request to reopen is categorically unreviewable. The key distinction lies in whether the denial itself is a decision “made after a hearing.” In this case, the Board conducted a formal hearing on Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application. A hearing officer heard testimony, reviewed evidence, and issued a written decision. The Board’s denial of the reopening request was thus made after a hearing, satisfying the statutory requirement of *Califano*.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that a decision made after a hearing is not “final” simply because it concerns a procedural matter. In *Henderson v. Shinseki*, 562 U.S. 428 (2011), the Court held that the Veterans Court’s review of a VA decision denying benefits is “unusually protective” of claimants and that the time limits for seeking review are jurisdictional. Id. at 437. The Court emphasized that the statutory framework for veterans’ benefits is designed to ensure that claimants receive meaningful access to judicial review. Id. at 438. The same principle applies here: the Railroad Retirement Act is a “specialized” benefit program that “provides for the protection of the rights of railroad workers and their dependents.” *Lindahl v. Office of Personnel Management*, 470 U.S. 768, 777 (1985). To deny judicial review of a decision that affects a claimant’s right to benefits—especially one made after a hearing—undermines the integrity of the entire system.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced. That decision was based on a circuit split and a narrow reading of *Califano*. But the Court in *Califano* did not hold that all denials of reopening requests are unreviewable. It held only that such denials are not “final decisions” *if made without a hearing*. The Court in *Califano* explicitly recognized that “an interpretation that would allow a claimant judicial review simply by filing—and being denied—a petition to reopen his claim would frustrate the congressional purpose, plainly evidenced in § 205(g), to impose a 60-day limitation upon judicial review of the Secretary’s final decision on the initial claim for benefits.” 430 U.S. at 102. That concern is not present here. Salinas did not seek review of the original 2006 denial; he sought to reopen it after a new application was filed. The Board’s decision to deny that request was not a re-litigation of the original claim but a determination on a new procedural issue—whether the claim should be reopened based on new and material evidence or administrative error.

The Board’s regulations confirm that a denial of a request to reopen is a final decision. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2(b) provides that a request to reopen “shall be decided by the Board” and that “the decision of the Board shall be final.” This language, read in context with § 355(f), supports the conclusion that such decisions are subject to judicial review.

Finally, the Court has long recognized that “the right to judicial review is not a mere formality.” *Dada v. Mukasey*, 554 U.S. 1, 10 (2008). To hold otherwise would allow the Board to insulate its procedural decisions from scrutiny, even when they affect a claimant’s substantive rights. The Fifth Circuit’s rule of orderliness, which prevents a panel from reconsidering *Roberts*, cannot override the constitutional and statutory imperative to ensure that administrative decisions are subject to meaningful review.

For these reasons, the Board’s decision denying Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 application was a “final decision” made after a hearing and is therefore subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
