**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s Facilities Constitute a Limited Public Forum Open to All Nonreligious Speech, and the Denial of Access to Lamb’s Chapel Based on the Religious Content of Its Speech Violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause.**

The Second Circuit erred in concluding that the School District’s facilities were a “limited public forum” closed to religious speech. When a government entity opens its property for public use, it creates a forum for speech and may not exclude speakers based on the content of their message. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985) (“[I]n a limited forum the government may impose restrictions on speech that are ‘reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum’ and ‘viewpoint neutral.’”). The District’s policy of allowing secular groups to use its facilities while barring religious groups based on the content of their speech is unconstitutional content discrimination.

The Court has repeatedly held that once a government opens a forum for expressive activity, it may not exclude speakers based on viewpoint. *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (“[W]henever the State establishes a forum for speech, it may not discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint.”). In *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), this Court reversed a decision nearly identical to the Second Circuit’s, holding that a school district’s denial of access to a religious group for a film series violated the First Amendment. The Court explained that the school’s facilities were a limited public forum open to all nonreligious groups, and the exclusion of religious speech was impermissible content discrimination. *Id.* at 393-94.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), and *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is misplaced. Those cases involved student groups seeking access to school facilities under policies that allowed other student groups to use the space. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 267; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 236. Lamb’s Chapel, by contrast, is an outside organization seeking access to a forum that the District had opened to the general public. The Second Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Lamb’s Chapel* on the ground that the petitioners were outsiders rather than students is unavailing. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 830 (rejecting the argument that viewpoint discrimination is permissible when the speaker is not a student).

**II. The District’s Policy Violates the First Amendment’s Establishment Clause by Discriminating Against Religious Speech.**

The District’s policy also violates the Establishment Clause by singling out religious speech for disfavored treatment. The Court has made clear that laws and policies that discriminate against religion violate the Establishment Clause. *Larson v. Valente*, 456 U.S. 228, 244-45 (1982) (“[T]he clearest command of the Establishment Clause is that one religious denomination cannot be officially preferred over another.”). In *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university’s refusal to fund a student religious publication while funding secular publications violated the Establishment Clause. 515 U.S. at 841-42. The same principle applies here: the District may not exclude religious speech from a public forum while allowing secular speech.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier*, 484 U.S. 260 (1988), is inapposite. *Hazelwood* involved a school’s authority to regulate school-sponsored speech in a student newspaper, a context where the government acts as an educator rather than a proprietor of a public forum. *Id.* at 270. The District here is not acting as an educator but as a proprietor of a limited public forum open to the general public.

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Neutral or Generally Applicable and Violates the Free Exercise Clause.**

The District’s policy is not neutral or generally applicable, and thus violates the Free Exercise Clause. The Court has held that laws and policies that target religious conduct for disfavored treatment violate the Free Exercise Clause. *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah*, 508 U.S. 520, 533-34 (1993). The District’s policy is not neutral because it explicitly excludes religious speech while allowing secular speech. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831 (holding that a policy is not neutral if it “regulates speech based on its content or viewpoint”).

**IV. The District’s Policy Violates the Equal Protection Clause by Discriminating Based on Religion.**

The District’s policy also violates the Equal Protection Clause by discriminating against religious speech. The Court has held that laws and policies that discriminate based on religion are subject to strict scrutiny. *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye*, 508 U.S. at 542. The District’s policy fails strict scrutiny because it is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest. *See id.* at 546.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Second Circuit should be reversed. The District’s policy violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech, Establishment, and Free Exercise Clauses, as well as the Equal Protection Clause.
