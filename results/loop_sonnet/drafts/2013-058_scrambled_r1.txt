**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code exempts only funds that function as retirement savings for the debtor at the time the petition is filed.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds" that are held in tax-favored accounts. Whether an asset qualifies for that exemption is measured by its character *at the commencement of the case*, not by what it once was in someone else's hands. This Court has long held that the status of exempt property is fixed as of the filing date. In *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310 (1924), the Court explained that the bankrupt's exemptions "are to be determined as of the date of the filing of the petition," because the Code looks to the estate as it actually exists when bankruptcy begins. *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), likewise held that exemption depends on the property's actual legal character under the law governing it when the petition is filed, not its provenance. Applying that rule here, the inherited IRA must be evaluated as what it is in the Clarks' hands on the petition date—a fund that must be drawn down within a fixed term, without regard to Heidi Heffron-Clark's retirement. It is not, and cannot become, a retirement account "of" the debtor merely because it once belonged to her mother.

**II. The functional character of the asset, not its label, controls.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that exemption and tax provisions turn on the substance and function of an asset, not its formal name. In *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465 (1935), the Court declined to accept a transaction's formal characterization and instead examined its economic substance, concluding that the arrangement served no purpose the relevant statute was designed to reach and existed only as a device to reduce tax liability. The word "IRA" in "inherited IRA" is a similar formal label; it does not change the fact that, functionally, the account no longer serves a retirement purpose for its current holder. The same functional approach governs bankruptcy's own treatment of exempt property. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), this Court examined the practical operation of the asset at issue—a tax loss-carryback refund—rather than resting on a mechanical label, in deciding whether it belonged to the estate. The inherited IRA should receive the same functional scrutiny: it is, in economic reality, a time-limited, freely spendable fund that must be distributed on an accelerated schedule wholly divorced from Heidi's own retirement date.

This Court's older insurance-exemption cases confirm that the "retirement" or "protective" character of an exempt fund depends on whether it continues to serve its protective purpose in the debtor's hands, not on its origin. In *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905), the Court tied the exemption of a life-insurance policy to its ongoing function of providing for dependents, and in *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459 (1913), the Court likewise reasoned from the continuing protective purpose the policy served for the insured and his family. Here, by contrast, the inherited IRA serves no analogous ongoing retirement-protective function for the Clarks; the very tax rules governing such accounts compel current, not future, consumption.

**III. Exempting inherited IRAs would contradict, not serve, the purpose of the bankruptcy exemption scheme.**

Exemptions exist to secure a "fresh start" for the debtor who has fallen on hard times—not to shelter windfalls from creditors. As this Court explained in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), one of the Bankruptcy Act's primary purposes is "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor…a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." That purpose is served by protecting funds a debtor has actually set aside, through sacrifice, for his or her own retirement. It is not served by shielding an inheritance that the debtor never contributed to, has no obligation to preserve, and must currently spend down by statute. Treating such funds as exempt "retirement funds" would extend the exemption beyond its purpose to protect ordinary creditors' legitimate expectations, a result this Court has cautioned against when a literal or formalistic reading would produce consequences at odds with the statute's aim. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must look to "the purpose of the act" when a literal reading would yield results the legislature could not have intended).

For these reasons, the inherited IRA held by the Clarks is not "retirement funds" within the meaning of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
