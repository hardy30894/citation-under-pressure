## Argument

The National Park Service properly enforced its hovercraft regulation on the Nation River, which lies within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act does not strip the NPS of authority to regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within conservation system units when the regulation does not apply “solely to public lands.”

### I. The Plain Text of Section 103(c) Does Not Bar NPS’s Regulation

Section 103(c) provides that no regulation “applicable solely to public lands” within conservation system units shall apply to lands conveyed to the State. The hovercraft ban is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands.” It prohibits the operation of hovercraft on all lands and waters within the preserve, regardless of ownership. The plain meaning of the statute controls. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (where the language is clear, “the duty of interpretation does not arise”). Congress used the word “solely” deliberately. If the regulation applies to both federal and non-federal lands, it is not “solely” for public lands. The NPS regulation applies to the entire preserve, including state-owned submerged lands. Therefore, § 103(c) does not limit its enforcement.

### II. The Federal Government Has Long-Standing Authority to Regulate Navigable Waters

The Nation River is a navigable water of the United States. The federal government’s regulatory authority over navigable waters is paramount and predates statehood. *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. 557, 563 (1871) (the power to regulate commerce includes the authority over “all navigable waters of the United States”). This authority extends to activities that affect navigation and the preservation of federal resources. *United States v. Rio Grande Dam & Irrigation Co.*, 174 U.S. 690, 701 (1899) (the federal government may “preserve the navigable capacity of the waters of the United States”). Even though the State of Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River, that ownership is subject to the federal navigational servitude. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 224 (1845) (state title to submerged lands is “subject to the paramount authority of Congress”). The hovercraft regulation is a proper exercise of this authority. It protects the preserve’s resources and ensures safe navigation. NPS’s enforcement on state-owned waters within the preserve is consistent with the federal government’s constitutional power.

### III. NPS’s Interpretation Is Entitled to Deference

If the statute were ambiguous, the NPS’s interpretation of § 103(c) is reasonable and entitled to deference. *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944) (agency interpretations are entitled to respect “to the extent that they have the power to persuade”). The NPS has consistently interpreted § 103(c) as preserving its authority to enforce general regulations on non-federal lands within park boundaries. This interpretation is supported by the statute’s text and purpose. Congress enacted ANILCA to protect the unique resources of Alaska’s parks, not to create a patchwork of regulatory voids. The NPS’s reading is persuasive and should be upheld.

### Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that NPS’s hovercraft regulation applies to state-owned lands and waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Section 103(c) does not bar enforcement of a regulation that is not “applicable solely to public lands.” The federal government’s authority over navigable waters further supports NPS’s action. The judgment below should be affirmed.
