ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of the prior-conviction allegation. The Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offence after acquittal.” Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 168 (1873). It applies once the State has failed to prove an allegation that increases punishment. In re Nielsen, 131 U.S. 176, 188 (1889). The Clause is “fundamental to the American scheme of justice” and binds the States. Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969).

A prior-conviction allegation tried to the court after the substantive verdict is a distinct prosecution. United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896). When the evidence is found insufficient, the defendant stands acquitted of that allegation. Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100, 126 (1904). Retrial would subject him to jeopardy a second time for the same matter. Grafton v. United States, 206 U.S. 333, 349 (1907).

The State cannot evade this bar by labeling the allegation a sentencing factor. The Clause forbids successive prosecutions “for the same offence,” Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299, 304 (1932), and the prior-conviction finding is an element the prosecution must establish. Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957). Once the reviewing court determines the evidence failed to meet the required standard, “the Constitution absolutely requires” that the defendant not again be placed in jeopardy. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969).

The same principle governs when the trial court itself conducts the post-verdict proceeding. The acquittal is no less final because the finder of fact was the judge rather than the jury. Ball, 163 U.S. at 671. Allowing a second proceeding would nullify the protection against repeated attempts to convict. Green, 355 U.S. at 187. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should therefore be reversed.
