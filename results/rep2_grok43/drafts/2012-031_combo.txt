The district court erred in certifying the class. Class actions bind absent members only when the named parties fairly represent interests common to all. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 42 (1940). That requirement is not satisfied when damages cannot be shown through evidence common to the class. Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302 (1853).

Proof of injury is an essential element of every private antitrust claim. Without evidence that damages are measurable on a class-wide basis, individual questions necessarily predominate. Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). Damages must be established with reasonable certainty; speculation or conjecture will not suffice. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931). A methodology that fails to isolate the effect of the challenged conduct on each class member therefore cannot support certification.

The same principle governs the measurement of damages in restraint-of-trade cases. United States v. Trenton Potteries Co., 273 U.S. 392, 401 (1927). Evidence must demonstrate that the alleged violation produced a common impact susceptible to uniform proof. Standard Oil Co. v. United States, 221 U.S. 1, 75 (1911). Where the record contains no admissible evidence that such a methodology exists, the predominance requirement remains unmet. Northern Securities Co. v. United States, 193 U.S. 197, 402 (1904).

Rule 23 demands more than a plausible theory; it requires concrete evidence that common questions will drive the resolution of damages for the entire class. Loewe v. Lawlor, 208 U.S. 274, 301 (1908). The district court’s acceptance of an untested model that cannot reliably quantify damages across thousands of distinct transactions therefore constitutes an abuse of discretion. Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921). Certification in these circumstances denies Comcast the opportunity to litigate individualized defenses and violates the due-process limits on representative actions. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
