# ARGUMENT

## I. GUAM'S SECTION 107 COST-RECOVERY CLAIM REMAINS TIMELY BECAUSE THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT "RESOLVE" GUAM'S CERCLA LIABILITY UNDER SECTION 113(f)(3)(B)

### A. CERCLA's Text Requires Resolution of *CERCLA* Liability, Not Mere Settlement of Any Environmental Claim

Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits a contribution action only where a party "has resolved its liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action" through "an administrative or judicially approved settlement." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). This Court has recognized that CERCLA's contribution provisions were designed to address a specific statutory gap—the absence of any express right to contribution among parties liable under Section 107. *See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161-62 (2004) (explaining that Section 113 was added because "CERCLA contained no provision expressly providing for a right of action for contribution"). Critically, this Court held in *Cooper* that a party may pursue contribution under Section 113(f)(1) only "during or following a civil action under" Section 106 or Section 107. *Id.* at 166. The 2004 Consent Decree arose not from a CERCLA enforcement action, but from a Clean Water Act suit alleging unpermitted discharges into "waters of the United States." 33 U.S.C. § 1362(7). The Decree resolved liability under an entirely different statutory scheme—one that does not require a finding of "hazardous substances" release, the operative trigger for CERCLA liability. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a).

This Court's decision in *Cooper* forecloses the D.C. Circuit's expansive reading. There, the Court emphasized that Section 113(f)(3)(B)'s contribution right is a narrow, textually bounded remedy triggered by specific statutory circumstances—not a catch-all provision activated by any settlement touching environmental contamination. *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167-68. Extending Section 113(f)(3)(B) to encompass Clean Water Act settlements untethered to any CERCLA liability determination stretches the statute beyond its text and purpose.

### B. The Consent Decree's Express Disclaimer of Liability Precludes a Finding That Guam "Resolved" Its Liability

The Consent Decree expressly states it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." This unambiguous disclaimer is fatal to the United States' position. While some circuits have discounted such disclaimers where a decree's substantive obligations otherwise mirror a liability finding, *see Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017), this Court has consistently held that contractual and consent-based instruments must be interpreted according to their plain terms. *See Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co. v. Martin*, 283 U.S. 209, 220-21 (1931) (parties' express intent as manifested in a consent instrument controls its legal effect).

Guam did not merely disclaim liability as boilerplate; the Decree explicitly recited that it was entered "before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law." This is definitionally the opposite of a "resolution" of liability. Congress used the word "resolved" deliberately in Section 113(f)(3)(B), and this Court "must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there." *Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). An agreement that expressly disclaims any adjudication of liability cannot simultaneously be said to have "resolved" that liability.

### C. Guam's Section 107 Claim Serves CERCLA's Remedial Purpose

This Court has recognized that CERCLA's overarching purpose is to "ensure that responsible parties foot the bill" for hazardous waste contamination. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA responds to "the serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution"). The Navy operated the Ordot Dump for nearly fifty years, depositing "munitions and chemicals, allegedly including [DDT] and Agent Orange." Denying Guam the six-year statute of limitations applicable to Section 107 claims—while simultaneously holding that the three-year contribution period had already lapsed *before Guam even knew it was running*—produces the "such inequitable results" that this Court has cautioned against when construing remedial statutes. *See Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967) (remedial statutes should be construed "not technically and restrictively, but flexibly to effectuate its remedial purposes").

The D.C. Circuit's rule creates a perverse incentive structure: territories and states settling Clean Water Act claims will unknowingly extinguish their CERCLA cost-recovery rights, years before they discover the full scope of federal contamination. This cannot be reconciled with CERCLA's design, which channels the Navy's liability for the Ordot Dump onto Guam by administrative fiat while insulating the actual polluter from responsibility.

### D. The Decree's Reference to "Removal or Remedial Action" Does Not Transform a CWA Settlement into a CERCLA Resolution

The United States contends that because the Decree required construction of a "cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," and CERCLA defines "remedial action" to include "collection of leachate and runoff," 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24), the Decree necessarily resolved CERCLA liability. This argument conflates *terminology* with *legal resolution*. That a settlement's remedial measures happen to overlap with actions CERCLA also authorizes does not mean the settlement resolved liability *under CERCLA*. The Clean Water Act independently authorizes injunctive relief requiring remediation of unpermitted discharges, 33 U.S.C. § 1319(b), entirely apart from any CERCLA framework. Guam's obligations arose from Clean Water Act permitting violations, not from any adjudicated or admitted CERCLA liability.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
