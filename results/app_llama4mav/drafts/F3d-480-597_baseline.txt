ARGUMENT

I. The Government Failed to Prove the Requisite Mens Rea for the Machine Gun Conviction

The conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) for unlawful possession of a machine gun must be reversed because the government failed to prove that Belen Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 rifle she possessed was capable of firing automatically, a necessary element of the offense. The Supreme Court has held that to sustain a conviction under a statute that imposes severe penalties, the government must prove that the defendant knew the facts that made her conduct illegal. Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994). In Staples, the Court interpreted 26 U.S.C. § 5861(d), which prohibits the possession of an unregistered machine gun, and held that the government must prove the defendant knew the weapon was a machine gun. Id. at 619. The Court reasoned that the statute's silence on the mens rea requirement did not imply that none was required, especially given the statute's severe penalties and the potentially innocent nature of the conduct proscribed. Id. at 605-06, 618-19.

The same reasoning applies to 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), which prohibits the possession of a machine gun. To convict under this statute, the government must prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun, i.e., that it was capable of firing automatically. See United States v. Rogers, 270 F.3d 1076, 1080-81 (6th Cir. 2001) (applying Staples to § 922(o) and holding that the government must prove the defendant knew the firearm was a machine gun).

The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun. The government's evidence showed only that Nieves-Castaño knew the bag contained an AK-47 rifle and that she was storing it for a friend named Alexis. There was no evidence that she knew the rifle was capable of firing automatically or that she had ever handled or inspected the rifle beyond opening the bag and observing its presence. The government failed to meet its burden of proving the requisite mens rea, and the conviction under § 922(o) must be reversed.

The government may argue that the AK-47's characteristics or Nieves-Castaño's statements to the agent imply knowledge of its automatic capabilities. However, without evidence that she was aware of the rifle's ability to fire automatically, the conviction cannot stand. The absence of such evidence renders the conviction under § 922(o) unsustainable.
