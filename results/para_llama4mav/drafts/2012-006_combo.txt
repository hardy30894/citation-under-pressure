ARGUMENT

The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. Withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period does not shift this burden to the defendant. As established in Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), and subsequent cases, the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period.

In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court held that the government must show that the conspiracy was ongoing and that the defendant was a part of it during the relevant period. The Court emphasized that mere association with conspirators is insufficient to establish membership in the conspiracy during the relevant period (Lutwak v. United States, 344 U.S. 604 (1953)). 

The burden remains on the government to prove the defendant's involvement in the conspiracy during the relevant period. As the Court stated in Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the government must show that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy and that the conspiracy continued into the statute of limitations period. The government cannot rely on proof of the defendant's earlier involvement in the conspiracy; rather, it must demonstrate that the defendant was still a part of the conspiracy during the relevant period (Blumenthal v. United States, 332 U.S. 539 (1947)).

Furthermore, in Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), the Court held that a defendant's liability for the acts of co-conspirators is limited to the period during which the defendant was a member of the conspiracy. If the defendant withdrew from the conspiracy before the statute of limitations period, the government must prove that the defendant's earlier involvement continued into the relevant period.

The Court has also emphasized the importance of proving the defendant's knowledge and intent to be part of the conspiracy during the relevant period (United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205 (1940)). In Direct Sales Co. v. United States, 319 U.S. 703 (1943), the Court held that the government must show that the defendant had knowledge of the conspiracy and intended to further its goals during the relevant period.

In the present case, the defendants withdrew from the conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period. Therefore, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants were still members of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The government must demonstrate that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period and that the defendants were part of it.

The lower court's decision affirming the convictions is inconsistent with these precedents. The court should reverse the lower court's decision and remand the case for further proceedings.

The government's burden to prove the defendant's membership in the conspiracy during the relevant period is supported by the principles established in Marino v. United States, 91 F.2d 691 (9th Cir. 1937), and United States v. Cohen, 145 F.2d 82 (2d Cir. 1944), which emphasize the need for evidence of the defendant's ongoing involvement in the conspiracy.

In conclusion, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. Withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period does not shift this burden to the defendant. The court should reverse the lower court's decision and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with the precedents established in Fiswick, Hyde, Lutwak, Kotteakos, Blumenthal, Pinkerton, Falcone, and Direct Sales Co.
