**ARGUMENT**

The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars an action once the plaintiff possesses “actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” That phrase requires knowledge of the facts constituting the alleged breach, not subjective appreciation that those facts were imprudent or unlawful. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that a plaintiff must also have been “actually aware” that the disclosed acts were imprudent—contradicts the statute’s text and this Court’s precedents.

Section 1113(2) measures the limitations period from “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” When Congress enacted ERISA, it distinguished actual knowledge from constructive knowledge; the latter provision, repealed in 1987, turned on what a plaintiff “could reasonably be expected to have obtained.” Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. The surviving actual-knowledge standard therefore demands only that the plaintiff knew the underlying facts. See Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985) (“section 1113 is triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law”).

This Court has repeatedly applied the same principle. In Meagher v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), the court held that receipt of reduced pension checks supplied actual knowledge of the transaction even though the plaintiff “may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.” Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), likewise started the clock when the plaintiff learned the facts of the challenged retention of funds, without requiring awareness of fiduciary breach. Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991), confirmed that “actual knowledge is measured from the ‘earliest date’ on which [the plaintiff] knew of the breach,” not from later realization of its legal significance.

Intel’s disclosures supplied precisely that knowledge. The 2010-2012 Fund Fact Sheets, Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, Summary Plan Description, and website materials identified the alternative-investment allocations, their percentages, and the resulting performance differential. Sulyma accessed some of these documents. Under Blanton and its progeny, that access constituted actual knowledge of the transactions alleged to be imprudent. The Ninth Circuit’s insistence that Sulyma must also have understood their imprudence adds an element the statute does not contain and that Congress deliberately removed when it eliminated the constructive-knowledge provision.

The court of appeals’ rule would also nullify the limitations period in any case involving complex investment decisions, because a plaintiff could always claim he did not appreciate the prudence of disclosed facts. That result is incompatible with the statute’s purpose of providing repose three years after the plaintiff learns the operative facts. See Phillips, 944 F.2d at 521 (rejecting a construction that “essentially reads the ‘actual knowledge’ standard out of the statute”).

Because the undisputed record shows Sulyma received disclosures revealing the challenged allocations more than three years before suit, § 1113(2) bars his claims. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
