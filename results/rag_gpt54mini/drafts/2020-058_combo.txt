ARGUMENT

Section 1254a(f)(4) does not make TPS an “admission” under § 1255. The INA uses “admission” as a term of art: “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This Court has long treated admission as a physical entry event, not a mere legal status. See Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185, 188–90 (1958); Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193, 195–96 (1958); United States Ex Rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy, 353 U.S. 72, 73–75 (1957); Ceballos v. Shaughnessy, 352 U.S. 599, 600–01 (1957); United States Ex Rel. Stapf v. Corsi, 287 U.S. 129, 134–35 (1932); Cheung Sum Shee v. Nagle, 268 U.S. 336, 340–41 (1925); Chang Chan v. Nagle, 268 U.S. 346, 349–50 (1925); Valdes v. Central Altagracia, Inc., 225 U.S. 58, 60–61 (1912). TPS changes status; it does not rewrite history to create a lawful entry that never occurred.

The statutory text confirms that distinction. Congress said a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). It did not say the recipient “shall be considered admitted.” Where Congress chose to excuse the admission requirement in other parts of § 1255, it said so expressly. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i), (m). The omission is decisive. See I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987). And this Court has repeatedly warned against collapsing distinct statutory concepts. Leng May Ma, 357 U.S. at 188–90; Rogers, 357 U.S. at 195–96; Hintopoulos, 353 U.S. at 74–75.

Petitioners’ reading also conflicts with the structure of the Act. Section 1255(a) makes “inspection[] and admission[] or parole[]” a threshold requirement for adjustment. Section 1255(c)(2) then separately bars aliens who have failed to maintain lawful status. If lawful status itself satisfied admission, the separate admission requirement would do no work. Courts should not read statutes to make provisions “inoperative or superfluous.” Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). The same canon applies with force here. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. at 432; Hibbs, 542 U.S. at 101.

The Court’s parole cases are especially instructive. In Leng May Ma, an alien paroled into the United States argued parole made her “within the United States” for purposes of relief. The Court rejected that contention, holding that parole did not alter her excluded status or accomplish entry. 357 U.S. at 188–90. Likewise in Rogers, the Court held that paroled excluded aliens were not “within the United States” for the relevant relief provision. 357 U.S. at 195–96. Those decisions reflect the same principle that governs here: a government-granted permission to remain temporarily is not the same thing as a lawful admission. See also Stapf, 287 U.S. at 134–35.

The decisions on lawful entry and admission reinforce the point. In Chang Chan and Cheung Sum Shee, the Court applied the immigration statutes as written, distinguishing between those seeking admission and those already lawfully present. Chang Chan, 268 U.S. at 349–50; Cheung Sum Shee, 268 U.S. at 340–41. In Ceballos, the Court recognized that an alien may enter on a temporary visa and remain beyond the period authorized, yet that overstay does not transform the original entry into something else. 352 U.S. at 600–01. And in McGrath v. Kristensen, 340 U.S. 162, 166–67 (1950), the Court treated immigration status as the product of statutory conditions, not judicial invention.

Finally, petitioners’ argument would defeat Congress’s carefully limited TPS scheme. TPS is temporary protection from removal during dangerous conditions abroad. It is not “a program of entry for an alien.” De Leon-Ochoa v. Att’y Gen., 622 F.3d 341, 353–54 (3d Cir. 2010). Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to create an admission would convert a temporary humanitarian program into a backdoor path to permanent residence, contrary to the statute’s design. Where Congress intends to confer a legal fiction, it says so clearly. Here it did not. This Court should hold that TPS does not constitute an “admission” under § 1255.
