ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC'S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON STELWAGON'S ROBINSON-PATMAN ACT CLAIM BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES.

To recover damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act, a plaintiff must prove that it suffered actual antitrust damages as a result of the defendant's violation of the Robinson-Patman Act. See Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562-63 (1931); Bruce's Juices, Inc. v. American Can Co., 330 U.S. 743, 757 (1947). The mere fact that a defendant has engaged in price discrimination is insufficient to establish damages; rather, the plaintiff must show that it suffered a concrete injury as a result of the defendant's actions. See Enterprise Industries, Inc. v. Texas Co., 240 F.2d 457, 459 (2d Cir. 1957).

In this case, Stelwagon failed to present sufficient evidence of actual antitrust damages. The district court's reduction of the damages award from $2,272,000 to $450,383.60 underscores the speculative nature of the jury's initial award. See Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (noting that damages awards must be based on "just and reasonable inferences" rather than speculation). The lack of concrete evidence supporting Stelwagon's damages claim is fatal to its Robinson-Patman Act claim. See American Can Co. v. Russellville Canning Co., 191 F.2d 38, 60 (8th Cir. 1951) (reversing damages award where plaintiff failed to prove actual damages).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff must show a causal link between the defendant's antitrust violation and its alleged damages. See Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp., 392 U.S. 481, 489 (1968); see also Keogh v. Chicago & N.W. Ry. Co., 260 U.S. 156, 165 (1922) (noting that damages must be "the direct result" of the defendant's antitrust violation). Stelwagon failed to establish this causal link, and its damages claim is therefore without merit.

The district court's denial of Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law was in error, as Stelwagon's failure to prove actual antitrust damages precludes recovery under the Clayton Act. See Wolfe v. National Lead Co., 225 F.2d 427, 432 (9th Cir. 1955) (reversing judgment where plaintiff failed to prove damages); see also Package Closure Corp. v. Sealright Co., 141 F.2d 972, 979 (2d Cir. 1944) (noting that a plaintiff must prove damages with "reasonable certainty").

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN SUBMITTING STELWAGON'S BREACH OF CONTRACT CLAIM TO THE JURY.

The district court erred in submitting Stelwagon's breach of contract claim to the jury, as the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support a finding of breach. See Federal Intermediate Credit Bank v. Duffy, 59 F.2d 691, 694 (2d Cir. 1932) (noting that a plaintiff must prove breach with "reasonable certainty"). The oral, exclusive distributorship agreement between Tarmac and Stelwagon was ambiguous, and the parties' course of performance under the agreement was subject to multiple interpretations. See Williston on Contracts § 623 (3d ed. 1961) (citing cases).

In light of the foregoing, we respectfully submit that the district court's judgment should be vacated insofar as it awards Stelwagon treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act, and that Tarmac's motion for judgment as a matter of law should be granted.
