# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF THE TAX CODE REQUIRES A SEPARATE ASSESSMENT AGAINST EACH TAXPAYER BEFORE THE TEN-YEAR COLLECTION PERIOD RUNS AGAINST THAT TAXPAYER

The statutory scheme governing federal tax collection draws a clear line: assessment is a taxpayer-specific act with taxpayer-specific consequences. Section 6203 provides that an "assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer." 26 U.S.C. § 6203 (emphasis added). The implementing regulation confirms that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Section 6502(a), the provision creating the ten-year extension at issue here, likewise speaks only of "the taxpayer" against whom an assessment "has been made." 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a).

The Code defines "person" to include both a "partnership" and an "individual" as distinct categories. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1). Each carries its own taxpayer identification number and constitutes a separate "taxpayer" under § 7701(a)(14). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, "[t]he Partnership is a 'taxpayer' within the meaning of the statute, but so is each individual Debtor a separate 'taxpayer.'" *United States v. Galletti*, 314 F.3d 336 (Amended Op.) (9th Cir. 2003). Because Congress used the singular, taxpayer-specific term "the taxpayer" throughout §§ 6203, 6501, and 6502, an assessment against one taxpayer—the partnership—cannot extend the limitations period applicable to a different taxpayer—the individual partner.

This Court has long recognized that statutes of limitations on tax collection are to be strictly construed in favor of the taxpayer, precisely because the assessment mechanism is the trigger for significant governmental collection powers, including the summary lien and levy authority that ordinarily requires no judicial proceeding. *See Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935) (describing assessment as giving the government "the right to distrain" without judicial process). Given the extraordinary nature of these collection tools, the statutory prerequisite—assessment against "the taxpayer"—should not be read loosely to permit the government to bootstrap an assessment against one entity into collection authority over a legally distinct person.

## II. THE COURT'S OWN NINTH CIRCUIT PRECEDENT CONFIRMS THAT PARTNER LIABILITY REQUIRES INDEPENDENT ASSESSMENT OR JUDGMENT

The government's reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. That case held only that a partner who had *already paid* a tax assessed against his partnership could not obtain a refund merely because the assessment failed to name him individually. The district court in *Young* expressly distinguished the situation presented here, explaining that although naming the individual was "unnecessary" to establish substantive liability under state law, it remained necessary "to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action." *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). In other words, *Young* confirms that absent an individual assessment, the IRS must resort to a judicial collection action against the partner—exactly what it failed to do here within the limitations period.

*United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), reinforces this reading. There, the district court held that a tax lien could not attach to an individual partner's property because he "never was assessed and no lien exists without such an assessment." *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). While this Court affirmed on other procedural grounds, nothing in the opinion disturbed the fundamental principle that assessment is a per-taxpayer requirement for the government's summary collection remedies to attach.

## III. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT'S DECISION IN WRIGHT DOES NOT SUPPORT THE GOVERNMENT'S POSITION

The government's principal case, *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is inapposite. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed *both* the partnership and the individual partners, extending the six-year statute of limitations then applicable to both. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question presented was whether the limitations period as to the partners was tolled during the partnership's bankruptcy under 26 U.S.C. § 6503(h). *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. The Seventh Circuit never confronted—much less resolved—whether an assessment against the partnership *alone* suffices to bind individual partners who were never separately assessed. As the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized, "the Seventh Circuit therefore had no opportunity to address the question before us." *Galletti*, 314 F.3d 336.

## IV. CALIFORNIA LAW REQUIRES A JUDGMENT AGAINST THE INDIVIDUAL PARTNER, WHICH THE GOVERNMENT NEVER OBTAINED

Even accepting that partners are jointly and severally liable for partnership debts as a matter of substantive state law, California law does not permit a creditor to collect that debt from a partner's individual assets without first obtaining a judgment against the partner specifically. California Corporations Code § 16307(c) states plainly: "A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). Commentators confirm that although a partner need not be named in the action against the partnership, "the partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner's personal assets." 9 B.E. Witkin, *Summary of California Law* § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001).

The government obtained no such judgment against Respondents. It cannot now use the bankruptcy claims process to circumvent both the federal assessment requirement and the state-law judgment requirement, particularly where the three-year limitations period for either an individual assessment or a collection suit against Respondents has long since expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

## CONCLUSION

Because the assessment against the Partnership extended the ten-year collection period only as to the Partnership, and because the IRS never separately assessed Respondents or obtained judgments against them within the applicable three-year window, its claims are "unenforceable against the debtor[s] and property of the debtor[s]" under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
