The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB's standard, as articulated in Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989), requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll. However, this standard is too stringent and renders employer polls "useless and illegitimate" (Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984)).

The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have rejected the NLRB's standard, holding that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support" (A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984)). This standard is more reasonable and allows employers to test employee sentiment without committing an unfair labor practice.

The NLRB's justification for its standard is that polling can be "potentially disruptive and unsettling" (Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62). However, this concern is mitigated when the employer follows the procedural safeguards set out in Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), as Allentown Mack did in this case. The NLRB's standard is not entitled to deference because it is not a reasonable interpretation of the NLRA (NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990)).

Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The record shows that a significant number of employees expressed dissatisfaction with the union, and the company's poll, conducted with procedural safeguards, revealed that a majority of employees did not support the union. The NLRB's conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt is arbitrary and capricious, as it ignores the objective evidence of employee sentiment (Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d at 868).

The Court should hold that the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is not rational and consistent with the NLRA and that the NLRB's factual determination is not supported by substantial evidence. The Court should reverse the NLRB's order and remand for further proceedings.
