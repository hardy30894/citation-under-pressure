## ARGUMENT

### I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not specifically address employer polling of employee union support. The Board’s standard—requiring the same objective evidence to poll as to withdraw recognition—effectively nullifies the polling option Congress intended to provide employers with sincere doubts. See *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). Three circuits have correctly rejected this standard because it renders polling a “useless” tool. *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982); *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981).

The Board’s rationale—that polling is “potentially disruptive”—is insufficient to justify a standard that makes polling all but impossible. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). An employer who already possesses evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition has no need to poll. The Board’s claim that polling then provides “certainty” is illusory; the employer could simply withdraw recognition and force the union to file a charge. By requiring the same evidence, the Board eliminates the very purpose of polling: to allow an employer to test union support without risking an unfair labor practice when it lacks definitive proof. *See Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299.

The Board’s argument that it prefers Board-supervised RM elections over polls is inconsistent with its own standard. As the dissent below recognized, the Board’s standard makes polling *more* difficult than an RM election, which requires only a 30% showing. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 159(c)(1)(B). This anomaly is irrational. The proper standard, adopted by the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits, permits polling when the employer has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition.” *A.W. Thompson*, 651 F.2d at 1145. This standard balances the employer’s need to avoid bargaining with a minority union (a violation of § 8(a)(2)) against the stability of collective bargaining. *Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867.

The Board’s standard also undermines the Act’s goal of promoting industrial peace. An employer with a sincere doubt should be encouraged to poll rather than unilaterally withdraw recognition, which is more disruptive. *See Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1298. This Court should adopt the standard of the three circuits and hold that the Board’s contrary rule is arbitrary and capricious. *See Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978) (courts defer to Board only when its choice is rational).

### II. Even Under the Board’s Standard, the Factual Determination That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.

The Board found that only seven of thirty-two employees made statements repudiating the union. This finding arbitrarily excluded probative evidence and ignored the totality of circumstances. The record contains overwhelming objective evidence of loss of union support.

First, the Board improperly discounted employee statements made during job interviews. Mike Ridgick stated that “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” The Board deemed this equivocal because the interviewer mentioned the company would be non-union. But the Board’s own precedent, *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), applies only when the employer’s statement is coercive, not merely informative. No coercion occurred here. Dennis Marsh said he was “not getting his money’s worth” from union dues. The Board treated this as mere dissatisfaction, but an employee’s complaint about dues is a clear indication of opposition to continued representation. *See Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995) (dissatisfaction with union performance can support reasonable doubt).

Second, the Board rejected second-hand reports from employees Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr. Bloch stated that the entire night shift of five or six employees did not want the union. Mohr, the union steward, told management that the union would lose a vote and that he did not think employees wanted a union. The Board requires independent verification, but no such requirement exists in the Act. Employers must rely on what they hear from credible sources. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements is arbitrary, especially given that Mohr was a union steward whose opinion carries weight. *See Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 867 (reversing Board where it ignored similar evidence).

Third, the Board used an arbitrary cutoff date of January 25, 1991, to exclude employees who were hired or left after that date. While the date of the company’s letter refusing recognition is relevant, the Board’s rigid application ignores that the poll itself was conducted on February 1, and the employer’s doubt could reasonably be informed by evolving circumstances. The presumption of majority status is “rebuttable,” *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987), and the Board’s mechanistic approach defeats that rebuttal.

Finally, the poll itself—conducted by secret ballot under a priest’s supervision—resulted in a 19-13 vote against the union. While the Board challenges the poll’s legality, the result is powerful evidence of the union’s actual lack of support. This Court has recognized that polls can be “useful and legitimate” when conducted fairly. *Mingtree*, 736 F.2d at 1299. The Board’s decision to ignore the poll’s outcome while faulting the company for not having enough evidence is a classic Catch-22. *See Thomas Industries*, 687 F.2d at 868 (reversing Board where “overwhelming unrefuted evidence” showed loss of support).

The substantial evidence standard requires the Board’s findings to be supported by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate.” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 787 (1990). Here, the Board’s findings are unsupported. The record compels a conclusion that Allentown Mack had a good-faith reasonable doubt, and the Board’s order must be reversed.
