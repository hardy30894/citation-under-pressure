The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series on family values and child rearing constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech.

The District's facilities are a limited public forum because they have been opened for use by the general public for certain purposes. As the Supreme Court has held, when the government creates a limited public forum, it is bound by the same First Amendment standards as a traditional public forum, at least with respect to entities of a character similar to those the government admits to the forum (Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985)). The government's power to regulate expression in a limited public forum is thus limited, and content-based exclusions are subject to strict scrutiny (Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260, 267 (1988)).

The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is content-based because it is based on the religious content of the speech (Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 247 (1990)). The District's policy of excluding religious groups from using its facilities is not viewpoint-neutral, as it discriminates against religious speech (Lamb's Chapel Br. at 20; see also Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981)). The District's actions are not justified by a compelling state interest, and the exclusion is not narrowly drawn to serve that interest (Perry, 460 U.S. at 45).

The Court of Appeals' reliance on the fact that the District has not previously allowed religious groups to use its facilities is misplaced. As the Supreme Court has held, the government's decision to open a limited public forum to certain groups does not give it carte blanche to exclude other groups based on their viewpoint (Mergens, 496 U.S. at 247). The District's policy of excluding religious groups is not justified by a legitimate concern for avoiding an Establishment Clause violation, as the Supreme Court has held that equal access to school facilities for religious groups does not necessarily violate the Establishment Clause (Widmar, 454 U.S. at 273; Mergens, 496 U.S. at 247).

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals' distinction between "religious purposes" and "secular purposes" is not a valid basis for excluding Lamb's Chapel's film series. As the Supreme Court has held, the government may not discriminate against speech based on its content, even if that content is religious (Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 828 (1995)). The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is thus unconstitutional.

The lower court's decision is also inconsistent with the principles established in Heffron v. Int'l Soc'y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc., 452 U.S. 640 (1981), and United States v. Kokinda, 497 U.S. 720 (1990), which emphasize the importance of viewpoint neutrality in government regulation of speech. The District's policy of excluding religious groups from using its facilities is not viewpoint-neutral and therefore violates the First Amendment.

In addition, the Court of Appeals' decision is at odds with the Supreme Court's holding in Capitol Square Review and Advisory Bd. v. Pinette, 515 U.S. 753 (1995), which established that the government's exclusion of religious speech from a public forum must be justified by a compelling state interest. The District has not demonstrated such an interest.

The District's actions also run afoul of the principles established in Police Dept. of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92 (1972), which held that government regulation of speech must be content-neutral. The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is content-based and therefore unconstitutional.

In conclusion, the District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The Court should reverse the lower court's decision and hold that the District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is unconstitutional.
