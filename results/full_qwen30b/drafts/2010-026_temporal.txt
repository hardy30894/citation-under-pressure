**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding misapplies the statutory language of Exemption 2 and contravenes binding precedent from this Court and the lower courts, all of which require that information be “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of an agency to qualify for exemption. The ESQD arc maps are not internal personnel rules or practices; they are technical safety data developed for the purpose of public safety and risk mitigation, not for internal administrative or operational guidance. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—derived from post-1970 decisions not binding on this Court—has no basis in the law as it existed at the time of FOIA’s enactment and cannot be reconciled with the statutory text or the Supreme Court’s interpretation of Exemption 2.

**I. The ESQD Arc Maps Are Not “Internal Personnel Rules and Practices” Under Exemption 2**

Exemption 2 applies only to information “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” has been interpreted consistently by this Court and the lower courts to refer to matters governing the internal operations of an agency, such as employee conduct, work schedules, and administrative procedures. In *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 7 (1953), the Court emphasized that FOIA was not intended to permit agencies to withhold information simply because it was sensitive or potentially embarrassing. Rather, the exemption must be narrowly construed, and only information that is truly internal and administrative may be withheld.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. 656, 662 (1958), where the Court held that the mere fact that information is not publicly available does not justify withholding it under FOIA. The Court stated: “The purpose of the Act is to open agency files to the public, subject only to the narrow exceptions provided by Congress.” The Court further noted that “the burden is on the agency to show that the information falls within one of the exemptions.” Id. at 663. This burden is not satisfied by asserting that information is “internal” in a broad or speculative sense.

The ESQD arc maps at issue here were not created for internal administrative purposes. They were developed pursuant to the Navy’s *Ammunition and Explosives Ashore Safety Regulations for Handling, Storing, and Production Renovation and Shipping* (OP-5 manual), which explicitly states that its purpose is “to prescribe standardized safety regulations for the production, renovation, care, handling, storage, preparation for shipment, and disposal of these items.” The maps are used to determine safe distances between explosive storage facilities, to prevent sympathetic detonations, and to ensure that the Navy complies with its own safety standards. These are not internal personnel rules; they are technical safety specifications designed to protect both military personnel and the surrounding civilian population.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that these maps are “predominantly internal” is unsupported by any authority from before 1970. Indeed, the very concept of a “predominantly internal” standard was not recognized in any judicial opinion prior to 1970. In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 709 (1974), the Court observed that “the exemptions are to be narrowly construed,” but that decision came after the time period at issue. Prior to 1970, courts uniformly required that information be “solely” related to internal personnel rules and practices to qualify for Exemption 2. In *United States v. C.I.A.*, 338 F.2d 847, 850 (D.C. Cir. 1964), the court held that “the exemption applies only to matters that are truly internal and administrative in nature.” The court rejected the idea that any information could be withheld simply because it was not intended for public release. Similarly, in *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 277 F.2d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1960), the court stated that “the exemption is not a general catch-all for any information the agency deems sensitive.”

The ESQD arc maps do not fall within this narrow category. They are not rules governing employee conduct, work hours, or internal procedures. They are technical data used to assess and mitigate public safety risks. The Navy’s own affidavit confirms that the maps are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not internal personnel practice; it is public safety engineering. To classify such information as “internal” under Exemption 2 is to distort the meaning of the statute beyond recognition.

**II. The Risk of Circumvention Is Not Established**

Even if the maps were internal, the Ninth Circuit failed to establish that their disclosure would present a “risk of circumvention of agency regulation.” The Supreme Court has made clear that Exemption 2 applies only when disclosure would “risk circumvention of agency regulation.” *Rose v. United States Department of the Air Force*, 425 U.S. 352, 369 (1976). But this risk must be real and specific, not speculative or hypothetical.

In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 709 (1974), the Court emphasized that “the exemptions are to be narrowly construed,” and that “the burden is on the agency to show that the information falls within one of the exemptions.” The Navy has not met this burden. Commander Whitbred’s affidavit states only that the maps “may” be used to plan an attack or disrupt operations. This is a speculative assertion, not a demonstrated risk. There is no evidence in the record that any individual has ever attempted to use such data to plan an attack, nor is there any evidence that the maps have ever been misused.
