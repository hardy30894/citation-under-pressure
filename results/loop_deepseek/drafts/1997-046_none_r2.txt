**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

Clemency is, and always has been, an act of executive grace—not a right subject to judicial enforcement. The pardon power is plenary and absolute, vested solely in the executive branch. *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120 (1925) (“The power of the President to pardon is plenary.”). A pardon is “an act of grace” that “cannot be demanded as a matter of right.” *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). Its exercise is committed to the unreviewable discretion of the executive. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866) (“The power of pardon is a power of grace, not of right.”).

Because clemency is a discretionary act, an inmate possesses no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process itself. The Due Process Clause does not create property or liberty interests in mere hopes or unilateral expectations. *Flemming v. Nestor*, 363 U.S. 603, 611 (1960) (no “accrued property rights” in statutory benefits that are matters of legislative grace). Where the state retains unfettered discretion to grant or withhold a benefit, no protected interest arises. *Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895-96 (1961) (due process protection depends on the nature of the interest; a “gratuity” does not trigger constitutional safeguards). The Ohio Governor’s discretion over clemency is virtually unfettered. Accordingly, Woodard cannot claim a legitimate claim of entitlement to clemency.

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that clemency is an “integral part” of the adjudicatory system cannot be reconciled with this Court’s precedents. Clemency is not a stage of trial or appeal; it is a separate executive function that occurs after all judicial proceedings are final. This Court has long recognized that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts.” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. That principle rests on history and tradition, not the logic of *Evitts v. Lucey*, which addressed a first appeal as of right—a mandatory judicial proceeding. Here, Ohio has created no entitlement. The option of an interview does not transform a discretionary act into a protected interest.

**II. The Voluntary Interview Option Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination protects only against *compelled* incrimination—not against voluntary choices. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (the privilege applies only to “compulsory” incrimination). The core of the privilege is the right to remain silent in the face of governmental coercion. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598 (1896) (the privilege does not protect “voluntary” disclosures). No such coercion exists here.

Ohio’s clemency procedure imposes no compulsion whatsoever. The inmate may freely decide whether to request an interview. If he requests one, he remains free to speak or remain silent. The regulation does not require him to answer any question, nor does it condition clemency on a waiver of his Fifth Amendment rights. The mere possibility that a voluntary statement could later be used does not render the procedure unconstitutional. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 438 (1956) (the privilege is not “a general right to silence” insulating all voluntary statements from later use). A state may offer a benefit without creating an unconstitutional condition so long as the recipient retains the choice not to accept it. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 32 (1953) (no compulsion where the taxpayer could choose not to engage in the taxed activity).

Woodard’s claim of a “Hobson’s choice” is without foundation. He is not forced to incriminate himself or forfeit an existing right. The choice he faces is simply whether to accept an opportunity to speak—a choice that the Fifth Amendment does not forbid. Any claim of future self-incrimination is speculative and premature. *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616, 634 (1886) (courts do not decide “abstract questions” or hypothetical scenarios). The district court correctly declined to adjudicate a risk that may never arise.

**Conclusion**

Woodard has no protected interest in clemency, and Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
