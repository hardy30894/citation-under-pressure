**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit Erred in Imposing a Heightened “Reasoned Choice” Standard for Competency to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that a criminal defendant be competent to stand trial. *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). The standard for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Id.* This Court has never held that a higher standard applies when a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel. To the contrary, the competency inquiry is the same: whether the defendant understands the proceedings and can assist in his defense. *See Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 398 (1993) (holding that “the competency standard for pleading guilty or waiving the right to counsel is the same as the competency standard for standing trial”).

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a defendant must be capable of making a “reasoned choice” among alternatives—a standard that demands a higher level of mental functioning than *Dusky*—has no basis in this Court’s precedents. *See Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 267 (9th Cir. 1992). This Court has consistently held that the *Dusky* standard governs all competency determinations, including those involving waivers of constitutional rights. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 398. The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard improperly conflates the competency inquiry with the separate question of whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary. *See id.* at 401 (distinguishing between competency and the validity of a waiver).

**II. The State Court Properly Found Moran Competent Under the Correct Standard.**

The record demonstrates that the state court applied the correct *Dusky* standard when it found Moran competent. Two court-appointed psychiatrists examined Moran and concluded that he understood the charges, the potential penalties, and could assist counsel if he chose. *See* O’Gorman Psychiatric Evaluation at 2; Jurasky Psychiatric Evaluation. The state court relied on these evaluations in denying post-conviction relief. *Moran v. State*, 734 P.2d 712, 714 (Nev. 1987). The Ninth Circuit erred in concluding that the state court applied the wrong standard. The state court’s single reference to the psychiatrists’ findings does not demonstrate that it applied a lower standard; rather, it reflects the court’s proper reliance on expert evidence of competency. *See Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 402 (noting that a court may rely on psychiatric evaluations in making competency determinations).

**III. The Ninth Circuit Improperly Substituted Its Judgment for That of the State Court.**

The Ninth Circuit conducted a de novo review of the competency determination, concluding that the record could not support a finding of competency under the “reasoned choice” standard. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 268. This approach violates the deference owed to state court factual findings under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *See Marshall v. Lonberger*, 459 U.S. 422, 432 (1983) (holding that state court factual findings are entitled to a presumption of correctness). The state court’s competency determination is a factual finding that must be upheld unless it is “not fairly supported by the record.” *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 181 (1975). Here, the psychiatric evaluations and Moran’s own statements at the plea hearing—where he demonstrated an understanding of his rights and the consequences of his pleas—amply support the state court’s finding. *See Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 403 (noting that Moran’s responses at the plea hearing were “entirely coherent” and demonstrated an understanding of the proceedings).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
