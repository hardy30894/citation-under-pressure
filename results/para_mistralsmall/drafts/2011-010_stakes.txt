**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that § 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act ("LHWCA") coverage to injuries occurring on land, so long as there is a "substantial nexus" between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf. *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP*, 6933 F.3d 6936, 6961 (9th Cir. 2010). This interpretation contravenes the plain text of § 1333(b), which requires that the injury occur "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The statute’s use of the phrase "conducted on" unambiguously limits coverage to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. *See Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (directing courts to give effect to the plain meaning of unambiguous statutory text).

The Ninth Circuit’s "substantial nexus" test finds no support in the statutory text or in this Court’s precedents. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that OCSLA’s coverage provisions are geographically limited. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court explained that OCSLA’s scope is "determined principally by locale," not by the status of the injured individual. *Id.* at 219. The Court further noted that § 1333(b) "has no bearing" on a case not involving a suit by an injured employee under that provision. *Id.* at 219 n.2. This dictum, while not dispositive, underscores that § 1333(b) is not a broad remedial statute but a narrow extension of LHWCA coverage tied to operations on the outer Continental Shelf. *See also Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 424 (1985) (holding that OCSLA coverage is limited to injuries occurring on the shelf or its adjacent waters).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is unpersuasive. The *Mills* court relied on dicta from *Herb’s Welding* that was not essential to the holding and ignored the plain text of § 1333(b). *See id.* at 361-62. This Court has cautioned against affording dispositive weight to passing remarks in Supreme Court opinions. *See Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (explaining that general expressions in judicial opinions must be read in context). The *Mills* court’s "situs-of-injury" test is the correct interpretation of § 1333(b), as it ensures that OCSLA coverage is cabined to the geographic scope Congress intended.

**II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT LA CONCHITA IS A MARITIME SITUS UNDER THE LHWCA.**

The Ninth Circuit also erred in reversing the Benefits Review Board’s ("BRB") denial of LHWCA benefits on the ground that La Conchita, Pacific Operations’ onshore oil-processing facility, qualifies as a maritime situs under § 903(a) of the LHWCA. *Valladolid*, 6933 F.3d at 6959. The BRB correctly applied the "functional relationship" test established in *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978), and concluded that La Conchita is not an "adjoining area" within the meaning of § 903(a). *See id.* at 6959-60. This Court should affirm the BRB’s decision.

Under *Herron*, courts must consider several factors to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs, including: "(1) the particular suitability of the site for the maritime uses referred to in the statute; (2) whether adjoining properties are devoted primarily to uses in maritime commerce; (3) the proximity of the site to the waterway; and (4) whether the site is as close to the waterway as is feasible given all of the circumstances in the case." *Id.* at 141. The BRB correctly found that La Conchita fails to satisfy these factors. The facility is separated from the ocean by a highway and railroad tracks, has no direct access to any pier or dock, and is three miles from the nearest maritime facility. *See id.* at 6959. Moreover, La Conchita’s primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry extracted from offshore platforms—a non-maritime activity—not to load or unload vessels. *See Herb’s Welding*, 470 U.S. at 422-24 (holding that a facility’s suitability for non-maritime uses weighs against a finding that it is a maritime situs).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), is misplaced. *Pfeiffer* involved warehousemen who handled cargo within a port, a quintessential maritime situs. *Id.* at 71, 83. The Supreme Court held that these workers were engaged in maritime employment because their activities were "intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation." *Id.* at 82-83. Unlike the warehousemen in *Pfeiffer*, Valladolid’s work at La Conchita did not involve the direct transfer of cargo between ship and land transportation. The scrap metal he was handling had already been unloaded at the Casitas Pass Pier by third-party longshoremen and transported to La Conchita by third-party truck drivers. *See Valladolod*, 6933 F.3d at 6960. Thus, La Conchita is not an "adjoining area" within the meaning of § 903(a).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that (1) § 1333(b) of the OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself, and (2) La Conchita is not a maritime situs under the LHWCA.
