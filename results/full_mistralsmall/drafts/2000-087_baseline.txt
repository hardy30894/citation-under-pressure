**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). The Supreme Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 690-91. The Court held that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." *Id.* at 701.

The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that § 1231(a)(6) authorized Zadvydas’s continued detention as long as the INS made "good faith efforts to effectuate deportation." *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). This standard improperly required Zadvydas to show that deportation was impossible, rather than merely unlikely. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 704. The Supreme Court rejected this approach, holding that the habeas court must ask whether detention exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *Id.* at 704.

II. THE CONSTITUTION FORBIDS PROLONGED DETENTION WITHOUT A REASONABLE LIKELIHOOD OF REMOVAL

The Due Process Clause prohibits indefinite detention of aliens ordered removed but who cannot be repatriated. *Id.* at 692. The Court recognized that § 1231(a)(6) applies broadly to aliens ordered removed for various reasons, not just dangerous individuals. *Id.* at 699. The Court declined to defer to the political branches on this issue, noting that the statute does not narrowly target dangerous individuals. *Id.* at 699-700.

The Court established a presumptively reasonable period of six months for post-removal detention. *Id.* at 705. After this period, the alien must show that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, and the government must rebut that showing with evidence of a significant likelihood of removal. *Id.* As the period of detention grows, the "reasonably foreseeable future" shrinks accordingly. *Id.*

III. ZADVYDAS’S DETENTION EXCEEDS THE STATUTORY AND CONSTITUTIONAL LIMITS

Zadvydas has been detained for years beyond the removal period with no realistic prospect of removal. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that Zadvydas had been in post-removal custody for over three years when it reconsidered the case. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, No. 99-30949 (5th Cir. 2001) (per curiam). The Supreme Court noted that Lithuania had not accepted Zadvydas and that his Lithuanian citizenship reapplication was still pending. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 683 n.1. Under these circumstances, continued detention is not authorized by § 1231(a)(6) and violates due process.

The government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien held at the border, not a resident alien with a final removal order. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700. The Court distinguished *Mezei*, holding that the due process concerns are heightened for resident aliens. *Id.*

IV. THE REMEDY IS RELEASE WITH CONDITIONS

The Court held that if detention is no longer reasonable, the alien must be released, subject to conditions. *Id.* at 705. The Fifth Circuit properly conditioned Zadvydas’s release on reporting requirements, employment, and residence restrictions. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, No. 99-30949 (5th Cir. 2001) (per curiam). The government may seek Zadvydas’s return to custody only if removal becomes reasonably foreseeable. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The statute does not authorize indefinite detention, and Zadvydas’s prolonged detention violates due process. The judgment below should be affirmed.
