**ARGUMENT**

I. The post-removal-period statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period.

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) provides that an alien ordered removed “shall be detained” during the 90-day removal period and, if not removed, “shall, pending removal, be subject to supervision under regulations prescribed by the Attorney General.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(1)-(3) (1994). The statute further authorizes continued detention beyond the removal period for certain categories of aliens, including those deemed a “risk to the community” or “unlikely to comply with the order of removal.” *Id.* § 1231(a)(6). However, this provision does not permit indefinite detention. As this Court has recognized, “[a] statute must be construed, if fairly possible, so as to avoid not only the conclusion that it is unconstitutional but also grave doubts upon that score.” *United States v. Thirty-seven Photographs,* 402 U.S. 363, 374 (1970) (citing *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.,* 213 U.S. 366, 407 (1909)). Construing § 1231(a)(6) to permit indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns under the Due Process Clause. *See* *Wong Wing v. United States,* 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896) (holding that “[a]ll persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection named in the [Fifth] Amendment”).

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause prohibits the government from depriving any person of liberty without due process of law. *See* U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long held that “[l]iberty is the rule, and detention prior to trial is the carefully limited exception.” *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy,* 347 U.S. 260, 268 (1954) (Jackson, J., dissenting). Detention that is not reasonably necessary to effectuate removal violates due process. *See* *United States ex rel. Potash v. District Director,* 169 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1948) (Hand, J.) (“[D]etention for a purpose other than to hold the alien for removal is arbitrary and unlawful.”).

The post-removal-period statute must be read to incorporate a “reasonable time” limitation. *See* *United States v. Jin Fuey Moy,* 241 U.S. 394, 401 (1916) (“[W]here a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter.”). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutes authorizing detention must be construed narrowly to avoid constitutional infirmity. *See* *Carlson v. Landon,* 342 U.S. 524, 545 (1952) (“[D]etention in custody pending a hearing or determination of the cause is not imprisonment in the technical sense, but is only so far imprisonment as is necessary to hold the person for the purpose of the proceeding.”) (quoting *United States ex rel. McGowan v. Schultz,* 127 F.2d 986, 988 (2d Cir. 1942) (L. Hand, J.)).

Here, the government has made no showing that petitioner’s continued detention is reasonably necessary to effectuate removal. Petitioner has been detained for years beyond the removal period, and no country will accept him. The government’s inability to effectuate removal does not justify indefinite detention. *See* *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy,* 338 U.S. 537, 549 (1950) (“[O]nce an alien lawfully enters the country, the legal incidents of his status are determinable by the Constitution.”).

II. The Attorney General’s authority to detain beyond the removal period is limited to the time reasonably necessary to secure removal.

This Court has long recognized that the government’s power to detain is not unlimited. In *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy,* 347 U.S. 260 (1954), this Court held that the Attorney General’s discretion to detain an alien pending deportation is subject to judicial review to ensure that detention does not exceed constitutional bounds. *Id.* at 266-68. Similarly, in *United States ex rel. Potash v. District Director,* 169 F.2d 747 (2d Cir. 1948), Judge Learned Hand observed that “[d]etention for a purpose other than to hold the alien for removal is arbitrary and unlawful.” *Id.* at 750.

The government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei,* 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. In *Mezei,* the Court upheld the detention of an excludable alien at Ellis Island pending determination of his admissibility. *Id.* at 215. The Court emphasized that Mezei was an excludable alien who had never effected entry into the United States. *Id.* at 212. By contrast, petitioner is a lawful permanent resident who has been ordered removed but cannot be deported due to the government’s failure to secure a destination country. *See* *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy,* 338 U.S. 537, 549 (1950) (distinguishing between excludable and deportable aliens).

The government’s argument that indefinite detention is permissible so long as it continues “good faith efforts” to effectuate removal is untenable. *See* *United States v. Witkovich,* 355 U.S. 1027 (1958) (per curiam) (holding that the government’s “good faith” in enforcing immigration laws does not immunize its actions from judicial review). The Due Process Clause requires that detention be limited to the time reasonably necessary to effectuate removal. *See* *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy,* 347 U.S. at 268 (Jackson, J., dissenting) (“Liberty is the rule, and detention prior to trial is the carefully limited exception.”).

III. Petitioner’s continued detention violates the Due Process Clause.

This Court has long held that the Due Process Clause prohibits arbitrary and indefinite detention. In *Wong Wing v. United States,* 163 U.S. 228 (1896), the Court held that “[a]ll persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection named in the [Fifth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 237. The Court further recognized that “[j]udicial officers must be called upon to determine whether the prisoner is detained in accordance with the law.” *Id.* at 238.

Petitioner’s detention is arbitrary and indefinite. He has been held for years beyond the removal period, and no country will accept him. The government’s inability to effectuate removal does not justify his continued detention. *See* *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy,* 338 U.S. at 549 (“[O]nce an alien lawfully enters the country, the legal incidents of his status are determinable by the Constitution.”).

The government’s reliance on *Carlson v. Landon,* 342 U.S. 524 (1952), is misplaced. In *Carlson,* the Court upheld the detention of deportable aliens pending determination of their deportability. *Id.* at 540. However, the Court emphasized that detention must be “reasonably necessary” to effectuate removal. *Id.* at 545. Here, petitioner’s detention is not reasonably necessary to effectuate removal, as no country will accept him.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that the post-removal-period statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. Petitioner’s continued detention violates the Due Process Clause.
