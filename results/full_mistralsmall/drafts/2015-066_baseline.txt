**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Therefore the Use of Bryant’s Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment.**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies only to criminal prosecutions in federal and state courts, not to tribal courts. *See* **Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.,** 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (holding that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes). Tribal courts are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, which does not provide a right to appointed counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. *See* **United States v. Ant,** 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir. 1989) (recognizing that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts). Because Bryant’s tribal court convictions were obtained in proceedings that did not violate the Sixth Amendment, they are valid predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in *Ant* and its progeny is erroneous. The court improperly imported Sixth Amendment protections into tribal court proceedings, disregarding the principle that tribal sovereignty is not subject to the Bill of Rights. *See* **Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez,** 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (holding that federal courts must respect tribal self-governance and cannot intrude upon tribal sovereignty). The court’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States,* 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced because *Nichols* addressed sentencing enhancements, not the use of prior convictions as elements of an offense. *See id.* at 746–47 (limiting its holding to sentencing contexts).

II. **The Use of Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Does Not Violate Due Process.**

Due process does not require the exclusion of valid tribal court convictions, even if they were obtained without appointed counsel. Tribal courts operate under their own legal frameworks, and their judgments are entitled to comity. *See* **Wilson v. Marchington,** 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997) (extending comity to tribal courts and recognizing that federal courts must respect tribal jurisprudence). The Supreme Court has repeatedly deferred to tribal sovereignty in matters of criminal jurisdiction. *See* **United States v. Lara,** 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (upholding Congress’s plenary power to legislate regarding tribal courts).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates an unwarranted distinction between tribal and federal/state convictions, imposing a Sixth Amendment requirement that does not exist. This approach undermines tribal sovereignty and disregards Congress’s intent in enacting § 117(a) to address the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country. *See* **United States v. Wheeler,** 435 U.S. 313, 323–24 (1978) (affirming tribal sovereignty in punishing tribal offenders).

III. **The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with Precedent and Creates a Circuit Split.**

The Ninth Circuit’s holding conflicts with decisions from the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have permitted the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses. *See* **United States v. Shavanaux,** 647 F.3d 993, 997–98 (10th Cir. 2011); **United States v. Cavanaugh,** 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir. 2011). This circuit split underscores the need for this Court’s intervention to resolve the issue.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Ant* is misguided. *Ant* predates *Nichols* and does not account for the Supreme Court’s later clarification that sentencing enhancements do not implicate the Sixth Amendment in the same way as predicate offenses. *See* **Nichols,** 511 U.S. at 746–47. The court’s attempt to distinguish *Nichols* on procedural grounds is unpersuasive, as *Nichols* did not address the use of tribal court convictions in federal prosecutions.

**CONCLUSION**

The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, and Bryant’s tribal court convictions are valid predicate offenses under § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with precedent and undermines tribal sovereignty. This Court should reverse the judgment below.
