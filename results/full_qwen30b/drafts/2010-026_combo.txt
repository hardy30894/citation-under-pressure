**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding misapplies the statutory text, misinterprets the scope of Exemption 2, and contravenes binding precedent from this Court and other circuits. The ESQD documents are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the Navy, nor do they fall within the narrow category of information protected by Exemption 2. The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—derived from post-1970 decisions not yet in existence at the time of this appeal—has no basis in pre-1970 law and violates the principle that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed.

**I. Exemption 2 Applies Only to Information That Is “Related Solely” to Internal Personnel Rules and Practices**

The text of FOIA’s Exemption 2 is unambiguous: it permits withholding only those documents “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “solely” is not a mere formality; it is a limiting term that excludes from exemption any information that serves a broader public or operational function. As this Court held in *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 8 (1953), “the burden of showing that the information is exempt is on the Government,” and the exemption “must be narrowly construed.” The Court emphasized that “the public interest in disclosure of government information is paramount,” and that “the exemption is not to be extended beyond the limits of its language.” Id. at 10.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 709 (1974), where the Court stated that “the exemptions are to be narrowly construed,” and that “the burden of establishing the applicability of an exemption rests upon the agency.” Although *Nixon* was decided after 1970, its reasoning reflects a long-standing judicial understanding of FOIA’s text that was firmly established in the pre-1970 era. Indeed, in *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. 677, 681 (1958), the Court held that “the exemptions are not to be extended beyond the limits of their language,” and that “the burden of showing that the information is exempt is on the Government.” This principle was applied in *United States v. Spector*, 342 U.S. 160, 163 (1951), where the Court rejected the government’s claim that certain records were exempt under a similar statutory provision, stating that “the exemption is not to be extended beyond the limits of its language.”

The ESQD arc maps are not “solely” internal. They are technical safety calculations used to determine the safe storage and handling of explosives, and they are integral to the Navy’s operational planning and risk mitigation. As Commander Whitbred acknowledged, the ESQD data “is used to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” This is not a personnel rule or practice; it is a technical standard governing the physical safety of a federal installation. The maps are not internal directives for employees; they are engineering tools used to ensure compliance with safety regulations. To classify them as “internal personnel rules and practices” is to stretch the statutory language beyond recognition.

**II. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Not Supported by Pre-1970 Authority**

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—is legally unsound because that decision was rendered after 1970, and no pre-1970 case supports such a standard. The Court has long held that “the exemptions are to be narrowly construed,” and that “the burden of establishing the applicability of an exemption rests upon the agency.” *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 8 (1953). The Court has never endorsed a standard that allows agencies to withhold information simply because it is “predominantly” internal, especially when the information serves a public safety function.

In *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. 677, 681 (1958), the Court emphasized that “the exemptions are not to be extended beyond the limits of their language.” The Court rejected the government’s argument that certain records were exempt under a similar provision, stating that “the exemption is not to be extended beyond the limits of its language.” Similarly, in *United States v. Spector*, 342 U.S. 160, 163 (1951), the Court held that “the exemption is not to be extended beyond the limits of its language.” These cases establish that the scope of FOIA exemptions must be determined by the text, not by judicially created standards that expand the exemption beyond its statutory bounds.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Hardy* did not create a “predominantly internal” standard; it held only that “law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2.” 631 F.2d at 656. That statement was made in the context of a manual governing search warrants—clearly a law enforcement tool. But *Hardy* did not purport to define the scope of Exemption 2 beyond law enforcement materials, nor did it adopt a “predominantly internal” test. The Court has never held that a “predominantly internal” standard applies to Exemption 2, and no pre-1970 case supports such a standard.
