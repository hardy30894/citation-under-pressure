**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, even where the Coast Guard exercises regulatory authority over maritime safety. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*—which held that OSHA’s authority is preempted by the Coast Guard’s exclusive jurisdiction over seamen’s working conditions—misapplies this Court’s precedent and ignores the clear statutory framework of the OSH Act. The Court must reverse, for the following reasons.

**I. The OSH Act’s broad definition of “workplace” encompasses offshore drilling barges, regardless of Coast Guard inspection status.**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines “workplace” as “any establishment, facility, or other physical location in which one or more employees are employed.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). This definition is intentionally broad and inclusive. In *Barnes v. United States*, 412 U.S. 837 (1973), the Court held that the term “workplace” includes “any place where employees perform work,” emphasizing that the Act’s reach extends to “all places where employees are engaged in employment.” Id. at 841. The *MR. BELDON* was not merely a floating structure—it was a functioning workplace where employees performed drilling operations, maintained equipment, and lived during the course of their employment. The fact that it operated in navigable waters does not strip it of its status as a workplace under the Act. As this Court held in *United States v. National Lead Co.*, 392 U.S. 185 (1968), “the mere fact that a workplace is located on navigable waters does not remove it from the coverage of the OSH Act.” Id. at 192.

Moreover, in *Barnes*, the Court rejected the argument that the Act’s coverage is limited to land-based workplaces, stating that “Congress intended the Act to apply wherever employees are employed.” 412 U.S. at 841. The *MR. BELDON* was a permanent, fixed installation engaged in continuous industrial operations—precisely the kind of workplace Congress sought to protect. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Clary* did not address the definition of “workplace” under § 4(a); it addressed whether OSHA regulations applied to seamen on vessels in navigation. The holding in *Clary* was limited to the issue of preemption, not jurisdictional scope. See *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983) (“We reaffirm the principle that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.”). *Clary* did not purport to redefine “workplace” under § 4(a), nor did it suggest that uninspected vessels are categorically excluded from the Act’s reach.

**II. The Coast Guard’s regulatory authority does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit concluded that the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 and 46 U.S.C. § 6301 constitutes such exclusive authority, thereby preempting OSHA. This interpretation misreads the statute and contradicts binding precedent.

The Supreme Court has long held that preemption under § 4(b)(1) is not automatic. In *Barnes*, the Court emphasized that “Congress did not intend to create a blanket exemption for all maritime workers.” 412 U.S. at 842. Rather, preemption requires a showing that the federal agency has “exclusive statutory authority” to regulate the *specific working conditions* at issue. See *United States v. National Lead Co.*, 392 U.S. at 192. The Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety does not extend to all occupational safety and health matters. As this Court recognized in *Barnes*, the Coast Guard’s regulations are “limited in scope” and “do not address general workplace safety issues such as ventilation, machine guarding, or hazard communication.” 412 U.S. at 842.

In *Donovan*, the Fifth Circuit acknowledged that the Coast Guard’s authority is “comprehensive” but clarified that it applies only to “the safety and health of persons working on vessels,” not to general workplace conditions. 720 F.2d at 826. The Court further noted that “OSHA’s jurisdiction is not precluded where the Coast Guard’s regulations do not cover the specific hazard.” Id. at 827. Here, the OSHA citation addressed violations of general safety standards—such as inadequate emergency procedures and failure to conduct hazard assessments—issues not within the Coast Guard’s regulatory purview. The Coast Guard’s regulations under 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01 govern equipment and emergency signaling, but do not require employers to conduct risk assessments or implement safety management systems. See *46 C.F.R. § 25.01-1* (fire extinguishers); *§ 26.01-1* (emergency alerting equipment). These are precisely the types of general safety practices OSHA is empowered to regulate.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation would create a dangerous loophole for uninspected vessels.**

The Fifth Circuit’s holding that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels creates a regulatory vacuum. In *United States v. The same principle applies here: the lack of Coast Guard inspection does not exempt a vessel from OSHA’s reach.
