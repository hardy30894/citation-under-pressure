**ARGUMENT**

**I. Woodard Possesses a Protected Liberty Interest in Ohio’s Clemency Process.**

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the state’s procedures for determining the finality of a criminal sentence are critical stages of the adjudicative process. In *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956), the Court held that the right to appeal is a critical stage of the criminal process. This principle was extended to the right to counsel in *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985). The dissent in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 471 (1981) (Stevens, J., dissenting), correctly identified that the decision to grant or withhold commutation is a "regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process employed by the State to determine the magnitude of its deprivation of the prisoner's liberty." Because clemency is a critical component of the state’s decision to deprive a prisoner of life, Woodard possesses a protected liberty interest in the clemency process itself. The State cannot treat this final stage of the adjudicative system as a mere formality devoid of constitutional protections.

**II. Ohio’s Clemency Interview Procedure Imposes an Unconstitutional Condition on the Exercise of Woodard’s Fifth Amendment Rights.**

Ohio’s regulation creates a "Hobson's choice" for death row inmates, forcing them to choose between participating in a clemency interview and asserting their Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination. While the regulation is facially voluntary, the risk of self-incrimination is substantial. In *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976), the Supreme Court held that prison disciplinary proceedings must not be used to coerce self-incrimination. Similarly, the State cannot condition the availability of a fair clemency review on the waiver of constitutional rights. The dissent correctly notes that the regulation "does not empower the parole board... to compel a prisoner... to answer a single question." However, the mere *option* to speak creates a risk that statements will be used against the inmate in future criminal proceedings. The State cannot require a prisoner to waive his Fifth Amendment rights to obtain a meaningful opportunity to be heard. This creates an unconstitutional condition on the exercise of a liberty interest.
