**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because the Undisputed Facts Show That Four of the Seven Calls Did Not “Use” a Prerecorded Voice as Required by the TCPA.**

The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) prohibits “mak[ing] any call” that “uses any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A). The plain meaning of “use” requires actual employment of a prerecorded voice during the call. *See Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (statutory words “are to be taken in their ordinary and familiar signification”). The district court’s contrary interpretation—that the mere capacity to play a prerecorded message suffices—violates this fundamental canon. *See United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 396 (1867) (“The language of the statute is to be interpreted according to its common and ordinary meaning.”).

The record shows that DISH’s Cisco Dialer plays a prerecorded message only when it detects a “positive voice”—either a human answer or a voicemail greeting. The undisputed call logs reveal that Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5 resulted in an “Answering Machine” or a “Data Error—No Value.” For these calls, no positive voice was detected, and therefore no prerecorded message was played. The district court nonetheless held that DISH “admitted to using a prerecorded voice in calls from the 8047 number regardless of whether Ybarra triggered it.” That holding misreads the record. DISH’s admission was that the Cisco Dialer *can* use a prerecorded voice, not that it *did* use one on every call. A party cannot be held liable for an act that did not occur. *See The Schooner Exchange v. M’Faddon*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 116, 136 (1812) (liability requires proof of the act alleged).

Summary judgment is proper only when “the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact.” *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 624 (1944) (quoting Fed. R. Civ. P. 56). Here, a genuine dispute exists as to whether the Cisco Dialer actually transmitted a prerecorded voice during Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5. The call logs indicate “Answering Machine” or “Data Error,” but they do not confirm that any message played. Because the district court resolved this factual dispute against DISH without any evidence, it erred. *See Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co.*, 398 U.S. 144, 153 (1970) (summary judgment improper when non-movant’s evidence raises a material fact issue). Even if the district court believed the “Answering Machine” result necessarily meant a voicemail greeting triggered the message, that inference is unsupported by the record. A voicemail greeting is a “positive voice,” but the logs do not state that a greeting occurred. The ambiguity precludes summary judgment.

Moreover, the TCPA’s prohibition on “us[ing]” a prerecorded voice must be strictly construed because the statute imposes civil liability. *See United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (penal statutes are “construed strictly”). The district court’s broad interpretation—that any call placed using a system capable of prerecorded messages is a violation—would sweep in calls that never actually transmit a message. Congress did not intend such an absurd result. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts avoid constructions that lead to “unreasonable results”).

**Conclusion**

For Calls 2, 3, 4, and 5, there is no evidence that DISH “used” a prerecorded voice. The district court therefore erred in granting partial summary judgment as to those calls. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to enter judgment for DISH on those four calls.
