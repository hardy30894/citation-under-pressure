**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist Regarding Whether Ms. Erdman Accumulated the Requisite 1,250 Hours of Service Under the FMLA.**

The Family and Medical Leave Act of 1993, 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A), defines an “eligible employee” as one who has been employed for at least 12 months and has worked at least 1,250 hours of service during the previous 12-month period. The District Court concluded that Ms. Erdman failed to meet this hours threshold as a matter of law. That conclusion was erroneous because the court ignored disputed evidence that Ms. Erdman regularly worked uncompensated overtime hours that should have been counted toward her eligibility.

**A. The Record Contains Credible Evidence That Ms. Erdman Worked Extra Hours That Nationwide Authorized, Creating a Jury Question.**

Ms. Erdman’s supervisor, Patty Sarno, consistently authorized payment for extra hours worked outside the office or permitted Ms. Erdman to use those hours as “comp” time. See Record at 124–25. Ms. Erdman testified that she regularly worked additional hours with Sarno’s knowledge and approval. See Record at 130. Although Nationwide later claimed that Ms. Erdman’s overtime was unapproved, Ms. Erdman’s e-mail to Stella Getgen in September 2002—seeking clarification about “comp” time—went unanswered. Getgen’s silence, combined with her later acknowledgment that Ms. Erdman’s vacation time calculation was correct and her failure to object to the use of “comp” time until February 10, 2003, creates a genuine dispute about whether Ms. Erdman’s extra hours were authorized. See Record at 140–42.

Under well-settled principles, summary judgment is improper where the nonmoving party presents evidence that, if believed, would establish a material fact. *See Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment should be used sparingly in cases where motive and intent are at issue). Here, a reasonable jury could find that Ms. Erdman’s extra hours were authorized and thus counted toward the 1,250-hour requirement.

**B. The District Court Improperly Resolved Credibility Disputes and Weighed Evidence, Which Is the Province of the Jury.**

In granting summary judgment, the District Court credited Nationwide’s version of events—that Ms. Erdman’s overtime was unapproved—over Ms. Erdman’s contrary testimony. This was error. The Supreme Court has long held that at the summary judgment stage, the court must view all evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and draw all reasonable inferences in that party’s favor. *See Adickes v. S.H. Kress & Co.*, 398 U.S. 144, 157 (1970). Credibility determinations, the weighing of evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge. *See Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944).

Ms. Erdman’s testimony that she regularly worked additional hours with Sarno’s approval—supported by Sarno’s own testimony that she authorized payment for those hours—creates a genuine issue of material fact. The District Court erred by resolving this factual dispute in favor of Nationwide. *See Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473.

**C. The Dispute Over Comp Time Directly Affects the Calculation of Ms. Erdman’s Hours and Therefore Her Eligibility.**

The FMLA defines “hours of service” by reference to the Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, and the applicable regulations. Under the FLSA, “hours worked” includes all time during which an employee is suffered or permitted to work, even if not requested. *See Tennessee Coal, Iron & R. Co. v. Muscoda Local 123*, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944). If Ms. Erdman’s extra hours were suffered or permitted by Nationwide—as the evidence suggests—they must be counted toward her FMLA eligibility. The District Court’s failure to consider this legal standard, and its reliance on Nationwide’s unilateral claims that the hours were unauthorized, was reversible error.

**II. The District Court Also Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on the FMLA Retaliation Claim Because Ms. Erdman Presented Sufficient Evidence of Pretext.**

To establish a prima facie case of FMLA retaliation, Ms. Erdman must show that she engaged in protected activity and that an adverse employment action followed. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Ms. Erdman requested FMLA leave on April 21, 2003, and was fired on May 9, 2003—just 18 days later. The temporal proximity alone creates an inference of retaliation. *See* Record at 150.

Nationwide’s proffered reasons—insubordination and profanity—are disputed. Ms. Erdman testified that the profane remark was a non-work-related personal call, and that Nationwide’s policy prohibits monitoring such calls. See Record at 145. A reasonable jury could find that Nationwide’s reasons were pretextual, particularly given the suspicious timing and the shifting explanations for Ms. Erdman’s termination. *See Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the District Court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for trial.
