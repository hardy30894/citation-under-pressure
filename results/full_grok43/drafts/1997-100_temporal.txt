The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States by the Fourteenth Amendment, Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), “protects against a second prosecution for the same offence after acquittal.” North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This guarantee applies once jeopardy has attached to a distinct charge or allegation that the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957).

A prior-conviction allegation under California’s three-strikes law constitutes such a charge. The State must establish the existence and character of the earlier conviction as an element that increases the prescribed range of punishment. When the trier of fact determines that the evidence is insufficient, that determination is an acquittal on the allegation. United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896). Retrial is therefore forbidden. Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100, 126 (1904).

The same result follows from the prohibition against multiple punishments for the same offense. Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163, 168 (1873). Once the court has imposed sentence without the enhancement, any subsequent proceeding to prove the allegation would expose the defendant to an additional punishment for the same prior conduct. Grafton v. United States, 206 U.S. 333, 349 (1907). The Clause therefore bars the State from attempting to relitigate the allegation.

The procedural safeguards that attended the original proceeding confirm that jeopardy attached. The defendant was entitled to notice, to confront witnesses, and to require the prosecution to meet its burden of proof. These incidents render the hearing the functional equivalent of a trial on a distinct charge. Trono v. United States, 199 U.S. 521, 529 (1905). An acquittal obtained under those conditions may not be set aside for a second attempt at proof.

Accordingly, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of the prior-conviction allegation.
