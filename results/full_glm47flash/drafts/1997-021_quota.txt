**Argument**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for employer polling is an arbitrary judicial intrusion into labor policy that renders the polling process "marginally useful" to employers. The Board admits that polling is "potentially disruptive and unsettling" (*Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989)), yet it requires the same high evidentiary threshold for polling as for a Board-conducted "RM" election, which the Board identifies as the "preferred method" for testing employee support (*Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061). This creates an "anomaly" where an employer can more easily conduct an unsupervised poll than a Board-supervised election (*Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1062). The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits correctly rejected this approach, holding that polling requires only "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support" (*Thomas Indus., Inc.*, 687 F.2d 863, 868 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc.*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984)). The D.C. Circuit’s deference to the Board’s expertise cannot override the Board’s own admission that its standard is inconsistent with its own preference for elections (*Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063).

**II. The Board’s Factual Finding That Allentown Lacked Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s finding that Allentown lacked "objective reasonable doubt" about the union’s majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The record contains clear statements from seven employees repudiating the union, and the successor unit’s presumption of support is weaker than that of a fully retained unit (*Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp.*, 482 U.S. 27, 36-41 (1987)). The Board improperly discounted statements made during job interviews where the employer had indicated a non-union stance, finding such statements "unlikely to be sincere" (*Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978)). Furthermore, the Board erred in treating employee dissatisfaction with union services as opposition to representation, a distinction recognized in *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51, 51 (1988), and *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc.*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). The ALJ’s finding that the statements were "at best equivocal" is not supported by the record, which shows clear anti-union sentiment. The Board’s conclusion that the employer committed an unfair labor practice in the face of this evidence is arbitrary and capricious.
