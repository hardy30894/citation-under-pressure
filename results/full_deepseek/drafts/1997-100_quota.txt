**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial of a Sentencing Enhancement When the Evidence Is Insufficient**

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that “[n]o person shall . . . be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). When a defendant has been acquitted—or when a court finds the evidence insufficient to support a finding—the Clause bars a second attempt to prove the same allegation. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 18 (1978). A determination that the prosecution failed to prove a prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt is the functional equivalent of an acquittal, and thus retrial is prohibited. *See Smalis v. Pennsylvania*, 476 U.S. 140, 144 (1986).

**II. The Reasoning of *Bullington* and *Rumsey* Applies to Noncapital Sentencing Proceedings**

In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 446 (1981), this Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a death sentence after a jury imposed life imprisonment because the capital sentencing proceeding “had the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence.” The Court later extended this principle in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209 (1984), holding that a finding favorable to the defendant in a capital sentencing hearing is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes.

The California Supreme Court erred in limiting *Bullington* and *Rumsey* to capital cases. Nothing in those opinions suggests that their reasoning depends on the severity of the punishment. Rather, the critical inquiry is whether the sentencing proceeding “resemble[s] a trial on guilt or innocence.” *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. Here, California’s three-strikes proceeding required the prosecution to prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt, allowed the defendant to confront and cross-examine witnesses, and applied the rules of evidence. *See People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993); *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal.4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996). These “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” compel the application of double jeopardy protections.

**III. This Court Has Never Held That *Bullington* Is Limited to Capital Cases**

The California Supreme Court relied on dicta in *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), to suggest that *Bullington* might not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. But *Caspari* expressly declined to address the merits of whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing enhancements. *Id.* at 389. The Court simply noted that, for purposes of a *Teague* retroactivity analysis, a reasonable jurist would not have considered the application of the Clause to noncapital proceedings “dictated by our precedents.” *Id.* at 393. That observation does not foreclose such application; it merely acknowledges that the issue remained open. Similarly, *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28, 30 (1985) (per curiam), declined to decide the question.

**IV. The “Hallmarks of Trial” Analysis Applies to California’s Prior Conviction Proceeding**

California’s proceeding to determine the truth of a prior conviction allegation is fundamentally trial-like. The prosecution must prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. *Tenner*, 6 Cal.4th at 566. The defendant has the right to a jury trial, *People v. Vera*, 15 Cal.4th 269, 274 (1997), the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, *Reed*, 13 Cal.4th at 228 n.6, and the privilege against self-incrimination, *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal.3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974). Full evidentiary rules apply. *Reed*, 13 Cal.4th at 224. These protections mirror those of a criminal trial. Thus, when the Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to support the prior conviction allegation, that determination constituted an acquittal barring retrial. *See Burks*, 437 U.S. at 18.

**V. The States’ Historical Practice of Informal Sentencing Does Not Control**

The California Supreme Court relied on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 245 (1949), for the proposition that states need not provide a trial-like sentencing proceeding. But *Williams* addressed due process requirements, not double jeopardy. And *Williams* itself acknowledged that states may choose to provide greater procedural protections. *Id.* at 250. Having elected to provide a trial-like proceeding with the hallmarks of a criminal trial, California cannot now deny the double jeopardy protections that accompany such a proceeding. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438 n.12.

**Conclusion**

The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of a prior conviction allegation after the evidence has been found insufficient. This Court should reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
