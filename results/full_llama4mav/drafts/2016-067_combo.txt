The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The question presented is whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander), a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after the debts had fallen into default, is a "debt collector" subject to the FDCPA. The answer is no.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The key phrase here is "owed or due another." As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the plain meaning of a statute is the best guide to its interpretation. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). In this case, the plain meaning of "owed or due another" indicates that a debt collector is one who collects debts on behalf of another, not for itself.

The petitioners argue that Santander is a debt collector because it acquired the debts after they had defaulted. However, this argument ignores the fact that Santander collected the debts for its own account, not on behalf of the original creditor, CitiFinancial Auto. As the Court has held, a person who collects debts for itself is not a debt collector. See United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P. R. Co., 282 U.S. 311, 326 (1931) (holding that a statute should be interpreted in light of its plain meaning and the context in which it is used).

The FDCPA's definition of "creditor" also supports this interpretation. A creditor is defined as "any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). When Santander purchased the debts from CitiFinancial Auto, it became a creditor, as it was now the entity to whom the debt was owed. See Louisville & N. R. Co. v. United States, 282 U.S. 740, 757 (1931) (holding that a transferee of a debt becomes the creditor).

The petitioners rely on a flawed interpretation of the FDCPA, which would render the distinction between "debt collector" and "creditor" meaningless. As the Court has held, a statute should be interpreted to give effect to all its provisions. See United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955). Under the petitioners' interpretation, any entity that collects debts for itself and also collects debts for others would be subject to the FDCPA for all its collection activities. This is not a reasonable interpretation of the statute.

The Court has long recognized that a statute's language and structure are important guides to its interpretation. See Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 439 (1935). In this case, the FDCPA's language and structure indicate that a debt collector is one who collects debts on behalf of another, not for itself. See also Ozawa v. United States, 260 U.S. 178, 194 (1922) (holding that a statute's language should be given its ordinary meaning).

Furthermore, the legislative history of the FDCPA supports this interpretation. The Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA indicates that the term "debt collector" is intended to cover "all third persons who regularly collect debts for others." S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977). This language suggests that Congress intended to regulate entities that collect debts on behalf of others, not those that collect debts for themselves.

In addition, the Court has held that a statute should be interpreted in a way that avoids absurd or unreasonable results. See Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 460 (1892). The petitioners' interpretation would lead to an absurd result, as it would subject creditors who collect debts for themselves to the FDCPA, even if they are not collecting debts on behalf of others.

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it collected the debts for its own account, not on behalf of another. The plain meaning of the statute, the definition of "creditor," and the legislative history all support this interpretation. Therefore, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
