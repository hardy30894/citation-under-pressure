**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATE’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A CONSTITUTIONALLY PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST UNDER THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT

The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process is not a mere act of grace, but a structured, state-created mechanism that, when invoked, triggers a constitutionally protected liberty interest. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a liberty interest arises not only from the mere existence of a state procedure, but from the *substance* and *predictability* of that procedure. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of the Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 10 (1979), the Court held that “a prisoner has a liberty interest in parole release” when the state has established a system that permits release under general, objective standards. The Court emphasized that “the liberty interest is derived solely from the existence of a system that permits criminal offenders to serve their sentences on probation or parole.” Id. at 25 (Marshall, J., dissenting in part). This principle applies with equal force to clemency proceedings.

Here, Ohio has established a formal, mandatory clemency review process under Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, which requires the Ohio Adult Parole Authority to schedule a clemency review 45 days before any scheduled execution date. The process is not discretionary in timing; it is triggered automatically by the state’s own procedural clock. Moreover, the state has created a structured framework for review, including the option of a personal interview, the consideration of written submissions, and the eventual recommendation to the Governor. This is not a mere “grace” process. As the Court observed in *Board of Pardons v. Allen*, 482 U.S. 369, 375 (1987), “the Due Process Clause applies to parole-release determinations” when the state has created a system that “permits criminal offenders to serve their sentences on probation or parole.” The same logic applies to clemency: when the state creates a predictable, structured process for reviewing death sentences, it creates a liberty interest that cannot be stripped without due process.

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), is misplaced. In *Dumschat*, the Court held that a prisoner had no protected liberty interest in commutation because the state had not established a system that created a reasonable expectation of release. Id. at 465. But here, Ohio has done precisely that: it has created a mandatory, time-bound process that is not contingent on the prisoner’s initiative. The state’s own regulation, the “Death Penalty Clemency Procedure,” mandates a review and provides a clear path for participation. This is not the “unpredictable” or “arbitrary” process the Court found in *Dumschat*. Rather, it is a system that, by its very structure, gives inmates a reasonable expectation of a meaningful review. As the Court stated in *Greenholtz*, “the liberty interest is not created by the mere existence of a parole system, but by the fact that the system is structured so as to create a reasonable expectation of release.” 442 U.S. at 17. Ohio’s clemency process satisfies that standard.

II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS THE STATE FROM FORCING A PRISONER TO WAIVE HIS RIGHT TO REMAIN SILENT

The State’s clemency process, by offering a personal interview as part of the review, creates an unconstitutional condition: a prisoner must either waive his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination or forfeit the opportunity to be heard. This is not a mere risk—it is a coercive structure that compels a waiver.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a person may not be compelled to incriminate himself.” *United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. 181, 188 (1977). In *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976), the Court emphasized that “it would be premature” for a federal court to require a state to provide relief against a risk of self-incrimination that is “purely academic.” But here, the risk is not academic. The Ohio regulation explicitly states that “any statement given by the prisoner at the interview shall be considered at the clemency hearing.” This is not a hypothetical. It is a direct invitation to self-incrimination, and the state has no legitimate interest in compelling such a waiver.

The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a state may condition a benefit on the surrender of a constitutional right. In *Jago v. Van Curen*, 454 U.S. 14, 20 (1981), the Court held that “the State may not deprive a prisoner of a liberty interest without due process,” and that “the mere fact that a benefit is discretionary does not mean that the State may deny it without providing the procedural safeguards required by the Due Process Clause.” Here, the state has created a benefit—clemency review—and tied it to a condition that violates the Fifth Amendment. The prisoner is not merely “invited” to speak; he is placed in a position where silence may be interpreted as a lack of remorse or cooperation, and participation may result in self-incrimination. This is precisely the kind of “Hobson’s choice” the Court has condemned.

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that the risk of self-incrimination is “theoretical” is untenable. The state’s own regulation makes clear that statements made during the interview will be used in the clemency process. If the state can use such statements, and if the prisoner is not protected from their use in future criminal proceedings, then the risk is real. The Court has held that “the Constitution protects the individual against the use of his own words against him in a criminal prosecution.” *United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. at 188. Ohio’s process, by failing to provide a mechanism to shield such statements from future use, violates that principle.
