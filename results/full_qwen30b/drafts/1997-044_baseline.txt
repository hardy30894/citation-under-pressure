**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT COHEN’S DEBT WAS NONDISCHARGEABLE UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A) BECAUSE HE DID NOT MAKE A FALSE REPRESENTATION, AND THEREFORE THE ELEMENTS OF FRAUD UNDER THE BANKRUPTCY CODE WERE NOT MET.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud.” *Brown v. Gardner*, 347 U.S. 483, 485 (1954). To establish such fraud, a creditor must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the debtor made a material misrepresentation, knew it was false or acted with gross recklessness as to its truth, intended to deceive, and that the creditor reasonably relied on the misrepresentation and suffered damages as a proximate result. *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1992). The critical threshold inquiry is whether a false representation—either express or implied—was made.

Here, the Bankruptcy Court concluded that Cohen impliedly represented that the rents he charged were lawful. But this conclusion misapplies the law. *Actual fraud* under § 523(a)(2)(A) requires more than mere silence or failure to disclose. As this Court has held, “a mere failure to disclose, without more, does not constitute a false representation.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). Silence, absent a duty to speak, does not equate to fraud. *See also* *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1117 (3d Cir. 1995) (“The law does not impose a general duty to disclose information, even when such information is material.”). Cohen had no legal obligation to inform tenants of the rent control limits. He was not a fiduciary, nor was there any special relationship that would have imposed a duty to disclose. The tenants were not misled by any affirmative statement; they were simply charged rent, and later informed by the Rent Leveling Board that the amounts exceeded the legal limit.

The Court of Appeals erred in equating the act of charging rent with an implied representation of legality. That would transform every rent overcharge into a fraud under the Bankruptcy Code—regardless of intent—simply because the rent exceeded a regulatory cap. Such a rule would eviscerate the distinction between regulatory violations and fraudulent conduct. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195, 198 (9th Cir. 1991) (“Punitive damages are not dischargeable only if they are based on fraud, not merely on a violation of a statute.”). The mere fact that Cohen failed to consult the Rent Control Board or read the ordinance does not amount to a false representation. His ignorance of the law is not equivalent to a knowing misrepresentation. *See In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991) (recklessness requires more than negligence).

Moreover, the Bankruptcy Court’s finding that Cohen acted with “reckless indifference” to the truth is clearly erroneous. Recklessness under § 523(a)(2)(A) requires “unreasonable conduct in disregard of a known or obvious risk from which it is highly probable that harm would follow.” *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. at 834 (quoting *Prosser & Keeton on Torts*). Here, Cohen believed he was permitted to charge market rent, subject to a 6% annual increase. He had no reason to believe he was violating the law. He inquired about passing on tax and water surcharges—actions consistent with good faith compliance. His conduct, while negligent in hindsight, was not reckless. *See In re Van Horne*, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir. 1987) (“Recklessness is not established by mere negligence.”). The Court of Appeals’ conclusion that Cohen’s conduct constituted reckless disregard is unsupported by the record and must be reversed.

II. THE TREBLE DAMAGES AWARDED UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE DISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE THEY ARE PUNITIVE IN NATURE AND NOT “OBTAINED BY” FRAUD.

The Court of Appeals held that the treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable because they were “foreseeable” and “codified.” But this reasoning misreads the Bankruptcy Code. Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only debts “to the extent obtained by” fraud. *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198. The key inquiry is not whether the penalty was foreseeable, but whether the debt itself was *obtained by* fraud. The compensatory damages—equal to the overcharged rent—were obtained by fraud and thus nondischargeable. But the treble damages are not. They are punitive in nature and serve to deter future misconduct, not to compensate the victim. *See Molzof v. United States*, 502 U.S. 301, 305 (1992) (“The meaning of the term ‘punitive damages’ as used in a federal statute is a federal question.”).

The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages for violations, but this statutory penalty is not a debt “obtained by” fraud. It is a penalty imposed by law, not a debt incurred through fraudulent conduct. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d at 198 (holding that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not “obtained by” fraud). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which the Court of Appeals relied upon, is distinguishable. There, the court held that punitive damages were nondischargeable because they were part of a single judgment. But *
