**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” STANDARD REQUIRES KNOWLEDGE OF THE TRANSACTION, NOT THE IMPRUDENCE OF THE ACT.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a plaintiff must have “actual knowledge” of the *imprudence* of an investment to trigger the three-year statute of limitations under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This standard is inconsistent with the circuit’s precedent, which holds that the statute is triggered by knowledge of the *transaction* that constitutes the alleged violation, not by knowledge of the law. See *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1341 (9th Cir. 1994) (“[T]he statute of limitations is triggered by the [trustees’] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.”). See also *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991 (9th Cir. 1985) (holding that the statute of limitations is triggered by the knowledge of the transaction, not the knowledge of the law). See also *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (quoting *Blanton*).

In *Waller*, the court held that the statute of limitations was triggered when the plan administrators were informed that the selection of a particular option would result in the insurance company retaining a substantial portion of pension funds. *Waller*, 32 F.3d at 1341. The court reasoned that the administrators had actual knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation. Id. Similarly, in *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), the court held that the administrators had actual knowledge of the ERISA violation when the insurance company informed them that the selection of the “market value” option would result in the insurance company’s retaining a substantial portion of pension funds. Id. These cases establish that the statute of limitations is triggered by knowledge of the underlying transaction, not by a plaintiff’s legal conclusion regarding the imprudence of that transaction.

The Supreme Court has also emphasized that a plaintiff must have knowledge of the breach to bring a Section 1104 claim. *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1830 (2015). However, the “breach” in this context is the *act* of investing in alternative assets. The fact that the plaintiff did not read the documents to determine if that act was “imprudent” does not negate the fact that the act occurred. The statute of limitations should run from the date the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the transaction, not from the date the plaintiff chose to investigate the legal implications of that transaction.

**II. THE PLAINTIFF’S FAILURE TO READ THE DOCUMENTS CONSTITUTES CONSTRUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE.**

The Ninth Circuit erred in finding a genuine dispute of material fact regarding whether Sulyma had actual knowledge of the breach. The documents disclosing the investment strategy were available to Sulyma. Under Ninth Circuit law, a plaintiff’s failure to read information that is available to them constitutes constructive knowledge. *Murray v. United States*, 945 F.2d 97, 100 (9th Cir. 1991) (defining “actual knowledge” as “actual awareness” of the facts). See also *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that the limitations period runs from the “earliest date on which [the plaintiff] knew of the breach”).

Here, Intel provided Sulyma with detailed Fund Fact Sheets and other disclosures explaining the investment strategy and the risks involved. Sulyma testified that he did not recall seeing these documents. However, the availability of the documents to Sulyma is dispositive. The statute of limitations is designed to prevent stale claims, and a plaintiff cannot avoid the limitations period by ignoring information that is readily available. *Murray*, 945 F.2d at 100. By failing to read the documents, Sulyma cannot claim that he lacked actual knowledge of the breach. The district court correctly found that Sulyma had actual knowledge of the breach more than three years before filing suit.

**III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “KNOWLEDGE OF IMPRUDENCE” STANDARD IS INCONSISTENT WITH ERISA’S PURPOSE.**

The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that a plaintiff must have “actual knowledge” that the investment was “imprudent” effectively allows plaintiffs to ignore information until they are ready to sue. This interpretation defeats the purpose of the statute of limitations, which is to provide certainty and finality to litigation. *Ziegler*, 916 F.2d at 552. See also *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996) (stating that ERISA imposes a duty of care upon plan fiduciaries).

If a plaintiff must have knowledge of the *imprudence* of an act, they can simply ignore the information provided by the fiduciary until they are ready to file suit. The statute of limitations would then effectively be meaningless, as the plaintiff could always claim that they did not know the act was “imprudent” until they were ready to sue. The correct standard, as established in *Waller*, *Blanton*, and *Meagher*, is that the statute of limitations is triggered by knowledge of the transaction itself. The fact that the plaintiff did not read the documents to determine if the transaction was “imprudent” is irrelevant to the statute of limitations analysis. The statute of limitations ran when Sulyma had constructive knowledge of the investment strategy, which was more than three years before he filed suit.
