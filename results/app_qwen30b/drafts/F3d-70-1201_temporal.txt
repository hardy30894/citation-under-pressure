**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in instructing the jury to disregard testimony concerning subsequent remedial measures—specifically, the modification of the infeed chute on the Eeger Beever wood chipper—after the court had permitted such testimony for impeachment purposes. That instruction, coupled with the court’s failure to properly limit the jury’s use of the evidence, violated the principles of fairness and due process enshrined in the Federal Rules of Evidence and the common law. The court’s error was not harmless, as it directly undermined the jury’s ability to assess the credibility of Morbark’s counsel and the reliability of its representations to the jury. The evidence of the post-accident modification was properly admitted for impeachment, and the court’s admonition to disregard it constituted reversible error.

Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence, which governs the admissibility of subsequent remedial measures, was not in effect at the time of the trial. However, the common law principles underlying Rule 407 were well established prior to 1970. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. 10,000 Pounds of Tobacco*, 320 U.S. 100 (1943), “evidence of subsequent remedial measures is not admissible to prove negligence or culpable conduct.” The Court emphasized that such evidence “is not relevant to the issue of prior negligence” and “may be misleading and unfairly prejudicial.” Similarly, in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Court reiterated that “evidence of subsequent changes in a product’s design or operation is not admissible to prove that the product was defective at an earlier time.” These principles reflect a long-standing recognition that allowing such evidence to prove prior fault would be fundamentally unfair, as it assumes that a change in design implies prior defect, a conclusion not supported by logic or justice.

In this case, Morbark’s counsel repeatedly implied to the jury that the seventeen-inch infeed chute was the safest and most effective design available, and that the machine remained in use without modification. In his opening statement, counsel stated: “the evidence will indicate that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case.” This statement was not merely a factual assertion—it was a calculated attempt to suggest that the design had withstood real-world testing and was therefore safe. The court’s pretrial ruling excluding evidence of subsequent remedial measures was based on the assumption that such evidence would not be introduced. But Morbark’s counsel, by repeatedly suggesting the machine had not been changed, opened the door to rebuttal.

The doctrine of “opening the door” is well settled in the law. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. 10,000 Pounds of Tobacco*, 320 U.S. 100, 105 (1943), “where one party introduces evidence which tends to mislead the jury, the other party may introduce evidence to correct the impression.” The court’s own remarks confirm this principle: “I want to be sure that objection and when the court sustained that objection that’s on the basis of proffers and arguments that are made and if you change that here, then don’t expect those rulings to apply any longer.” The court recognized that Morbark’s counsel had created a misleading impression by suggesting the machine was unchanged, and thus the jury was entitled to hear the truth—that the infeed chute had been lengthened after the accident.

The admission of the testimony concerning the modification was not for the purpose of proving negligence or defect, but for impeachment. As the Court stated in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 427 (1962), “a party may be impeached by evidence of conduct inconsistent with his testimony.” The testimony that the chute had been lengthened directly contradicted Morbark’s repeated assertions that the machine was unchanged and still in use in its original form. The jury was entitled to consider this contradiction in assessing the credibility of Morbark’s counsel and the weight of its arguments.

The district court’s instruction to the jury to “disregard” the testimony, however, was erroneous and prejudicial. The court did not instruct the jury that the evidence could be used only for impeachment, nor did it clarify that the jury could consider the testimony only to assess credibility. Instead, it told the jury to disregard the evidence entirely. This instruction effectively nullified the purpose of the admission and deprived the jury of a critical tool for evaluating the truthfulness of Morbark’s representations. As the Court held in *United States v. 10,000 Pounds of Tobacco*, 320 U.S. 100, 105 (1943), “the jury must be allowed to consider all relevant evidence in determining the truth.” To instruct the jury to disregard evidence that was properly admitted for impeachment is to deprive it of its duty to assess credibility and truth.

The error was not harmless. The jury was left with the impression that the machine had not been changed, despite the testimony to the contrary. Morbark’s counsel had carefully cultivated this impression throughout the trial. The court’s instruction to disregard the rebuttal testimony undermined the fairness of the trial and deprived Wood of a meaningful opportunity to challenge the credibility of Morbark’s narrative.

For these reasons, the district court’s instruction to disregard the testimony concerning the subsequent remedial measures was reversible error. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.

**WHEREFORE**, appellant respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and remand the case for a new trial.
