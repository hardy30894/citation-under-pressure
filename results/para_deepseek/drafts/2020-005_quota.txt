**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s in-state activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316–17 (1945). Ford Motor Company purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting business in Montana through extensive advertising, sales of vehicles and parts, and a network of authorized dealers. Because the claims against Ford—design defect, failure to warn, and negligence—directly relate to Ford’s design, manufacture, and nationwide distribution of the vehicle it marketed and sold in Montana, jurisdiction is proper.

**I. The “arise out of or relate to” standard does not require but-for causation.**

The Constitution requires only “minimum contacts” such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. This Court has consistently applied the standard flexibly, focusing on whether the defendant’s forum contacts are the “proximate cause” of the litigation. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472–73 (1985). In *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 780 (1984), the Court held that a magazine’s regular circulation in a state supported specific jurisdiction for a libel claim even though the plaintiff was not a resident and the injury occurred elsewhere; the “contacts” related to the claim because the defendant had intentionally targeted the forum. Similarly, in *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957), a single insurance contract in the forum was sufficient because the claim “arose from” that contract. Here, Ford’s deliberate marketing and sales in Montana are the *relational* foundation for claims involving a vehicle Ford designed and placed into the stream of commerce with the expectation of national use—including Montana.

**II. Ford purposefully availed itself of the Montana market.**

Ford does not argue that it lacked contacts with Montana; it sells vehicles and parts, operates authorized service centers, and advertises extensively within the State. This is not a case of a product’s “casual” entry into the forum. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (holding that a defendant must “purposefully avail[] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum”). Ford’s conduct goes far beyond mere placement of a product into the stream of commerce. It has actively cultivated a market in Montana through advertising and sales promotions. *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987) (plurality opinion) (requiring “additional conduct” such as designing the product for the forum market). Ford designed the Explorer for nationwide sale, directed its marketing to all fifty states, and established a dealer network in Montana. These actions constitute purposeful availment.

**III. The claims “relate to” Ford’s in-state activities.**

The phrase “arise out of or relate to” is not synonymous with “caused by.” *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 n.5 (2014) (noting that specific jurisdiction often depends on “the affiliation between the forum and the underlying controversy”). In *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017), the Court required “a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” That connection exists here because Ford’s nationwide design and manufacturing activities—which produced the same defective tire or design in every Explorer—are inherently *related to* the Montana accident. Ford sold identical vehicles in Montana; it designed, advertised, and warranted those vehicles for use in Montana. The defect that caused the accident is the very defect Ford could have corrected through its in-state safety campaigns and service networks. The relationship is direct: Ford’s in-state contacts involve the same product line, the same design, and the same failure-to-warn obligations that gave rise to the suit.

**IV. The Montana Supreme Court correctly found that jurisdiction is consistent with due process.**

The Montana Supreme Court concluded that Ford’s advertising and parts sales in the State “created a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” That conclusion is faithful to *International Shoe* and its progeny. Ford cannot insulate itself from jurisdiction by arguing that the particular vehicle was first sold outside Montana. The company designed, manufactured, and marketed the same vehicle nationwide, inviting customers in every state to buy and use it. When an allegedly defective product causes injury in a state where the manufacturer has deliberately cultivated a market, the claims “relate to” those forum contacts. *See J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 889 (2011) (Breyer, J., concurring) (noting that a manufacturer that “direct[s] its marketing and sales efforts” to a state may be subject to jurisdiction there).

The Due Process Clause does not require but-for causation; it requires a “relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation.” *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). That relationship is plainly satisfied when a global auto manufacturer aggressively markets and sells products in a state, and a plaintiff’s injury in that state arises from a defect inherent in the manufacturer’s nationwide product line. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
