**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS UNDER § 6502(a)**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for collecting from the individual partners. Section 6502(a) permits collection only if the assessment was made against “the taxpayer” whose liability is being enforced. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Here, the IRS assessed the partnership—not the individual partners—so the 10-year extension does not apply to them.

This principle is well established. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the court held that an assessment naming only the partnership was invalid as to an individual partner because the IRS failed to comply with § 6203’s requirement that the taxpayer be “identified.” *Id.* at 458. Similarly, in *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), the Seventh Circuit emphasized that the IRS must assess each partner individually to extend the limitations period against them. *Id.* at 563 (noting that assessments against the partnership and partners extended the period for both).

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. There, the court merely held that a partner could not seek a refund after paying his share of the partnership’s tax—it did not address whether the IRS could collect from a partner without an individual assessment. *Id.* at 910. The IRS’s broader argument—that an assessment against the partnership suffices—finds no support in the statute or precedent.

**II. CALIFORNIA PARTNERSHIP LAW DOES NOT ALLOW COLLECTION WITHOUT AN INDIVIDUAL JUDGMENT**

Even if the IRS could enforce the partnership’s debt against the partners, California law requires a judgment against each partner before their assets may be reached. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); *Witkin, Summary of California Law* § 60V. The IRS has neither obtained such judgments nor timely assessed the partners individually. Thus, its claims are unenforceable.

This principle is consistent with *United States v. Texas*, 507 U.S. 529 (1993), where the Supreme Court held that federal tax liens must comply with state law procedures for enforcing partnership debts. *Id.* at 534. Similarly, in *United States v. Western Products, Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d 84 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), the court required a judgment against a partner before permitting collection, despite the partnership assessment. *Id.* at 91.

**III. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS BARRING ASSESSMENT AGAINST THE PARTNERS HAS EXPIRED**

The IRS concedes it did not assess the partners within three years of their returns, as required by § 6501(a). Because the partnership assessment did not extend the limitations period for the partners, the IRS’s claims are time-barred. *See Goldston v. United States (In re Goldston)*, 104 F.3d 1198, 1200-01 (10th Cir. 1997) (holding that the IRS must timely assess or sue to preserve its claim).

**IV. THE IRS’S CLAIMS ARE UNENFORCEABLE IN BANKRUPTCY**

Under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), bankruptcy courts may disallow claims that are unenforceable under applicable law. Here, the IRS’s claims are unenforceable because:
1) The partners were not individually assessed, and
2) No judgment exists against them under California law.

*See Neilson v. Chang (In re First T.D. & Inv., Inc.)*, 253 F.3d 520, 526 (9th Cir. 2001) (reviewing de novo whether a claim is unenforceable).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the bankruptcy court’s disallowance of the IRS’s claims. The IRS failed to individually assess the partners or obtain judgments against them, and the statute of limitations has expired. The judgment below should be affirmed.
