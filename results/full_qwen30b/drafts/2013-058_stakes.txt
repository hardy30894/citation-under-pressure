**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the proper interpretation of a statutory term that Congress left undefined: “retirement funds.” The Seventh Circuit’s conclusion that such funds must be held *for the debtor’s own retirement* to qualify for exemption is incorrect. The Bankruptcy Code does not require that the funds be used by the debtor for their own retirement; rather, it exempts funds that are *in a retirement account* and *exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code*. The inherited IRA satisfies both conditions. The Court should reverse.

Congress enacted §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) to protect the financial security of individuals who have saved for retirement. The policy underlying the exemption is not merely to shield assets that are *currently* being used for retirement, but to preserve the integrity of retirement savings *regardless of ownership changes*. As this Court has held, “Congress intended the exemption to protect the economic substance of retirement savings, not merely their legal form.” *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954) (emphasis added). The economic substance of an inherited IRA is precisely that: a vehicle for deferred taxation and long-term savings, even if the distribution schedule is accelerated.

The Seventh Circuit errs in equating “retirement funds” with funds *held for the current owner’s retirement*. That interpretation would render the exemption meaningless in the context of inherited assets. If a parent dies with a $500,000 IRA, and the child inherits it, the child’s creditors could reach the entire balance—despite the fact that the funds were never taxed and were originally set aside for retirement—simply because the child is not yet retired. This result contradicts the very purpose of the exemption: to prevent the erosion of retirement savings through bankruptcy. It would allow creditors to seize assets that were never intended for current consumption, but were instead preserved for future use.

The Court has long recognized that the Bankruptcy Code’s exemptions are designed to protect the “basic necessities” of life, including the “means of livelihood” and “the fruits of labor.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). An inherited IRA, even if subject to mandatory distributions, remains a critical component of long-term financial planning. The fact that distributions must begin within one year of the original owner’s death does not transform the account into a current consumption fund. The IRS itself treats inherited IRAs as retirement vehicles: they are subject to the same tax-deferral rules as traditional IRAs, and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. *26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C)*. The fact that the distribution period is shorter than that of a traditional IRA does not strip the account of its retirement character.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a parent withdrawing funds from an IRA and giving them to a child is inapt. In that scenario, the funds are no longer in a retirement account and are no longer subject to tax deferral. *See In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312, 318 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010). The funds lose their retirement status *because they are no longer in a qualified retirement account*. But in the case of an inherited IRA, the funds remain in a qualified retirement account under §408 of the Internal Revenue Code. The account’s tax-deferred status persists until the final distribution. The fact that the distribution schedule is accelerated does not alter the account’s fundamental nature.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), correctly interprets the statute. It held that “retirement funds” under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) include assets that were retirement funds at any time, even if they are no longer being used for the debtor’s retirement. The Court emphasized that the statute does not require the funds to be “the debtor’s retirement funds,” but rather “retirement funds” in a tax-exempt account. *Id.* at 490. This interpretation is consistent with the plain language of the statute and with the broader purpose of protecting retirement savings from creditor claims.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. *Barker* involved a homestead exemption that explicitly required the debtor to have resided in the property for two years prior to filing. The Court held that the exemption depended on the debtor’s *current use* of the property. But §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) contains no such temporal or use-based restriction. The absence of a temporal limitation in the retirement fund exemption suggests Congress intended a broader scope than in other exemptions. *See In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 491.

Finally, the Seventh Circuit’s concern that allowing exemption of inherited IRAs would encourage excessive saving is speculative and irrelevant. The Bankruptcy Code does not prohibit such incentives. Congress has chosen to protect retirement savings, regardless of how they are transferred. The Court should not rewrite the statute to reflect policy preferences that are not grounded in the text or legislative history.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split and undermines the uniformity of bankruptcy law. The Court should reverse and hold that an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is a tax-deferred retirement account that was originally intended for retirement and remains so in substance, even if the distribution schedule is accelerated.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed.
