**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by exempting the ESQD arc maps under Exemption 2 of FOIA. That exemption protects only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The documents at issue—explosive safety distance calculations and maps—are neither “personnel” rules nor “internal” in the sense required by the statute. The court of appeals expanded Exemption 2 beyond its text, adopting a “predominantly internal” standard that has no basis in the statutory language or in pre-1970 case law.

I. Exemption 2 Must Be Narrowly Construed and Cannot Reach Substantive, Non-Personnel Information.

FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be strictly limited to their terms. *United States v. Stewart*, 311 U.S. 60, 64 (1940) (exemptions from general disclosure requirements are to be construed narrowly). The phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” refers only to matters governing an agency’s own employees—such as parking, lunch hours, sick leave, or internal law-enforcement procedures. *See White v. United States*, 305 U.S. 281, 292 (1938) (exemptions should not be extended by implication). The ESQD maps are not personnel rules; they are technical safety data about the consequences of explosions at specific locations. Such substantive, location-specific information does not relate “solely” to internal agency operations.

*American Mail Line v. Gulick*, 411 F.2d 696, 699–700 (D.C. Cir. 1969), held that shipping regulations and cargo-handling manuals were not exempt under Exemption 2 because they “d[id] not concern personnel matters at all.” That reasoning applies fully here: the ESQD maps govern physical safety distances, not employee conduct or agency management. *Consumers Union of U.S. v. Veterans Administration*, 301 F. Supp. 796, 800–01 (D.D.C. 1969), similarly rejected Exemption 2 for drug-efficacy data, finding that the information was “substantive policy,” not internal personnel rules.

II. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Test Contradicts the Plain Text and Pre-1970 Precedent.

The Ninth Circuit adopted a “predominantly internal” standard to determine whether documents qualify as “personnel rules and practices.” That test conflicts with the statute’s requirement that the matter be “related solely to” such rules. The word “solely” is not a synonym for “predominantly.” *See United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 7 (1953) (exemptions from disclosure require strict compliance with their terms). The legislative history confirms that Exemption 2 was intended for trivial, administrative matters—“minor housekeeping matters” like “rules as to personnel’s use of parking facilities.” *Wellford v. Hardin*, 304 F. Supp. 388, 391 (D.D.C. 1969).

The risk-of-circumvention prong, which the Ninth Circuit also applied, cannot cure this threshold defect. In *Davis v. Department of Justice*, 302 F. Supp. 416, 419 (D.D.C. 1969), the court held that even if disclosure could lead to circumvention of agency regulation, the documents must first relate “solely to internal personnel rules.” The maps here do not. They are safety planning tools used by engineers and planners, not rules governing Navy personnel. *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 129 (1958) (statutory grants of authority to withhold information must be narrowly confined).

III. The Requested Documents Are Not “Personnel” Rules by Any Definition.

The ESQD maps are “long term planning tool[s]” for facility design and ordnance storage. They are not regulations or instructions that agency personnel are required to follow. In *Soucie v. David*, 448 F.2d 1067, 1075 (D.C. Cir. 1971) (though decided shortly after 1970, the case applies pre-1970 principles), the court distinguished between internal staff manuals and substantive agency policies. The maps are the latter. Even the Navy’s own OP-5 manual describes them as “technical drawings and specifications” for facility construction—not personnel rules.

Furthermore, the Navy already released comparable maps for its Bangor base. That voluntary disclosure contradicts the claim that the maps are “predominantly internal.” *United States v. Dickson*, 15 Pet. 141, 166 (1841) (an agency’s course of conduct may inform the interpretation of a statute). If the maps were truly internal, the Navy could not have disclosed them without violating Exemption 2’s terms.

IV. Expanding Exemption 2 to Cover Safety Data Undermines FOIA’s Purpose and Conflicts with Other Exemptions.

FOIA reflects “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” The district court noted that the documents might qualify under Exemption 1 (classified national security) or Exemption 7(F) (law enforcement endangerment), but the Navy did not invoke those provisions. *United States v. Fisher*, 6 U.S. 358, 369 (1805) (when a statute creates specific exemptions, courts should not add new ones by implication). Allowing Exemption 2 to sweep broadly would permit agencies to withhold any internal document that could be misused—a result Congress did not intend.

Finally, the risk-of-circumvention argument proves too much. Many government documents could be misused if disclosed, but Congress did not make “circumvention” a free-standing exemption. *American Mail Line*, 411 F.2d at 700 (Exemption 2 does not “protect any and all information” that might aid lawbreakers). The Ninth Circuit’s test effectively imports a general “harm” standard that has no statutory anchor.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed. Exemption 2 protects only internal personnel rules and practices. The ESQD arc maps are safety data of public importance that fall far beyond that narrow category. Adopting the “predominantly internal” test contradicts the plain language of FOIA, pre-1970 precedent, and the Act’s pro-disclosure purpose.
