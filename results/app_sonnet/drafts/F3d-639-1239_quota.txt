# ARGUMENT

## I. THE RELATION-BACK DOCTRINE PRESERVES JURISDICTION OVER THE CLASS CLAIMS DESPITE BCR'S OFFER OF JUDGMENT TO LUCERO INDIVIDUALLY

The district court erred in treating *Clark v. State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co.*, 590 F.3d 1134 (10th Cir. 2009), as an inflexible rule requiring dismissal whenever a named plaintiff's individual claim is satisfied before certification. *Clark*'s general statement does not account for the doctrine, recognized by the Supreme Court and numerous circuits, that a class representative's motion for certification "relates back" to the filing of the complaint when a defendant attempts to moot the controversy through a strategic pre-certification settlement offer.

The foundation for this doctrine is *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393 (1975), where the Supreme Court held that once a class is certified, "the class of unnamed persons described in the certification acquires a legal status separate from the interest asserted by [the named plaintiff]." *Id.* at 399. The Court extended this reasoning in *U.S. Parole Commission v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388 (1980), holding that a named plaintiff retains a sufficient personal stake to appeal the denial of class certification even after his individual claim becomes moot, because "the plaintiff's substantive claims may be time barred or otherwise defeated" without destroying the separate, unresolved interest in obtaining class treatment. *Id.* at 402-04. The Court reasoned that the certification decision itself implicates concrete adverseness distinct from the merits of the individual claim. *Id.*

That same term, in *Deposit Guaranty National Bank v. Roper*, 445 U.S. 326 (1980), the Court confronted a defendant's attempt to moot a class action by tendering the maximum amount recoverable on the named plaintiffs' individual claims. The Court rejected that tactic, holding that plaintiffs retain a continuing "individual interest in class certification" tied to their stake in shifting litigation costs and vindicating the class device itself. *Id.* at 336. Critically, the Court warned that permitting defendants to "pick off" named plaintiffs with tendered judgments "would invite waste of judicial resources by stimulating successive suits brought by others claiming aggrievement." *Id.* at 339.

Applying these principles to circumstances materially identical to this case, the Third Circuit in *Weiss v. Regal Collections*, 385 F.3d 337 (3d Cir. 2004), held that an unaccepted Rule 68 offer of judgment for the full amount of an FDCPA plaintiff's individual claim does not moot the class action when the offer is made before the plaintiff has had a fair opportunity to seek certification. The court adopted the relation-back rule specifically to prevent defendants from exploiting the timing gap between filing and certification to "pick off" representative plaintiffs and thereby "eras[e] any legal effect of the offer of judgment." *Id.* at 348. The Fifth and Seventh Circuits reached the same conclusion decades earlier. *See Zeidman v. J. Ray McDermott & Co.*, 651 F.2d 1030, 1051 (5th Cir. 1981) (holding that a certification motion filed with "reasonable promptness" relates back to defeat mootness); *Susman v. Lincoln American Corp.*, 587 F.2d 866, 870 (7th Cir. 1978) (same).

This case presents precisely the scenario these courts anticipated. BCR tendered its Rule 68 offer on June 2, 2009—before the parties had even conducted class discovery, and while the court-ordered schedule contemplated a certification hearing nearly ten months later, on March 31, 2010. Lucero did not sit idle; he complied with the parties' jointly proposed discovery plan, which bifurcated class and merits discovery and set February 23, 2010, as the deadline for the certification motion. Aplt.App. 58-59. Lucero filed his motion on February 22, 2010—one day before that deadline. There was no undue delay; there was compliance with a schedule the district court itself adopted.

## II. REED V. HECKLER DOES NOT COMPEL A CONTRARY RESULT

BCR's reliance on *Reed v. Heckler*, 756 F.2d 779 (10th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. *Reed* did not involve a defendant's tactical offer of full relief made to forestall certification on an already-agreed schedule; it addressed mootness in the ordinary course, without the "picking off" concern that animated *Roper*, *Weiss*, *Zeidman*, and *Susman*. Nothing in *Reed* purports to foreclose the relation-back doctrine, and this Court should not read into it a categorical rule that the Supreme Court itself declined to adopt in *Geraghty* and *Roper*.

## III. THE POLICIES UNDERLYING RULE 23 REQUIRE APPLICATION OF THE RELATION-BACK DOCTRINE HERE

Permitting BCR to moot this action would allow any well-resourced defendant to defeat class treatment simply by tendering payment to whichever plaintiff first files suit—regardless of the scale of the alleged wrongdoing or the number of similarly situated consumers. The Supreme Court's admonition in *Roper* against "stimulating successive suits" applies with full force: absent relation back, defendants have every incentive to repeat this tactic against successive named plaintiffs, ensuring the class device is never available to vindicate small-dollar consumer claims like those arising under the FDCPA. 445 U.S. at 339. The district court's rigid application of *Clark* cannot be reconciled with the Supreme Court's teaching in *Sosna* and *Geraghty* that a plaintiff's interest in pursuing class certification survives the mooting of his individual claim, particularly where, as here, the plaintiff acted diligently and consistently with the court-approved schedule.

For these reasons, the district court's dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction should be reversed.
