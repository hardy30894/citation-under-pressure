The federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over every case that merely contains a patent issue. Section 1338(a) gives federal courts exclusive jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly limited that phrase. See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 807-09 (1988). A case arises under federal law only when the federal issue appears on the face of a well-pleaded complaint and is necessary to the plaintiff’s claim. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 112-13 (1936).

That principle is not new. In Shulthis v. McDougal, the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law merely because it “takes its origin in the laws of the United States”; it must “really and substantially involve[] a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Likewise, in Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co., the Court permitted jurisdiction only because the federal issue was “an essential one” and the plaintiff’s right to relief depended on the validity of federal bonds. 255 U.S. 180, 199-201 (1921). By contrast, where the federal issue is only part of the background, jurisdiction does not lie. Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

That settled rule defeats exclusive federal jurisdiction here. Minton’s claim is a state-law legal malpractice action. The elements of that claim are governed by state law, and the federal patent issue is relevant only as an embedded component of the “case within a case.” See, e.g., Alexander v. Turtur & Associates, Inc., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The malpractice claim does not ask the court to declare a patent valid or invalid, to construe the Patent Act as a stand-alone matter, or to enforce any federal patent right. At most, patent law supplies one issue in proving causation. That is not enough under Christianson, which held that federal jurisdiction exists only when patent law creates the cause of action or when patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims. 486 U.S. at 808-09.

The Court’s pre-1970 decisions confirm the same distinction between a substantial federal issue and a merely incidental one. In Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co., the Court recognized that a suit is not within exclusive patent jurisdiction simply because a patent is mentioned; the cause of action must itself be one arising under the patent laws. 185 U.S. 282, 287-88 (1902). In Hartell v. Tilghman, the Court held that federal jurisdiction did not exist where patent questions were only incidental to the dispute. And in The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., the Court likewise treated embedded patent issues as insufficient to convert an otherwise nonpatent controversy into a patent case. 228 U.S. 22, 25-26 (1913). Those cases foreclose the sweeping rule respondents seek.

Nor does the fact that patent law may be consulted in deciding causation make the federal interest “substantial” in the jurisdictional sense. The Court has long rejected jurisdiction based on a federal issue that is merely evidentiary or defensive. See Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 464-65 (1894); Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18. The patent question here is backward-looking and case-specific: whether, in the underlying litigation, counsel should have raised a particular defense. That sort of collateral patent inquiry is a far cry from the “direct and immediate” federal question required for exclusive jurisdiction. Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 570.

Finally, exclusive federal jurisdiction would upset the balance Congress struck between federal and state courts. State courts routinely adjudicate malpractice, negligence, and professional-responsibility claims. Nothing in the Patent Act suggests Congress intended to oust state courts from those traditional domains whenever a patent issue appears along the way. The Court has warned against reading jurisdictional statutes so broadly that they displace ordinary state authority absent clear congressional direction. Gully, 299 U.S. at 114-15; Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co., 211 U.S. at 152.

Because Minton’s complaint asserts a state-law malpractice claim and because any patent issue is only incidental to that claim, the federal courts lack exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment dismissing for want of jurisdiction should be reversed.
