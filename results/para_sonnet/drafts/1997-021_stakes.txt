**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board's "reasonable doubt" standard for polling should be rejected, or at minimum applied faithfully, because as administered it renders polling a functional nullity inconsistent with the Act's purposes.**

The Act does not speak directly to employer polling. In the absence of a governing statutory text, the Board's chosen standard must be rational and must serve, rather than frustrate, the Act's dual commitments to employee free choice and stable collective bargaining. See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (the Board bears "the primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy," but that responsibility must be exercised reasonably). The Board's own precedent recognizes that an incumbent union's majority status enjoys only a rebuttable presumption, which an employer may overcome "through objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status." Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987). Yet the Board applies that identical evidentiary threshold to three distinct employer options—outright withdrawal of recognition, a Board-supervised RM election, and an internal poll—even though only the first carries no procedural safeguards at all, and the poll here was conducted under the very safeguards the Board itself prescribed in Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967).

Three courts of appeals have recognized the irrationality of this equivalence. The Sixth Circuit held "that an employer may poll its employees to determine their union sentiment if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal." Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). The Ninth Circuit likewise held that "as long as the employer complies with the Struksnes conditions and procedural safeguards, it may poll its employees to determine their union sentiment if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal of recognition." Mingtree Rest., Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); accord A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981). A poll conducted with neutral, third-party safeguards—here, tabulated by a Roman Catholic priest who alone viewed the ballots—poses none of the coercive risks that justify a strict threshold for unilateral withdrawal. Collapsing the standards, as the Board does, means an employer with genuine but not-yet-conclusive doubt has no lawful means of testing that doubt before acting, contrary to the Act's preference for informed, non-coercive resolution of representation questions rather than blind guesses risking either an unlawful withdrawal or continued bargaining with a minority union.

**II. Even applying the Board's own "reasonable doubt" standard, the Board's finding that Allentown lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.**

Judicial review of Board factfinding requires examination of "the record in its entirety," and "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board here did the opposite: it isolated each item of evidence, discounted it individually on narrow technical grounds, and never asked whether the cumulative weight of the evidence would lead a reasonable employer to doubt the union's majority.

Seven employees made direct, unprompted statements to management repudiating the union. Two more—Ridgick and Marsh—expressed dissatisfaction that, considered together with the other seven and with the makeup of the newly constituted forty-employee unit (drawn from a larger, previously unionized workforce that Allentown had reduced by roughly a third), corroborated rather than contradicted a genuine doubt about majority support. Two additional employees, Bloch and Mohr—one of whom was the union's own steward—reported that entire segments of the workforce, including the night shift, did not want the union. The Board discarded these reports as unreliable hearsay because the reporting employees did not explain how they formed their impressions, yet the Board applied no comparable skepticism to the presumption of continued majority support itself, a presumption resting on nothing more than the union's historical certification of a materially different, larger unit under the predecessor employer. A presumption of continuing majority support is "rebuttable," Fall River, 482 U.S. at 37–41, and it cannot be insulated from rebuttal by evidentiary rules that credit every doubt against the employer while crediting none in its favor.

Universal Camera does not permit an agency to sustain its findings by taking evidence a piece at a time and discarding each piece for a different technical reason, without ever confronting what the assembled evidence shows. 340 U.S. at 488. Considered as a whole—nine direct statements of repudiation from a forty-employee unit, corroborated by two further reports describing widespread anti-union sentiment among coworkers, all obtained without any allegation of coercion—the record does not permit the conclusion that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith basis for doubting the union's continued majority. The Board's order should be vacated.
