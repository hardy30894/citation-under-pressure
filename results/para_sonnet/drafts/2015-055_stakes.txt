**ARGUMENT**

**Johnson's vagueness holding is a procedural rule that does not apply retroactively on collateral review.**

Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), "new constitutional rules of criminal procedure" ordinarily do not govern cases that are already final, because "[r]etroactivity is properly treated as a threshold question" separate from the merits. *Id.* at 300. *Teague* recognized only two narrow exceptions: rules that place "certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe," and "watershed rules of criminal procedure" implicating "the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding." *Id.* at 311, 313. Johnson's holding fits within neither exception.

*Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348 (2004), supplies the controlling framework for distinguishing substantive from procedural rules. "New substantive rules generally apply retroactively," while "[n]ew rules of procedure, on the other hand, generally do not apply retroactively." *Id.* at 351–52. The touchstone is whether the rule "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes," in which case it is substantive, or whether it instead "regulate[s] only the manner of determining the defendant's culpability," in which case it is procedural. *Id.* at 353. As the Court explained, "[r]ules that allocate decisionmaking authority" between judge and jury, or that govern *how* a fact is found, "are prototypical procedural rules." *Id.*

Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), did not narrow the substantive definition of "violent felony" in the way that, for example, a decision holding that certain drug offenses categorically fall outside a statute would. It struck down the ACCA's residual clause because applying it required courts to "estimat[e] the degree of risk posed by a crime in the abstract," a task that "produces more unpredictability and arbitrariness than the Due Process Clause tolerates." 576 U.S. at 598. That is a holding about the *method* by which risk is assessed—an evidentiary and adjudicative infirmity—not a holding that any category of persons or conduct is beyond Congress's power to punish with an enhanced sentence. The elements clause and the enumerated-offense clause of the ACCA remain fully intact, and any defendant whose predicate offense qualifies under those clauses—as Welch's Florida "strong arm" robbery conviction does, given its use of "force, violence, assault, or putting in fear"—remains subject to the fifteen-year mandatory minimum. Johnson thus did not "alter the range of conduct or the class of persons" ACCA punishes, *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353; it merely eliminated one indeterminate mode of proving that a prior conviction belongs to an already-existing class.

This case is therefore unlike *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998), where the Court's construction of a statute's mens rea element genuinely narrowed the substantive reach of the offense so that certain primary conduct could no longer be punished. Johnson did nothing of that kind. It resembles instead rules "that allocate decisionmaking authority," *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353, because it forecloses one analytical pathway for classifying predicate offenses while leaving the substantive predicate—whether the underlying conduct in fact qualifies as violent—unaffected.

Nor can Johnson be squeezed into Teague's watershed exception, which is reserved for procedural rules "without which the likelihood of an accurate conviction is seriously diminished." *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 313. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the watershed category is exceedingly narrow. In *Whorton v. Bockting*, 549 U.S. 406 (2007), the Court held that even the confrontation-clause rule of *Crawford v. Washington* was not a watershed rule, explaining that "it is unlikely that any [additional rule] ha[s] yet to emerge." *Id.* at 417. If a rule as central to accurate factfinding as the right of confrontation does not qualify, then a rule addressing only the constitutional adequacy of a sentencing classification's wording cannot qualify either. Johnson's residual-clause holding bears no relationship to the accuracy of the underlying factfinding process at trial or sentencing; it concerns only whether Congress spoke with sufficient clarity in one clause of a three-clause definitional statute.

Respondent recognizes that the elimination of the residual clause may affect the sentences of many federal prisoners. But the *consequences* of a rule cannot substitute for the *Teague* analysis; many procedural rules, if applied retroactively, would likewise affect numerous final sentences, yet *Teague*'s entire purpose is to prevent "a body of law . . . in a state of flux" from unsettling final judgments except in the narrowest circumstances. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 310. Because Johnson announced a new rule of criminal procedure that neither narrows the substantive class of persons ACCA punishes nor implicates the "bedrock procedural elements" essential to accurate adjudication, *Whorton*, 549 U.S. at 419, it does not apply retroactively to Welch's final conviction and sentence.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
