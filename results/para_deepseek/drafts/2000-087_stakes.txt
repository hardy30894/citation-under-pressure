**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period detention statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The text, structure, and constitutional backdrop compel a limiting construction: detention is permitted only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.

**I. The Statute’s Plain Language and Structure Foreclose Indefinite Detention**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The “removal period” is defined as 90 days. § 1231(a)(1). The statute thus creates a default rule: detention ends after 90 days unless the Attorney General exercises discretion to continue it. But nothing in the text suggests that this discretionary authority is limitless. To the contrary, the statute’s purpose—ensuring the alien’s presence for removal—implies a temporal limit. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention no longer serves that purpose and becomes unauthorized. *See* *Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 378 (2005) (statutory language must be read in light of its object).

The structure reinforces this reading. Section 1231(a)(3) mandates supervised release for aliens not removed within the removal period, subject to regulations. The “may be detained” exception in subsection (a)(6) is a narrow carve-out for those who pose a flight risk or danger. It does not transform the default rule of release into a grant of perpetual imprisonment. *Cf.* *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987) (preventive detention must be limited in duration and purpose).

**II. Constitutional Avoidance Requires a Reasonable-Time Limitation**

Even if the statute were ambiguous, the canon of constitutional avoidance demands that we construe it to avoid serious constitutional doubts. *Ashwander v. TVA*, 297 U.S. 288, 345–348 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring). Indefinite detention of a resident alien—a person who has entered the United States and established ties here—raises grave concerns under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

This Court has long recognized that “freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that Clause protects.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). Unlike an alien stopped at the border, a resident alien is “within the United States” and entitled to constitutional protections. *Id.* at 693; *see* *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Indefinite civil detention without a meaningful opportunity for release violates substantive due process unless it is narrowly tailored to a legitimate governmental interest. *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Here, the government’s interest in ensuring removal evaporates when removal is impossible. Detention then becomes punitive in effect, yet it is not imposed as punishment for any crime. *See* *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997) (civil commitment requires proof of dangerousness and treatability).

The Fifth Circuit’s standard—allowing detention as long as “good faith efforts” continue—would permit indefinite confinement based on the mere possibility, however remote, of future removal. That standard cannot be squared with due process. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701. The Constitution does not permit the government to imprison a person indefinitely on the hope that someday a foreign country might accept him.

**III. The Government’s Reliance on *Mezei* Is Misplaced**

*Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is inapposite. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien who had never effected an entry into the United States. Such an alien is “treated as if he had never entered the country.” *Id.* at 212. By contrast, Zadvydas was admitted as a lawful permanent resident. He is entitled to the full protections of the Due Process Clause. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 693. *Mezei* does not control here.

**IV. The Proper Remedy: Release When Removal Is Not Reasonably Foreseeable**

The statute, properly construed, limits post-removal detention to a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once the alien demonstrates that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is unauthorized. The habeas court must then order release, subject to appropriate conditions of supervision. § 1231(a)(3). This construction respects Congress’s intent while avoiding constitutional infirmity.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
