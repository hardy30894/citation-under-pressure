## Argument

I. An Inmate Does Not Have a Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from arbitrary governmental deprivations of life, liberty, or property. However, the Supreme Court has held that certain state-created procedures do not give rise to federally protected liberty interests. In Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court considered whether a Connecticut statute governing commutation of sentences created a protected liberty interest. The Court held that it did not, reasoning that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Id. at 464.

Similarly, Ohio's clemency procedure, as outlined in Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07 and the Ohio Department of Rehabilitation and Correction regulation, does not create a protected life or liberty interest. The governor's discretion to grant or deny clemency is "almost unfettered," as noted by the magistrate judge in the present case. The Ohio Constitution, Article III, § 11, empowers the governor to grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons "upon such conditions as the Governor may think proper." This broad discretion is not limited by the regulations governing the clemency process.

The Sixth Circuit's holding that Ohio's clemency procedure is an integral part of the adjudicatory process and therefore subject to due process scrutiny is not supported by Supreme Court precedent. In Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), the Court held that a state's creation of an appellate procedure as part of its adjudicatory process requires that procedure to meet the demands of the Due Process Clause. However, Evitts did not involve a clemency proceeding, and the Court has not extended its holding to such proceedings. In fact, the Court in Dumschat rejected a similar argument, holding that commutation decisions are not subject to the same level of judicial scrutiny as other stages of the adjudicatory process.

## II. The Option to Participate in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate an Inmate's Fifth Amendment Rights

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves in criminal proceedings. However, Ohio's clemency procedure does not compel inmates to participate in an interview or answer any questions that may incriminate them. The regulation governing the clemency process explicitly states that any statement given by the prisoner at the interview shall be considered at the clemency hearing, but it does not require the prisoner to answer any questions.

The Sixth Circuit's concern that the clemency interview may present a "Hobson's choice" between participating in the procedure and asserting a Fifth Amendment privilege is unfounded. As Judge Nelson noted in his concurrence, the regulation does not empower the parole board or its representatives to compel a prisoner to answer a single question. The prisoner remains free to say as much or as little as they wish during the interview.

Furthermore, any potential self-incrimination risk is speculative and premature, as the Supreme Court held in Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308 (1976). In Baxter, the Court reversed a lower court's holding that a state's disciplinary procedure violated an inmate's Fifth Amendment rights, reasoning that the relief granted was "premature" because it was based on hypothetical facts. Similarly, in this case, the risk of self-incrimination is hypothetical, and the district court's consideration of this issue on remand would be premature.

The judgment of the district court should be affirmed insofar as it held that Ohio's clemency procedure does not violate Woodard's due process rights. The Sixth Circuit's holding that the clemency procedure is subject to due process scrutiny and that the inmate's Fifth Amendment rights are implicated by the clemency interview should be reversed.
