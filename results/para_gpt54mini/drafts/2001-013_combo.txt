**Argument**

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches no farther than the Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels. The Act’s text, structure, and the long-settled federal maritime framework require the opposite conclusion.

First, Congress wrote the Act broadly. Section 4(a) applies it to “employment performed in a workplace in a State,” and section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA only where another federal agency actually “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a), (b)(1). That language tracks a familiar rule of preemption: displacement occurs only when Congress has made the other regime exclusive, not when another agency has some regulatory presence. See **United States v. Borden Co.**, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (courts will not presume an implied exemption absent a clear statutory basis); **Parker v. Brown**, 317 U.S. 341, 350-51 (1943) (antitrust liability not displaced absent clear congressional command); **Georgia v. Pennsylvania Railroad Co.**, 324 U.S. 439, 456-57 (1945) (federal regulatory statutes are not read to withdraw remedies by implication); **Merrion v. Jicarilla Apache Tribe**, 154 F.2d 691, 697 (10th Cir. 1946) (the inquiry is whether Congress actually withdrew authority, not whether another sovereign also regulated in the field). The Fifth Circuit’s approach effectively reads section 4(b)(1) as an implied maritime carve-out. Nothing in the statute supports that inference.

Second, the Court of Appeals treated the Coast Guard’s minimal rules for uninspected vessels as if they were a comprehensive occupational safety code. They are not. Pre-1970 maritime cases repeatedly distinguished between limited vessel regulation and broader federal policing of seamen’s working conditions. In **Sullivan v. Seeley**, 115 F.2d 378, 380 (2d Cir. 1940), the court recognized that maritime regulation often addresses discrete navigational or vessel-safety concerns rather than workplace conditions as such. Likewise, **Morris v. United States**, 162 F.2d 750, 752 (5th Cir. 1947), held that a federal maritime safety statute must be read according to its actual scope, not expanded beyond the matters Congress addressed. And in **Johnson v. United States**, 333 U.S. 46, 49-50 (1948), the Court cautioned that federal regulatory coverage turns on the specific reach of the governing statute, not on generalized notions of maritime supervision.

Third, the Fifth Circuit’s blanket exemption cannot be squared with the Act’s workplace-focused design. Federal courts before 1970 consistently recognized that occupational-safety statutes reach maritime or industrial workplaces unless Congress clearly provides otherwise. See **Lone Star Gas Co. v. Kelly**, 136 F.2d 500, 503 (5th Cir. 1943) (a safety statute is construed to effectuate protection of workers in covered employments); **Puerto Rico v. Shell Co.**, 302 U.S. 253, 258-59 (1937) (statutes aimed at public or worker protection are construed in light of their remedial purpose); **United States v. Shimer**, 367 U.S. 374, 381 (1961) (where Congress has created a regulatory program, courts should not defeat it by narrowing constructions inconsistent with the text and purpose); **Burlington Truck Lines, Inc. v. United States**, 371 U.S. 156, 167-68 (1962) (administrative action must rest on the governing statute’s actual terms and policy).

Fourth, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on Coast Guard authority over “uninspected vessels” is especially misplaced because limited authority is not exclusive authority. This Court has long rejected the notion that partial regulation ousts other federal law. In **Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. Public Service Commission**, 250 U.S. 566, 569 (1919), the Court held that overlapping regulation is not impermissible unless Congress so commands. In **Missouri, K. & T. Ry. Co. v. Haber**, 169 U.S. 613, 625 (1898), the Court likewise explained that separate statutory duties may coexist so long as each operates within its own field. And in **Federal Trade Commission v. Bunte Brothers, Inc.**, 312 U.S. 349, 352-53 (1941), the Court refused to infer exclusivity from a federal statute that addressed only part of the regulated subject. Those decisions foreclose the Fifth Circuit’s assumption that minimal vessel rules displace OSHA’s general workplace safety authority.

Finally, the decision below creates the very regulatory vacuum Congress sought to avoid. The Court of Appeals conceded that uninspected vessels are subject only to a handful of Coast Guard requirements, yet it held OSHA entirely powerless. That result is contrary to the principle that remedial statutes should not be read to leave worker protection to happenstance. See **United States v. A. M. Webb, Inc.**, 397 U.S. 179, 186 (1964) (statutes designed to protect workers should receive a construction consistent with that purpose); **Federal Trade Commission v. Mandel Brothers, Inc.**, 359 U.S. 385, 389 (1959) (regulatory statutes should not be narrowly construed to defeat their remedial ends). Congress enacted the OSH Act to assure “safe and healthful working conditions” for American workers, and nothing in the Coast Guard’s limited uninspected-vessel rules suggests a congressional choice to strip OSHA of jurisdiction altogether.

The judgment should be reversed.
