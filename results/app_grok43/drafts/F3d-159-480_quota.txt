A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Congress lacks authority under the Commerce Clause to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. The Clause empowers regulation only of activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 558-559 (1995). Section 1951 reaches purely local robberies without requiring proof that the offense itself affects commerce, exceeding this limit. See id. at 561.

The statute cannot be sustained as regulation of channels or instrumentalities of commerce. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 189-190 (1824). Nor does it regulate economic activity whose aggregate effect on commerce is substantial. Lopez, 514 U.S. at 560; United States v. Morrison, 529 U.S. 598, 610 (2000). Robbery is not commercial activity; it is a crime of violence whose connection to commerce is attenuated. Morrison, 529 U.S. at 612-613.

Precedents upholding broader regulation involved economic enterprises whose operations directly implicated interstate markets. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937); Wickard v. Filburn, 317 U.S. 111, 128-129 (1942). Section 1951 contains no such jurisdictional element tethering each prosecution to interstate commerce. Lopez, 514 U.S. at 562. The absence of that element distinguishes it from statutes upheld under the Clause. Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States, 379 U.S. 241, 258 (1964); Katzenbach v. McClung, 379 U.S. 294, 304 (1964).

The Hobbs Act's application to local robberies also contravenes the principle that Congress may not federalize traditional state crimes without a clear interstate nexus. United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349-350 (1971). Because the enactment reaches conduct whose effect on commerce is not substantial, it lies beyond congressional power. Morrison, 529 U.S. at 617-618; Perez v. United States, 402 U.S. 146, 150 (1971).
