# ARGUMENT

## I. Article III Confines Federal Courts to Live, Concrete Disputes, and a Bare Claim for a Token Sum Cannot Revive a Controversy Already Resolved.

The judicial power extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has long refused to render judgments that would amount to advisory opinions on questions no longer affecting the parties before it. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911). In *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), this Court explained that "the duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is to decide actual controversies by a judgment which can be carried into effect, and not to give opinions upon moot questions or abstract propositions, or to declare principles or rules of law which cannot affect the matter in issue in the case before it." *Id.* at 653. Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged Student Code provision, there was no live dispute left over the validity of rules that no longer governed anyone's conduct.

The touchstone for what remains justiciable after such intervening events is whether the case presents, in the words of *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227 (1937), "a real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts." *Id.* at 241. A demand for a nominal sum, wholly divorced from any pleaded actual injury, is precisely the kind of abstraction *Aetna* forecloses: it identifies no continuing legal relationship between the parties and seeks no relief beyond a symbolic label attached to a policy that has already been repealed.

## II. Claims Too Insubstantial to Reflect a Genuine Stake in the Outcome Do Not Preserve Jurisdiction Once the Underlying Dispute Is Resolved.

This Court has repeatedly dismissed cases as moot where the only thing technically remaining was a residual claim too slight to embody a genuine, ongoing dispute between adverse parties. In *California v. South Pacific Co.* (formerly *San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*), 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the State's suit to recover taxes became moot once the underlying obligation was released, notwithstanding a nominally surviving claim, because nothing of practical consequence remained to be adjudicated between the parties. And in *United States v. Alaska Steamship Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920), this Court held that when subsequent events destroy the practical significance of a dispute, the case is moot even though some technical vestige of the controversy persists on paper. *Id.* at 116.

The same logic controls here. Petitioners' request for nominal damages is precisely such a technical vestige. It survives the repeal of the challenged policies only in form, not in substance, because it is untethered to any concrete injury and seeks no relief capable of altering the legal relationship between the parties going forward.

## III. GGC's Voluntary Revision of the Challenged Policies Mooted This Case, and the Residual Nominal-Damages Request Cannot Restore a Live Controversy.

Where a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct and there is no reasonable expectation that the same controversy will recur, the case is moot. *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969). In *Golden*, this Court dismissed a challenge to an anonymous-handbill statute as moot because the legislator whose criticism had prompted the suit no longer held office, making it "most unlikely" the plaintiff would again confront the same restriction. *Id.* at 109. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held—and petitioners do not contest—that GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct mooted their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief. That same voluntary and complete abandonment of the offending provisions likewise strips the residual nominal-damages request of any live footing.

*St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), confirms that a technically surviving claim does not by itself preserve jurisdiction. There, this Court dismissed a contempt appeal as moot once the sentence had been served, because no substantial stake in the outcome remained notwithstanding lingering technical consequences. *Id.* at 42–43. By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court declined to find mootness because a genuine, substantial dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction project—an interest bearing directly on a real economic stake between the parties. *Id.* at 306 n.3. Nominal damages untethered from any pleaded actual injury are the inverse of the substantial cost dispute preserved in *Liner*; they are indistinguishable from the empty formalism this Court refused to entertain in *St. Pierre*.

Finally, this Court's settled practice when a civil case becomes moot while litigation is ongoing is to treat the underlying judgment as no longer subject to adjudication on the merits, precisely because no order the court could enter would affect the parties' actual rights. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). That principle applies with full force here: because the Prior Policies no longer exist, and because petitioners have alleged no actual injury flowing from them, a request for a token sum cannot substitute for the "real and substantial controversy" that Article III demands. *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 241.

## Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
