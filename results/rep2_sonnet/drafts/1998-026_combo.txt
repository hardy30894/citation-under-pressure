# ARGUMENT

A district court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not entitle him to habeas relief when the record establishes that the defendant possessed actual knowledge of that right. The writ of habeas corpus reaches only errors that resulted in actual prejudice to the petitioner; it does not provide a remedy for technical violations that had no effect on the outcome or on the defendant's exercise of his rights.

## I. Rule 32(a)(2)'s Purpose Is to Ensure Defendants Know of Their Appeal Rights, and That Purpose Was Fully Served Here

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure are not ends in themselves but instruments designed to secure substantive rights. As this Court has long recognized, procedural rules must be construed in light of their purpose rather than applied mechanically. *See Hormel v. Helvering*, 312 U.S. 552, 557 (1941) ("Rules of practice and procedure are devised to promote the ends of justice, not to defeat them."). Rule 32(a)(2)'s purpose is singular: to ensure that a defendant knows he has a right to appeal. Where a defendant already possesses that knowledge, the rule's animating purpose has been achieved regardless of the court's oversight.

This Court has consistently held that habeas corpus is reserved for defects that undermine the fundamental fairness or reliability of criminal proceedings. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), this Court held that a trial court's failure to comply with the requirement of Rule 32(a) that the defendant be personally addressed before sentencing did not provide a basis for collateral relief absent a showing of prejudice. The Court explained that "the failure of a trial court to ask a defendant represented by an attorney whether he has anything to say before sentence is imposed is not of itself an error of the character or magnitude cognizable under a writ of habeas corpus." *Id.* at 428. The same principle controls here: a technical omission in advising a defendant of a right he already knows does not rise to the level of a fundamental defect warranting collateral relief.

## II. The Harmless-Error Framework Applies to Rule 32(a)(2) Violations

This Court's decisions establish that even constitutional errors—let alone violations of procedural rules—do not automatically require reversal absent a showing of actual harm. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), this Court held that errors must be assessed for their actual effect, explaining that the inquiry is "not were they [the jurors] right in their judgment, regardless of the error or its effect upon the verdict. It is rather what effect the error had or reasonably may be taken to have had upon the jury's decision." *Id.* at 764. Where, as here, the defendant's own knowledge rendered the court's omission inconsequential, no such effect can be shown.

Similarly, in *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85 (1963), this Court reaffirmed that the harmless-error inquiry asks "whether there is a reasonable possibility that the evidence complained of might have contributed to the conviction." *Id.* at 86–87. Petitioner cannot make this showing, for the record demonstrates he was already aware of his right to appeal and elected, for reasons independent of any judicial omission, not to exercise it.

## III. Habeas Relief Requires a Showing of Actual Prejudice

The bedrock principle animating collateral review is that relief is unavailable absent demonstrated prejudice. In *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), this Court emphasized that habeas corpus is not a substitute for direct appeal and does not lie to correct every trial error, particularly where the petitioner suffered no actual harm. The Court noted the "inroads" that expansive collateral review would make upon "the concept of finality" that is essential to the criminal justice system. *Id.* at 179.

This principle finds further support in *Bator, Finality in Criminal Law and Federal Habeas Corpus for State Prisoners*, though this Court's own decisions in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), confirm that even fundamental constitutional claims raised on habeas require some showing that the error affected the proceeding. While *Fay* expanded the availability of habeas review, it did not abandon the requirement that the error complained of have some cognizable effect on the petitioner's rights.

Moreover, in *Machibroda v. United States*, 368 U.S. 487 (1962), this Court held that relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255—the statutory analog to habeas corpus for federal prisoners—requires a showing that the alleged defect resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice. *Id.* at 493. No such miscarriage occurred here: Petitioner knew of his appellate rights, and the court's technical omission caused no loss of any opportunity he would otherwise have exercised.

In sum, the consistent thread running through this Court's jurisprudence—from *Hormel* through *Hill*, *Kotteakos*, *Fahy*, *Sunal*, and *Machibroda*—is that procedural rules serve substantive ends, and violations that do not defeat those ends do not warrant the extraordinary remedy of habeas corpus. Because Petitioner indisputably knew of his right to appeal, Rule 32(a)(2)'s purpose was satisfied notwithstanding the district court's omission. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
