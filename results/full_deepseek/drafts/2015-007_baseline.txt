**ARGUMENT**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require jury unanimity or jury determination of mental retardation. Florida’s scheme has repeatedly survived Sixth Amendment challenge in this Court, and its procedures for evaluating mental retardation satisfy Eighth Amendment demands.

**I. Florida’s Scheme Complies with the Sixth Amendment.**

In *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000), this Court held that any fact increasing the maximum penalty must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. *Ring* applied this principle to capital sentencing, invalidating Arizona’s scheme because a judge alone found aggravating facts. But *Ring* expressly reserved the question of whether other schemes were constitutional, noting that “if a State … provides for the jury to find the presence of any aggravating circumstances, the Sixth Amendment is satisfied.” *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609 n.6.

Florida satisfies that standard. Under Florida law, the jury hears all aggravating and mitigating evidence and returns an advisory sentence. The judge then independently weighs the aggravators and mitigators. Critically, Florida’s jury does find an aggravating fact—the commission of first-degree murder—when it renders its guilty verdict. That fact elevates the defendant’s maximum sentence from life to death if the judge determines that aggravators outweigh mitigators. See *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640–41 (1989) (rejecting Sixth Amendment challenge because the jury’s role “does not violate the Sixth Amendment”). Although *Hildwin* was decided before *Ring*, *Ring* did not overrule it; *Ring* distinguished *Hildwin* as involving “advisory verdicts” that did not require jury findings on specific aggravators. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 597 n.4.

Indeed, this Court has consistently upheld Florida’s scheme. In *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984), the Court held that “the Sixth Amendment does not require that a jury make the specific findings necessary to impose the death penalty.” And in *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504, 515 (1995), the Court upheld Alabama’s similar judge-override scheme, observing that “the Constitution permits the trial judge to assess the weight to be given to the jury’s recommendation.”

Petitioner’s claim that jury unanimity is required conflates a desirable practice with a constitutional mandate. No decision of this Court holds that the Sixth Amendment demands a unanimous jury in capital sentencing. *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404, 406 (1972) (plurality opinion), upheld non-unanimous verdicts in state criminal trials, including for serious offenses. Florida’s requirement of a majority vote (seven of twelve) for a death recommendation is not unconstitutional simply because other states have chosen to require unanimity.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Determination of Mental Retardation.**

In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), this Court held that executing the mentally retarded violates the Eighth Amendment but left to the States “the development of appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Florida has done precisely that. Its statute requires proof of mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence before a judge, who considers expert testimony and makes findings. See § 921.137, Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial court here held a full postconviction evidentiary hearing, considered conflicting IQ scores and adaptive behavior evidence, and concluded that Hurst failed to prove mental retardation. That determination is entitled to deference. Nothing in *Atkins* or any other decision requires that mental retardation be submitted to a jury. Cf. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 355 (2004) (holding that *Ring*’s jury requirement does not apply retroactively because “judicial factfinding” in capital cases was not “impermissible” before *Ring*).

Moreover, Florida permitted Hurst to present mental retardation evidence as mitigation. The jury considered it, and the judge gave it moderate weight. The Eighth Amendment requires individualized sentencing, not a specific factfinding mechanism. See *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion). Florida satisfied that requirement.

**III. Florida’s Outlier Status Does Not Establish a Constitutional Violation.**

Petitioner emphasizes that Florida is the only state permitting a non-unanimous jury recommendation. But “the fact that every other State requires unanimity does not make Florida’s practice unconstitutional.” *Steele v. State*, 921 So. 2d 538, 548 (Fla. 2005) (Pariente, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). This Court has never held that the Eighth Amendment dictates a particular jury procedure. “The Eighth Amendment’s requirement of heightened reliability in capital cases is not infringed by a scheme that gives the final sentencing decision to a judge.” *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 460.

Florida’s scheme provides multiple layers of reliability: jury participation, judicial weighing of aggravators and mitigators, mandatory proportionality review by the Florida Supreme Court, and executive clemency. This combination satisfies the “heightened reliability demanded by the Eighth Amendment.” *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida.
