The petitioners' challenge to Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges is without merit. The Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) does not apply to appointed state judges because they are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).

The ADEA's exemption for "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" is not limited to the executive and legislative branches. The term "policymaking level" refers to the exercise of discretion and judgment, which is a hallmark of judging. As the Court has recognized, judges "make law" in the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents. See Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (recognizing that judges "make law" in certain circumstances).

The policymaking role of judges is not limited to their adjudicative functions. Many of Missouri's appointed judges exercise considerable supervisory power, such as the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, who has general supervisory powers over the Missouri court system. See Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 119-20 (1925) (recognizing the importance of judicial discretion and the role of judges in shaping the law).

The legislative history of the ADEA exemptions supports a broad interpretation of the "policymaking level" exception. See United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542-43 (1940) (emphasizing the importance of considering legislative history in statutory interpretation). The exemptions were intended to apply to high-level officials, including those in the judicial branch.

The petitioners' equal protection challenge to the mandatory retirement requirement also fails. The rational basis test applies to age-based classifications, and Missouri's requirement is rationally related to legitimate state interests. See Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483, 488-89 (1955) (upholding a state law under the rational basis test). The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its judges are capable of performing their duties, and a mandatory retirement age is a rational means of achieving this goal. See Railway Express Agency v. New York, 336 U.S. 106, 109-10 (1949) (recognizing that a state has a legitimate interest in regulating its judiciary).

The Court has consistently upheld age-based classifications in the context of public employment. See McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961) (upholding a state law that prohibited certain activities on Sundays, including the sale of goods). The mandatory retirement requirement is not arbitrary or capricious, and it is not subject to strict scrutiny. See Ferguson v. Skrupa, 372 U.S. 726, 732 (1963) (rejecting a substantive due process challenge to a state law).

In conclusion, the Missouri Constitution's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause. The ADEA's exemption for "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" applies to appointed state judges, and the mandatory retirement requirement is rationally related to legitimate state interests. The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.

The Court's decisions in cases such as Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602 (1935), and Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926), demonstrate the importance of considering the role of judges in the context of public employment. These cases highlight the need to balance individual rights with the interests of the state in regulating its judiciary. See also Sweezy v. New Hampshire, 354 U.S. 234, 255-56 (1957) (recognizing the importance of judicial independence). 

Additionally, the Court has long recognized the importance of state sovereignty and the need to respect state laws and regulations. See Coyle v. Oklahoma, 221 U.S. 559, 573 (1911) (recognizing the importance of state sovereignty). Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement is a legitimate exercise of its sovereignty, and it should be upheld.
