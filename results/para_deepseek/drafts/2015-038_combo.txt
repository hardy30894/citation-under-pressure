ARGUMENT

I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT BAR NPS FROM REGULATING NON-FEDERAL LANDS WITHIN PARK UNITS.

The relevant provision states that no regulation “shall apply solely to public lands within [conservation system] units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). NPS’s hovercraft ban applies to all lands and waters within the Yukon-Charley Preserve, not “solely to public lands.” As this Court has long instructed, “[i]t is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Here, the phrase “solely to public lands” unambiguously limits only those regulations that operate *only* on federal property. A ban that governs an entire preserve—including state-owned submerged lands—falls outside that prohibition.

The canon *expressio unius est exclusio alterius* reinforces this reading. Congress specifically restricted only regulations “applicable solely to public lands,” leaving untouched regulations applicable to all lands. See *United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R.*, 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929) (“[W]here a statute enumerates the things upon which it is to operate, it is to be construed as excluding from its operation all those not expressly mentioned.”). Because NPS’s regulation reaches beyond public lands, § 103(c) does not restrict it.

II. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT POSSESSES SOVEREIGN AUTHORITY OVER NAVIGABLE WATERS AND ADJACENT LANDS WITHIN PARK UNITS.

Even if the submerged lands beneath the Nation River are owned by Alaska, the Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate activities on navigable waters. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1, 196 (1824) (Congress may regulate “every species of commercial intercourse” on navigable waters). This authority extends to enforcing environmental and safety regulations, such as the hovercraft ban, on all waters within a federal preserve. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870) (navigable waters are “subject to the control of Congress for purposes of commerce”).

Moreover, the federal navigational servitude ensures that state title to submerged lands is “subject to the paramount power of the United States to control the waters for the purpose of commerce.” *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967). That servitude justifies NPS’s regulation of hovercraft on the Nation River, irrespective of state ownership. *Accord United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“The title of the State to the lands beneath navigable waters is subject to the power of Congress to regulate commerce.”).

III. ANILCA § 1319 PRESERVES NPS’S EXISTING REGULATORY AUTHORITY.

Congress explicitly provided that “[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing . . . existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. The NPS Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1 *et seq.*, grants the Secretary broad authority to “make and publish such rules and regulations as he may deem necessary or proper for the use and management of the parks.” The hovercraft ban is a proper exercise of that authority. This Court has long recognized that Congress may regulate public lands as it sees fit, see *United States v. Gratiot*, 39 U.S. 526, 534 (1840) (Congress has “the plenary power . . . to dispose of [public lands] as they may deem proper”), and that power extends to protecting the resources of federal enclaves, see *Fort Leavenworth R.R. Co. v. Lowe*, 114 U.S. 525, 537 (1885) (power to regulate activities within federal territory).

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT NPS’S REGULATION IS VALID.

Sturgeon’s interpretation would create an anomalous patchwork of regulatory authority, allowing conduct on state-owned lands that undermines park purposes. Because the plain text of § 103(c) does not support his view, this Court should respect Congress’s language. As *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 95 (1820) instructs, “where the language is clear, and the intention of the legislature is plainly expressed, there is no room for construction.” The statute is clear: regulations that do not apply “solely to public lands” are not barred.

Thus, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
