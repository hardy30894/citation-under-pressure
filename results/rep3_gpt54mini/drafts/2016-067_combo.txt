Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA when it collects debts it owns for its own account. The statute’s text, structure, and purpose all point in that direction. As the Court explained in *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 817 F.3d 131, 133-35 (4th Cir. 2016), the FDCPA “generally does not regulate creditors, when they collect debt on their own account,” and a purchaser of defaulted debt who collects for itself is ordinarily a creditor, not a debt collector. That is the correct reading of 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).

The Act defines “debt collector” principally as one “who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due ... another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The ordinary meaning of “another” is someone other than oneself. See *The Pizarro*, 15 U.S. (2 Wheat.) 227, 230 (1817); *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95-96 (1820). Congress used that ordinary distinction throughout the statute. A “creditor” is “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). A company that purchases a debt and then seeks payment for itself falls squarely within that definition. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 134-35; *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314-16 (11th Cir. 2015); *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1208-10 (9th Cir. 2013).

Petitioners’ contrary reading would rewrite the statute to turn on whether a debt was in default when acquired. But the FDCPA does not say that. Courts must give effect to the words Congress chose, not words it omitted. *United States v. Lacher*, 134 U.S. 624, 628 (1890); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917); *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926); *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) at 95-96. As *Henson* held, “the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition” in § 1692a(6). 817 F.3d at 135. The same conclusion follows from the statutory structure: the default-based exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) operates only after a person first falls within the general definition of “debt collector.” *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 134-35; *Commissioner v. Keystone Consol. Indus., Inc.*, 508 U.S. 152, 159 (1993).

The FDCPA’s purpose confirms that reading. Congress enacted the statute “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e), not to regulate every entity that seeks payment of a debt it owns. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 136; *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1315-18. A creditor collecting on its own account is not acting as an intermediary for “another,” and thus is not engaging in the sort of third-party collection conduct Congress targeted. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 133-36; *Schlegel*, 720 F.3d at 1209-10.

Petitioners’ position also conflicts with the canon against surplusage. Their reading would collapse the distinction between “creditor” and “debt collector,” making the creditor definition do little work. Courts avoid interpretations that render statutory distinctions meaningless. *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883); *D. Ginsberg & Sons, Inc. v. Popkin*, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932); *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (though later, consistent with earlier canons). Congress instead drew a clear line: those collecting for others are covered; those collecting their own debts are generally not. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 294 (1995); *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 135-36.  

Finally, petitioners’ reliance on the fact that the loans were in default when purchased proves too much. Default does not change who owns the debt at the time of collection. The FDCPA regulates conduct “in connection with the collection of any debt,” 15 U.S.C. § 1692e, and liability depends on the defendant’s status when the challenged conduct occurs. *Henson*, 817 F.3d at 136-37; *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318. Here, Santander purchased the receivables and then attempted to collect them for itself. Under the statute, that makes Santander a creditor, not a debt collector. The judgment should be affirmed.
