# ARGUMENT

## SECTION 1404(a) DOES NOT AUTHORIZE A TRANSFEREE COURT TO ASSIGN A MULTIDISTRICT CASE TO ITSELF FOR TRIAL

### A. The Plain Text of Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand

Section 1407(a) provides that any action transferred to a district for coordinated pretrial proceedings "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This Court has repeatedly instructed that "shall" is the language of command. *Lexecon* presents a straightforward question of statutory construction that must begin, and here end, with the statute's text. *See Consumer Product Safety Comm'n v. GTE Sylvania, Inc.*, 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980) ("[T]he starting point for interpreting a statute is the language of the statute itself.").

The word "shall" in § 1407(a) admits no exception for cases the transferee judge finds convenient to keep. Congress gave the Panel, not individual district judges, the authority to determine when pretrial proceedings have concluded and remand is due. Nothing in the statute's text permits a transferee court to short-circuit that remand obligation by invoking § 1404(a) to transfer the case to itself.

### B. Section 1404(a)'s "Any Civil Action" Language Does Not Displace the Mandatory Remand Requirement

Respondent contends that § 1404(a), which allows a district court to transfer "any civil action" to another district "for the convenience of parties and witnesses," 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a), permits a transferee court to transfer a case to itself. But self-transfer is not transfer at all in any ordinary sense of the word. As the Ninth Circuit majority itself acknowledged, the practice exists only because appellate courts adopted it "despite what appears to be a clear statutory mandate." *Lexecon Inc. v. United States Dist. Court*, 61 F.3d 911 (9th Cir. 1995) (mem.).

Judge Kozinski's dissent below correctly identified the doctrinal lineage of this error, tracing it to *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124–25 (2d Cir. 1971), which rejected the statutory command "as absurd" on policy grounds rather than textual analysis. *Id.* at 917 (Kozinski, J., dissenting). Even the district court in *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971), which endorsed self-transfer, candidly admitted "there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial." As Judge Kozinski observed, courts adopting this practice "simply disregarded the statutory mandate," *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 917, and "have simply taken their place at the head of an ever-growing string cite," *id.*

This Court has cautioned against construing statutes to reach outcomes their text does not support, however administratively convenient those outcomes may seem. *See Board of Governors, FRS v. Dimension Financial Corp.*, 474 U.S. 361, 374 (1986) ("Committee reports, floor debate, and even the drafts of committee members are not the law... we do not resort to legislative history to cloud a statutory text that is clear."). Section 1407(a)'s mandate is not clouded; it is unambiguous.

### C. The Statutory Scheme Confirms That Pretrial and Trial Authority Are Distinct

Section 1407(a) authorizes transfer "for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings" only. The statute's careful limitation to "pretrial proceedings"—repeated twice in the same sentence—demonstrates that Congress contemplated a temporally and functionally bounded grant of authority. As Judge Kozinski explained, "[p]retrial proceedings under 1407 are obviously limited to those raising issues common to other cases, primarily discovery; that's the entire point of the multidistrict process." *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 918 (Kozinski, J., dissenting).

Permitting a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial converts a temporary, discovery-focused assignment into a permanent divestiture of the transferor court's authority—precisely what § 1407(a)'s mandatory remand provision was designed to prevent. This reading harmonizes the two statutes rather than allowing § 1404(a)'s general transfer authority to swallow § 1407(a)'s specific remand command. *Cf. Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 550–51 (1974) (specific statutory provisions control over general ones addressing the same subject).

### D. Permitting Self-Transfer Undermines the Policy Choices Embedded in the Statute

Section 1407(a) reflects Congress's considered judgment that consolidation for pretrial efficiency should not come at the cost of a plaintiff's chosen forum for trial. As Judge Kozinski noted, "[c]ases may be transferred to remote locations, but only for the duration of pretrial procedures. When it comes to conducting a trial... plaintiffs retain their ancient right to select the forum." *Lexecon*, 61 F.3d at 918. Petitioner Lexecon filed suit in the Northern District of Illinois; it did not choose Arizona, and its presence there was purely a function of coordinated discovery obligations under § 1407(a).

Allowing transferee courts to self-transfer converts every MDL assignment into a potential permanent forum change, contrary to the remand mandate Congress enacted. Such a result cannot be squared with the statutory text, and mere administrative convenience does not license courts to disregard unambiguous congressional commands. *See TVA v. Hill*, 437 U.S. 153, 194 (1978) ("[C]ourts are not free to rewrite [a] statutory scheme.").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois as § 1407(a) requires.
