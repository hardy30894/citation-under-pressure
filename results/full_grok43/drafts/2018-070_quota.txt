The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States as surrogate federal law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” This text requires a gap in federal coverage before state law may be borrowed. Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (holding that OCSLA “define[s] a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the OCS). Congress enacted the statute precisely because “federal law standing alone would be inadequate.” Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue, 488 U.S. 19, 27 n.8 (1988) (quoting 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953)).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly confirmed that state law fills only those interstices left by federal statutes. Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97, 101 (1971) (state personal-injury limitations period applied because “federal law does not provide a limitations period”); see also United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293 (1958) (assimilative statutes operate only where Congress has not “preempt[ed] the field”). The Fifth Circuit has applied the same rule to wage-and-hour and maritime claims arising on the OCS. Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (“necessity to fill a significant void or gap”); Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Eng’g, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043, 1050 (5th Cir. 1990) (state law applies only if federal law is “silent”).

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a comprehensive scheme of minimum-wage, overtime, and meal-period regulation that leaves no such void. 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207, 213. Its savings clause, id. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws only where Congress has not occupied the field; it does not authorize their incorporation under OCSLA. Chrysler Corp. v. Brown, 441 U.S. 281, 295–96 (1979) (properly promulgated federal regulations carry the force of law and preempt contrary state rules). Because the FLSA already governs the precise subject matter of Newton’s claims, California wage-and-hour statutes are neither “applicable” nor needed to fill any gap. Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP, 604 F.3d 1126, 1130 (9th Cir. 2010) (OCSLA jurisdiction is “horizontal” and limited to the seabed and fixed structures).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent”—would convert OCSLA into a general choice-of-law statute rather than a gap-filling mechanism. That construction contradicts the statute’s text, its legislative history, and the uniform approach of the Supreme Court and the Fifth Circuit. The district court therefore correctly held that the FLSA precludes resort to California law on the OCS.
