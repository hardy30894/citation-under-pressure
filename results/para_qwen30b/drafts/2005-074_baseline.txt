**ARGUMENT**

I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE, 8 U.S.C. § 1232(a)(5), IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE WHEN APPLIED TO PETITIONER’S PRIOR DEPORTATION ORDER ISSUED BEFORE THE STATUTE’S ENACTMENT.

The central question before this Court is whether Congress may apply the reinstatement provision of the Immigration and Nationality Act—Section 241(a)(5)—to an alien who re-entered the United States *before* the statute’s enactment in 1997, thereby stripping him of any right to seek relief under the INA. The answer is no. To hold otherwise would violate the fundamental principle that laws cannot be applied retroactively to impair settled expectations, especially where such application results in the loss of a protected liberty interest.

This Court has long recognized that “a law which operates retroactively, and thus impairs rights clearly existing when the law was passed, is invalid.” *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). In *Landgraf*, the Court held that “Congress must make clear its intent to apply a new statute to events occurring before its enactment,” id. at 280, because “retroactive application of a statute may deprive individuals of rights they had previously enjoyed.” Id. at 265. Here, Fernandez-Vargas entered the United States illegally in 1982, was deported in 1981, and lived in the country for nearly two decades thereafter—until his arrest in 2001. During that time, he married a U.S. citizen and sought to adjust his status, a process that implicitly relied upon the possibility of lawful residence and relief under the INA. That expectation was not merely speculative; it was reasonable and foreseeable.

At the time of his re-entry in 1982, no law existed authorizing the automatic reinstatement of a prior deportation order. Indeed, under pre-IIRIRA law, aliens who re-entered unlawfully could still apply for relief—including adjustment of status—unless specifically barred by another provision. See *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 437 (1987) (“An alien who has been ordered removed may, under certain circumstances, apply for asylum or withholding of removal.”). Thus, Fernandez-Vargas reasonably believed that, despite his unlawful presence, he might one day qualify for permanent residence through marriage. This belief was not irrational—it was grounded in the legal framework that existed during his entire period of residence.

When Congress enacted Section 241(a)(5) in 1997, it did so without explicit language indicating retroactive application. The statute provides: “If an alien who has been ordered removed… reenters the United States… the prior order of removal shall be reinstated without further hearing.” 8 U.S.C. § 1232(a)(5). But nowhere does it state that this provision applies to orders issued *before* its effective date. Absent such clarity, we must presume Congress did not intend retroactive effect. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

Moreover, the consequences of applying the statute retroactively are severe. It strips Fernandez-Vargas of all avenues for relief—including adjustment of status—based solely on a prior deportation order that predated the law itself. This is precisely the kind of “impairment of rights” that *Landgraf* warned against. As the Court explained: “A law that alters the legal consequences of acts completed before its enactment… is retroactive.” Id. at 265. By denying Fernandez-Vargas any opportunity to seek relief based on his marriage to a U.S. citizen—a form of relief that was available at the time he acted—the statute effectively penalizes him for conduct that was lawful under the rules then in force.

The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that Fernandez-Vargas lacked a “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status. That conclusion ignores the very nature of due process protections in immigration law. An alien who has lived in the United States for years, built a family, and taken steps toward lawful status has a legitimate reliance interest in the continued availability of those pathways. To deny that interest simply because Congress later changed the rules is to allow the government to rewrite the terms of justice after the fact—an outcome this Court has consistently rejected.

II. CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY INTEND THE REINSTATEMENT PROVISION TO APPLY TO PRE-ENACTMENT ORDERS.

Even if the statute were not impermissibly retroactive, it must fail because Congress did not clearly intend it to apply to cases like Fernandez-Vargas’. The text of Section 241(a)(5) contains no reference to prior orders, and its legislative history reveals no such intent. The statute was designed to streamline removal proceedings for aliens who re-enter after being removed, but it was not meant to nullify the entire body of immigration law governing relief for those who had already established ties to the United States.

In *United States v. Lopez*, 462 U.S. 1018 (1983), the Court emphasized that “statutes should be construed to avoid constitutional doubts,” including those arising from retroactivity. Applying that principle here, we must interpret the reinstatement provision narrowly—especially given its drastic consequences. If Congress intended to strip aliens of all relief based on pre-existing orders, it would have said so explicitly. Instead, it chose silence.

The absence of clear intent is fatal. As this Court held in *Landgraf*: “Where a statute is silent about its temporal reach, courts must assume that it does not operate retroactively.” 511 U.S. at 280. The Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding—that Congress “intended” the statute to apply to pre-enactment orders—rests on speculation, not textual or legislative support. No provision in IIRIRA or its legislative history suggests that Congress meant to revoke the right to adjust status for millions of long-term residents who re-entered before 1997.
