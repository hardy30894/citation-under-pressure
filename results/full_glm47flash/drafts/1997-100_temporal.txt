The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states by the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a second prosecution for the "same offense." *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). Here, the State seeks to impose a longer sentence based on a prior conviction allegation. This constitutes a second prosecution for the "same offense" because the sentence is the "offense" for which the defendant was convicted. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957).

The Double Jeopardy Clause is a limitation on the power to impose a sentence. *United States v. Martin Linen Supply Co.*, 390 U.S. 393, 398 (1968). The Court in *Martin Linen* held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing because the sentence is the "offense." The Court stated that the Double Jeopardy Clause "is a limitation on the power to impose a sentence." *Id.* at 398. Therefore, the State cannot retry the prior conviction allegation after an acquittal on that specific issue without violating the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The California Supreme Court relied on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to argue that due process does not require a trial in sentencing. However, *Williams* involved a judge's personal knowledge, not a formal determination of a prior conviction by a court or jury. The Double Jeopardy Clause requires more than mere due process; it requires that the State not subject a defendant to a second trial for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Pearce*, the Court held that a defendant has a right to a jury trial in sentencing if the sentence is a matter of life or death, but the underlying principle is that the State cannot increase the penalty after acquittal without violating the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings. The State cannot retry the prior conviction allegation.
