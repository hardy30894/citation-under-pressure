ARGUMENT

Bond has no standing to press a Tenth Amendment objection to her conviction. The Supreme Court long ago held that private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to defeat federal law absent a cognizable injury to a protected state interest. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939), the Court rejected utility companies’ attempt to challenge federal action as an intrusion upon state sovereignty, explaining that “the [utilities], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That rule is grounded in the structure of the Constitution, which allocates the assertion of state sovereign interests to the States themselves, not to criminal defendants unhappy with their prosecution.

This Court should follow Tennessee Electric Power Co. and the Court’s other pre-1970 federalism cases. In Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips v. Atkinson Co., 313 U.S. 508, 534 (1941), the Court reiterated that a private litigant cannot litigate generalized federalism objections divorced from a State’s own asserted interests. Likewise, in Arizona v. California, 283 U.S. 423, 451 (1931), the Court recognized that federalism claims belong to sovereign parties with a direct stake in the allocation of governmental power. Bond, by contrast, is not the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, nor does she assert any injury other than her own criminal conviction.

The standing defect is especially clear because Bond seeks to convert a merits argument into a jurisdictional one. But standing turns on whether the litigant is the proper party to invoke the court’s power, not on whether the challenged statute may be unconstitutional in some abstract sense. Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 486-89 (1923), refused to entertain a generalized complaint about federal action where the asserted injury was too remote and public in nature. Frothingham v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 487-89 (1923), similarly held that a private taxpayer may not litigate a structural constitutional objection shared in common with the public at large. Bond’s Tenth Amendment theory is no different: she complains that Congress legislated too far into an area she believes should have remained within state criminal law, but that grievance is not a personal constitutional injury that confers standing.

Nor does Bond gain standing simply because she is criminally prosecuted. The Court has repeatedly distinguished between a defendant’s ability to defend against the charge actually brought and a generalized objection to the distribution of sovereign power. In Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes, 302 U.S. 464, 480-81 (1938), the Court held that a plaintiff lacks standing where the alleged harm is only the indirect consequence of government action on others. Bond’s claim is even weaker: she does not allege that § 229 inflicts a distinct constitutional injury on her apart from the fact that it supplied the basis for her prosecution. That is insufficient under longstanding standing principles.

Bond’s reliance on the Tenth Amendment also ignores that the Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered.” United States v. Sprague, 282 U.S. 716, 733 (1931). The question whether Congress validly enacted § 229 under the Treaty Power and Necessary and Proper Clause is a merits question about federal authority, not a standing question. A litigant cannot manufacture standing merely by repackaging disagreement with federal supremacy as a Tenth Amendment claim. See United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (Tenth Amendment “is but a truism” that the States retain powers not delegated); Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 297 U.S. 288, 341-56 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (courts avoid unnecessary constitutional decisions); Colegrove v. Green, 328 U.S. 549, 552-56 (1946) (courts should not entertain generalized grievances).

Finally, even if Bond could identify some abstract structural concern, she remains the wrong party to assert it. The Court has consistently required a concrete, personal stake in order to invoke judicial power. See Tennessee Electric Power Co., 306 U.S. at 144; Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. at 487; Frothingham v. Mellon, 262 U.S. at 487-89; Alabama Power Co., 302 U.S. at 480-81; Sprague, 282 U.S. at 733. Bond’s alleged injury is simply that she was convicted under a federal criminal statute. That gives her standing to contest whether the statute applies to her or whether the evidence supported conviction, but not to assert the State’s prerogative to prosecute first. The judgment should therefore be affirmed on standing grounds alone.
