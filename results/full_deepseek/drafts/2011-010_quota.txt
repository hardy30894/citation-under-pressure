**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer continental shelf itself. The plain text, Supreme Court precedent, and uniform Fifth Circuit authority compel this conclusion.

**I. The Statutory Text and Supreme Court Precedent Require a Situs-of-Injury Test**

Section 1333(b) provides coverage for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies the injury. The Supreme Court has consistently held that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). In *Tallentire*, the Court declined to extend OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision to an accident on waters above the shelf, emphasizing that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale.” *Id.* The Court specifically noted that § 1333(b) superimposes a status requirement on the “otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. This considered dictum reflects the Court’s understanding that a situs requirement applies to § 1333(b). *See United States v. Montero-Camargo*, 208 F.3d 1122, 1132 n.17 (9th Cir. 2000) (en banc) (treating Supreme Court dicta with “greater weight and deference”).

The Court’s decision in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray* (Herb’s Welding I), 470 U.S. 414 (1985), reinforces this geographic limitation. There, the Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* at 423. The Court noted that OCSLA “draws a clear geographic line” and that coverage depends on “the location of the injury.” *Id.* at 424. This language confirms that the situs of injury is determinative.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s En Banc Decision in *Mills* Correctly Adopts a Situs-of-Injury Test**

The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, adopted the situs-of-injury test in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). The court held that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the shelf. *Id.* at 362. This holding is consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance and with OCSLA’s structure, which is geographically limited to the shelf. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365-66 (1969) (OCSLA applies to “artificial islands and fixed structures” on the shelf).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this situs requirement. In *Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009), the court reaffirmed that OCSLA coverage requires injury on the shelf. In *Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc.*, 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001), the court denied coverage for a helicopter crash on land. In *Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc.*, 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998), the court required injury on the shelf. And in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray* (Herb’s Welding II), 766 F.2d 898, 900 (5th Cir. 1985), the court held that an injury on a platform in state waters is not covered.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Conflicts with Uniform Authority**

The Ninth Circuit rejected the situs test and adopted a “substantial nexus” test, but that test is vague and unworkable. It ignores the clear geographic limitation that Congress built into OCSLA. The Third Circuit’s decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), which applied a “but for” test, is also flawed. *Curtis* failed to give proper weight to the Supreme Court’s dictum in *Tallentire* and to the overall structure of OCSLA. The “substantial nexus” test would extend coverage to any injury with a remote connection to shelf operations, undermining the statute’s geographic focus.

**IV. The Legislative History Supports a Situs Requirement**

Congress intended OCSLA to provide a uniform legal regime for the shelf, not to extend coverage to onshore injuries. The “substantial nexus” test would extend coverage to any injury with a remote connection to shelf operations, undermining the statute’s geographic focus.

**V. Application to This Case**

Juan Valladolid was killed on land at an onshore processing facility. He was not injured on the outer continental shelf. Therefore, his widow is not entitled to OCSLA benefits. The Benefits Review Board correctly denied her claim.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
