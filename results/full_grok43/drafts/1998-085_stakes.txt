ARGUMENT

I. Title VII Requires Egregious or Outrageous Conduct for Punitive Damages.

Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only if the plaintiff demonstrates that the respondent engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language incorporates the common-law standard that punitive damages are available only for conduct involving “evil motive or reckless indifference to the rights of others.” Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 46 (1983) (quoting Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979)). The statute therefore demands more than intentional discrimination; it requires conduct that is independently egregious or outrageous. See, e.g., Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co., 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987) (punitive damages under § 1981 require that “the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application”).

Every circuit to address the question under § 1981a before this case held that punitive damages require proof of egregious conduct beyond the underlying discrimination. See, e.g., Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (award “must surmount a higher hurdle” than intentional discrimination); Kientzy v. McDonnell Douglas Corp., 990 F.2d 1051, 1062 (8th Cir. 1993) (same under § 1981). The legislative history confirms this understanding. See H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 72 (1991) (requiring “an even higher standard” than proof of intentional discrimination).

Kolstad’s evidence showed only that ADA officials preselected a male candidate and offered shifting explanations. Such facts may support a finding of pretext, see St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993), but they do not establish the “extraordinarily egregious” conduct Congress required for punitive damages. 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (daily ed. Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). The district court therefore correctly refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages.

II. The Court of Appeals Erred in Ordering a New Trial on Punitive Damages.

Because the record contains no evidence of malice or reckless indifference rising to the level of egregious or outrageous conduct, the judgment denying punitive damages should be affirmed.
