## Argument

### I. Rule 23 does not permit certification based on a damages theory unsupported by admissible evidence showing classwide measurement.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires more than a plausible story; the district court must find, by a preponderance of the evidence, that common issues predominate. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 316–20 (3d Cir. 2008). That inquiry is “rigorous,” and it “may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits” when necessary to decide whether the Rule 23 requirements are satisfied. *Id.* at 317, 318; *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 166, 168 (3d Cir. 2001). The court may not certify a class on assumptions or unsupported expert assertions; it must resolve factual disputes relevant to certification and determine whether the proponent has carried its burden with competent proof. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 320; *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 351–52 (2011).

That requirement is especially important where damages are central to predominance. A class may not be certified if liability or damages will require individualized proof that overwhelms common questions. *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623–24 (1997). Nor may a court presume that the existence of some common injury theory satisfies Rule 23(b)(3) without examining whether the proposed method can measure damages attributable to the certified theory. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 35–36 (2013).

### II. The Third Circuit’s approach, and the decision below, allowed certification without a proper fit between the damages model and the certified theory of liability.

The Third Circuit held that Plaintiffs needed only to show “a common methodology to quantify damages on a class-wide basis,” and it affirmed certification even though Comcast challenged whether the model measured damages attributable to the sole theory the district court certified. That was error. Rule 23 requires a damages model that measures only those damages “capable of proof through evidence common to the class,” not a model untethered to the particular theory of antitrust impact the court allowed to proceed. *Comcast Corp.*, 569 U.S. at 35–36.

This Court rejected the same approach in *Comcast*. There, as here, the district court certified a class after finding that plaintiffs had a common methodology for classwide damages. *Id.* at 31–32. This Court held that such a finding cannot stand where the model “identifies damages that are not the result of the injury upon which liability in this action is premised.” *Id.* at 35. Because the model must be consistent with the liability theory actually certified, a court abuses its discretion when it accepts a damages methodology without determining that it can measure damages attributable to the alleged antitrust impact on a classwide basis. *Id.* at 35–36.

The Third Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *Wal-Mart*. There, the Court explained that Rule 23 demands “significant proof” and that certification cannot rest on a district court’s acceptance of allegations that are not supported by admissible evidence. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350–52. A district court therefore may not certify a damages class on the basis of expert testimony that is speculative, disconnected from the certified theory, or incapable of common application. *Id.* at 357; *Comcast Corp.*, 569 U.S. at 35.

### III. Antitrust law does not relax Rule 23’s evidentiary burden.

Petitioners’ case arises under the Sherman Act, but antitrust claims still must satisfy Rule 23. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the antitrust laws do not create an exception to ordinary procedural and evidentiary requirements. *City of Lafayette v. Louisiana Power & Light Co.*, 435 U.S. 389, 394–95 (1978); *Community Communications Co. v. City of Boulder*, 455 U.S. 40, 50–51 (1982). And although antitrust plaintiffs often rely on common proof of market conduct, this Court has never held that certification may proceed without admissible evidence that damages can be measured consistently across the class. *Comcast Corp.*, 569 U.S. at 34–36.

Nor does the nature of the cable market alter the Rule 23 inquiry. While Congress has recognized that cable operators often possess local monopoly power, that background confirms the importance of exacting scrutiny, not the relaxation of certification standards. *Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC*, 512 U.S. 622, 661–62 (1994); *Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC*, 520 U.S. 180, 197–98 (1997). Where a plaintiff seeks to aggregate millions of individual claims, the district court must ensure that the proposed damages model fits the certified theory and can be applied classwide. *Amchem*, 521 U.S. at 623–24; *Comcast Corp.*, 569 U.S. at 35–36.

### IV. The judgment should be reversed.

The question presented is not whether Plaintiffs’ experts offered a model, but whether the district court could certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a classwide basis. Under *Hydrogen Peroxide*, *Wal-Mart*, and *Comcast*, the answer is no. The district court was required to conduct a rigorous analysis and find, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the model matched the certified theory and permitted classwide measurement of damages. 552 F.3d at 320; 564 U.S. at 351–52; 569 U.S. at 35–36. Because it did not, certification cannot stand.
