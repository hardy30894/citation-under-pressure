**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the court’s failure to advise him of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) was harmless. The Supreme Court has long held that a Rule violation does not warrant collateral relief where the defendant’s substantial rights were not affected. *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 784 (1979) (“[A]n error that does not affect substantial rights must be disregarded.”). Here, Petitioner was fully aware of his right to appeal and elected not to do so. The Third Circuit therefore properly applied harmless-error review and affirmed. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 735–36 (1993) (holding that an error is harmless if it did not “affect the outcome of the district court proceedings”).

Petitioner’s reliance on *United States v. Flores*, 50 F.3d 412 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Flores*, the Seventh Circuit held that a Rule 32(a)(2) violation is structural and thus not subject to harmless-error analysis. *Id.* at 414. However, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that all Rule violations are structural. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 8–9 (1999) (distinguishing between structural and non-structural errors). Moreover, the Third Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Smith*, 60 F.3d 547 (3d Cir. 1995), directly forecloses Petitioner’s argument. There, the court held that a Rule 32(a)(2) violation is subject to harmless-error review where the defendant was aware of his right to appeal. *Id.* at 551–52.

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights. *See Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (explaining that Rule 32’s predecessor served to apprise defendants of their right to appeal). Where, as here, the defendant was already aware of that right, the Rule’s purpose was satisfied. *See United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (holding that a Rule violation does not require reversal where the defendant’s substantial rights were not prejudiced). The District Court’s factual finding that Petitioner knew of his right to appeal is entitled to deference under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(1). *See Marshall v. Lonberger*, 459 U.S. 422, 431 (1983) (affording deference to state court factual findings in habeas proceedings).

Petitioner’s argument that *Timmreck* is inapplicable because it involved a non-constitutional Rule violation is unavailing. The Supreme Court has made clear that harmless-error analysis applies to all errors, whether constitutional or non-constitutional, so long as they do not affect substantial rights. *See Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 306–07 (1991) (applying harmless-error review to a coerced confession claim). The Third Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Vitiello*, 918 F.2d 506 (3d Cir. 1990), further supports this principle, holding that a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review where the defendant was aware of his appellate rights. *Id.* at 510.

Finally, Petitioner’s reliance on *United States v. Rosales*, 49 F.3d 145 (7th Cir. 1995), is unpersuasive. In *Rosales*, the Seventh Circuit held that a Rule 32(a)(2) violation is per se prejudicial. *Id.* at 147. However, the Supreme Court has rejected per se rules in harmless-error analysis. *See Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 638 (1993) (rejecting per se rules in habeas proceedings). The Third Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Smith*, 60 F.3d 547 (3d Cir. 1995), is controlling and aligns with Supreme Court precedent.

In sum, the District Court correctly applied harmless-error review and concluded that Petitioner was not entitled to relief. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
