# ARGUMENT

The decision below rests on two independent errors, either of which requires reversal. First, the Board's rule that polling requires the same "reasonable doubt" showing needed to withdraw recognition outright is not a rational implementation of the Act, even accepting the Board's own stated premises. Second, even measured against that flawed standard, the Board's finding that Allentown lacked a good-faith doubt is not supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.

## I. The Board's Unitary "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard for Polling Is Not a Rational Implementation of the Act.

The Act itself says nothing about employer polling; the practice is entirely a creature of Board policy, and the Board's policy choices must be rational ones. This Court has recognized the genuine dilemma facing an employer confronted with objective evidence that an incumbent union may have lost majority support: the employer must choose between continuing to bargain with a union that may no longer represent its employees and risking liability for prematurely withdrawing recognition. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594–600 (1969). A poll, no less than a Board-conducted election, serves the Act's central purpose of ensuring that a bargaining representative actually represents the employees for whom it purports to speak. *Cf. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (a bargaining order is warranted only where it reflects the employees' actual wishes).

The Board nonetheless imposes on polling the identical evidentiary threshold required for outright withdrawal of recognition, rather than a lesser showing calibrated to the far less drastic nature of a poll. That choice cannot be reconciled with reasoned administrative decisionmaking. An agency rule merits deference only where there is "a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." *Burlington Truck Lines v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962). The Board's own premise—that Board-supervised elections are preferable to polling precisely because elections carry procedural safeguards that polling lacks—points toward a *lower* evidentiary threshold for the comparatively modest step of polling, not an identical one. An agency's stated rationale must actually support the rule it adopts, for "the orderly functioning of the process of review requires that the grounds upon which the administrative agency acted be clearly disclosed and adequately sustained." *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943). Collapsing three distinct employer options—withdrawal of recognition, a Board-conducted election, and an internal poll—into a single undifferentiated standard, without regard to their plainly different consequences for employees and for the bargaining relationship, is precisely the kind of unreasoned equivalence *Chenery* forbids.

This Court has likewise insisted that the Board's broad policymaking discretion must accommodate the competing statutory interests actually at stake, rather than mechanically apply one rule across dissimilar circumstances. *See Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB*, 324 U.S. 793, 797–98 (1945). And the Board's procedural and remedial choices must serve the purposes of the Act itself, not administrative convenience. *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 194 (1941). By declaring elections categorically preferable to polling while simultaneously applying an identical evidentiary predicate to both, the Board has adopted a rule unmoored from its own justification—the hallmark of arbitrary agency action.

## II. The Board's Finding That Allentown Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Even under the Board's own standard, its finding that Allentown lacked an objective, good-faith doubt about the Union's continuing majority status does not withstand scrutiny. Review of Board factfinding is not a rubber stamp. Congress intended that reviewing courts "assume more responsibility for the reasonableness and fairness of Labor Board decisions than some courts had shown in the past," and directed that "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board's chosen inference is not conclusive merely because it is *an* available inference; it must be the reasonable inference when the record is considered as a whole. *Id.* at 490.

The Board's treatment of the record here reflects exactly the one-sided factfinding *Universal Camera* condemns. The ALJ and the Board discounted, one by one, every piece of evidence favorable to Allentown—dismissing individual employee statements as equivocal, refusing to credit reports of coworker sentiment because the coworkers themselves did not testify, and excluding from the count employees no longer on the payroll—while crediting nothing that corroborated the reasonableness of Allentown's doubt. Having narrowed the pool of probative evidence through these successive exclusions, the Board then declared the residue—a bare seven employees out of thirty-two—insufficient, even though the excluded evidence, properly considered, told a materially different story. This Court has cautioned against precisely this kind of selective factfinding, where the Board draws an inference that is not fairly the product of the record as a whole but is instead the product of discounting every fact that cuts the other way. *See NLRB v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 278, 291–92 (1965).

Nor can the Board's approach be defended as ordinary credibility assessment entitled to deference. Where the Board applies a categorical rule—discounting entire classes of evidence without individualized assessment—it has not performed the weighing *Universal Camera* demands; it has instead erected a standard that no employer's evidence could realistically satisfy. *See NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963) (Board findings must rest on the actual dynamics of the record, not on categorical assumptions). The cumulative testimony here—including statements from newly hired employees, reports from a union steward and other coworkers, and expressions of dissatisfaction with the incumbent representative—far exceeds the Board's truncated count and demonstrates that Allentown's doubt about the Union's continuing majority status was genuine, objective, and reasonable.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
