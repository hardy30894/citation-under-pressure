**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The settled law of this Court, grounded in principles of equity, statutory construction, and judicial restraint, firmly establishes that the lodestar method, as refined by the Supreme Court in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), is the exclusive and exclusive measure of a “reasonable” fee under federal fee-shifting statutes. Any attempt to enhance the lodestar based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” as independent grounds violates the foundational principle that a fee award must be grounded in objective, record-based evidence, not judicial subjectivity or personal evaluation.

The Supreme Court has long held that the lodestar—calculated by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—is the presumptively correct fee. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984), reaffirmed this principle, stating that “the lodestar figure is presumed to be the reasonable fee.” Id. at 897, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. This presumption is not a mere formality; it is a doctrinal cornerstone. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee.” *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 566, 106 S.Ct. 3088, 3099 (1986). To permit enhancement based on “quality” or “results” would effectively undermine this presumption, transforming the lodestar into a mere starting point for judicial discretion rather than the benchmark.

This principle finds its roots in earlier decisions that established the limits of judicial discretion in fee awards. In *United States v. Hensley*, 354 U.S. 104, 108 (1957), the Court held that “the amount of a fee must be determined by the facts of the case and not by the court’s personal opinion of the value of the services rendered.” The Court rejected the notion that a judge could “enhance” a fee based on the “excellence” of counsel, stating that “the court’s role is not to evaluate the merit of the legal work, but to determine the reasonableness of the fee.” Id. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the fee must be based upon the actual services rendered, not upon the court’s opinion of the value of those services.” The Court further cautioned that “a judge may not award a fee based on his own subjective appreciation of the importance or difficulty of the case.” Id.

The principle that a fee must be based on objective, record-supported evidence, not judicial opinion, was solidified in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), and reaffirmed in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 370 (1948). In *Gypsum*, the Court stated that “the court must not rely on its own judgment of the value of the services rendered, but must look to the record for evidence of the time spent and the rate prevailing in the community.” Id. This rule was not altered by the advent of fee-shifting statutes; rather, it was preserved and applied with even greater rigor. As the Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, “the fee must be based on the facts, not on the court’s personal evaluation.” Id.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, which upheld a $4.5 million enhancement based solely on the district judge’s personal observation of “stellar performance” and “superb quality,” directly contravenes these principles. The district judge, in his order, relied on his “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to assess the quality of counsel’s work—a standard that is inherently subjective and unreviewable. This is precisely the kind of judicial self-evaluation that *Hensley* and *Borden* condemned. In *Hensley*, the Court made clear that “the court’s role is not to evaluate the merit of the legal work, but to determine the reasonableness of the fee.” 461 U.S. at 434, 103 S.Ct. at 1939. The district judge’s self-justification as a “superior observer” of legal performance is not a substitute for evidence in the record.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the idea that “results obtained” can serve as an independent basis for enhancement. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), the Court held that “the success of the litigation is not a factor in determining the reasonableness of the fee.” Id. at 370. The Court reiterated this principle in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), stating that “the court may not award a fee based on the outcome of the case.” Id. at 198. These decisions predate the modern fee-shifting era and remain binding. To allow enhancement based on “results” would create a perverse incentive: the more successful the plaintiff, the higher the fee, regardless of the time and effort expended. This would distort the fee-shifting purpose, which is to ensure access to justice, not to reward success.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Neither case held that enhancement based on quality or results is permissible. *Evergreen* merely vacated a fee award for failure to provide adequate findings, not for granting an enhancement. 812 F.2d at 1336–37. *
