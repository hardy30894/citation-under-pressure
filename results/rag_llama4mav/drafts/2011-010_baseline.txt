The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act (LHWCA) to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of this provision requires a causal connection between the injury and operations on the outer Continental Shelf, not a geographic situs-of-injury requirement.

The Supreme Court has observed that OCSLA's coverage is "determined principally by locale." Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). However, this statement was made in the context of the choice-of-law provision, not the workers' compensation provision. The Court's footnote suggesting that § 1333(b) superimposes a status requirement on an "otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement" is dictum, as the issue was not before the Court. Id. at 219 n.2.

The Third Circuit has correctly interpreted § 1333(b) to require only a "but for" causal connection between the injury and operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988). This interpretation is consistent with the plain language of the statute and the Supreme Court's decision in Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), which emphasized the geographic limitation of OCSLA's incorporation of the LHWCA.

In contrast, the Fifth Circuit's adoption of a situs-of-injury requirement in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is an overly narrow reading of the statute. The Mills court's reliance on Herb's Welding I is misplaced, as that case involved an injury occurring in state waters, not onshore.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the "substantial nexus" test in this case, holding that an injury is "the result of" outer Continental Shelf operations if there is a substantial nexus between the injury and the operations. This test is consistent with the Third Circuit's "but for" test and ensures that OCSLA coverage is extended to employees injured in the course of their employment related to outer Continental Shelf operations.

The LHWCA provides coverage for maritime employees injured on navigable waters or in adjoining areas customarily used by employers in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The La Conchita facility, where Juan Valladolid was killed, is not a maritime situs. Applying the "functional relationship" test, the facility is not used as an integral part of longshoring operations, is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978).

The Benefits Review Board correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs. The court should affirm this denial and remand for further consideration of the OCSLA claim under the "substantial nexus" test.

The respondent respectfully requests that the Court affirm the Ninth Circuit's holding that the OCSLA does not have a situs-of-injury requirement and that the "substantial nexus" test applies to determine coverage under § 1333(b). The Court should also affirm the denial of benefits under the LHWCA, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs.
