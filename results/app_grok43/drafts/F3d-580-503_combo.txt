ARGUMENT

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint identification expert and continuance violated fundamental due process. "A fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process." Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927). This guarantee encompasses the right to present a complete defense, including expert testimony necessary to rebut the government's evidence. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) ("The right to offer the testimony of witnesses... is in plain terms the right to present a defense.").

Denial of resources essential to that defense offends the same principle. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 68-69 (1932) (observing that due process requires "an opportunity to be heard" through effective assistance). See also Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963) (recognizing that "every defendant stands equal before the law"). When the government introduces expert fingerprint testimony, fundamental fairness requires the accused an equivalent opportunity to develop and present contrary expert analysis. Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 17 (1956) (holding that "a State must afford to all defendants... the same rights and opportunities").

The magistrate's refusal to grant a continuance compounded the error. "The right of the accused to have a reasonable time to prepare his defense is a fundamental right." Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 235 (1940). See also In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) ("A person's right to reasonable notice of a charge against him, and an opportunity to be heard in his defense... are basic in our system of jurisprudence."). Winbush's request, though filed shortly before trial, sought only the minimal assistance needed to interpret the stipulated absence of latent prints—an issue the government itself placed in contention through its own experts.

A conviction resting on an unbalanced presentation of scientific evidence cannot stand. Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (condemning "a deliberate deception of court and jury"). Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 357 (1963) (emphasizing that "where the merits of the one and only appeal are decided without benefit of counsel, an unconstitutional line is drawn between rich and poor"). The district court's rulings drew precisely such a line, denying Winbush the tools required for a meaningful defense. The convictions and sentence must be reversed.
