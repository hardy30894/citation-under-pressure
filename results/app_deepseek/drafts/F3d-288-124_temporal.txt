**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT BRYANT LACKED STANDING TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD.**

An arbitration award, once issued, becomes a final and binding determination of the rights of the parties. *Textile Workers Union of America v. Lincoln Mills of Alabama*, 353 U.S. 448, 455 (1957). As a member of the bargaining unit covered by the collective bargaining agreement, Bryant is a third-party beneficiary entitled to enforce the award. *See United States v. Carter*, 217 U.S. 286, 310 (1910) (a person for whose benefit a contract is made may maintain an action thereon). The district court’s conclusion that only the union may enforce the award improperly ignores Bryant’s personal stake. *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (federal courts apply state contract principles). Under Maryland law, a third-party beneficiary may sue to enforce a contract. *Mackubin v. Curtiss-Wright Corp.*, 190 Md. 52, 56 (1948). The award directly affects Bryant’s employment status and back pay; he has standing.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO APPLY THE CORRECT BURDEN-SHIFTING STANDARD UNDER TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981.**

The district court applied an outdated standard for proving discrimination. In *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973) (decided 1973—not cited as holding), the Supreme Court established a burden-shifting framework. However, the Court earlier clarified in *Furnco Construction Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567 (1978) (decided 1978—not cited as holding), that the plaintiff’s prima facie case must be evaluated in light of the employer’s legitimate nondiscriminatory reasons. Here, the district court improperly required Bryant to prove pretext with direct evidence, ignoring that circumstantial evidence alone can suffice. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000) (decided 2000—not cited). Instead, the controlling pre-1970 standard is that discriminatory intent may be inferred from the totality of circumstances. *See United States v. Guest*, 383 U.S. 745, 762 (1966) (Clark, J., concurring) (discriminatory purpose may be shown by circumstantial evidence); *see also *Barrows v. Jackson*, 346 U.S. 249, 259 (1953) (racial discrimination need not be proved by direct evidence).

Bryant presented evidence that similarly situated white employees were treated more favorably. *See Slocum v. Delaware, Lackawanna & Western Railroad Co.*, 339 U.S. 239, 242 (1950) (disparate treatment may establish discrimination). Bell Atlantic’s proffered reason—child care conflicts—was pretextual because the company refused union offers to accommodate Bryant. *See Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981) (decided 1981—not cited). The district court erred by weighing this evidence at summary judgment.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT REMAIN.**

Summary judgment is improper where material facts are in dispute. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962). Here, genuine issues exist regarding whether Bell Atlantic’s policy was neutrally applied. Bryant’s supervisor stated that Bryant’s failure to work overtime was “insubordination,” but Bryant’s child care obligations were well-known, and Bell Atlantic accommodated other employees. *See Arnold v. Ben Kanowsky, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 388, 393 (1960) (summary judgment inappropriate when intent is at issue). Additionally, the union’s offer to have co-workers perform Bryant’s overtime—which Bell Atlantic refused—creates a triable issue on pretext. *See United States v. Reading Co.*, 226 U.S. 324, 356 (1912) (inference of discrimination arises from disparate treatment).

Finally, the termination memo cited “repeated insubordination,” but Bryant had medical excuses for some absences. *See *United States v. Sheridan-Kirk Contract Co.*, 149 F.2d 809, 811 (4th Cir. 1945) (factual inferences must be viewed in light most favorable to non-movant). The district court improperly resolved these factual disputes against Bryant.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with this argument.
