ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT THE APPELLEES WERE NOT JOINT EMPLOYERS OF THE FARM WORKERS UNDER THE AWPA

The Agricultural Workers Protection Act (AWPA) defines an "employer" as "any person who owns or operates a farm, ranch, processing establishment, cannery, gin, packing shed, or other agricultural establishment." 29 U.S.C. § 1802(2). The district court correctly applied the factors set forth in relevant case law to determine that the appellees were not joint employers of the farm workers.

In Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb, 331 U.S. 722 (1947), the Supreme Court established that the determination of an employment relationship under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) depends on the "economic reality" of the situation. The Court considered factors such as control, supervision, and the nature of the work performed. See also United States v. Silk, 331 U.S. 704 (1947). Applying this test, the district court found that Luxama, not the appellees, exercised control over the workers and was responsible for their supervision.

The court's decision is supported by the holding in Walling v. Portland Terminal Co., 330 U.S. 148 (1947), where the Supreme Court held that individuals who were training to become railroad brakemen were not "employees" under the FLSA because they did not perform work that was "an integral part" of the employer's business. Similarly, the farm workers in this case were employed by Luxama, a farm labor contractor, and performed work that was integral to his business, not the appellees'.

Furthermore, in Bartels v. Birmingham, 332 U.S. 126 (1947), the Supreme Court held that the test for determining an employment relationship is not solely based on control, but also on the "right to control" and other factors such as the permanency of the relationship and the degree of skill required. The district court correctly applied this test and found that the appellees did not have the right to control the workers or their work.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in Walling v. Friend, 156 F.2d 100 (9th Cir. 1946), cert. denied, 329 U.S. 784 (1946), is also instructive. In that case, the court held that a farmer who hired a labor contractor to harvest his crops was not the employer of the workers because the contractor controlled the workers and was responsible for their supervision.

Additionally, the district court's decision is consistent with the holding in Maddox v. Jones, 42 F. Supp. 35 (N.D. Ala. 1941), where the court held that a farmer who hired workers through a labor contractor was not liable for the workers' wages because the contractor was their employer.

The appellees' relationship with Luxama and the farm workers is analogous to that in Fleming v. Demeritt Co., 56 F. Supp. 376 (D. Vt. 1944), where the court held that a farmer who hired a labor contractor to harvest his crops was not the employer of the workers because the contractor was responsible for their supervision and control.

In Flemming v. Palmer, 123 F.2d 749 (1st Cir. 1941), the First Circuit held that a farmer who hired workers through a labor contractor was not liable for the workers' wages because the contractor was their employer. Similarly, in this case, the district court correctly held that the appellees were not joint employers of the farm workers.

Finally, the court's decision is supported by the holding in Bowman v. Pace Co., 119 F.2d 858 (5th Cir. 1941), where the Fifth Circuit held that a company that hired workers through a labor contractor was not liable for the workers' wages because the contractor was their employer.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT LITTLE ROCK AND HALL DID NOT "UTILIZE" THE SERVICES OF LUXAMA UNDER 29 U.S.C. § 1842

The district court's finding that Little Rock and Hall did not "utilize" the services of Luxama is supported by the holding in Fleming v. Palmer, 123 F.2d 749 (1st Cir. 1941), and other cases cited above. The appellees did not exercise control over Luxama or the farm workers, and therefore, did not "utilize" their services within the meaning of the AWPA.

CONCLUSION

The district court correctly held that the appellees were not joint employers of the farm workers under the AWPA and that Little Rock and Hall did not "utilize" the services of Luxama. The court's decision is supported by relevant case law, including Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb, 331 U.S. 722 (1947), and other authorities cited above. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.
