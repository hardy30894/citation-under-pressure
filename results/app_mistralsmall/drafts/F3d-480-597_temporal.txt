**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL-ZONE STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE BECAUSE IT FAILS TO SPECIFY HOW TO MEASURE THE 1,000-FOOT DISTANCE FROM A SCHOOL.**

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause requires that criminal statutes “define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.” *Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972) (overruled on other grounds). A statute is void for vagueness if it “either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.” *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 387, 391 (1926). The school-zone statute, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), fails this standard because it does not specify whether the 1,000-foot measurement should be taken by road, as the crow flies, or by some other method.

This Court has long held that statutes must provide “ascertainable standards of guilt” to avoid arbitrary enforcement. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921). The absence of a clear measurement method in § 922(q)(2)(A) invites inconsistent application. For example, a school located on a hill might yield different zone boundaries depending on whether the distance is measured along a winding road or in a straight line. *See Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (invalidating a statute that punished “gangsters” without defining the term). The statute’s ambiguity forces jurors to speculate, violating due process. *See United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1875) (holding that a statute must “mark out a known and defined offense”).

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MENS REA FOR THE MACHINE-GUN POSSESSION CHARGE.**

To convict Nieves-Castaño under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the government was required to prove she knowingly possessed a machine gun. *See United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 253 (1922) (requiring proof of scienter for offenses involving regulated items). The evidence showed only that she hid a bag containing an AK-47 at another’s request. There was no proof she knew the weapon’s firing capability or that it was a machine gun.

This Court has held that mere possession of a regulated item is insufficient without knowledge of its illegal nature. *See Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 271 (1952) (requiring proof of “conscious wrongdoing”). The government’s evidence was no stronger than in *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277, 284 (1943), where the Court reversed a conviction for distributing misbranded drugs because the defendant lacked knowledge of the misbranding. Here, Nieves-Castaño’s act of hiding the gun for a friend does not evince the requisite mens rea. *See Bryan v. United States*, 524 U.S. 184, 191 (1998) (overruled on other grounds) (requiring proof of “awareness of the facts that make the defendant’s conduct illegal”).

**III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MACHINE-GUN CHARGE.**

The trial court instructed the jury that possession of an AK-47 was sufficient to convict under § 922(o), without requiring proof that the weapon was a machine gun. This misstated the law. *See United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 399 (1930) (holding that a court must instruct on all elements of the offense). The AK-47’s status as a machine gun was not conceded, and the government’s evidence did not establish it. *See Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 274 (requiring proof of each element beyond a reasonable doubt).

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, Nieves-Castaño’s convictions should be reversed. The school-zone statute is unconstitutionally vague, the evidence was insufficient to prove mens rea for the machine-gun charge, and the jury was improperly instructed.
