### A. The district court’s supplemental instruction on “shielding” was erroneous and prejudicial.

A criminal defendant is entitled to have the jury instructed on the elements of the offense accurately and in a manner that does not expand the statute beyond its text. See *United States v. Resendiz-Ponce*, 549 U.S. 102, 108 (2007); *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 8-10 (1999). Where a term in a statute is ordinary English, courts generally give it its common meaning; but an instruction that goes beyond the statute’s language and imports an extra-textual standard may misstate the law. *Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137, 144-45 (1995); *United States v. Aguilar*, 515 U.S. 593, 599-600 (1995).

That is what happened here. Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) makes it a crime to “conceal[], harbor[], or shield[] from detection” an alien who is unlawfully present. Congress used three separate verbs, and each must be given independent effect. *Bailey*, 516 U.S. at 146 (“We assume that Congress used two terms because it intended each term to have a separate meaning.”). The district court, however, responded to the jury’s request by defining “shielding” in a way that tracked a nonstatutory formulation developed in some other circuits rather than the text enacted by Congress. That was error because it risked collapsing “shielding” into a broader, judge-made standard untethered to the statutory language. See *United States v. Costello*, 666 F.3d 1040, 1047-49 (7th Cir. 2012) (rejecting an overbroad reading of “harbor” that would sweep in ordinary landlord-tenant conduct and emphasizing that § 1324 must be read in light of its text and structure); *United States v. Cuevas-Reyes*, 572 F.3d 119, 122-23 (1st Cir. 2009) (recognizing that § 1324 terms are not boundless and must be limited by statutory language).

The government may argue that the court had discretion to clarify an ambiguous term after the jury’s question. But that discretion is limited by the requirement that the clarification be legally correct and not misleading. *United States v. Van Dyke*, 14 F.3d 415, 423 (8th Cir. 1994); *United States v. Meade*, 175 F.3d 215, 224 (1st Cir. 1999). A court may explain a term, but it may not rewrite the element. See *United States v. Aguilar*, 515 U.S. at 600. Here, the supplemental instruction invited conviction on a theory broader than concealment from detection as Congress wrote it.

The error was especially serious because the instruction came after the jury had asked for a definition. A supplemental instruction can have outsized influence, and where the jury specifically seeks guidance on an element, an erroneous answer is highly prejudicial. See *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 612-13 (1946) (“When a jury makes explicit its difficulties a trial judge should clear them away with concrete accuracy.”); *United States v. Park*, 421 U.S. 658, 675 (1975). Instead of giving “concrete accuracy,” the court adopted an expansive formulation that risked allowing conviction based on Ye’s mere employment decisions, housing assistance, or failure to create paperwork—conduct that, without more, does not necessarily amount to “shielding from detection.” See *Costello*, 666 F.3d at 1048-49; *United States v. Shum, 496 F.3d 390, 394-95 (5th Cir. 2007)*.

The government cannot show harmlessness. An instructional error on an element is harmless only if it is clear beyond a reasonable doubt that the verdict rested on a proper theory. *Neder*, 527 U.S. at 15; *United States v. Montalvo*, 331 F.3d 1052, 1057-58 (9th Cir. 2003). The record here shows the opposite: the jury struggled with the meaning of “shielding,” and the government’s case depended on characterizing routine employment and housing conduct as criminal concealment. Because the court’s definition expanded the statutory term and likely supplied the decisive legal framework for conviction, Ye’s conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed. See *Bollenbach*, 326 U.S. at 614.

### B. The evidence was insufficient to prove intent to prevent detection.

Even if the instruction had been proper, the conviction cannot stand because the evidence was insufficient to prove that Ye acted with the purpose of preventing government detection of his employees. A conviction supported by insufficient evidence violates due process. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). On review, the question is whether any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.*; *United States v. Jones*, 371 F.3d 363, 371 (7th Cir. 2004).

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) requires proof that the defendant knowingly concealed, harbored, or shielded aliens “in order to” help them remain in the United States illegally and, under the charged theory, that he intended to prevent detection. See *United States v. Costello*, 666 F.3d at 1048; *United States v. Shum*, 496 F.3d at 394. Mere employment of undocumented workers, without evidence of affirmative steps directed at hiding them from authorities, is not enough. *Costello*, 666 F.3d at 1048-49.

The government’s proof here showed that Ye hired undocumented workers, failed to maintain employment records, paid cash, and provided housing and transportation. But those facts, while relevant to the hiring count, do not establish a distinct intent to prevent detection. See *United States v. Vargas-Cordon*, 733 F.3d 366, 382-83 (2d Cir. 2013) (requiring proof of conduct directed at shielding from authorities, not merely facilitating work or residence). Ye did not use aliases, false walls, locked rooms, or other classic concealment devices. Compare *United States v. Merkt*, 764 F.2d 266, 273-74 (5th Cir. 1985). The evidence that he advised workers to obtain documents also shows an effort to regularize employment, not necessarily to evade detection. At most, the record supports suspicion that Ye employed unauthorized workers; it does not prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he intended to prevent the government from detecting them. *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319.

Because the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law, this Court should reverse Ye’s conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). At minimum, the case should be remanded for a new trial with a correct instruction confined to the statutory text.
