**ARGUMENT**

The text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the conclusion that a grant of Temporary Protected Status under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.

**I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1254a(f)(4) EQUATES TPS WITH ADMISSION FOR ADJUSTMENT-OF-STATUS PURPOSES**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This language does not merely confer a status; it directs that TPS recipients be treated as nonimmigrants for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. The term “nonimmigrant” is defined in the INA as “an alien who is admitted into the United States” for a specific purpose. 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15). By operation of § 1254a(f)(4), a TPS recipient is deemed to be a nonimmigrant—and thus deemed to have been admitted.

The Third Circuit erred by drawing a rigid distinction between “admission” and “status.” *See Sanchez v. Mayorkas*, 997 F.3d 89, 97 (3d Cir. 2020). But the INA itself blurs that line. Section 1255(a) requires that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Section 1254a(f)(4) does not use the word “admitted,” but it uses the phrase “lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” Because nonimmigrant status necessarily presupposes admission, the statutory command that TPS recipients be treated as nonimmigrants necessarily includes the admission requirement. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (“By the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.”).

**II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE INA CONFIRMS THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION**

Congress knows how to distinguish between admission and status when it wishes. In § 1255(k), for example, Congress separately references “lawful admission” and “lawful status.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(2). But in § 1254a(f)(4), Congress did not use the word “admission”; it used the broader phrase “lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” That choice is significant. *See I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely.”).

The Third Circuit’s interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) largely superfluous. If TPS recipients who entered without inspection remain ineligible for adjustment of status, then § 1254a(f)(4) serves no meaningful function for the very aliens most in need of its protections. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (courts should avoid interpretations that render statutory provisions “inoperative or superfluous”).

**III. THE PURPOSE OF TPS SUPPORTS PETITIONERS’ INTERPRETATION**

TPS was designed to provide humanitarian relief to nationals of countries experiencing temporary crises. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s holding undermines that purpose by forcing TPS recipients who entered without inspection to choose between remaining in the United States indefinitely without a path to permanent residence or leaving the country to seek lawful entry—often to a homeland that remains unsafe. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964. Congress could not have intended such an anomalous result. *See Flores v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Servs.*, 718 F.3d 548, 554 (6th Cir. 2013) (holding that TPS constitutes an admission and noting that the contrary interpretation “would yield an anomalous result”).

**IV. THE AGENCY’S LONGSTANDING INTERPRETATION IS ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE**

The United States Citizenship and Immigration Services has consistently interpreted § 1254a(f)(4) to mean that TPS recipients are considered to have been admitted for purposes of adjustment of status. *See* USCIS Policy Manual, Vol. 7, Part B, Ch. 2(A)(5) (2019). This interpretation is reasonable and entitled to deference under *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944), because it is “thorough, valid, and reasonable.” *See also Flores*, 718 F.3d at 554 (deferring to the agency’s interpretation).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” into the United States under § 1255.
