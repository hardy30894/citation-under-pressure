**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation. The Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Although sentencing determinations ordinarily fall outside its reach, *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919), the Clause applies when a sentencing proceeding “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981).

California Penal Code § 1025 grants a statutory right to jury trial on prior-conviction allegations. The prosecution must prove the allegation “beyond a reasonable doubt,” *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993), and the defendant enjoys the rights to confrontation, cross-examination, and the privilege against self-incrimination, *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974). These procedures “resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209 (1984). An appellate finding of evidentiary insufficiency therefore constitutes an acquittal that precludes retrial. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 445; *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 10-11 (1978).

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that *Bullington* is confined to capital cases rests on an erroneous reading of *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994). *Caspari* addressed only whether the Eighth Circuit’s extension of *Bullington* to noncapital recidivism proceedings announced a “new rule” under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); it did not decide the merits. Nothing in *Bullington* or *Rumsey* limits their reasoning to capital proceedings. Both decisions turned on the procedural character of the hearing—whether the state assumed the burden of proving specific facts beyond a reasonable doubt before a jury—not on the penalty ultimately at stake.

Because the section 1025 proceeding is functionally indistinguishable from the capital sentencing hearings in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
