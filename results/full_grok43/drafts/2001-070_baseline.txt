ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 170 (1976). A punishment is unusual if it is contrary to the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). Those standards are measured “by objective factors to the maximum possible extent.” Harmelin v. Michigan, 501 U.S. 957, 1000 (1991) (Kennedy, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment).

At the time of trial, “two state statutes” barred execution of the mentally retarded, and “no other legislative enactments” evidenced a national consensus against the practice. Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 334 (1989). The Virginia Supreme Court correctly held that this legislative record, together with the continued imposition of death sentences on mentally retarded offenders in jurisdictions permitting such punishment, demonstrated the absence of the requisite consensus. Atkins v. Commonwealth, 260 Va. 375, 385, 534 S.E.2d 312, 319 (2000).

Petitioner’s reliance on professional organizations and public-opinion polls does not alter the analysis. This Court has repeatedly declined to treat such sources as controlling when they conflict with “the judgments of those who are ‘delegated by the people to make and carry out’ penal policy.” Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 377 (1989) (quoting Gregg, 428 U.S. at 175). Because the Eighth Amendment inquiry is “an objective one,” the “clearest and most reliable evidence” remains “the legislation enacted by the country’s legislatures.” Penry, 492 U.S. at 331.

Even if a national consensus were assumed, the punishment would remain constitutional unless it also failed the second, independent requirement that it be grossly disproportionate to the offender’s culpability. Harmelin, 501 U.S. at 1001 (Kennedy, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment). Mental retardation, while relevant to mitigation, does not categorically preclude the moral culpability necessary for capital punishment. Penry, 492 U.S. at 338. The trial court’s finding that petitioner possessed sufficient understanding to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct therefore satisfies constitutional demands.

The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
