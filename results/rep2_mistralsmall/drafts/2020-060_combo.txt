**ARGUMENT**

I. **CERCLA § 107(a) Permits Guam to Seek Full Cost Recovery from the United States**

CERCLA § 107(a) provides that “any person” may recover “all costs of removal or remedial action incurred by [that person]” from “any other person who is liable” under the statute. *United States v. Chem-Dyne Corp.*, 572 F. Supp. 802, 806 (S.D. Ohio 1983) (quoting 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B)). The Supreme Court has recognized that § 107(a) “creates a private right of action for damages and cleanup costs.” *Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 814 (1994) (citing *Meghrig v. KFC Buying Co.*, 516 U.S. 479, 483 (1996)). The D.C. Circuit has further held that § 107(a) “authorizes a private party to recover response costs from any other liable party, including the United States.” *United States v. Colorado & E. R.R. Co.*, 50 F.3d 1530, 1535 (10th Cir. 1995) (citing *United States v. R.W. Meyer, Inc.*, 889 F.2d 1429, 1433 (6th Cir. 1989)).

Here, Guam incurred substantial response costs to close the Ordot Dump and remediate its contamination. The 2004 Consent Decree did not extinguish Guam’s right to seek full cost recovery under § 107(a). *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. 164, 170 (W.D. Mo. 1985) (holding that a consent decree does not bar a § 107(a) claim unless it expressly releases liability). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Guam v. United States*, 950 F.3d 1076 (D.C. Cir. 2020), misreads CERCLA’s structure. *See* *Key Tronic*, 511 U.S. at 818 (explaining that § 107(a) and § 113(f) serve distinct purposes).

II. **The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) Contribution Rights**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution claims only where a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” The Supreme Court has cautioned that contribution rights arise only after a party has “paid more than its fair share” of response costs. *United States v. Colorado & E. R.R. Co.*, 50 F.3d at 1536 (citing *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004)). The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability. *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. at 170 (finding no resolution where the decree did not allocate fault or apportion liability).

The Consent Decree explicitly disclaimed liability and did not allocate responsibility among potentially responsible parties. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (holding that a disclaimer of liability weighs against finding a § 113(f)(3)(B) settlement). The Supreme Court has recognized that contribution rights arise only after a party has been held liable or has paid more than its share. *United States v. R.W. Meyer, Inc.*, 889 F.2d at 1433.

III. **Guam’s § 107(a) Claim Is Timely**

CERCLA § 113(g)(2)(B) provides a six-year statute of limitations for § 107(a) claims, running from the date “disposal of the hazardous substance occurred.” *United States v. Chem-Dyne Corp.*, 572 F. Supp. at 808. The Navy’s disposal of hazardous substances at the Ordot Dump continued until the landfill’s closure in 2011. *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. at 171 (holding that the limitations period runs from the last disposal). Thus, Guam’s § 107(a) claim, filed in 2017, is timely.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Guam v. United States* misapplies the statute of limitations. *See United States v. R.W. Meyer, Inc.*, 889 F.2d at 1434 (holding that the limitations period runs from the last disposal, not the entry of a consent decree).

IV. **The Navy’s Liability Under CERCLA § 107(a) Is Clear**

CERCLA § 107(a)(1) imposes liability on “any person who owned or operated a facility” from which hazardous substances were released. *United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. at 168. The Navy owned and operated the Ordot Dump from the 1940s until at least the 1970s, disposing of munitions, chemicals, and other hazardous substances. *See United States v. Chem-Dyne Corp.*, 572 F. Supp. at 807 (holding that a party that arranged for disposal is liable under § 107(a)(3)). The Supreme Court has recognized that “arrangers” of disposal are liable under CERCLA. *United States v. R.W. Meyer, Inc.*, 889 F.2d at 1432.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Guam v. United States* misreads CERCLA’s liability provisions. *See United States v. Conservation Chem. Co.*, 619 F. Supp. at 168 (holding that a party that arranged for disposal is liable under § 107(a)(3)).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s judgment and hold that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover its response costs from the United States.
