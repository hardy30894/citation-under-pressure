**Argument**

Section 241(a)(5) of the INA does not reach Fernandez-Vargas. Congress did not clearly provide that the reinstatement provision would apply to aliens who reentered before the statute’s effective date, and the canon against retroactivity therefore controls. See *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994); *Lindh v. Murphy*, 521 U.S. 320, 328-29 (1997). A statute has impermissible retroactive effect when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. Here, Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry occurred in 1982, long before Congress enacted § 241(a)(5) in 1996 and made it effective in 1997. The Government seeks to use that earlier reentry as the trigger for a new and much harsher legal regime: automatic reinstatement of the old deportation order and categorical elimination of discretionary relief. That is retroactivity in the ordinary *Landgraf* sense. See *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 357-58 (1999).

The statute’s text does not rebut that presumption. Section 241(a)(5) applies when an alien “has reentered” illegally after having been removed. But that language is naturally read as addressing reentries occurring after the statute’s effective date, not as sweeping in all earlier reentries regardless of when they occurred. When Congress intends unmistakable retroactive coverage, it knows how to say so. See *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001). In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that a statutory change eliminating discretionary relief could not be applied to an alien who pleaded guilty before the change because he likely relied on the possibility of relief in deciding whether to forgo trial. Id. at 321-24. The same principle governs here: Fernandez-Vargas lived for years in the United States under the pre-1997 regime, during which he remained eligible to seek adjustment of status and other relief. The new statute extinguishes that settled legal landscape after the fact. See *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325.

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion conflicts with this Court’s retroactivity cases. The relevant inquiry is not whether Fernandez-Vargas had a vested right to discretionary relief, but whether the statute “impose[s] new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. It does. By making the prior deportation order automatically executable and foreclosing all INA relief, § 241(a)(5) changes the legal consequences of his completed 1982 reentry and his subsequent long-term presence in the country. See *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-23; *Martin*, 527 U.S. at 357-58.

Even if the Court concludes that § 241(a)(5) may apply to pre-enactment reentrants, this application is impermissibly retroactive as applied to Fernandez-Vargas. Retroactivity analysis is “sensitive to the nature of the parties’ rights,” *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321, and turns on whether the challenged law disrupts settled expectations. Fernandez-Vargas did not merely remain in the country after 1997; he married a U.S. citizen in 2001 and affirmatively sought to adjust status under the prior legal framework. Before the reinstatement statute, his unlawful presence did not itself extinguish all avenues of relief. The statute now does exactly that. *St. Cyr* rejected the Government’s attempt to strip relief from aliens whose conduct predated the new law because they “almost certainly relied” on the availability of relief. 533 U.S. at 325. Fernandez-Vargas’s long residence, marriage, and application for adjustment all reflect reliance interests the new statute upends.

The Government may argue, as the courts below did, that Fernandez-Vargas could not have “relied” on the possibility of legalization because his entry was unlawful. But *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr* do not limit retroactivity to formal contractual reliance. A statute is retroactive if it “alters the legal consequences” of prior conduct in a way that is “manifestly unjust.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270, 269 n.24. That is precisely what happened here. Congress may regulate future illegal entries, but it may not, without clear statement, impose the 1997 reinstatement regime on a man who reentered fifteen years earlier. See *Lindh*, 521 U.S. at 328-29; *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 316-20.

Because the statute does not clearly authorize this sweep, and because its application to Fernandez-Vargas would attach new legal consequences to a completed pre-enactment reentry, the judgment below should be reversed.
