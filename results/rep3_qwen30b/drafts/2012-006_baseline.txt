**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension in the law of criminal conspiracy: the balance between the government’s burden to prove guilt and the defendant’s right to be acquitted if the government fails to meet that burden.

The lower court erred in affirming the conviction without requiring the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Calvin Smith remained a member of the conspiracy during the period covered by the indictment. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit held that the government need only prove the existence of a single conspiracy, and that the jury could infer continued membership based on circumstantial evidence—even where the alleged leaders of the conspiracy had split up before the limitations period began. This holding misapplies settled principles of criminal law and undermines the constitutional guarantee of due process.

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Every Element of the Crime, Including Conspiracy Membership, Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The Constitution does not permit the government to shift the burden of persuasion to the defendant on any element of a criminal offense. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This principle applies with particular force in conspiracy cases, where the government must prove not only the existence of an agreement but also the defendant’s knowing and voluntary participation in that agreement during the relevant time frame. *United States v. Brown*, 478 U.S. 573, 578 (1986). The burden of proving membership in a conspiracy is not a mere evidentiary burden; it is a constitutional one. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995) (“The prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.”).

The government’s burden does not diminish simply because the conspiracy allegedly continued after a key leader withdrew. The mere fact that Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray separated before the limitations period does not, by itself, prove that the conspiracy survived or that Smith remained a member. The government must still prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Smith joined or remained in a continuing conspiracy during the period charged. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d 1352, 1357 (9th Cir. 1978) (“The government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time of the offense.”).

**II. The Lower Court’s Rule Allows the Government to Prove Conspiracy Membership by Inference Alone, Violating Due Process**

The Court of Appeals held that the jury could infer continued conspiracy membership based on “circumstantial evidence” of Smith’s conduct after the alleged split. But this approach permits the government to satisfy its burden through a chain of inferences so attenuated that it effectively relieves the government of its constitutional duty to prove membership beyond a reasonable doubt.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected such “inference stacking” in criminal cases. *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. 499, 505 (1983) (“The prosecution must prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, not merely that the defendant’s conduct is consistent with guilt.”). In *United States v. Gaudin*, the Court emphasized that “the jury must be able to find the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt,” and that “the government cannot rely on speculation or conjecture.” 515 U.S. at 510. The Court of Appeals’ rule, which allows the jury to infer conspiracy membership from isolated acts of association or proximity, fails this test.

Moreover, the government’s evidence in this case was insufficient to support such an inference. There was no direct evidence that Smith participated in any criminal activity after Moore and Gray split. The only evidence linking Smith to the conspiracy during the limitations period was his prior association with the group and his presence at a few meetings. But “mere presence at a meeting” or “association with known conspirators” is not enough to prove membership in a conspiracy. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1357. The government cannot convert a defendant’s past association into present guilt through a series of speculative inferences.

**III. The Rule of Withdrawal Does Not Eliminate the Government’s Burden to Prove Continued Membership**

The Court of Appeals’ decision rests on a flawed understanding of the law of withdrawal. It is true that a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy and thereby absolve himself of liability for future acts. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1357. But withdrawal does not relieve the government of its burden to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *United States v. Hasting*, 461 U.S. at 505. The government must still prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the defendant was a member at the time of the charged offenses.

The Court of Appeals’ rule effectively shifts the burden to the defendant to prove that he withdrew. But the Constitution does not permit such a shift. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. If the government fails to prove that Smith remained in the conspiracy during the limitations period, the jury must acquit. The mere fact that Smith was once a member does not justify convicting him of crimes committed after he allegedly withdrew.

**IV. The Court Should Reverse and Remand for a New Trial**

The government’s failure to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Smith was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period renders the conviction constitutionally infirm. *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 510. The Court of Appeals’ decision to affirm the conviction based on speculative inferences violates the due process clause of the Fifth Amendment.

This Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand for a new trial, at which the government must prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Smith was a member of the conspiracy during the period charged. The burden of persuasion must remain with the government at all times. The Constitution does not permit the government to rely on inference stacking or to shift the burden to the defendant.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
