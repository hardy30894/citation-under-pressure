**ARGUMENT**

The Federal Circuit’s affirmance of an award of Samsung’s entire profits on the accused smartphones rests on a misreading of the design-patent damages statute that this Court has repeatedly rejected. When a design patent covers only a component of a larger article, the patentee may recover only those profits “properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912); see also Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884).

This Court has long held that a design patent does not entitle its owner to the infringer’s profits on the entire article unless the design is shown to be the sole driver of those profits. In Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court reversed an award of the full profit on infringing carpets because “there was no evidence as to the value imparted to the carpet by the design.” Id. at 444. The same rule was applied in Dobson v. Dornan, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), where the Court again required proof that the patented design, rather than “the carding, spinning, dyeing and weaving,” accounted for the profits. Id. at 17. These decisions establish that the patentee bears the burden of “separat[ing] or apportion[ing] the defendant’s profits … between the patented feature and the unpatented features.” Westinghouse, 225 U.S. at 614.

The statute governing design-patent damages, 35 U.S.C. § 289, incorporates this apportionment principle. Its text permits recovery of “the total profit made by [the infringer] from the manufacture or sale … of the article of manufacture to which [the] design … has been applied.” When the patented design is applied only to a component, the “article of manufacture” is that component, not the entire product. Any contrary reading would revive the very rule this Court repudiated in the Dobson cases and in Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480 (1854), where damages for an improvement were limited to the value added by the improvement itself.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach—permitting recovery of all profits on the smartphone whenever the design is applied to any part—cannot be reconciled with these precedents. As the Court explained in Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp., 309 U.S. 390 (1940), even when infringement is clear, “only that part of the profits found to be attributable to the use of the copyrighted material” may be awarded. Id. at 396. The same limitation applies to design patents.

Because Apple presented no evidence apportioning Samsung’s profits between the patented designs and the unpatented features of the phones, the judgment cannot stand. The case should be remanded for a proper determination of the profits, if any, attributable to the patented components alone.
