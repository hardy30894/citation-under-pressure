**ARGUMENT**

**I. GGC's comprehensive revision of the challenged policies extinguished the controversy over their validity.**

Article III confines federal courts to the resolution of "cases and controversies," and forbids opinions on "moot questions or abstract propositions." Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). A suit becomes moot, and must be dismissed, once "it appears that the plaintiff... could no longer be granted any effective relief." California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co., 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893); accord United States v. Alaska S.S. Co., 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920). GGC rescinded the "Freedom of Expression Policy" and struck the challenged Student Code provisions before judgment, replacing them with a policy permitting speech throughout campus. No live rule remains to enjoin or declare unconstitutional.

Petitioners cannot escape this conclusion by invoking the voluntary-cessation doctrine. It is true that "mere voluntary cessation of allegedly illegal conduct does not moot a case," lest defendants be left "free to return to [their] old ways." United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). But that doctrine protects against sham repeals designed to evade review, not permanent, structural policy changes of the kind GGC adopted here. Where subsequent events make it plain that the challenged practice cannot reasonably be expected to recur, the controversy is at an end. Golden v. Zwickler, 394 U.S. 103, 108-10 (1969) (declaratory judgment claim moot where changed circumstances made renewed enforcement of the challenged statute against the plaintiff speculative). GGC's wholesale replacement of both provisions, superseding the "Prior Policies" in "full force and effect" since February 2017, forecloses any reasonable expectation of recurrence. The claims for injunctive and declaratory relief are therefore moot—a conclusion Petitioners do not even contest on appeal.

**II. A bare demand for nominal damages, untethered to any actual injury, cannot supply the live controversy Article III requires.**

Petitioners' sole remaining tether to a live case is a request for nominal damages. But the judicial power extends only to "the right to determine actual controversies arising between adverse litigants." Muskrat v. United States, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911). Courts do not sit to vindicate abstract disagreement about the legality of past conduct once nothing "actually controverted" remains to be settled. Mills, 159 U.S. at 653. This Court has long insisted that adjudication proceed only in a genuine adversary posture presenting a concrete stake, not "a friendly, non-adversary proceeding." Ashwander v. TVA, 297 U.S. 288, 346 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring). A token damages request, disconnected from any showing of actual pecuniary or personal loss, does not convert what is otherwise an abstract dispute about a superseded policy into the kind of concrete controversy Article III demands.

This case illustrates why. Petitioners never alleged that they suffered any quantifiable loss—no lost wages, no out-of-pocket expense, no proven reputational or emotional harm. Their complaint mentions "injury" only in the abstract, resting entirely on the bare assertion that their constitutional rights were violated. That is fundamentally different from a claim like the one in Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), where the plaintiff's continuing entitlement to back salary—an actual, quantifiable pecuniary loss flowing directly from the challenged exclusion—kept the controversy alive notwithstanding the end of the underlying congressional term. A fixed, nominal sum awarded "without regard" to any proven loss does not perform that same function; it does not depend on, and cannot be defeated or enlarged by, any fact about the plaintiff's actual circumstances, and it corresponds to nothing but the abstract legal question Petitioners ask this Court to resolve. Permitting such a request to sustain jurisdiction after the challenged policy has been repealed would effectively authorize the very type of advisory adjudication that Muskrat and Ashwander forbid.

**III. Petitioners' failure to plead, and improperly attempt to add, a claim for compensatory damages leaves no live claim to litigate.**

Even accepting that a properly pleaded claim for actual, compensatory damages might present a continuing controversy notwithstanding the policy's repeal, Petitioners pleaded no such claim. Their prayer for relief sought only nominal damages alongside injunctive and declaratory relief; nowhere did the complaint allege the kind of "actual injury"—monetary loss, physical or emotional harm, or reputational damage—necessary to support an award of compensatory damages. Cf. Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 47 (1957) (even under liberal notice pleading, a complaint must give the defendant fair notice of the claim and the grounds upon which it rests).

Nor were Petitioners entitled to remedy that deficiency by embedding a request to amend within their opposition to the motion to dismiss. Leave to amend should be "freely given," Foman v. Davis, 371 U.S. 178, 182 (1962), but that liberality presupposes a proper motion identifying the substance of the proposed amendment—not an offhand aside in a brief opposing dismissal. Having failed to plead actual injury, and having failed to seek amendment through any proper vehicle, Petitioners cannot now manufacture a live claim for compensatory relief to rescue an otherwise moot suit.

Because no policy remains to challenge, no properly pleaded compensatory claim survives, and a bare nominal-damages request cannot itself supply the concrete stake Article III requires, the judgment below should be affirmed.
