**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT PAKISTAN’S EXPROPRIATION OF BEG’S PROPERTY DID NOT FALL WITHIN THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION TO FOREIGN SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY.

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (“FSIA”) provides that a foreign state is not immune from suit in any case “in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the commercial activity exception is triggered where a foreign state acts in a proprietary, rather than sovereign, capacity. *See, e.g., National City Bank of New York v. Republic of China*, 348 U.S. 356, 359 (1955) (“When a foreign government resort[s] to the courts for the collection of a debt arising out of purely commercial transactions, it [is] not exercising powers peculiar to sovereign authority.”); *United States v. Pink*, 315 U.S. 203, 228–29 (1942) (holding that a foreign state’s expropriation of property for commercial purposes does not cloak the act with sovereign immunity).

Here, Beg’s complaint plainly alleges that Pakistan expropriated his land for the purpose of constructing military housing—a quintessentially commercial endeavor. *See Republic of Iraq v. First Nat’l City Bank*, 353 F.2d 47, 50 (2d Cir. 1965) (finding commercial activity where a foreign state used expropriated funds for commercial purposes). The fact that the property was later transferred to military personnel does not transform the underlying expropriation into a sovereign act. *See Oetjen v. Central Leather Co.*, 246 U.S. 297, 302 (1918) (“The conduct of a foreign government in seizing property within its jurisdiction for public use is not a commercial act.”); *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. 324, 332 (1937) (distinguishing between acts of public authority and acts of a proprietary nature).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT IMPROPERLY REQUIRED A “DIRECT EFFECT” IN THE UNITED STATES TO TRIGGER THE COMMERCIAL ACTIVITY EXCEPTION.

The district court erred in holding that the commercial activity exception requires a “direct effect” in the United States. The plain text of § 1605(a)(2) does not impose such a requirement. *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992) (noting that the FSIA’s text does not condition jurisdiction on a direct effect in the U.S.). Rather, the Supreme Court has held that the commercial activity exception applies where the foreign state’s conduct has a “substantial and direct effect” in the United States. *See International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945) (discussing the “minimum contacts” test in the context of commercial activity).

Beg’s allegations satisfy this standard. The expropriation of his property—valued at $10 million—deprived him of a substantial asset, and the subsequent transfer of the property to military housing constitutes a commercial use. *See Republic of Iraq v. First Nat’l City Bank*, 353 F.2d at 50 (finding commercial activity where expropriated funds were used for commercial purposes). The fact that Beg now seeks compensation in U.S. courts further demonstrates the requisite connection to this jurisdiction. *See Oetjen v. Central Leather Co.*, 246 U.S. at 302 (recognizing that foreign states may be held accountable in U.S. courts for commercial acts).

III. THE DISTRICT COURT’S RULING CONTRADICTS LONGSTANDING PRECEDENT HOLDING THAT FOREIGN STATES MAY NOT INVOKE SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY FOR COMMERCIAL ACTS.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that foreign states may not invoke sovereign immunity for acts that are commercial in nature. *See, e.g., National City Bank of New York v. Republic of China*, 348 U.S. at 359; *United States v. Pink*, 315 U.S. at 228–29. The district court’s ruling—requiring a “direct effect” in the U.S. and ignoring the commercial nature of Pakistan’s expropriation—departs from this well-established precedent. *See Oetjen v. Central Leather Co.*, 246 U.S. at 302 (rejecting sovereign immunity for commercial acts); *United States v. Belmont*, 301 U.S. at 332 (same).

The Eleventh Circuit has likewise recognized that the commercial activity exception applies where a foreign state acts in a proprietary capacity. *See, e.g., Vencedora Oceanica Navigacion, S.A. v. Compagnie Nationale Air France*, 387 F.2d 473, 476 (5th Cir. 1967) (applying the commercial activity exception where a foreign state engaged in commercial shipping). The district court’s erroneous interpretation of the FSIA undermines this precedent and frustrates Congress’s intent to subject foreign states to suit for commercial acts. *See Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. at 614 (noting Congress’s intent to limit sovereign immunity for commercial activity).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Beg’s complaint. The expropriation of Beg’s property was a commercial act, and the FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
