**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing this action for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), strips immunity for claims based upon “commercial activity carried on in the United States” or upon “an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity elsewhere.” The court below failed to recognize that Pakistan’s offer of substitute land through a representative physically present in the United States constituted precisely such commercial activity.

**I. The Offer of Substitute Land Was a Commercial Activity**

Under the long-settled restrictive theory of sovereign immunity, a foreign state is immune only for sovereign or public acts, not for private commercial conduct. *Victory Transport Inc. v. Comisaria General de Abastecimientos y Transportes*, 336 F.2d 354, 360 (2d Cir. 1964) (listing immune activities as legislative, executive, internal administrative, and military, while all others are commercial). The sale or exchange of land is a transaction in which private parties regularly engage. When the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States to offer Beg an alternative parcel, it acted as a property owner, not as a sovereign. See *National City Bank of New York v. Republic of China*, 348 U.S. 356, 362 (1955) (holding that a foreign state entering the marketplace as a private actor subjects itself to the same legal consequences as a private party). Expropriation of the original land may have been a public act, but the subsequent negotiation and offer of a substitute tract was a transaction that a private person could have performed. *Victory Transport*, 336 F.2d at 360. That commercial offer was made in the United States.

**II. The Commercial Activity Occurred in the United States**

Pakistan’s representative came to the United States and there tendered the alternative parcel to Beg. This “act performed in the United States” falls squarely within the second clause of § 1605(a)(2), which requires no additional “direct effect” because the act itself is domestic. The district court’s conclusion that no commercial activity occurred ignored the undisputed fact of the representative’s presence and offer. As *The Schooner Exchange v. M’Faddon*, 11 U.S. 116, 147 (1812), recognized, foreign sovereigns are not entitled to immunity when they descend “to the level of a private individual” within the forum. Here the descent was physical: a government agent crossed the border to propose an exchange of land. That is commerce, not sovereignty. See *Berizzi Bros. Co. v. S.S. Pesaro*, 271 U.S. 562, 572 (1926) (holding that a vessel used for trading was immune only because it was owned and operated by the government; later repudiated by the restrictive theory’s adoption).

**III. Alternatively, the Commercial Activity Caused a Direct Effect in the United States**

If this Court were to find that the commercial activity occurred outside the United States, the third clause of § 1605(a)(2) applies: an act outside the United States “in connection with a commercial activity elsewhere” that “causes a direct effect in the United States.” Beg resides in the United States. Pakistan’s expropriation followed by a promise of substitute land—accepted by the Lahore High Court—created a contractual obligation that, when breached, directly harmed Beg in his American home. A direct effect occurs when the plaintiff suffers financial loss within the forum. *Petrol Shipping Corp. v. Kingdom of Greece*, 360 F.2d 103, 107 (2d Cir. 1966) (holding that nonpayment under a charter party caused direct effect in New York where payment was due). Here the offer was made and accepted in the United States; its subsequent invalidation by Pakistan’s Supreme Court left Beg uncompensated in the United States. That is a direct effect.

**IV. The District Court’s Focus on Expropriation Was Misplaced**

The district court reasoned that expropriation of land for military housing is a sovereign act. That is correct for the original taking. But the complaint does not rest solely on the expropriation. It rests on the subsequent commercial offer of an alternative parcel—an offer made in the United States. The sovereign act of taking does not insulate Pakistan from liability for the independent commercial transaction that followed. *Republic of Mexico v. Hoffman*, 324 U.S. 30, 35 (1945) (directing courts to examine the “particular activity” giving rise to the suit). A foreign state cannot enjoy immunity for its commercial dealings simply because they relate to an earlier sovereign act. *Victory Transport*, 336 F.2d at 360.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court had jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2) because Pakistan engaged in commercial activity in the United States by offering substitute land. This Court should reverse the dismissal and remand for further proceedings.
