The Constitution answers the question presented. It does so in text, structure, and precedent: states may regulate the time, place, and manner of federal elections, but they may not add to the exclusive qualifications for service in Congress that the Constitution itself enumerates. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated the qualifications for federal office as fixed by the Federal Constitution and not subject to state supplementation. Wiley v. Sinkler, 179 U.S. 58 (1900); Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. 232 (1921); Hawke v. Smith (No. 1), 253 U.S. 221 (1920).

I. The Constitution creates exclusive qualifications for Members of Congress.

Article I specifies the qualifications for Representatives and Senators. Those provisions are not mere defaults; they are the Constitution’s own terms for who may sit in Congress. In Wiley, the Court explained that the office of Representative “is created by [the Constitution] and by that alone,” and that the Constitution “adopts the qualification thus furnished” by the States only for voters, not for candidates. 179 U.S. at 65. That distinction is fatal to Arkansas Amendment 73. The State cannot redefine who may be a candidate for Congress any more than it can alter the age, citizenship, or residency requirements the Constitution sets.

Newberry underscores the same point. There, the Court discussed congressional elections as creatures of federal constitutional design, not state invention. 256 U.S. at 250-51. And in Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham, 279 U.S. 597 (1929), the Court reaffirmed that Congress’s constitutional power to judge elections and qualifications rests on the federal charter itself, not on state law. Id. at 613-15. The State’s attempt here to impose a term-limit disability on former Members of Congress is therefore an unconstitutional additional qualification.

II. States have no reserved power to add qualifications Congress did not enact.

The State will likely invoke its general police power or residual sovereignty. But the Constitution rejected that premise by creating a national legislature with national qualifications. In Hawke, the Court held that state law cannot interpose itself where the Federal Constitution assigns authority over federal constitutional matters. 253 U.S. at 231-32. Likewise, Fortson v. Morris, 385 U.S. 231 (1966), recognizes that where a state acts within its sphere, its choices are valid; but the case does not suggest that a state may invade a field the Constitution has occupied exclusively. Id. at 233-35. Here, the Arkansas amendment does not regulate procedure; it bars otherwise qualified citizens from candidacy because of prior service. That is a qualification, not a manner regulation.

The distinction matters. In Brockington v. Rhodes, 396 U.S. 41 (1969), the Court addressed ballot-access rules and did not treat them as authority for creating new substantive qualifications for congressional office. Id. at 42-43. Ballot access and constitutional eligibility are not the same thing. A state may administer ballots; it may not declare that a constitutionally qualified citizen “shall not be eligible” for Congress.

III. The federal cases on congressional elections confirm the national character of the office.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that congressional elections are governed by federal constitutional design. Wiley held that state voter qualifications are merely adopted by the Constitution for a federal election because the Constitution itself says so. 179 U.S. at 65. United States v. Bathgate, 246 U.S. 220 (1918), and United States v. Gradwell, 243 U.S. 476 (1917), likewise treat Congress and its elections as matters of federal concern defined by federal law. Bathgate, 246 U.S. at 227-29; Gradwell, 243 U.S. at 483-84. That federal character is incompatible with a patchwork of state-imposed disqualifications.

The same principle appears in Hughes v. WMCA, Inc., 379 U.S. 694 (1965), which addressed the practical administration of elections but did not suggest that States may revise constitutional qualifications for federal officeholders. Id. at 696-99. Election administration is one thing; changing eligibility is another. Arkansas did the latter.

IV. The Arkansas court’s severability analysis cannot save the federal term limits provision, but it cannot save the State’s broader theory either.

Even if sections 1 and 2 of Amendment 73 could stand under state law, section 3 cannot. A state constitutional amendment cannot override the United States Constitution. U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2; Hawke, 253 U.S. at 231-32. The Arkansas court’s effort to sever the state term limits from the federal ones cannot alter the federal constitutional defect in section 3. The defect is not merely local; it is structural. Once the State attempted to add a disqualification for Congress, it exceeded its authority. Wiley, 179 U.S. 58; Newberry, 256 U.S. 232; Barry, 279 U.S. 597.

The judgment should be affirmed.
