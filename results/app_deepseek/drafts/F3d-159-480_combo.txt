**ARGUMENT**

**A. Congress Exceeded Its Commerce Clause Authority in Enacting 18 U.S.C. § 1951 as Applied to This Local Robbery**

The Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, criminalizes robbery that “in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce.” The statute’s reach, however, is limited by the Constitution’s grant of power over interstate commerce. The evidence here—that Mr. Goodcénts purchases goods from out-of-state suppliers and that the stolen money would have been used for such purchases—establishes only an attenuated, de minimis connection to interstate commerce. Under well-settled pre-1970 Commerce Clause precedent, such a minimal effect cannot support federal jurisdiction.

The Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate only those intrastate activities that have a direct and substantial effect on interstate commerce. *United States v. E.C. Knight Co.,* 156 U.S. 1, 12 (1895) (manufacturing is not commerce, and local activities “can only be regulated by the state”). In *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States,* 295 U.S. 495, 546 (1935), the Court struck down federal regulations because the intrastate sales of poultry had “no direct relation to interstate commerce.” Similarly, *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.,* 298 U.S. 238, 304 (1936), held that mining—a “local” activity—could not be regulated under the commerce power. *United States v. Butler,* 297 U.S. 1, 68 (1936), invalidated the Agricultural Adjustment Act because it regulated agricultural production, which “is not interstate commerce.” These cases establish that the federal power does not extend to purely local conduct that only indirectly or marginally touches interstate markets.

The robbery of a single restaurant in Wichita, Kansas, is precisely the kind of local crime that states have traditionally policed. The stolen money was taken from a local establishment; the robbery’s effect on the restaurant’s ability to purchase out-of-state goods is speculative and remote. In *Hammer v. Dagenhart,* 247 U.S. 251, 276 (1918), the Court held that Congress could not ban child-made goods from interstate commerce because the “effect” on commerce was “indirect.” Here, the chain of causation is even weaker: the robbery did not involve goods moving in commerce, and the store’s future purchases are too attenuated to satisfy the commerce requirement.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Stirone v. United States,* 361 U.S. 212, 218 (1960), which interpreted the Hobbs Act, reinforces that the effect on commerce must be real and not merely hypothetical. *Stirone* held that the indictment must allege—and the government must prove—that the robbery “affected commerce.” 361 U.S. at 218. The government’s proof here—that Mr. Goodcénts is part of a national chain and that the stolen funds would have been used to buy out-of-state goods—does not demonstrate the required nexus. The restaurant’s purchases are routine, and the robbery’s impact on them is inconsequential. *Cf. Kidd v. Pearson,* 128 U.S. 1, 17 (1888) (holding that manufacturing for export is not interstate commerce because the goods are not yet in commerce). If every robbery of a business that buys from out-of-state suppliers could be federalized, then virtually every local theft would fall within the Hobbs Act, eviscerating the constitutional limits on federal power. *Coe v. Errol,* 116 U.S. 517, 528 (1886) (goods not yet in interstate commerce are subject only to state regulation).

The government’s theory—that the stolen money would have been used to purchase out-of-state goods—is no more substantial than the aggregation of local wheat farming held insufficient in *Wickard v. Filburn,* 317 U.S. 111, 125 (1942). But *Wickard* itself emphasized that the effect must be “substantial,” and the Court there upheld regulation only because homegrown wheat, when aggregated, had a “substantial” effect on the national wheat market. 317 U.S. at 128. Here, the effect of a single restaurant robbery on the interstate market for sub sandwiches or supplies is de minimis. The government cannot meet the substantial-effect test by relying on the restaurant’s general business operations.

Because the robbery of Mr. Goodcénts is a purely local crime with only an incidental and indirect connection to interstate commerce, Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause. Mr. Pearson’s convictions under § 1951 must be reversed.
