The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court's decision in Zadvydas v. Davis, while not directly on point due to its later date, guides our understanding of the statute's limitations. However, to address the question presented, we must examine the constitutional and statutory framework that underpins the Court's reasoning.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment protects individuals from arbitrary governmental actions, including indefinite detention. As the Court noted in Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), detention that is not reasonably related to a legitimate governmental purpose is unconstitutional. The principle that the government may not detain an individual indefinitely without a legitimate purpose is well-established. See Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886).

In the context of immigration, the government's authority to detain aliens is not unlimited. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government's power to exclude or deport aliens is subject to constitutional limitations. See Chae Chan Ping v. United States, 130 U.S. 581 (1889) (also known as the Chinese Exclusion Case). However, the Court has also emphasized that the detention of aliens must be reasonably related to the purpose of effectuating their removal. See United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), which, while dealing with an excludable alien, highlights the importance of the government's purpose in detaining aliens.

The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), must be interpreted in light of these constitutional principles. As Justice Jackson noted in his dissent in Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), "detention is inherently a coercive sanction" that must be justified by a legitimate governmental purpose. The government's authority to detain aliens under § 1231(a)(6) is not unfettered; it is subject to the implicit limitation that detention be for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien's removal. See Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), which, while addressing a different issue, underscores the importance of reasonable and lawful procedures in the context of deportation.

The legislative history and statutory text of § 1231(a)(6) do not explicitly authorize indefinite detention. In the absence of clear congressional intent to permit such detention, the statute should be construed to avoid serious constitutional concerns. See United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253 (1905), which highlights the importance of interpreting statutes in a manner consistent with constitutional principles.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that § 1231(a)(6) does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute must be interpreted to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, consistent with the constitutional principles that underlie the government's authority to detain aliens. The government's detention authority under § 1231(a)(6) is thus limited to a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien's removal, and indefinite detention is not authorized.
