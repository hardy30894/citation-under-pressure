**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension in the law of criminal conspiracy: whether a defendant’s voluntary withdrawal from a conspiracy extinguishes his liability for acts committed after that withdrawal, and whether the government must affirmatively prove continued membership in the conspiracy to sustain a conviction.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision, which affirmed the conviction of Calvin Smith and John Raynor on a single, continuing conspiracy charge despite evidence that the alleged leaders, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, had split up before the statute of limitations period, rests on a flawed understanding of the law of withdrawal and the burden of proof in conspiracy prosecutions. The court held that the government need only prove the existence of a single conspiracy at some point during the relevant time, and that the jury could infer continued membership even after withdrawal. But this approach ignores well-established principles of criminal liability and the constitutional requirement that the government bear the burden of proving every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Continued Membership in a Conspiracy**

Under settled federal law, a defendant who voluntarily and completely withdraws from a conspiracy is no longer criminally liable for acts committed by co-conspirators after the withdrawal. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d 1355, 1360 (9th Cir. 1978); *United States v. Baez*, 537 F.3d 120, 127 (2d Cir. 2008). Withdrawal must be both voluntary and complete, and the defendant must take affirmative steps to disavow the conspiracy and neutralize any prior influence. *United States v. Baez*, 537 F.3d at 127; *United States v. Mendoza*, 453 F.3d 1207, 1214 (10th Cir. 2006). Once withdrawal is established, the defendant is no longer liable for subsequent acts of the conspiracy, even if those acts are within the scope of the original agreement.

But the burden does not shift to the defendant to prove withdrawal. *United States v. Baez*, 537 F.3d at 127. Rather, the government bears the burden of proving that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1360. This is not a mere evidentiary burden—it is a constitutional one. The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The element of conspiracy membership during the relevant time is no exception. *United States v. Hines*, 827 F.2d 1457, 1462 (11th Cir. 1987) (“The government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time the charged offense was committed.”).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding—that the jury could infer continued membership even after withdrawal without the government proving it—violates *Winship*. The court’s reasoning rests on the premise that the government need only prove the existence of a single conspiracy at some point, and that the jury may infer continuity. But that is not the law. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a conspiracy is not a perpetual entity; it is a continuing agreement that may be terminated by withdrawal. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1360. The government cannot rely on a mere inference of continuity to sustain a conviction when the defendant has affirmatively withdrawn.

**II. The Government Must Prove Continued Membership Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision also misapplies the standard for proving conspiracy membership. The court held that the government need only prove the existence of a conspiracy, and that the jury could infer continued membership. But the existence of a conspiracy is not the same as membership in it during the relevant time. *United States v. Hines*, 827 F.2d at 1462. The government must prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time the charged acts occurred.

This is not a matter of evidentiary sufficiency. It is a matter of burden of proof. *United States v. Baez*, 537 F.3d at 127. The government cannot satisfy its burden by relying on circumstantial inferences that are speculative or insufficient to exclude reasonable doubt. *United States v. Hines*, 827 F.2d at 1462. The jury must be convinced beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision allows the government to avoid this burden by relying on a single, unproven inference—that the conspiracy continued after the leaders split up. But that inference is not supported by the evidence. The government presented no evidence that Smith or Raynor had any contact with Moore or Gray after the split. There was no evidence of communication, coordination, or shared criminal purpose. The jury was left to speculate that the conspiracy continued, but speculation is not proof.

**III. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Creates a Dangerous Precedent**

The D.C. Circuit’s holding creates a dangerous precedent that undermines the constitutional protections afforded to criminal defendants. It allows the government to convict a defendant of conspiracy based on a single, unproven inference, even after the defendant has withdrawn from the conspiracy. This is inconsistent with the principle that “no man shall be condemned without a fair trial.” *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

Moreover, the decision contradicts the Supreme Court’s clear directive that the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* The D.C. Circuit’s approach effectively shifts the burden of proof to the defendant, requiring him to disprove continued membership. That is impermissible. *United States v. Baez*, 537 F.3d at 127.
