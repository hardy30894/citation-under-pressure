The Controlled Substances Act does not violate Article I. Under the long-settled “intelligible principle” standard, Congress may confer discretion on executive officials so long as it supplies an ascertainable policy and standards to guide execution. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 425-26 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947). The CSA easily meets that test. Congress identified the objective—protecting the public from dangerous drugs—specified the criteria the Attorney General must consider, and required consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(a), (b), (c); see Yakus, 321 U.S. at 426 (upholding delegation where Congress set standards and procedures).

This Court has repeatedly upheld broad delegations in technical, rapidly changing regulatory fields. In Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 693-94 (1892), the Court sustained a tariff statute authorizing the President to suspend favorable duties upon specified findings, explaining that Congress may “vest” authority to ascertain facts on which statutory consequences depend. In Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813), the Court upheld a statute making trade restrictions turn on a presidential proclamation. In Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), the Court sustained delegation to administrative officers to exclude “inferior” tea, because Congress had fixed a legislative standard. And in United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-18 (1911), the Court held that Congress may authorize officials to fill in details and enforce regulations, even when violations carry criminal penalties.

Petitioners’ attempt to invoke Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), fails. That case struck down a statute that conferred largely standardless authority. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 430. The CSA is different: it cabins temporary scheduling to an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” requires consideration of specified factors, and imposes a short duration and notice requirements. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h); see Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Division of Department of Labor, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941) (upholding delegation where Congress directed agency to act under stated standards). The statute is therefore far closer to the delegations sustained in Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424-27, and Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250, than to the open-ended grants invalidated in 1935.

Nor does the fact that drug scheduling has criminal consequences change the analysis. Congress may attach criminal penalties to violations of validly promulgated administrative rules. Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 518-19; see also United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892) (upholding criminal enforcement of regulations where Congress authorized administrative rulemaking). The constitutional question is whether Congress supplied a sufficient standard, not whether the delegated power can affect criminal liability. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424-27.

Petitioners’ subdelegation argument likewise fails. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate functions under the drug laws. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). Long before enactment of the CSA, this Court recognized that executive officers may act through subordinates when Congress has entrusted functions to a department. See Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 318-19 (1903); United States v. George, 228 U.S. 14, 24-25 (1913). The DEA Administrator acted within that established administrative structure.

Finally, the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority is particularly appropriate because Congress faced a fast-moving problem involving new synthetic drugs and expressly designed an emergency procedure for prompt action. Rapid response in a technical field is a classic basis for upholding delegation. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-26; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250; American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946). The judgment below should be affirmed.
