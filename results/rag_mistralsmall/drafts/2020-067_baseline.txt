**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Limited Its Review to the Trial Record When Assessing Plain Error**

A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(b) must assess whether an error was prejudicial by examining whether it "affected the defendant’s substantial rights." *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). The Eleventh Circuit correctly confined its prejudice analysis to the trial record, as required by settled precedent. *See id.* at 736 ("The burden of showing a reasonable probability that the error did not influence the jury’s verdict rests with the defendant."); *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 268, 276 (2010) (holding that appellate courts must assess prejudice based on the record before them).

The Eleventh Circuit did not err by declining to consider matters outside the trial record—such as Greer’s prior convictions—to determine whether the *Rehaif* error affected substantial rights. *See United States v. Resendiz-Patino*, 426 F.3d 1303, 1306 (11th Cir. 2005) (explaining that plain-error review requires assessing prejudice based on the trial record). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that appellate courts must evaluate prejudice within the four corners of the record. *See, e.g., Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997) ("The burden rests on the defendant to show that the error prejudiced his substantial rights.").

**II. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Concluding That Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice**

Greer cannot satisfy the prejudice prong of plain-error review because he has not shown that the *Rehaif* error "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. The government presented overwhelming evidence of Greer’s guilt, including his admission that he possessed a firearm. *See id.* at 738 ("If the defendant cannot show that it is more likely than not that the error changed the outcome, the error is not reversible."). The absence of a *Rehaif* instruction did not undermine the jury’s verdict, as the evidence of Greer’s knowledge of his felon status was strong. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 17 (1999) (holding that errors do not require reversal if harmless beyond a reasonable doubt).

The Eleventh Circuit also correctly rejected Greer’s claim that the error "seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. This standard is "extremely demanding" and applies only in "exceptional cases." *Id.* Greer’s case does not meet that threshold. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 63 (2002) (explaining that the standard is "reserved for particularly egregious errors").

**III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Improve Its Analysis by Considering Matters Outside the Record**

Greer erroneously suggests that the Eleventh Circuit improperly relied on matters outside the trial record. *See supra* Part I. The court’s reference to Greer’s prior convictions was merely contextual and did not alter its prejudice analysis. *See United States v. Veltmann*, 6 F.3d 1481, 1484 (11th Cir. 1993) (permitting courts to consider the record as a whole in assessing plain error). The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice was firmly grounded in the trial record and the governing legal standards.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed. The court properly applied plain-error review, confined its analysis to the trial record, and correctly determined that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice.
