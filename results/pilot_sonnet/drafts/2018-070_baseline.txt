ARGUMENT

I. OCSLA Incorporates State Law Only to Fill Gaps Left by Federal Law

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) declares that "[t]he Constitution and laws...of the United States are extended" to the outer Continental Shelf (OCS) "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). This Court has recognized that Congress modeled this provision on the federal enclave framework, under which state law is borrowed only "as a supplement," not as an independently operative body of law. Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 365 (1969) (explaining that Congress intended the OCS to be treated "as an upland federal enclave"). OCSLA's choice-of-law clause, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), borrows state law "[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with" federal law—a formulation drawn directly from the Assimilative Crimes Act and analogous enclave statutes, which this Court has explained operate only to "fill in gaps" in federal coverage. See Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355–57 (tracing OCSLA's structure to the "federal enclave" model of adopting local law "as federal law" only where federal law is silent).

This Court confirmed that understanding in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), describing OCSLA as adopting adjacent-state law "as surrogate federal law" applicable to the subsoil and seabed of the OCS. Id. at 480. The word "surrogate" is decisive: state law does not apply on the OCS in its own right, as it would in a diversity case within a State's territory. It applies only as a stand-in for absent federal law—that is, only where there would otherwise be a "significant void or gap" in the governing federal scheme. Cont'l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). The Fifth Circuit—which has the longest experience adjudicating OCSLA disputes because most drilling platforms lie off the Louisiana and Texas coasts—has consistently required a genuine gap before assimilating state law, precisely because the exclusive-federal-enclave model that Rodrigue endorsed presupposes that state law fills interstices rather than displacing or duplicating federal regulation. See Cont'l Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036.

The Ninth Circuit's alternative reading—that state law applies whenever it is not "inconsistent" with federal law, without regard to whether federal law already occupies the field—cannot be squared with the enclave framework this Court adopted in Rodrigue. If any state law that does not directly conflict with a federal statute may operate independently on the OCS, then state law would function not as a "surrogate" filling gaps but as a freestanding regulatory layer operating alongside comprehensive federal law. That result is foreclosed by Gulf Offshore's characterization of state law as merely "surrogate federal law," 453 U.S. at 480, and it disregards the enclave analogy on which Rodrigue rested its entire analysis, 395 U.S. at 365.

II. The FLSA Comprehensively Occupies the Field of Wage-and-Hour Regulation on the OCS, Leaving No Gap for California Law

The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., is a detailed, nationwide regulatory scheme governing minimum wages, overtime, and related employment conditions. Congress built into the FLSA its own savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), specifying precisely when and how state law may supplement federal minimum-wage and overtime standards—for employment occurring within state territorial boundaries. Congress did not extend that savings clause to the OCS, an area Congress separately and specifically brought under exclusive federal jurisdiction through OCSLA. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1).

Because the FLSA already speaks comprehensively to minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements—the precise subject matter of respondent's claims—there is no "significant void" for California law to fill. Cont'l Oil, 417 S.2d at 1036. Respondent's claims concerning meal breaks and overtime pay are simply California's version of subjects the FLSA already regulates; they are not claims addressing some employment condition about which federal law is silent. Permitting state law to operate merely because it offers greater protection than the FLSA—rather than because federal law is silent—would convert OCSLA's narrow gap-filling mechanism into a vehicle for importing the entire body of each adjacent State's employment code onto the OCS, so long as the state code does not flatly contradict federal text. That is not the "surrogate" role OCSLA envisions. Gulf Offshore, 453 U.S. at 480.

III. Congress's Structural Choices Confirm that Federal Law, Not State Law, Governs Comprehensively on the OCS

Congress rejected incorporating the OCS into the boundaries of adjacent States and instead placed it under exclusive federal jurisdiction, extending only such state law as federal law failed to address. See Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355–57. That structural choice reflects a considered judgment that uniform federal standards, not a patchwork of California, Texas, Louisiana, and Alaska wage laws, should govern activity on the Shelf. The Ninth Circuit's test undermines that uniformity by making the extent of a platform worker's rights depend on the "adjacent State," even though OCSLA's very purpose was to avoid such balkanized regulation of a zone under federal sovereignty. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1).

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that OCSLA permits the application of state law only to fill a gap in federal law, and because the FLSA comprehensively addresses wage-and-hour standards, no such gap exists here. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
