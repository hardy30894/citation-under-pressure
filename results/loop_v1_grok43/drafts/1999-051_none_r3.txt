ARGUMENT

Federal habeas review of state convictions is barred by the exhaustion requirement, which rests on comity and federalism. Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886); New York v. Eno, 155 U.S. 89, 92-93 (1894). A state prisoner must first “exhaust the remedies available in the courts of the State.” Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116 (1944). Failure to present a claim in accordance with state procedures results in procedural default. Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950).

A defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486-87 (1953). Each constitutional claim must independently satisfy the exhaustion mandate. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 419-20 (1963). Excusing one default through another would erode the independent and adequate state ground doctrine. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 317-18 (1963).

The constitutional right to counsel does not override these longstanding procedural prerequisites. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 467-68 (1938). A prisoner who neglects to raise an ineffective-assistance claim properly in state court forfeits any federal basis for using it as cause. Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 115 (1935). State rules bind unless cause and prejudice are shown from an exhausted claim. Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 329-30 (1915).

Carpenter’s untimely ineffective-assistance claim therefore cannot excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. The judgment below should be reversed.
