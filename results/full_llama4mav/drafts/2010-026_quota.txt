The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard for Exemption 2 is unfounded and contradicts the statutory language and Supreme Court precedent.

Exemption 2 exempts from disclosure matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this exemption narrowly, holding that it applies to materials that are "used for internal purposes" and are not of "genuine and significant public interest." Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 363-64 (1976). The Court has also emphasized that the exemptions under FOIA are "explicitly exclusive" and must be "narrowly construed." U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting Adm'r FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on the D.C. Circuit's decision in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C.Cir.1981) (en banc), is misplaced. While Crooker did adopt the "predominantly internal" standard, it did so in the context of law enforcement materials, which are not at issue here. Moreover, the Ninth Circuit's own precedent in Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir.1980), held that "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2." Id. at 656. This decision does not support the broader "predominantly internal" standard adopted by the Ninth Circuit.

Furthermore, the ESQD arc maps at issue in this case are not "related solely" to internal personnel rules and practices. They are used to design and construct ammunition storage facilities and to mitigate risks to the surrounding community. As such, they are of significant public interest and are not exempt from disclosure under Exemption 2. See Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir.1997) (holding that goshawk nesting site data does not "relate 'solely,' or even predominantly, 'to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency'").

The Navy's failure to classify the ESQD arc maps under Exemption 1 also undermines its claim that they are exempt from disclosure. Exemption 1 specifically exempts classified matters "kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). If the disclosure of the ESQD arc maps is as dangerous as the Navy claims, it is acting irresponsibly by not classifying them. See Lahr v. NTSB, 569 F.3d 964, 973 (9th Cir.2009) (emphasizing the importance of narrowly construing FOIA exemptions).

In addition, Exemption 7(F) is inapplicable because the documents were not "compiled for law enforcement purposes." Church of Scientology v. U.S. Dep't of Army, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir.1979). The Navy's use of the ESQD arc maps for designing and constructing ammunition storage facilities is not an "adjudicative or enforcement purpose." Id.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to expand Exemption 2 to protect information that the Navy has not seen fit to classify distorts Congress's careful balance between the public's right to access information and the government's need to protect certain information. See John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). The court's decision is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's instruction that FOIA exemptions "must be narrowly construed" and are "explicitly exclusive." Rose, 425 U.S. at 361; Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. at 151 (quoting Robertson, 422 U.S. at 262).

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit erred in exempting the ESQD arc maps from disclosure under Exemption 2. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is unfounded, and the documents are not "related solely" to internal personnel rules and practices. The Navy's failure to classify the documents under Exemption 1 and the inapplicability of Exemption 7(F) further support disclosure. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
