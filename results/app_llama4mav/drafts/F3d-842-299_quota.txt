Argument

I. The District Court Lacked Subject Matter Jurisdiction Over This Case

The district court's denial of the Pressls' motion to remand was erroneous because it lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this case. APCO's removal was based on the assertion that the federal district court had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p due to FERC's licensing of the Smith Mountain hydroelectric project. However, the Pressls' complaint raises only state law claims regarding the interpretation of the flowage easement and their rights under it.

The Supreme Court has held that federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 requires a substantial federal question. See Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 312 (2005). In this case, the Pressls' complaint does not raise a federal question; it seeks a declaration of their rights under a state law property instrument—the flowage easement. The mere fact that APCO operates the hydroelectric project under a FERC license does not transform the Pressls' state law claims into federal ones. See Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804, 813 (1986) (holding that a state law claim does not arise under federal law simply because it involves a federal issue).

Furthermore, 16 U.S.C. § 825p does not provide an independent basis for federal jurisdiction. See California v. FERC, 495 U.S. 490, 497 (1990) (noting that § 825p is a jurisdictional statute that does not create a private right of action). The district court's reliance on § 825p was misplaced, as it only grants jurisdiction over suits to enforce FERC orders, not to interpret state law property rights. See id.

The Fourth Circuit has held that a federal court must strictly construe its removal jurisdiction. See Mulcahey v. Columbia Organic Chems. Co., 29 F.3d 148, 151 (4th Cir. 1994). In this case, APCO failed to meet its burden of establishing federal jurisdiction. See Dixon v. Coburg Dairy, Inc., 369 F.3d 811, 816 (4th Cir. 2004) (en banc) (holding that the party seeking removal bears the burden of establishing federal jurisdiction).

II. The District Court Erred in Dismissing the Pressls' Complaint

Even if the district court had jurisdiction, it erred in dismissing the Pressls' complaint. The district court's analysis of the flowage easement under Virginia law was flawed. Under Virginia law, the interpretation of an easement is a matter of contract interpretation. See Pyramid Dev., L.L.C. v. D & J Assocs., 262 Va. 750, 754 (2001). The easement grants APCO the right to remove structures below the 800-foot contour, but it also reserves to the landowners the right to "possess and use said premises in any manner not inconsistent with" APCO's flowage easement.

The district court failed to consider the scope of the Pressls' reserved rights under the easement. See Cushman Va. Corp. v. Barnes, 204 Va. 245, 252 (1963) (holding that an easement grantor retains all rights not explicitly granted to the easement holder). The Pressls' complaint alleges that their proposed dock is consistent with the maintenance of APCO's hydroelectric project, and the district court should have considered this allegation in interpreting the easement.

Moreover, the district court erred in holding that the Pressls needed to raise their complaints with FERC prior to filing suit. See First Iowa Hydro-Elec. Coop. v. FPC, 328 U.S. 152, 164 (1946) (holding that FERC's jurisdiction is limited to regulating hydroelectric projects, not resolving private property disputes). The Pressls' complaint raises a state law property dispute that is not within FERC's exclusive jurisdiction. See id.

In sum, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction over this case, and even if it had jurisdiction, it erred in dismissing the Pressls' complaint. This Court should vacate the district court's judgment and remand with instructions to remand the case to state court. See 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c); see also In re Blackwater Sec. Consulting, LLC, 460 F.3d 576, 584 (4th Cir. 2006) (holding that a federal court must remand a case to state court if it lacks subject matter jurisdiction).
