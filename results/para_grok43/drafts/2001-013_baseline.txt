ARGUMENT

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act provides that nothing in the Act applies to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Preemption therefore turns on whether the Coast Guard has in fact exercised that authority with respect to the particular working conditions at issue. See, e.g., Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825, 827 (5th Cir. 1983) (noting that overlapping regulation is forbidden only where another agency has exercised its authority).

The Coast Guard’s regulations governing uninspected vessels address only discrete matters such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and alerting equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq.; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. Those provisions do not prescribe or enforce standards for the well-control and blowout-prevention procedures that gave rise to the citations here. Where an agency “has not promulgated … regulations” covering the relevant conditions, section 4(b)(1) leaves OSHA’s jurisdiction intact. Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120, 1122 (5th Cir. 1980).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on Clary and Donovan is misplaced. Both decisions addressed inspected vessels or working conditions already subject to comprehensive Coast Guard standards. Neither opinion considered whether the Coast Guard had exercised authority over uninspected vessels with respect to the specific hazards involved. “The broad language of Clary does not turn on any such distinction,” and the record here shows no exercise of authority over the cited conditions. Because the Coast Guard has not acted, section 4(b)(1) does not displace OSHA.

Rig 52 was therefore a “workplace” subject to the Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(a), and the citations were within OSHA’s jurisdiction.
