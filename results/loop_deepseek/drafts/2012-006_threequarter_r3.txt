**ARGUMENT**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a charged conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period. This fundamental principle, rooted in the presumption of innocence and the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, compels reversal. *See Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) (proof of every fact necessary to constitute the crime is required); *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525 (1958) (the government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt); *Holland v. United States*, 348 U.S. 121, 138 (1954) (burden of proving guilt rests on the prosecution). A conspiracy is a continuing offense; it must extend into the limitations period for a conviction to stand. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607–08 (1910) (conspiracy is a continuing offense and evidence must show its continuity). Consequently, the jury must find that the defendant remained a member of the conspiracy within the statutory period.

Withdrawal from a conspiracy, when it occurs before the limitations period, does not shift the burden of persuasion to the defendant. Rather, it negates an essential element of the government's case—membership during the limitations period. This Court has long recognized that a defendant's unilateral acts may sever his connection to the conspiracy, *United States v. Rabinowich*, 238 U.S. 78, 86 (1915) (withdrawal requires affirmative act), but the government retains the burden to prove that the defendant was a member during the limitations period. Critically, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that, notwithstanding any evidence of withdrawal, the defendant remained a member during the relevant time. *See Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (a statutory presumption may not "undercut the requirement that the prosecution must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt"); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274 (1952) (the government must prove an essential ingredient of the offense and no presumption can supply that proof).

The lower court erred by effectively requiring petitioners to disprove their membership during the limitations period. The government's evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy split up before the limitations period began. That evidence, at a minimum, created a reasonable doubt as to whether petitioners were still part of any single, ongoing conspiracy. The jury should have been instructed that the government had the burden of proving membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt, and that any evidence of withdrawal—even if insufficient to constitute an affirmative defense—could be considered in determining whether the government had carried its burden. *See United States v. Liss*, 105 F.2d 144, 146 (2d Cir. 1939) (government must prove the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and defendant was then a party to it); *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 844 (2d Cir. 1939) (same). The court's failure to place this burden squarely on the government cannot be excused as harmless error, because the jury could have credited the withdrawal evidence and still convicted on the mistaken belief that the defendants bore the burden of proving they were not members during the limitations period.

Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed, and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions on the burden of persuasion.
