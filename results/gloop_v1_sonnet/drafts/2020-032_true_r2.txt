**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Revision of GGC's Policies Eliminated the Live Controversy That Article III Requires.**

Article III confines federal courts to the resolution of genuine, ongoing disputes, not to the adjudication of policies that no longer govern the parties' relations. This Court has measured the existence of a live controversy by whether the challenged restriction continues to threaten the plaintiff's conduct. In *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), the Court explained that "[i]f a regulation were adopted by school officials forbidding discussion of the Vietnam conflict, or the expression by any student of opposition to it anywhere on school property except as part of a prescribed classroom exercise, it would be obvious that the regulation would violate the constitutional rights of students," *id.* at 509–10, and the Court's review proceeded because that very regulation still governed the students before it. Here, by contrast, GGC repealed the speech-zone policy and the challenged provision of its Student Code of Conduct, replacing them with a policy permitting expression throughout campus subject only to limited exceptions. There is no regulation left to forecast disruption of, and no live threat left to enjoin.

This Court's practice of resolving constitutional challenges to campus and public-forum restrictions has always proceeded on the premise that the offending rule remains operative. In *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105 (1943), the Court invalidated a licensing ordinance that continued to burden the "distribution of religious literature," *id.* at 108–09, because the ordinance was still being enforced against the petitioners' activities. In *Jones v. Opelika*, 316 U.S. 584 (1942), review turned on the continuing operation of the municipal ordinances under which petitioners remained subject to prosecution. Where, as here, the offending policy is gone, the predicate for that mode of review disappears as well.

Nor does petitioners' underlying grievance survive merely because it once implicated important interests. In *Zwicker v. Boll*, 391 U.S. 353 (1968), this Court summarily affirmed the dismissal of a challenge to a state disorderly-conduct statute notwithstanding the students' allegation that they had been engaged only in peaceful and constitutionally protected protest activities, because the posture of the case did not present the kind of live, continuing threat that would warrant equitable intervention. A policy that GGC has affirmatively repealed, with no reasonable basis to expect its return, presents even less occasion for continued adjudication than the statute in *Zwicker*.

**II. A Bare Request for Nominal Damages, Untethered to Any Actual Injury, Cannot Revive an Otherwise Moot Dispute.**

This Court's damages jurisprudence has consistently distinguished between damages that compensate a demonstrated injury and requests that are not tied to any showing of loss. In *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), and *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), the Court examined damages awards only where a jury had found, on a developed record, a genuine, litigated injury—there, a "verdict for $60,000 in general damages and for $3,000,000 in punitive damages," later reduced by remittitur, *Curtis Publishing*, 388 U.S. at 133—not a nominal sum requested as a placeholder for a right rather than for any actual harm. Petitioners' complaint contains no comparable allegation of completed, compensable injury; it seeks only a token award divorced from any demonstrated loss.

The justiciability of a claim, moreover, depends on the presence of parties with a concrete stake in the outcome, not simply on the assertion of a constitutional interest in the abstract. In *Board of Education of Central School District No. 1 v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), this Court confirmed that the threshold question of a party's ability to press a constitutional claim depends on a genuine, adverse stake in the controversy—there, whether the school boards had "standing to attack the validity of a state statute" at all. *Id.* at 241. A nominal-damages request that is not moored to any real, adjudicable injury fails that same test of adverseness. Likewise, in *Monroe v. Board of Commissioners*, 391 U.S. 450 (1968), the relief this Court approved was calibrated to a demonstrated constitutional violation and its concrete effects on identified students, not to the vindication of a right in the abstract once the offending practice had been corrected.

Finally, *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), and *Millsaps College v. City of Jackson*, 275 U.S. 129 (1927), illustrate that this Court reviews the operation of a challenged policy as it actually affects the parties' concrete legal relations—a released-time program actively in force, *Zorach*, 343 U.S. at 308, or a tax presently assessed, *Millsaps College*, 275 U.S. at 131—not policies that have already been abandoned. Petitioners identify no comparable ongoing legal relation here. Once GGC's speech-zone policy and disciplinary provision were rescinded, and once no compensable injury was pled, nothing remained but a request to adjudicate a superseded rule for its own sake. Article III does not permit that exercise, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
