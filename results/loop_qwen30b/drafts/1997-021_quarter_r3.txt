**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational, inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board’s rule—requiring an employer to possess “objective indications” sufficient to raise a “reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll—creates a legal paradox: it permits an employer to conduct a poll only when it already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, thereby rendering the poll superfluous. This standard is not only illogical but also contrary to settled precedent, which affirms that an employer may lawfully poll employees to test union support when it has *substantial, objective evidence* of a loss of majority support, even if that evidence falls short of justifying immediate withdrawal. The Board’s contrary rule is arbitrary and capricious, and its factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The National Labor Relations Act does not mandate a rigid evidentiary threshold for polling. Rather, it entrusts the Board with the authority to “develop and apply national labor policy” in a manner consistent with the Act’s purposes. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 590 (1969). The Board’s current standard, however, fails to serve the Act’s goal of promoting stable collective bargaining relationships. By requiring an employer to have already met the standard for withdrawal of recognition before polling, the Board effectively eliminates the utility of polling as a tool for resolving uncertainty. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the purpose of a poll is to determine the truth of a union’s claim of a majority.” *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). If the employer already has sufficient grounds to withdraw recognition, the poll adds no new information. The Board’s rule thus undermines the very purpose it purports to serve.

The Board’s standard is also inconsistent with the well-established principle that an employer may conduct a poll when it has *substantial, objective evidence* of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient to justify immediate withdrawal. In *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139 (5th Cir. 1981), the court held that an employer may poll employees “if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” provided the poll complies with procedural safeguards. *Id.* at 1145. The court emphasized that the purpose of polling is to “test the validity of the union’s claim of majority support,” and that such testing is permissible when the employer has “substantial, objective evidence” of a decline in support. *Id.* This standard was reaffirmed in *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295 (9th Cir. 1984), where the court held that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for an employer in “sincere doubt” about a union’s majority status, and that the employer may poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal. *Id.* at 1299.

The Sixth Circuit similarly upheld the permissibility of polling under a lower standard in *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863 (6th Cir. 1982). There, the court held that an employer may poll employees “if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is insufficient in itself to justify withdrawal.” *Id.* at 867. The court rejected the Board’s argument that the employer must have a “reasonable doubt” before polling, noting that the Board’s standard “would render polling useless” and “undermine the employer’s ability to test union sentiment.” *Id.* at 868. The court concluded that the Board’s finding that the employer lacked a good faith doubt was not supported by substantial evidence. *Id.* The same is true here.

The Board’s current standard is further undermined by its failure to recognize that the presumption of continuing majority support is rebuttable after a reasonable period, particularly in the context of a successor employer. In *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 590 (1969), the Supreme Court held that an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support, but that the presumption may be overcome by objective evidence of a loss of support. *Id.* The Court emphasized that the presumption is not absolute and that “the employer may overcome the presumption through objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” *Id.* This language confirms that the standard for polling should be no higher than the standard for withdrawal of recognition, and that the Board’s current rule—requiring a higher threshold for polling than for withdrawal—is irrational.

Moreover, the Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good faith doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The record shows that seven employees made statements repudiating the union, and that two employees—Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr—reported that the union lacked support among their coworkers. Although the ALJ discounted these statements, the Board failed to provide a reasoned explanation for doing so. *See* *NLRB v. Wyman-Gordon Co.*, 394 U.S. 759, 766 (1969) (agency’s findings must be supported by “substantial evidence” and “reasoned analysis”). The Board’s refusal to credit these statements, particularly in light of the lack of contrary evidence, was arbitrary and capricious.
