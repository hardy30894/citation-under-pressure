ARGUMENT

The text of 18 U.S.C. §875(c) does not specify a mens rea element. Under the settled principle that criminal statutes require proof of criminal intent unless Congress clearly indicates otherwise, this Court has long held that intent is an element of every crime. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The presumption of mens rea applies with full force to statutes that criminalize speech, because the First Amendment demands that speakers receive fair warning before their words are punished. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707–08 (1969). The Third Circuit’s objective standard—asking only whether a reasonable speaker would foresee his statement being interpreted as a threat—dispenses with the subjective intent that this Court has consistently required.

This Court has applied the presumption of intent to threat statutes. In *United States v. Russell*, 255 U.S. 138, 143 (1921), the Court interpreted a statute prohibiting threats against the President and held that the government must prove the defendant “intended to threaten.” The Court explained that the statute “requires an intent to do the thing which the statute prohibits.” *Id.* Similarly, in *Pierce v. United States*, 252 U.S. 239, 244 (1920), the Court held that a conviction for threatening a federal officer required proof that the defendant “intended to make a threat.” These decisions establish that subjective intent is an essential element of a threat offense.

The same principle governs §875(c). In *Watts*, the Court considered a conviction under 18 U.S.C. §871 for threatening the President. The Court held that the statute requires proof of a “true threat,” which it defined as a “serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.” 394 U.S. at 708. The Court reversed the conviction because the defendant’s statement was “political hyperbole” rather than a genuine expression of intent. *Id.* at 708. The *Watts* Court did not adopt an objective test; it examined the speaker’s actual intent. The Third Circuit’s objective standard would criminalize statements that a reasonable person might misinterpret as threats, even if the speaker had no intent to threaten. That result is incompatible with *Watts*.

The First Amendment independently requires a subjective intent standard. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), this Court held that even advocacy of violence is protected unless it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” That standard requires proof that the speaker intended to incite. The true threats exception must be similarly limited. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942), recognized that the “fighting words” exception applies only to words that “by their very utterance inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace.” That definition inherently requires an intent to provoke. The same logic applies to true threats: only statements made with the intent to threaten fall outside the First Amendment’s protection.

This Court has consistently required subjective intent when speech is criminalized. In *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 307–08 (1940), the Court reversed a breach-of-peace conviction because the defendant did not intend to incite violence. The Court stated that “the offense is not committed unless the defendant’s conduct constitutes a clear and present danger of a breach of the peace.” *Id.* at 308. That inquiry turns on the speaker’s intent. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), the Court held that a breach-of-peace ordinance could not punish speech that “stirs people to anger” unless the speaker intended to provoke violence. These cases confirm that the First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a threat.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also conflicts with the general presumption against strict liability in criminal law. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250. This Court has recognized that “the existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence.” *Id.* at 252. The Court has applied that rule to statutes that are silent on intent, *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–52 (1922), and has required clear congressional intent to dispense with mens rea. No such intent appears in §875(c). The statute does not use language like “willfully” or “knowingly,” but that silence does not authorize courts to adopt an objective standard. *Morissette* teaches that courts must presume intent, not invent a substitute.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the fear of violence is misplaced. The objective standard chills protected speech because speakers cannot know whether a reasonable person will misinterpret their words. *Watts* recognized that “the statute initially requires the Government to prove a true ‘threat,’” and that “political hyperbole” is not a threat. 394 U.S. at 708. A subjective intent requirement ensures that only speakers who actually intend to threaten are punished, while protecting those who engage in hyperbole, rap lyrics, or other expressive conduct. The First Amendment demands no less.

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten.
