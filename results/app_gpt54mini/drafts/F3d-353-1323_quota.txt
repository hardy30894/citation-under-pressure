## Argument

The district court erred in dismissing this action for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. Under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, a foreign state is immune only unless a statutory exception applies. 28 U.S.C. §§ 1604, 1605; *Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 355 (1993). The commercial-activity exception reaches cases “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state,” or upon “an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere” that causes a “direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2); *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 611–12 (1992).

### I. Pakistan’s post-expropriation conduct was commercial in nature.

The district court treated the case as a noncommercial taking. That was error because Beg’s claim is not limited to the sovereign act of expropriation; it also challenges the later conduct of Pakistan and Punjab in offering substitute land, representing that title existed, and then failing to provide the promised compensation. Whether conduct is commercial turns on “the nature of the course of conduct or particular transaction or act,” not its purpose. 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d); *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614. A state acts commercially when it exercises powers “that can also be exercised by private citizens,” rather than sovereign powers unique to government. *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 360.

A government’s agreement to transfer property or provide compensation is paradigmatically commercial. In *Weltover*, the Court held that issuing and restructuring debt instruments was commercial because such financial obligations are the kind private parties routinely undertake. 504 U.S. at 614–15. Likewise, in *Republic of Argentina v. NML Capital, Ltd.*, 573 U.S. 134, 145 (2014), the Court recognized that sovereign debt instruments can constitute commercial activity because they involve market-like obligations. Here, the alleged offer of an alternative parcel in exchange for the taking of Beg’s land is the kind of property exchange private actors can make. The fact that the offer was made by government officials does not make the transaction sovereign; the relevant question is the nature of the act, not the identity of the actor. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 614.

The district court’s contrary conclusion improperly collapsed Beg’s compensation claim into the initial taking. But the FSIA directs courts to examine the conduct on which the suit is “based.” *Nelson*, 507 U.S. at 356–57. Beg’s complaint is based, at least in substantial part, on Pakistan’s later promise of compensation and its failure to honor that promise. Those allegations concern a commercial undertaking to settle a property dispute by tendering land in exchange for title relinquishment. That is not a sovereign prerogative; it is a contractual exchange.

### II. Pakistan’s conduct had a direct effect in the United States because Beg sought and was denied monetary compensation here.

The district court also erred in finding no direct effect in the United States. Under *Weltover*, an effect is “direct” if it follows as an “immediate consequence of the defendant’s . . . activity,” and it need not be substantial or foreseeable. 504 U.S. at 618. The Court rejected any requirement that the effect be the “most direct” one. Id. at 618–19.

Beg alleges that Pakistan’s refusal to compensate him left him uncompensated for property worth $10 million and forced him to seek monetary relief in federal court in the United States. That deprivation is a direct financial injury to a U.S. forum plaintiff seeking payment here. Courts have repeatedly treated the failure to pay money owed as a direct effect where the obligation was to be satisfied in the United States or where the nonpayment produced immediate financial loss in this country. *Hanil Bank v. PT. Bank Negara Indonesia (Persero)*, 148 F.3d 127, 132–33 (2d Cir. 1998); *Princz v. Federal Republic of Germany*, 26 F.3d 1166, 1172–73 (D.C. Cir. 1994). At minimum, Pakistan’s refusal to provide compensation had its immediate consequence in Beg’s inability to obtain redress in the United States for the loss of his property.

The district court’s emphasis on the location of the land misses the point. An act outside the United States can have a direct effect here even if the underlying property is abroad. *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618; *Voest-Alpine Trading USA Corp. v. Banco de Desarollo Comercial de El Salvador, S.A.*, 142 F.3d 887, 894–95 (5th Cir. 1998). The question is where the legal and financial consequences are felt. Beg’s complaint alleges that those consequences are felt here because he seeks compensation in a U.S. federal forum and was denied the payment promised by Pakistan.

### III. At minimum, Beg was entitled to jurisdictional discovery before dismissal.

Even if the Court concludes the record is incomplete, dismissal was premature. A plaintiff need only make a nonfrivolous showing that an FSIA exception may apply to obtain jurisdictional discovery. *EM Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 473 F.3d 463, 486 (2d Cir. 2007); *Texas Trading & Milling Corp. v. Federal Republic of Nigeria*, 647 F.2d 300, 315–16 (2d Cir. 1981). Beg’s allegations of an in-country compensation proposal, governmental acceptance by a high court, and later repudiation easily warranted discovery into the nature of the transaction and its effects.

### Conclusion

Because Beg’s complaint alleges commercial conduct by Pakistan connected to a compensation exchange and a direct financial effect in the United States, the commercial-activity exception applies. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
