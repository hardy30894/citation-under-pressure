**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Possess Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Law Malpractice Claims That Merely Implicate Patent Law Issues.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim, which requires proof of a "case within a case" involving an underlying patent infringement suit, necessarily "arises under" federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. The Supreme Court has long held that federal question jurisdiction under § 1338(a) is not triggered merely because a state-law claim requires reference to or resolution of a federal patent issue. *See Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) ("A suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws."). Rather, the federal issue must be the "real and substantial" controversy upon which the entire case depends. *Id.*

**A. The Federal Patent Issue in This Case Is Not "Substantial" Within the Meaning of This Court's Precedents.**

This Court has consistently held that a federal issue is not "substantial" when it arises only as an incidental or hypothetical element of a state-law claim. In *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936), the Court explained that federal jurisdiction requires "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." A state malpractice claim that requires a plaintiff to prove that he would have prevailed in an underlying patent suit does not present a substantial federal issue; it merely requires a state court to apply settled patent law to a hypothetical set of facts. *See also Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199 (1921) (federal jurisdiction exists only when the "controversy depends upon the construction or effect of the Constitution, or some law of the United States").

Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception would have saved Minton's patent from the on-sale bar—is not a live controversy between the parties. It is a historical question that must be resolved to determine causation in a state malpractice action. This Court has never held that such a backward-looking, hypothetical inquiry constitutes a "substantial" federal issue. *See Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908) (federal question must appear on the face of the well-pleaded complaint, not as a potential defense or hypothetical).

**B. The Federal Issue Is Not "Actually Disputed" Between the Parties.**

The requirement that the federal issue be "actually disputed" is rooted in this Court's longstanding insistence that federal jurisdiction cannot be manufactured by the mere presence of a federal question. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 113 ("To bring a case within the statute, a right or immunity created by the Constitution or laws of the United States must be an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff's cause of action."). In this case, the parties do not dispute the meaning of the experimental use exception or the on-sale bar. The only dispute is whether Minton's attorneys were negligent in failing to raise that defense. The federal patent law is not "actually disputed"; it is merely a backdrop against which the state-law negligence claim is evaluated. *See Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 570 (federal jurisdiction does not lie when the federal issue is "merely incidental" to the state-law claim).

**C. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

This Court has long recognized that state courts are fully competent to adjudicate state-law claims that require reference to federal law. *See Claflin v. Houseman*, 93 U.S. 130, 136 (1876) ("The laws of the United States are laws in the several states, and their just interpretation and enforcement, in cases not involving a federal question, is as much a duty of the state courts as of the federal courts."). Allowing federal courts to exercise exclusive jurisdiction over state malpractice claims that merely touch on patent law would "disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities" that Congress has carefully maintained. *See Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005) (citing *Mottley*, 211 U.S. at 152). State courts routinely resolve questions of federal law in the course of adjudicating state-law claims, and there is no reason to treat patent law differently. *See also The Mayor v. Cooper*, 73 U.S. 29, 30 (1867) (state courts have concurrent jurisdiction over federal questions unless Congress expressly provides otherwise).

**D. The Federal Circuit's Contrary Approach Is Inconsistent with This Court's Precedents.**

The Federal Circuit's decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), are irreconcilable with this Court's precedents. Those decisions held that any state malpractice claim requiring proof of an underlying patent infringement case necessarily "arises under" federal patent law. But this Court has never adopted such a broad rule. To the contrary, in *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988), the Court held that § 1338(a) jurisdiction extends only to cases where "patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." A state malpractice claim does not "arise under" patent law simply because it requires proof of a hypothetical patent case; the patent issue is merely a predicate fact, not the source of the plaintiff's right to relief. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 115 ("A suit does not arise under a law of the United States unless the law is the source of the right sued upon.").

**II. The State Courts Have Concurrent Jurisdiction Over This Malpractice Claim.**

Because the federal patent issue in this case is not substantial, not actually disputed, and would not be disturbed by state-court adjudication, the state courts retain concurrent jurisdiction. *See Claflin*, 93 U.S. at 136. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in dismissing this case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for a decision on the merits.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that the federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over state-law legal malpractice claims that require proof of an underlying patent infringement case. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed.
