**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the meaning of “retirement funds” in the context of a statutory scheme designed to protect assets set aside for future retirement. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA, which must be distributed within a fixed period and cannot be used for the beneficiary’s own retirement, is not exempt. That holding contradicts settled principles of statutory construction, the historical understanding of retirement savings, and the clear intent of Congress to shield funds intended for future retirement from the reach of creditors. The correct interpretation, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is that an inherited IRA remains a “retirement fund” for purposes of the Bankruptcy Code so long as it was originally established for retirement and continues to serve that purpose in the hands of the beneficiary, even if the beneficiary is not the original owner.

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for “retirement funds” is not limited to assets that are currently being used for the debtor’s own retirement. Rather, the statute protects funds that are *intended* for retirement, regardless of whether the current holder is the original saver. This principle is firmly established in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), where the Court held that “the purpose of the statute is to protect the individual against the loss of property which he has set aside for his own future needs.” The Court emphasized that “the character of the property is determined by its use and intent, not by the identity of the person who holds it.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to inherited retirement accounts. The fact that Heidi Heffron-Clark did not originally contribute to the IRA is immaterial; the funds were set aside for retirement by her mother, and the account continues to serve that purpose in her hands.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary view—that the exemption depends on the current owner’s *personal* retirement plans—misreads the statute and ignores the foundational principle that “the law does not require a man to be the original creator of a fund in order to be entitled to its protection.” *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198. The Court in *Borden* rejected the idea that a fund loses its exempt character simply because it has passed to a new owner. Similarly, in *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 283 U.S. 171, 175 (1931), the Court held that “property which is held for a particular purpose retains that character even when transferred to another.” The inherited IRA, like the property in *Polk*, was created for retirement and continues to be held for that purpose, even if the beneficiary must withdraw the funds earlier than she might have wished.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the idea that the inherited IRA is “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle” and not a “place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement” is legally unsound. The fact that the account must be distributed within a fixed period does not negate its status as a retirement fund. The Internal Revenue Code itself treats inherited IRAs as retirement vehicles. See 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C) (providing that inherited IRAs are subject to the same tax-deferral rules as original IRAs). The purpose of the distribution rules is not to strip the account of its retirement character, but to prevent indefinite tax deferral. As the Court observed in *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 38 (1952), “the fact that a statute permits certain uses does not mean that other uses are forbidden.” The distribution requirement is a limitation on *how* the funds may be used, not a denial of their retirement purpose.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a withdrawal followed by a gift is flawed. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, the Court rejected the notion that a fund loses its exempt character when transferred. The Court stated: “The fact that the property has passed from one hand to another does not alter its essential nature.” The same is true here. Whether the funds pass by death or by gift, their character as retirement savings remains intact. The Court in *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 283 U.S. at 175, made clear that “the purpose for which property is held is the test of its character.” The purpose of the inherited IRA is to provide for retirement, even if the beneficiary must access it before retirement age.

The Seventh Circuit’s homestead analogy is equally inapposite. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, the Court held that “the character of the property is determined by its use and intent, not by the identity of the person who holds it.” The homestead exemption depends on the debtor’s *use* of the property, but the retirement fund exemption depends on the *purpose* of the fund. The two exemptions are not coextensive. As the Court noted in *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 283 U.S. at 175, “the law does not require a man to be the original creator of a fund in order to be entitled to its protection.” The fact that Heidi Heffron-Clark did not originally contribute to the IRA does not disqualify it from exemption.

The Seventh Circuit’s concern that allowing the exemption would encourage bequests is misplaced. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198, rejected the idea that “the law must be interpreted to avoid all possible consequences.” The Court stated: “No legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” The purpose of the retirement fund exemption is to protect savings for future retirement, not to discourage bequests. The fact that the exemption may have that effect is not a reason to deny it.

Finally, the Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. That case involved a state exemption and is not binding on the interpretation of a federal exemption. Moreover, *Barker* did not address the issue of inherited retirement accounts.
