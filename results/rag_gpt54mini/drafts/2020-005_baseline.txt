Respondents’ judgment should be affirmed because Ford’s Montana contacts are sufficient for specific jurisdiction even though those contacts did not causally produce the particular accident. The Due Process Clause requires only that a suit “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S. A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915 (2011). That standard is deliberately disjunctive: a claim may relate to forum conduct without being caused by it. Id. Specific jurisdiction is proper where there is an “affiliatio[n] between the forum and the underlying controversy,” and it is confined to claims “deriving from, or connected with, the very controversy that establishes jurisdiction.” Id.

Ford’s contrary rule would collapse specific jurisdiction into strict causation, but the Court has never imposed that limitation. In Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462 (1985), the Court held that the constitutional touchstone is whether the defendant “purposefully established minimum contacts” in the forum State, and emphasized that the defendant must “reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.” Id. at 474-476. The point is not whether the forum contacts were the final link in a causal chain; it is whether the defendant deliberately engaged the forum in a way that makes litigation there fair. Id.

That principle is especially clear in cases involving nonresident motorists and products used on state highways. In Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a nonresident motorist under a statute designed to secure a forum for in-state accidents, reasoning that a state may condition use of its roads on amenability to suit. Id. at 356-357. Likewise, in Wuchter v. Pizzutti, 276 U.S. 13 (1928), the Court sustained a statute deeming a nonresident motorist’s in-state driving an appointment of an agent for service where the action arose “out of or by reason of any accident or collision occurring within the State.” Id. at 15-16. And in Olberding v. Illinois Central Railroad, 346 U.S. 338 (1953), the Court acknowledged the constitutionality of state processes “to secure the presence of an out-of-state motorist in the state courts to respond to damages.” Id. at 340. These decisions reflect a settled premise: when a defendant deliberately serves a state market and state roads, the forum may protect its residents through jurisdiction over suits tied to that presence. Id.; Hess, 274 U.S. at 356-357; Wuchter, 276 U.S. at 15-16.

Ford’s Montana contacts easily satisfy that standard. Ford does not merely place products into commerce once and wash its hands of them. It systematically markets, sells, and services Ford vehicles and replacement parts in Montana. The Montana Supreme Court correctly recognized that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business in that state.” See Burger King Corp., 471 U.S. at 475. Ford’s forum activity is not random, fortuitous, or attenuated. Id. at 475. The very controversy here concerns the safety of a Ford Explorer on Montana roads and the adequacy of Ford’s design and warnings for Montana consumers. That is an obvious relation between the forum and the claims. Goodyear, 564 U.S. at 919.

Ford relies on causation language, but the Court’s precedents do not support a narrow proximate-cause requirement. Goodyear expressly distinguished specific jurisdiction from general jurisdiction and described the former as turning on claims that “arise out of or relate to” forum conduct. 564 U.S. at 923-924. A claim “relate[s] to” forum activities when, as here, the defendant’s extensive in-state commercial activity is part of the same product market in which the injury occurred and the same alleged defect is at issue. Id. This is consistent with the Court’s jurisdictional cases requiring a practical, not mechanical, connection between defendant, forum, and litigation. Burger King Corp., 471 U.S. at 476.

Nor does due process require Montana to pretend Ford was an outsider. Ford is “essentially at home” only in Michigan and Delaware, so general jurisdiction is unavailable. Goodyear, 564 U.S. at 924. But specific jurisdiction exists precisely to permit suit where the defendant has deliberately exploited the forum and the litigation bears a substantial relation to that forum conduct. Id. Ford’s Montana business made it foreseeable that it could be sued there over the very product it marketed there for use on Montana roads. Burger King Corp., 471 U.S. at 474-476; Hess, 274 U.S. at 356-357.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
