The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation. The court’s decision is consistent with the principle that a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome, as established in Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969).

The appellants’ claim for nominal damages cannot save their otherwise moot constitutional challenge. As this Court held in Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248, 1265-66 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law. The appellants’ attempt to distinguish their case from Flanigan’s is unpersuasive, as their complaint, like the one in Flanigan’s, fails to allege any actual injury that would support a claim for compensatory damages.

The district court properly construed the appellants’ complaint as seeking only nominal damages. The complaint’s allegations of "monetary damages" and "damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence and this Court" are too vague to support a claim for compensatory damages. As the Eleventh Circuit noted, compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant, rather than on the "abstract value" of the constitutional rights that may have been violated, Slicker v. Jackson, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). The appellants’ complaint fails to allege any such actual injury.

Furthermore, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the appellants leave to amend their complaint. The appellants’ request to amend was embedded in their response to the motion to dismiss, which is not a proper procedural mechanism for seeking leave to amend, Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). Moreover, the appellants failed to specify the substance of their proposed amendment or attach a copy of the proposed amendment to their motion, Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018).

The appellants’ reliance on cases such as Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), and Memphis Community School District v. Stachura, 477 U.S. 299 (1986), is misplaced. While these cases establish that nominal damages can be awarded for the violation of constitutional rights, they do not hold that a claim for nominal damages can save an otherwise moot case. In fact, the Court in Flanigan’s distinguished Carey and Stachura on the grounds that they did not involve mootness, 868 F.3d at 1266.

The decision below is also consistent with the principle that a defendant’s voluntary cessation of allegedly unlawful conduct does not necessarily moot a case, Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000). However, in this case, the school’s revision of its policies was a genuine cessation of the challenged conduct, and there is no indication that the school will return to its prior policies, see Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc., 568 U.S. 85, 91-92 (2013).

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the appellants’ constitutional challenge to the school policy was rendered moot by the revision of the policy during litigation. The court’s decision is consistent with the principles established in Flanigan’s, Powell, and other cases, and should be affirmed.
