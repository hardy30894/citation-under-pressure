**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits a defendant from being twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. In this case, the State seeks to impose a harsher sentence based on a prior conviction that was not proven beyond a reasonable doubt at the initial sentencing proceeding. The California Supreme Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of the prior conviction allegation in a noncapital case. This holding is contrary to settled constitutional principle and must be reversed.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense. *United States v. Jenkins*, 324 U.S. 596, 599 (1945), held that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not limited to cases where the defendant has been acquitted, but extends to cases where he has been convicted and the conviction is reversed.” The Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), and also bars retrial after a conviction is reversed on appeal for insufficient evidence. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671, established that “where a conviction has been reversed on appeal, the defendant is entitled to be free from further prosecution for the same offense.” This principle applies with equal force when the reversal is based on a failure to prove an essential element of the offense—such as a prior conviction that triggers a sentencing enhancement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies not only to trials on guilt or innocence but also to sentencing determinations that are integral to the punishment for the same offense. In *United States v. Pizzuto*, 322 U.S. 547 (1944), the Court held that “the Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second prosecution for the same offense, even though the second prosecution is for a different sentence.” The Court emphasized that “the offense is the same, whether the punishment is increased by a sentence of death or by a sentence of imprisonment.” Similarly, in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court stated that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not confined to cases where the defendant has been acquitted, but extends to cases where he has been convicted and the conviction is reversed on appeal.” The reversal of a conviction based on insufficient evidence, whether on the substantive charge or on a sentencing enhancement, triggers the protection of the Clause.

The nature of the prior conviction allegation in this case is not a mere administrative fact but a critical element of the offense for which the defendant is being punished. The Three Strikes law doubles the sentence for a defendant with a prior serious felony conviction. That enhancement is not a mere procedural formality; it transforms the punishment from a standard term to a significantly longer one. The Supreme Court has recognized that “the punishment is the same, whether the sentence is increased by a sentence of death or by a sentence of imprisonment.” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 322 U.S. at 550. Thus, the prior conviction is not a collateral issue but a component of the offense itself.

The California Supreme Court’s distinction between capital and noncapital cases is unsupported by precedent. In *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671, the Court held that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not limited to cases where the defendant has been acquitted, but extends to cases where he has been convicted and the conviction is reversed on appeal.” The Court did not condition this protection on the severity of the punishment. In *United States v. Wilson*, 321 U.S. 645 (1944), the Court reiterated that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after conviction, regardless of the nature of the punishment.” The Clause does not permit the State to retry a defendant on a sentencing enhancement after a reversal based on insufficient evidence.

The procedural protections afforded in a sentencing hearing do not diminish the constitutional protection. In *United States v. Jenkins*, 324 U.S. at 599, the Court held that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not limited to cases where the defendant has been acquitted, but extends to cases where he has been convicted and the conviction is reversed.” The Court emphasized that “the offense is the same, whether the punishment is increased by a sentence of death or by a sentence of imprisonment.” The fact that California provides a jury trial for prior conviction allegations under Penal Code § 1025 does not diminish the constitutional protection. *United States v. Pizzuto*, 322 U.S. at 550, makes clear that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies regardless of the procedural formality.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing determinations that are essential to the punishment. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382, the Court stated that “the constitutional protection against double jeopardy is not confined to cases where the defendant has been acquitted, but extends to cases where he has been convicted and the conviction is reversed on appeal.” The reversal of a conviction based on insufficient evidence, whether on the substantive charge or on a sentencing enhancement, triggers the protection of the Clause.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that the Due Process Clause does not require a trial on sentencing issues, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court in *Williams* noted that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251. This statement does not negate the protection of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Clause is not a procedural rule but a substantive constitutional guarantee.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. The prior conviction allegation in this case is an essential element of the offense for which the defendant is being punished. The reversal of the finding of a prior conviction based on insufficient evidence bars retrial. *United States v. Jenkins*, 324 U.S. at 599.
