**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government may collect a partnership’s unpaid employment taxes from individual partners without first assessing those partners individually—must be answered in the negative. The Internal Revenue Code, as interpreted by binding precedent, requires that each individual partner be separately assessed before the Government may collect the partnership’s tax debt from them. The Government’s failure to make such assessments, and the consequent expiration of the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a), bars its claims against the individual partners. This conclusion is compelled by the plain language of the Code, the settled doctrine of separate taxpayer status, and the Supreme Court’s consistent interpretation of the relationship between partnership and individual liability.

First, the Internal Revenue Code treats the partnership and its individual partners as distinct taxpayers. Section 7701(a)(1) defines “person” to include “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation,” and § 7701(a)(14) defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax.” These definitions make clear that an individual partner is a “taxpayer” in his own right, distinct from the partnership. The Government’s argument that an assessment against the partnership suffices to bind the partners ignores this fundamental distinction. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, “each has its, his, or her own taxpayer identification number,” and “the IRS’s failure to assess tax deficiencies against Debtors within the three-year period provided under § 6501(a) bars it from collecting the unpaid debts of the Partnership directly from Debtors.” *United States v. Galletti*, 298 F.3d 1107, 1155 (9th Cir. 2002) (amended opinion). The Code’s structure reflects a clear intent that assessments be made against each taxpayer individually. Section 6203 requires that an assessment “be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary in accordance with rules or regulations prescribed by the Secretary,” and the regulations under § 6203 mandate that the assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The Government’s assessment against the partnership alone fails this requirement as to the individual partners.

Second, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a partnership is not a mere alter ego of its partners, but a distinct legal entity with its own liabilities. In *Forsyth v. Woods*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 484, 492 (1871), the Court emphasized that “the partnership is a distinct thing from the partners themselves,” and that “debts of the firm are different in character from other joint debts of the partners.” This distinction is critical: while partners may be jointly and severally liable under state law, that liability does not arise automatically from a partnership assessment. Rather, it must be judicially enforced. As the Court stated in *Francis v. McNeal*, 228 U.S. 695, 700 (1913), “the hypothesis is that some of the partners are in, but that the firm has remained out, and provision is made for its continuing out.” This language confirms that the partnership and its members are treated separately under the Bankruptcy Act, and that the Government cannot collect from one without first establishing liability against the other.

Third, the Government’s claim that it may collect under state law is foreclosed by the Supreme Court’s holding in *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 264 U.S. 499, 503 (1924). There, the Court affirmed that a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. The Court held that “a judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co. (Dale v. Same. Breeze v. Same)*, 268 U.S. 426, 428 (1925), where the Court reiterated that a partner may not be compelled to pay a partnership debt without a separate judgment against him. The Government has not obtained such a judgment against the Gallettis or the Briguglios. Indeed, the Government concedes that no such judgment was ever entered. Thus, under the law as interpreted by the Supreme Court, the Government’s claim is unenforceable.

Fourth, the Government’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. That case did not hold that an assessment against the partnership could bind the partners without individual assessment. Rather, the district court in *Young* held that a partner who had paid his share of the tax could not recover a refund, even though he had not been individually assessed, because state law made him liable. The Ninth Circuit affirmed on the ground that the partner was “personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership.” *Young*, 283 F.2d at 910. But this statement was not a holding that the Government could collect without assessment—it was a statement of substantive liability, not procedural enforceability. The district court itself acknowledged that “the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). Thus, *Young* supports the proposition that individual assessment is necessary to enable collection, not that it is unnecessary.

Fifth, the Government’s argument that the statute of limitations is extended by the partnership assessment is contradicted by the plain text of § 6502(a), which provides that the ten-year collection period applies “only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—(1) within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” The assessment in question was made against the partnership, not the individual partners. The statute does not extend the limitations period for collection from partners unless the partners themselves are assessed. The Government’s attempt to equate the partnership’s assessment with an assessment against the partners is contrary to the Code’s structure and to the Supreme Court’s interpretation of the term “taxpayer.”
