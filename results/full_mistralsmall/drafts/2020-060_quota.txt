**ARGUMENT**

I. **GUAM’S SECTION 107 CLAIM IS TIMELY AND UNAFFECTED BY THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE**

The court of appeals erred in holding that the 2004 consent decree “resolved” Guam’s liability under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and thereby barred Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery action. CERCLA § 107(a) permits “any person” who has incurred cleanup costs to seek full recovery from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs), while § 113(f) provides a separate contribution mechanism for PRPs who have resolved their liability in a settlement. *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161–62 (2004). The D.C. Circuit’s decision conflates these distinct remedies and misapplies the statutory framework.

Contrary to the court of appeals’ conclusion, the consent decree did not “resolve” Guam’s liability for CERCLA purposes. A settlement resolves liability only if it “adjudicates or settles the merits of a PRP’s liability for response costs.” *United States v. Asarco LLC*, 866 F.3d 1111, 1122 (9th Cir. 2017) (quoting *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015)). Here, the decree explicitly disclaimed any finding or admission of liability, stating it was “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law.” *Consent Decree*, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam Jan. 24, 2008), at 3. Such disclaimers are not dispositive, but they weigh strongly against a finding that liability was resolved. *See id.* at 1123 (noting that parties often refuse to concede liability even while assuming obligations consistent with liability).

Moreover, the decree addressed Clean Water Act violations—not CERCLA liability. The EPA’s complaint alleged unlawful discharges of pollutants into waters of the United States, not the release of hazardous substances under CERCLA. *Complaint for Injunctive Relief*, United States v. Guam, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam 2002), ¶¶ 14, 21. While CERCLA and the Clean Water Act may overlap in remediation efforts, the consent decree did not resolve Guam’s CERCLA liability. *See Key Tronic Corp. v. United States*, 511 U.S. 809, 814 (1994) (noting that CERCLA and other environmental statutes serve distinct purposes).

The court of appeals’ reliance on *United States v. Colorado & Eastern Railroad Co.*, 50 F.3d 1530 (10th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. That case involved a consent decree that expressly resolved the PRP’s liability for response costs. *Id.* at 1534. Here, no such adjudication occurred. The decree merely required Guam to close the landfill and install a cover system—obligations that do not equate to a resolution of liability. *See Asarco*, 866 F.3d at 1123.

II. **GUAM’S § 107 CLAIM IS NOT BARRED BY THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS**

The court of appeals further erred in suggesting that Guam’s § 107 claim is time-barred. CERCLA § 107 provides a six-year limitations period, while § 113’s three-year period applies only to contribution claims. *CERCLA §§ 113(g)(2)(B), 113(g)(3)*. Because Guam’s claim is brought under § 107—not § 113—it is governed by the longer limitations period.

The United States argues that Guam’s claim accrued when the consent decree was entered in 2004, but this misreads CERCLA’s accrual provisions. A § 107 claim accrues when a plaintiff incurs response costs, not when a settlement is reached. *See United States v. Shell Oil Co.*, 294 F.3d 1045, 1053 (9th Cir. 2002) (holding that a § 107 claim accrues when costs are incurred, not when liability is resolved). Guam did not incur its remediation costs until it closed the landfill in 2011, well within the six-year limitations period. *See id.*

III. **THE NAVY’S LIABILITY AS A PRP IS ESTABLISHED BY CERCLA § 107(a)**

The Navy is a PRP under CERCLA § 107(a)(1) because it arranged for the disposal of hazardous substances at the Ordot Dump. *See Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States*, 556 U.S. 599, 613 (2009) (holding that a party may be liable under § 107(a)(3) if it “arranged for disposal” of hazardous substances). The Navy’s use of the landfill for munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange during the Korean and Vietnam Wars satisfies this standard. *See id.* at 610 (noting that liability extends to parties who “played a role in the contamination”).

The United States’ argument that Guam, as the landfill’s owner and operator, is solely liable is unavailing. CERCLA imposes joint and several liability on PRPs unless they can prove divisibility of harm. *See id.* at 613. The Navy cannot escape liability by pointing to Guam’s ownership; it actively contributed to the contamination. *See id.* at 610.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and hold that Guam’s § 107 claim is timely and that the Navy is financially responsible for the Ordot Dump’s remediation costs.
