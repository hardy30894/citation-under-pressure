**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims That Merely Require Application of Federal Patent Law.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim that requires a court to apply federal patent law necessarily “arises under” federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. The Supreme Court has long held that “arising under” jurisdiction is not triggered merely because a state-law claim involves a federal issue. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313 (2005). Instead, federal jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution without disturbing the congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Id.* at 314. Minton’s claim fails at least three of these four requirements.

**A. The Federal Patent Issue Is Not “Substantial” in the Context of This State Malpractice Claim.**

A federal issue is “substantial” only when its resolution is important to the federal system as a whole, not merely to the parties. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313. Here, the only federal issue is whether the experimental use exception would have saved Minton’s patent from the on-sale bar. That question is case-specific and does not implicate the uniformity of patent law. As the Supreme Court has explained, “the federal issue is not substantial if it is ‘fact-bound and situation-specific.’” *Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006) (quoting *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 315). The experimental use exception turns on the particular facts of Minton’s lease with Stark—whether the lease was primarily experimental or commercial. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Resolving that factual question in a state malpractice suit will not affect the validity of any patent or alter the rights of any third party. It will merely determine whether Minton’s attorneys were negligent. That is not the kind of “substantial” federal issue that warrants exclusive federal jurisdiction.

**B. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities.**

The Supreme Court has cautioned that federal courts must be “sensitive to the potential disruption of the state judicial system” when considering whether to exercise jurisdiction over state-law claims. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Legal malpractice is a quintessentially state-law claim. States have a strong interest in regulating the conduct of attorneys licensed to practice within their borders. *See, e.g., Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004) (setting forth elements of legal malpractice under Texas law). Allowing every state malpractice claim that touches on patent law to be removed to federal court would flood the federal docket with routine negligence cases. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the federal courts should not be burdened with” claims that are “primarily a matter of state law.” *Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 811 (1986). The balance Congress struck in § 1338(a) was intended to ensure uniform resolution of patent validity and infringement disputes, not to transform every patent-related state claim into a federal case. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 812 (1988) (noting that § 1338(a) jurisdiction is “narrow”).

**C. The Federal Issue Is Not “Necessary” to Resolution of the Malpractice Claim.**

Minton’s malpractice claim requires proof that Gunn’s negligence caused him to lose his patent infringement suit. To establish causation, Minton must show that, but for Gunn’s failure to plead the experimental use exception, he would have prevailed. That inquiry may require a state court to apply federal patent law, but it does not require the court to decide the patent issue in a way that binds future litigants. The state court’s resolution of the experimental use question is merely a step in determining whether Gunn breached a duty of care. It is not a final adjudication of patent validity. As the Supreme Court has held, “a case does not arise under federal law unless the federal issue is an ‘essential element’ of the plaintiff’s claim.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 312. Here, the federal issue is not an element of Minton’s malpractice claim; it is merely a factual predicate for proving causation. That is insufficient to confer exclusive federal jurisdiction.

**II. The Federal Circuit’s Contrary Precedent Is Inconsistent with Supreme Court Authority.**

The Supreme Court of Texas relied on *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), to conclude that exclusive federal jurisdiction exists. But those decisions fail to apply the *Grable* framework faithfully. In *Air Measurement*, the Federal Circuit held that a state malpractice claim “arises under” patent law whenever “establishing patent infringement is a necessary element” of the claim. 504 F.3d at 1273. That test ignores *Grable*’s requirement that the federal issue be “substantial” and that federal jurisdiction not disturb the state-federal balance. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. The Federal Circuit’s approach would sweep into federal court every state malpractice case that requires a court to apply any federal patent law, no matter how routine or fact-bound. That result is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s repeated admonition that “arising under” jurisdiction is “narrow.” *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 812.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings in state court.
