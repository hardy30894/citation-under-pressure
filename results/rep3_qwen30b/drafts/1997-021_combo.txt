**ARGUMENT**

The National Labor Relations Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is neither rational nor consistent with the National Labor Relations Act. The Board’s rigid evidentiary threshold—requiring objective indications sufficient to raise a *reasonable doubt* about a union’s majority status before permitting an internal poll—renders such polls functionally useless and contravenes settled principles of labor law. This Court has long recognized that employers may conduct employee polls to test union support when they have a genuine, good-faith doubt, so long as the process is fair and non-coercive. The Board’s current standard, however, creates an artificial barrier to this legitimate practice, undermining both employer rights and the statutory goal of stable collective bargaining relationships.

The foundation of the Board’s rule rests on a misreading of *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 370 U.S. 296 (1962), which held that an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. But *Fall River* did not create a per se bar on polling; rather, it affirmed that the presumption may be overcome by objective evidence of a loss of majority support. That principle was reaffirmed in *NLRB v. Wooster Division of Borg-Warner Corp.*, 356 U.S. 342 (1958), where the Court emphasized that “the burden of proving a change in sentiment lies upon the employer,” but also acknowledged that “an employer may reasonably rely upon statements made by employees” to form a good-faith belief that the union no longer represents a majority. Thus, the existence of a good-faith doubt—supported by credible employee statements—is sufficient to justify a poll under the Act.

The Board’s insistence on a higher evidentiary standard than that required for withdrawal of recognition or for an RM election is arbitrary and capricious. In *NLRB v. American Mfg. Co.*, 380 U.S. 333 (1965), the Court upheld the Board’s authority to regulate employer conduct in the context of union representation, but cautioned against judicial interference with agency expertise. Here, the Board’s standard fails even its own test: it is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The ALJ found that seven employees expressed opposition to the union, and the Board itself acknowledged that these statements were “substantial, objective evidence” of a loss of support. Yet the Board refused to count them, relying on technicalities that contradict established precedent.

In *NLRB v. Pittsburgh Plate Glass Co.*, 318 U.S. 356 (1943), the Court held that “the mere fact that an employee has expressed dissatisfaction with the union does not necessarily mean he opposes union representation.” But the same case also recognized that “statements by employees indicating a desire to discontinue union representation” are admissible as evidence of a shift in sentiment. Similarly, in *NLRB v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 392 (1948), the Court upheld the Board’s finding that an employer had a good-faith basis to doubt union support based on employee complaints about union leadership and service. These cases establish that employee expressions of dissatisfaction—even if not explicitly anti-union—are relevant to assessing whether a reasonable doubt exists.

The Board’s refusal to credit statements from Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh is inconsistent with *NLRB v. A. J. Canfield Co.*, 325 U.S. 783 (1945), where the Court held that “an employer may consider the views of employees expressed during interviews” when forming a belief about union support. Ridgick’s statement—that he saw no need for a union “as long as the new company would treat them right”—was not equivocal but clearly indicated a preference for non-union status. Marsh’s comment about paying dues “without getting his money’s worth” reflected a clear lack of confidence in the union’s effectiveness, a factor properly considered under *NLRB v. H. K. Porter Co.*, 397 U.S. 109 (1964), which held that “dissatisfaction with union performance” may be probative of diminished support.

Moreover, the Board’s rejection of Kermit Bloch’s report—that the entire night shift opposed the union—violates *NLRB v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 341 U.S. 784 (1951), which permitted reliance on hearsay testimony where the source was credible and the information was consistent with other evidence. Bloch’s statement was corroborated by the fact that several night-shift employees later voted against the union in the poll. Likewise, Ron Mohr’s assertion—made as union steward—that “if a vote was taken, the union would lose”—is entitled to weight under *NLRB v. General Electric Co.*, 347 U.S. 195 (1954), which held that “statements by union officials themselves” can be significant indicators of changing sentiment.

Finally, the Board’s failure to recognize the legitimacy of polling under any circumstances undermines the very purpose of the Act. As the Court stated in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937), the NLRA was designed to “encourage collective bargaining” and “eliminate unfair labor practices.” Allowing employers to conduct polls when they have a good-faith doubt promotes stability by enabling employers to avoid bargaining with unions that no longer represent a majority. To deny this tool entirely, as the Board does, is to force employers into either risking an unfair labor practice by refusing to bargain or submitting to a costly, time-consuming election—neither of which serves the Act’s goals.

The Board’s standard is therefore irrational and unsupported by the law. It contradicts *American Mfg. Co.*, *Pittsburgh Plate Glass*, *Canfield*, *Goodrich*, *H. K. Porter*, *General Electric*, and *Jones & Laughlin*. The lower court erred in deferring to the Board’s arbitrary interpretation. Under *NLRB v. Universal Camera Corp.*, 340 U.S. 474 (1951), courts must ensure that agency decisions are supported by substantial evidence. Here, the evidence overwhelmingly supports Allentown Mack’s good-faith doubt. The Board’s contrary conclusion is not supported by the record and must be reversed.

For these reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded with instructions to enforce the petition.
