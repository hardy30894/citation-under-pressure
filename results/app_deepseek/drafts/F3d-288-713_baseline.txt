**Argument**

**I. The district court erred in denying habeas relief because the prosecutor’s dual role as witness and advocate deprived Mr. Riddle of a fundamentally fair trial in violation of the Due Process Clause.**

The State’s chief prosecutor, Danny Hill, took the stand as a rebuttal witness to contest Mr. Riddle’s testimony that Hill had threatened to seek the death penalty. Hill then resumed his role as lead advocate, and his assistant used Hill’s testimony in closing to argue that Mr. Riddle was a liar. This conduct so “infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process.” *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974); *Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 181 (1986). The Supreme Court has long admonished that a prosecutor’s interest is not to win a case but that “justice shall be done.” *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). When a prosecutor becomes a witness, he “occupies a dual role” that creates an “intolerable” risk of prejudice because the jury may view him as having unique access to the truth. *United States v. Johnston*, 690 F.2d 638, 643 (7th Cir. 1982). That risk materialized here.

Hill’s testimony was not a mere rebuttal of a collateral point. It directly contradicted the defendant’s account of a threat that, if true, would have explained Mr. Riddle’s fear and his prior escape attempts. By choosing to testify, Hill put his own credibility in issue and then continued to advocate for the State. His subsequent presence at counsel table and his office’s closing argument necessarily reinforced the impression that the State’s chief law enforcement officer had personally vouched for the prosecution’s version of events. The assistant prosecutor’s explicit appeal to Hill’s testimony—arguing that Mr. Riddle should be disbelieved because Hill proved him a liar—compounded the error. This was not harmless under *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993). A jury’s verdict cannot stand where the prosecutor “has placed the weight of his office” behind the credibility contest. *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88. The district court therefore erred in granting summary judgment to the State.

**II. The district court abused its discretion by denying Mr. Riddle an evidentiary hearing on his ineffective-assistance claim.**

Mr. Riddle alleged that trial counsel rendered constitutionally ineffective assistance by failing to object to Hill’s dual role and by failing to investigate and present evidence that would have corroborated his testimony. The state habeas court denied relief without a hearing. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2), a federal evidentiary hearing is barred only when the applicant “failed to develop the factual basis of a claim in State court proceedings.” Here, Mr. Riddle diligently pursued state relief; he filed a state application and the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals denied it without a hearing. He therefore did not fail to develop the facts—the state court prevented him from doing so. *See Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 420, 432 (2000) (petitioner who is denied the opportunity to develop facts in state court has not failed to develop them). The district court’s grant of summary judgment without an evidentiary hearing was premature.

To prevail on a claim of ineffective assistance, a petitioner must show both deficient performance and prejudice. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). Here, there are material factual disputes: whether Hill made the alleged threat, whether trial counsel’s failure to object was a deliberate strategic choice, and whether counsel’s investigation would have uncovered evidence supporting Mr. Riddle’s account of consent and self-defense. These disputes are exactly the type that require a hearing. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963). The district court instead credited the State’s summary-judgment evidence, notwithstanding Mr. Riddle’s sworn testimony to the contrary. That was error. Because the state court never resolved the factual questions, the district court could not defer to any state-court finding under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). By denying a hearing, the district court effectively deprived Mr. Riddle of any opportunity to prove his claim.

This Court should reverse the district court’s denial of relief and remand with instructions to grant an evidentiary hearing on the ineffective-assistance claim and, if necessary, to consider the merits of the prosecutorial-misconduct claim under the correct standard. The prosecutorial conduct, combined with the denial of a hearing, deprived Mr. Riddle of the fundamentally fair trial that the Constitution guarantees.
