## ARGUMENT

### I. The judgment on the sex-discrimination claims cannot stand because the evidence does not permit a finding that Sieben’s reasons were a pretext for discrimination.

A plaintiff proceeding under Title VII and the Missouri Human Rights Act must prove that sex was a motivating factor in the challenged employment action, not merely that she disagreed with management’s business judgments. *Hicks v. St. Mary’s Honor Center*, 509 U.S. 502 (1993); *Texas Dep’t of Community Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248 (1981). Once the employer produces legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons, the ultimate burden remains on the plaintiff to show intentional discrimination. *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 253; *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133 (2000). And appellate review of a jury verdict must respect the record, but may not uphold liability where the proof is insufficient as a matter of law. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986); *Brennan v. GTE Gov’t Sys. Corp.*, 150 F.3d 21 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

Sieben produced ample legitimate explanations for each challenged decision. Rich’s memoranda reflected concerns about Feltmann’s sales technique, her “team player” issues, her low gross profit, and her continued violation of the demo-car restriction. Those are classic performance-based reasons. Employers may make subjective judgments about sales style, profitability, and compliance with policy without violating Title VII. *Fisher v. Pharmacia & Upjohn*, 225 F.3d 915 (8th Cir. 2000); *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502 (1993).

The record does not show that these reasons were false. Feltmann concedes that her gross profits were the lowest of all consultants from December 1992 through April 1993. Rich testified that she sold cars in a way that reduced profits. The probation decision followed low performance in January and February. The demo restriction applied companywide to control costs, and Feltmann admittedly continued weekly out-of-area use for months after being told to stop. A plaintiff cannot establish pretext by simply disputing an employer’s business judgment or showing that the employer may have been mistaken. *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 259; *Davidson v. State of Iowa*, 133 F.3d 692 (8th Cir. 1998).

Nor does the record support a sex-based inference from the Chicago sales contest. Rich offered the trip to the top two consultants; when they declined, he did not reopen it to any consultants, male or female, who later expressed interest. That is facially neutral. Likewise, the demo restriction was imposed after overall sales dropped and costs rose; no male employee was shown to have been similarly situated and insubordinate after receiving an express restriction. Disparate treatment requires proof that similarly situated comparators were treated differently. *Bielusz v. Jefferson County*, 447 F.3d 887 (8th Cir. 2006); *Clark v. Runyon*, 218 F.3d 915 (8th Cir. 2000).

The isolated vulgar remark by an employee from another franchise does not change the analysis. Title VII is not a general civility code, and stray remarks by nondecisionmakers ordinarily do not prove discriminatory intent. *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228 (1989); *EEOC v. HBE Corp.*, 135 F.3d 543 (8th Cir. 1998). Sieben promptly investigated, reprimanded Anzalone, and warned him of termination for any recurrence. That response is inconsistent with discriminatory animus.

### II. The retaliatory-discharge verdict likewise lacks evidentiary support.

To establish retaliation, Feltmann had to prove protected activity, a materially adverse action, and a causal connection between the two. *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53 (2006); *Cohen v. Fred Meyer, Inc.*, 686 F.2d 793 (9th Cir. 1982). Temporal proximity alone is rarely enough, especially where the employer had already begun documenting performance problems. *Kipp v. Missouri Highway & Transp. Comm’n*, 280 F.3d 893 (8th Cir. 2002); *Kiel v. Select Artificials, Inc.*, 169 F.3d 1131 (8th Cir. 1999) (en banc).

Here, the protected complaint concerned Anzalone’s vulgar comment in November 1992. Sieben immediately took corrective action: Rich reported the incident, the owners met with Feltmann, reprimanded Anzalone, and invited future reporting. Months later, Rich’s January memo addressed sales technique and guest drives, not the complaint. In March, probation resulted from low sales in January and February. The demo restriction was issued because of cost concerns and Feltmann’s repeated out-of-area use. The chronology shows intervening, documented performance reasons, not retaliatory causation. *St. Mary’s Honor Center*, 509 U.S. 502; *Kiel*, 169 F.3d 1131.

A retaliation claim fails where the employer can point to legitimate reasons supported by the record and the plaintiff cannot show those reasons were pretextual. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. 133; *Smith v. Allen Health Sys., Inc.*, 302 F.3d 827 (8th Cir. 2002). Here, no reasonable jury could conclude that Sieben escalated discipline because of Feltmann’s complaint when the company did exactly what Title VII encourages—investigate and correct the objectionable conduct. See *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 (1986).

### III. At minimum, the evidence supports judgment for Sieben because the verdict rests on speculation rather than proof.

A verdict cannot stand on conjecture. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 256; *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 148. Feltmann may have been a good salesperson in some months, but Title VII does not authorize a jury to second-guess ordinary management decisions absent proof of unlawful motive. *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 511; *Fisher*, 225 F.3d 915. Because the record shows legitimate, consistently applied performance-based reasons for Sieben’s actions and no substantial evidence of sex-based or retaliatory intent, the judgment should be reversed.
