The reinstatement statute, Section 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date in 1997. Even if it does, its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.

First, the statutory text and legislative history do not clearly indicate that Congress intended the reinstatement statute to apply retroactively to pre-enactment re-entries. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), the Court established a two-step test for determining whether a statute applies retroactively. The first step is to determine whether Congress has clearly indicated the statute's temporal reach. Id. at 280. If not, the court proceeds to the second step, assessing whether the statute has an impermissible retroactive effect. Id. Here, the INA's reinstatement provision lacks clear congressional intent regarding its application to pre-enactment re-entries.

The government may argue that the statute's language is broad enough to encompass pre-enactment re-entries. However, as the Court held in INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), the absence of explicit language regarding retroactivity suggests that Congress did not intend the statute to apply retroactively. Id. at 320. The reinstatement statute's silence on this issue implies that it should not be applied to Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the United States in 1982.

Even if the reinstatement statute is deemed to apply to Fernandez-Vargas, its application is impermissibly retroactive. Under Landgraf's second step, a statute has an impermissible retroactive effect if it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." 511 U.S. at 270. The reinstatement statute denies relief under the INA to aliens whose prior deportation orders are reinstated. By applying this statute to Fernandez-Vargas, the government is depriving him of the opportunity to adjust his status, a consequence that did not exist when he re-entered the United States.

The Court's decision in Fernandez-Antonia v. Gonzales, 442 F.3d 1058 (7th Cir. 2006), is distinguishable. There, the Seventh Circuit held that the reinstatement statute applied to an alien who re-entered before its enactment. However, that court's reasoning relied heavily on the statute's language and the alien's continued illegal presence in the United States. In contrast, Fernandez-Vargas had established a life in the United States, married a U.S. citizen, and was in the process of adjusting his status when the reinstatement statute was applied to him.

The Tenth Circuit's holding that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" is unfounded. As the Court held in St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 323-24, an alien's reliance on pre-existing law is a significant factor in determining whether a statute has an impermissible retroactive effect. Fernandez-Vargas relied on the law as it existed in 1982 when he re-entered the United States, and the reinstatement statute's application to him disrupts his settled expectations.

In conclusion, the reinstatement statute should not be applied to Fernandez-Vargas, as it lacks clear congressional intent regarding retroactivity and has an impermissible retroactive effect. The government's application of the statute to Fernandez-Vargas is therefore unlawful, and the Tenth Circuit's decision should be reversed.
