**ARGUMENT**

This Court should hold that inherited individual retirement accounts are not exempt from a debtor’s bankruptcy estate under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The plain language, statutory context, and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code compel the conclusion that an inherited IRA—which the beneficiary must begin liquidating immediately—does not constitute “retirement funds.”

**I. The Plain Text of § 522 Does Not Exempt Inherited IRAs**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. The critical statutory phrase is “retirement funds.” As this Court has instructed, courts must “presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says.” *Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). An inherited IRA does not hold “retirement funds” because the beneficiary cannot use the account to fund her own retirement. The Internal Revenue Code requires distributions to begin within one year of the original owner’s death, 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B), and prohibits additional contributions, 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). This temporal structure prevents an inherited IRA from functioning as a retirement savings vehicle. As the Seventh Circuit correctly recognized, an “inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013).

**II. Statutory Context Confirms That the Exemption Requires Current Retirement Purpose**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts retirement funds based on their present character, not their historical origin. When Congress intended to exempt assets based on the debtor’s relationship to a predecessor, it did so explicitly. Compare § 522(b)(3)(B) (exempting “any interest in property in which the debtor had . . . an interest as a tenant by the entirety”) with § 522(b)(3)(C) (lacking similar language). This Court has repeatedly cautioned that “when Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another,” the omission is intentional. *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983). Here, Congress omitted any reference to the original owner’s retirement purpose, requiring instead that the *funds* themselves be “retirement funds.” *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563; see also *In re Kuchta*, 434 B.R. 837, 840 (Bankr. N.D. Ohio 2010) (“[T]he exemption focuses on the nature of the account in the hands of the debtor.”).

The homestead exemption illustrates this point. Under § 522(b)(3)(A), a debtor may exempt her “domicile”—but only if she has lived there for the 730 days preceding bankruptcy. A debtor who inherits a house from her mother cannot claim the exemption unless she has made the house her own home. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. Similarly, an inherited IRA is exempt only if the debtor is using it for retirement. Cf. *In re Ellis*, 945 F.2d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 1991) (exemptions are construed narrowly in favor of creditors).

**III. The Purpose of the Bankruptcy Exemption Supports the Seventh Circuit’s Holding**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds seeks to protect assets “set aside for retirement,” not assets that may be consumed before retirement. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 561. An inherited IRA, by contrast, “represent[s] an opportunity for current consumption.” *Id.* at 565. Permitting exemption would “allow[] a debtor to shield from creditors an asset that she may use for any purpose, including a pre-retirement vacation or a new car.” *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312, 316 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010), *aff’d*, 466 B.R. 135 (W.D. Wis. 2012). This outcome would undermine the statutory purpose.

The contrary view, adopted by the Fifth Circuit in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), erroneously focused on the funds’ origin rather than their current function. As the Seventh Circuit explained, funds that “used to be ‘retirement funds’ but aren’t now” do not qualify. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562. This Court has long recognized that exemptions must be “construed with reference to the situation of the debtor at the time of the filing of the petition.” *Zartman v. First Nat’l Bank of Waterloo*, 216 U.S. 134, 138 (1910); see also *Owen v. Owen*, 500 U.S. 305, 313 (1991) (exempt property is determined as of the petition date).

**IV. Policy Considerations Do Not Justify a Broader Reading**

Respondents argue that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage savings for bequests. But “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987). Congress could have exempted inherited retirement accounts but chose not to. See *In re Marro*, 501 B.R. 39, 46 (Bankr. D. Mass. 2013). If the exemption were as broad as respondents urge, it would create a loophole: a debtor could convert nonexempt assets into inherited IRAs and shield them from creditors. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. The Bankruptcy Code does not authorize such evasion. *In re Goerg*, 844 F.2d 1562, 1566 (11th Cir. 1988).

**Conclusion**

Because an inherited IRA does not constitute “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
