## ARGUMENT

The constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled principle.

### I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Navigable Riverbeds in States at Statehood

Under the equal footing doctrine, upon admission to the Union, a state acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 230 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57 (1894). This doctrine ensures that new states enter the Union with the same sovereign rights and proprietary interests as the original thirteen states. *Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 230. The title to the beds of navigable waters passes to the state “as an incident of sovereignty” and is held in trust for the public. *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57.

### II. The Test for Navigability for Title Purposes Is Whether the River Was Susceptible of Use as a Highway for Commerce at Statehood

The controlling test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river, in its natural and ordinary condition, was susceptible of being used as a highway for commerce at the time of statehood. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870); *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 441 (1874). This test focuses on the river’s “capability of use by the public for purposes of transportation and commerce,” not on actual use or artificial improvements. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563. The relevant time is the date of statehood, not the present. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935); *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922).

In *United States v. Utah*, this Court held that navigability for title purposes must be determined “as of the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” 283 U.S. at 75. The Court explained that the inquiry is whether the river “was then navigable in its natural state.” *Id.* at 76. Similarly, in *United States v. Oregon*, the Court stated that “the title of the State to the beds of navigable rivers within its borders is determined by the condition of the river at the time of the admission of the State.” 295 U.S. at 14. This temporal requirement is essential because the equal footing doctrine vests title at the moment of statehood, and subsequent changes cannot alter that vested right. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. at 591.

### III. The Montana Supreme Court Correctly Applied the Historical Navigability Test

The Montana Supreme Court properly required the trial court to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable in 1889, the year Montana became a state. The court considered extensive historical evidence, including government surveys, expedition records, and contemporaneous accounts of river use. This evidence demonstrated that each river segment was susceptible to commercial navigation at statehood. The court’s approach aligns with this Court’s repeated instruction that navigability for title must be assessed based on the river’s condition at statehood, not on modern conditions. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926); *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77, 87 (1922).

### IV. The Petitioner’s Proposed Test Would Undermine the Equal Footing Doctrine

Petitioner PPL Montana argues that the trial court should have considered modern navigability or applied a different standard. But this Court has consistently rejected such arguments. In *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407 (1940), the Court reaffirmed that the test is whether the river “in its natural state” was “used or susceptible of being used” as a highway for commerce at the time of statehood. The Court emphasized that “the navigability of a stream is not destroyed because it is not in fact used for commerce.” *Id.* at 409. The proper focus is on the river’s “natural condition” at statehood, not on later alterations or improvements. *Donnelly v. United States*, 228 U.S. 243, 259 (1913).

Requiring a trial court to determine navigability at statehood is not only constitutionally required but also administrable. Courts routinely rely on historical evidence, as the Montana Supreme Court did here. *See, e.g., United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82 (examining historical records of river use). The petitioner’s alternative would destabilize settled property rights and contravene the equal footing doctrine’s core purpose.

### Conclusion

The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes mandates that a trial court determine whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court faithfully applied that test. This Court should affirm.
