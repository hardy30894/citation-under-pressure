**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below should be affirmed. The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies the nondelegation doctrine because Congress supplied an intelligible principle that confines the Attorney General’s discretion. That principle is the “imminent hazard to the public safety” standard, reinforced by enumerated factors and a fixed duration. This Court has upheld delegations with far less guidance.

**I. Congress Provided an Intelligible Principle.**

Congress may delegate authority to an executive official if it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to which the official must conform. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The temporary scheduling provision satisfies that test. The Attorney General may schedule a drug temporarily only upon finding that it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). In addition, the statute directs the Attorney General to consider three factors: the substance’s “history and current pattern of abuse,” its “scope, duration, and significance of abuse,” and “what, if any, risk there is to the public health.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6). Finally, the authority expires automatically after one year, subject to a single six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2).

This standard is stricter than those this Court has repeatedly sustained. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), upheld a delegation to fix prices that were “fair and equitable.” *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), upheld a delegation to prescribe regulations for the “orderly and efficient” operation of savings and loans. *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-79 (1948), upheld a delegation that required consideration of “such factors as he may deem advisable.” If those delegations withstood constitutional scrutiny, the temporary scheduling provision necessarily does as well.

The temporary scheduling provision is also consistent with this Court’s recognition that Congress may grant broad discretion in technical and rapidly changing fields. *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 216 (1943), recognized that the “complex nature” of radio regulation justified a flexible delegation to the Federal Communications Commission. The designer drug market is similarly volatile; Congress cannot be expected to anticipate every new synthetic substance. *See Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 432 (noting that emergency conditions may justify broader delegations).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases invalidated delegations that were utterly lacking in standards. In *Panama Refining*, the President was authorized to act without “any criterion” to govern his course. 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President could approve “codes of fair competition” with no definition of “fair competition,” leaving his discretion “virtually unfettered.” 295 U.S. at 538. The temporary scheduling provision is the opposite: the Attorney General must find an imminent hazard, consider specific factors, and act within a limited time. That is far from an unfettered discretion.

**II. The Criminal Penalties Attached to Temporary Scheduling Do Not Require a More Stringent Test.**

Petitioners contend that because temporary scheduling subjects conduct to criminal penalties, the delegation must be judged under a stricter standard. This Court has never adopted such a rule. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911), the Secretary of Agriculture was authorized to make regulations governing national forests, violations of which were crimes. The Court upheld the delegation because Congress had defined the policy and the Secretary could “fill up the details.” *Id.* at 517. The Attorney General’s authority under § 811(h) is similarly confined by an intelligible principle. *Schechter Poultry* is not to the contrary; it condemned a delegation that left the President’s discretion “virtually unfettered.” 295 U.S. at 538. No such unfettered discretion exists here.

**III. The Attorney General’s Subdelegation to the DEA Was Lawful.**

The Controlled Substances Act delegates temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General and separately authorizes him to “promulgate and enforce any rules, regulations, and procedures which he may deem necessary.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). This Court has long held that an officer may subdelegate powers Congress conferred on him unless the statute plainly or impliedly forbids it. *Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887); *United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926). The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), subdelegating the scheduling function to the DEA Administrator, is a routine and appropriate administrative measure. *See Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496-97 (1904) (approving subdelegation within an executive department). Nothing in the Controlled Substances Act forbids this arrangement.

**IV. Conclusion**

Because Congress supplied an intelligible principle, and because the Attorney General was authorized to subdelegate to the DEA, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
