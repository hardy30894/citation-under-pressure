The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff's claims "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's activities within the state. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), established that a defendant's activities within a state can subject it to jurisdiction there, even if it is not incorporated or headquartered in that state. The Court in International Shoe held that "due process requires only that in order to subject a defendant to a judgment in personam, if he be not present within the territory of the forum, he have certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend 'traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.'" Id. at 316.

In this case, Ford Motor Company has substantial contacts with Montana, including advertising and selling parts within the state. By doing so, Ford has availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the state, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws. See id. at 319. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford's activities within Montana were sufficient to establish specific jurisdiction.

The exercise of specific jurisdiction over Ford in Montana is consistent with the principles of due process. The Court has long recognized that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant that has "purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). By advertising and selling parts in Montana, Ford has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of doing business in that state.

Furthermore, the Court has held that a defendant's activities within a state can be sufficient to establish jurisdiction, even if those activities are not directly related to the plaintiff's claims. In Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co., 342 U.S. 437 (1952), the Court held that a state court could exercise general jurisdiction over a foreign corporation that had continuous and systematic contacts with the state, even though the plaintiff's claims did not arise out of those contacts. While Perkins involved general jurisdiction, it demonstrates that a defendant's activities within a state can be sufficient to establish jurisdiction.

In this case, Ford's activities within Montana, including advertising and selling parts, demonstrate a sufficient connection to the state to justify the exercise of specific jurisdiction. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford's contacts with Montana were sufficient to establish specific jurisdiction, and this Court should affirm that decision.
