I.

The district court erred in admitting the opinion of American Steamship’s witness on the cause of the line’s parting. Only testimony that rests on specialized knowledge or skill qualifies as expert opinion. Pennsylvania R.R. v. Chamberlain, 288 U.S. 333, 338 (1933). The witness possessed no such basis; his conclusion rested solely on post-accident observation of the rope. Admission of lay speculation dressed as expertise invaded the province of the jury. Funk v. United States, 290 U.S. 371, 381 (1933).

II.

The district court should have granted judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness. A vessel is unseaworthy when its gear is inadequate for the intended service. The Osceola, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903). The manila line had been stored wet and frozen, then rigged under time pressure after the master delayed deck work. Such conditions rendered the appliance unfit. Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co., 321 U.S. 96, 99 (1944). The shipowner’s duty is absolute and non-delegable. Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960). No evidence showed the line was sound when put to use; therefore the issue should not have reached the jury. Galloway v. United States, 319 U.S. 372, 395 (1943).

III.

The same record, however, presented a jury question on comparative negligence. The Jones Act preserves the defense and permits apportionment of fault. O’Donnell v. Great Lakes Dredge & Dock Co., 318 U.S. 36, 39 (1943). Cook’s decision to apply an open flame near the rigged line, together with the crew’s hurried rigging, supplied evidence from which reasonable jurors could find partial fault. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). The court therefore correctly denied the motion on that issue.

IV.

Evidence of Cook’s prior alcohol-related conditions was properly admitted. A defendant may show pre-existing physical impairment that bears on the extent of injury caused by the accident. Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469, 475 (1948). The ailments were relevant to damages and did not open unrelated character issues. Because the errors concerning expert testimony and unseaworthiness require a new trial, the judgment must be vacated. Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn, 346 U.S. 406, 409 (1953).
