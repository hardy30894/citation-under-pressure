ARGUMENT

Arizona law permissibly confines expert mental-health evidence to the insanity defense. This Court has long recognized that “the Due Process Clause does not require a State to adopt one procedure over another.” *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952). States therefore retain “wide latitude” to define the elements of offenses and to prescribe the manner in which affirmative defenses may be proved. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201 (1977); see also *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 43 (1996) (plurality opinion).

The right to present a defense is not absolute. “The accused does not have an unfettered right to offer testimony that is incompetent, privileged, or otherwise inadmissible under standard rules of evidence.” *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 410 (1988). Arizona’s rule is a standard evidentiary limitation that channels mental-health testimony to the issue on which the defendant bears the burden of proof. It does not prevent the State from satisfying its obligation to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

Nor does the rule offend fundamental fairness. In *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), this Court held that a state procedure for allocating the burden on competency to stand trial did not violate due process. The same deference applies to rules governing the presentation of evidence on mens rea when an insanity defense is available. Because Clark received a full opportunity to litigate insanity, due process required nothing more.

The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
