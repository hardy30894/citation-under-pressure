**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), which governs the choice of law on the Outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that state wage and hour laws may apply on the OCS so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statutory text and disregards binding precedent. The correct interpretation is that state law applies only when federal law leaves a gap—i.e., when federal law is silent or inadequate to address the issue at hand. The Court should reverse.

### I. The OCSLA’s Choice of Law Provision Requires a Gap in Federal Law Before State Law May Apply

The plain language of § 1333(a)(2)(A) does not authorize the automatic application of state law to the OCS. It provides that state laws “are declared to be the law of the United States for that portion of the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf... to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). The phrase “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent” is not a blank check for state law to supplant federal law. Rather, it is a limitation: state law applies only to the extent that federal law does not already govern the matter.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835, 23 L.Ed.2d 360 (1969), which held that state law may be applied on the OCS only when federal law does not provide a remedy. In *Rodrigue*, the Court rejected the argument that state wrongful death law should apply simply because it was “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law. Instead, the Court emphasized that the federal remedy under the Death on the High Seas Act was unavailable because the accident occurred on a fixed platform, not on the high seas, and thus did not fall within the scope of traditional maritime law. *Id.* at 360, 89 S.Ct. 1835. The Court concluded that “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through § 1333(a)(2)” was removed only because federal law did not cover the injury. *Id.* at 366, 89 S.Ct. 1835. This is not a case of state law being applied *in addition* to federal law; it is a case where federal law was absent, and state law was adopted to fill the gap.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—holding that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—would render the “gap” requirement superfluous. If state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent, then the phrase “to the extent that they are applicable” becomes meaningless. Congress did not intend such a result. As the Court has long held, “we must give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.” *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538, 75 S.Ct. 513, 99 L.Ed. 615 (1955). The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation violates that principle.

### II. The Fair Labor Standards Act Is a Comprehensive and Exclusive Federal Scheme

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., is a comprehensive and exclusive federal scheme governing minimum wage, overtime, and meal period requirements. The FLSA contains a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that explicitly permits states to impose more protective wage and hour standards. But that clause does not mean that state laws are automatically applicable on the OCS. It means only that, in the absence of a federal gap, states may enact more protective laws *within their own jurisdictions*. The FLSA itself, however, is not silent on the OCS. Congress has extended the FLSA to the OCS through the OCSLA’s general jurisdictional provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1), which declares that federal law applies to the OCS “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” The FLSA is a federal law that governs employment on the OCS, and it is not inconsistent with California’s wage and hour laws—indeed, it expressly permits them. But the mere fact that state law is not inconsistent does not mean it is applicable.

The Fifth Circuit has long held that state law may not be applied on the OCS unless federal law leaves a gap. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court stated that “the absence of federal law is not a prerequisite to the application of state law, but the necessity to fill a significant void or gap is.” *Id.* The court emphasized that “where federal law is comprehensive and exclusive, state law cannot be applied as a surrogate.” *Id.* This principle has been reaffirmed in subsequent cases, including *Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc.*, 713 F.3d 208, 214 (5th Cir. 2013), where the court held that “maritime law applies only where federal law does not.” The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of this well-established test is erroneous.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Approach Would Create a Federal-Statutory Anomaly
