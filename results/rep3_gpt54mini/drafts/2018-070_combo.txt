**Argument**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act does not invite a free-floating choice among all state laws touching the subject matter of a dispute. It incorporates adjacent state law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That text establishes two independent limitations: state law must be applicable to the situs, and it must not conflict with federal law. Nothing in the statute suggests that state law may be borrowed merely because it addresses the same general topic and is not expressly preempted. To the contrary, OCSLA was designed to create a federal body of law for fixed structures on the Outer Continental Shelf, not to import every adjacent state rule whenever federal law is less generous. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-66 (1969); Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 F.2d 1336, 1339-40 (5th Cir. 1972) (describing OCSLA’s incorporation scheme as one of surrogate federal law).

Congress enacted OCSLA against a backdrop of uncertainty over jurisdiction and applicable law offshore. The Act “define[d] a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the Shelf. Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355. Congress did not simply preserve all state regulation of offshore labor matters. Rather, it made adjacent state law federal law only where federal law and regulation do not already supply the rule of decision. That structure mirrors other federal incorporation statutes, which borrow state law only as a gap-filler. See United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293-94 (1958); Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94, 99-100 (1940); James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 292 U.S. 131, 137-38 (1934). It would be anomalous to read OCSLA more expansively than those enactments, even though OCSLA’s operative words are narrower than the “applicable and not inconsistent” formula found in those statutes.

This Court’s pre-1970 decisions confirm that OCSLA operates as a selective federal adoption of state law, not as an open-ended license for state supplementation. In Rodrigue, the Court held that fixed drilling platforms are to be treated as “artificial islands,” and that maritime law does not govern simply because the platform is offshore. 395 U.S. at 356-66. The Court explained that when no federal maritime remedy applied, “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” was removed. Id. at 366. That reasoning is gap-based: state law enters only when federal law does not already govern the issue. Likewise, in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 507-09 (1947), the Court treated forum-selection principles as a matter of federal common law, not a reason to import state law whenever it was more plaintiff-friendly. The same discipline applies here.

The better reading is also compelled by the decisions construing the OCSLA’s companion and predecessor models of assimilation. In Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. at 99-100, the Court held that state law applies on a federal enclave only insofar as it is needed to avoid a legal vacuum; it does not displace federal law already in force. Earlier, in James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 292 U.S. at 137-38, the Court similarly recognized that state provisions become operative only as federal law and only to the extent federal law is silent. Those decisions embody the principle that incorporation statutes are not mechanisms for state-law enhancement of federal rights.

Respondent’s broader reading also conflicts with the structure of federal labor law. When Congress creates a comprehensive federal scheme, courts do not presume concurrent state regulation unless Congress clearly says so. See Howard v. Commissioners of the Sinking Fund of Louisville, 344 U.S. 624, 626-27 (1953); International Ass’n of Machinists v. Gonzales, 356 U.S. 617, 619-20 (1958). The Fair Labor Standards Act reflects such a comprehensive federal regime. If OCSLA were read to adopt California wage-and-hour rules whenever they “pertain” to offshore employment, state law would displace the uniform federal standard Congress established for the very subject addressed by the FLSA.

Finally, respondent’s construction would transform OCSLA’s limited incorporation clause into a general invitation to state supplementation whenever state law is more protective. That is not how Congress legislates. The statute says “applicable and not inconsistent,” not “whenever helpful.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Court should therefore hold that OCSLA permits state law only when federal law leaves a genuine gap in the offshore rule of decision. Because the FLSA governs the wage-and-hour issues here, California law is not incorporated as surrogate federal law. Compare Parker Drilling Mgmt. Servs., Ltd. v. Newton, 139 S. Ct. 1881 (2019) with Rodrigue, 395 U.S. 352 (1969).
