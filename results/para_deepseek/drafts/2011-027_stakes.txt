# ARGUMENT

## I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Confirms State Title to Riverbeds That Were Navigable at Statehood

Upon admission to the Union, Montana acquired title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood in 1889 under the equal footing doctrine. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). The constitutional test for navigability for title purposes is whether the river was “used, or susceptible of being used, in its natural and ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce” at the time of statehood. *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. 557, 563 (1871). This test requires a pragmatic, historical inquiry into each discrete stretch of river, not a uniform determination for an entire river system. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 82 (1931). The trial court correctly applied this test to each relevant stretch of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers.

## II. The Trial Court Properly Found Navigability at Statehood Based on Historical Evidence

The trial court examined historical evidence—including steamboat logs, government surveys, newspapers, and other contemporaneous records—to determine whether each stretch of river was used or susceptible to commercial use in its natural condition at statehood. This evidence established that the Missouri River above Fort Benton, the Madison River, and the Clark Fork River were all navigable for commercial purposes in 1889. *See* *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 23 (1935) (relying on similar historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood). The Montana Supreme Court affirmed these findings, holding that the historical record—including evidence of steamboat traffic, log drives, and commercial transport—supported the trial court’s conclusion.

The petitioner’s suggestion that the trial court must apply a heightened, “segment-by-segment” analysis that requires proof of actual commercial use on every discrete mile finds no support in this Court’s precedents. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82, the Court recognized that “the question of navigability must be determined with reference to the particular segment of the river” but did not require a rigid, mile-by-mile inquiry. Rather, the Court held that a river is navigable if it is “in its natural state capable of being used for purposes of commerce” as a whole. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 406 (1940). The trial court properly examined the character of each river at statehood, considering obstacles, seasonal flows, and actual commercial use. That is precisely the pragmatic, fact-intensive inquiry this Court has endorsed.

## III. The State More Than Satisfied Its Burden of Proof

The State bears the burden of proving navigability at statehood by a preponderance of the evidence. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 559. The trial court found that the historical evidence met this standard. The Montana Supreme Court reviewed the record and concluded that the evidence was sufficient. This Court reviews factual findings on navigability for clear error. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 23. The petitioner cannot show clear error, as the historical record—including accounts of steamboat landings, log drives, and federal surveys—amply supports the finding of navigability.

The petitioner’s reliance on the requirement that navigability be determined “as of the time of statehood” is unavailing. The trial court did precisely that. It did not consider later improvements or artificial changes to the rivers. *See* *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87 (rejecting evidence of post-statehood improvements to prove navigability). The court’s reliance on historical sources—including the Foley affidavit and exhibits—was proper. Those sources are admissible under the ancient documents and public records exceptions to the hearsay rule, as the Montana Supreme Court correctly held. *See* *United States v. Kaylor*, 491 F.2d 1127, 1130 (2d Cir. 1974) (holding that historical records are admissible to prove navigability at statehood).

## IV. The Petitioner’s Proposed Test Would Undermine the Equal Footing Doctrine

The petitioner argues that the trial court should have required proof that each specific stretch of river was independently navigable, rather than considering the river as a whole. This Court has never adopted such a rigid rule. The test is whether the river, in its natural condition, was “capable of being used for purposes of commerce” in the ordinary course. *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. at 563. Many rivers contain obstacles that require portage or seasonal low flows, but they remain navigable if they are “in their natural state capable of being used for purposes of commerce.” *Appalachian Elec. Power*, 311 U.S. at 406. The trial court’s findings—that the Missouri River above Fort Benton, the Madison River, and the Clark Fork River were all used for commercial steamboat and log transport at statehood—are consistent with this standard.

The equal footing doctrine is a “bedrock principle” of state sovereignty. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 3 How. 212, 228 (1845). To adopt the petitioner’s narrow, segment-by-segment test would invite endless litigation over discrete river miles and risk depriving states of title to historically navigable stretches. This Court has consistently rejected such formalism, instead requiring a practical, holistic assessment. *See* *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926) (examining the “natural and ordinary condition” of the river, not each individual section).

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision should be affirmed.
