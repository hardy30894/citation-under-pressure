**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE FACIALLY UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE THEY IMPRISON THE FIRST AMENDMENT RIGHTS OF LOBBYISTS BY REQUIRING THE DISCLOSURE OF SPEECH, EXPENDITURES, AND SOURCES OF FUNDING WITHOUT A NECESSARY AND PROPORTIONAL RELATIONSHIP TO A SUBSTANTIAL GOVERNMENTAL INTEREST.

The First Amendment protects not only the content of speech but also the right to engage in anonymous or pseudonymous political expression. *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n*, 514 U.S. 334, 349 (1995). The disclosure requirements imposed by Chapter 93-121—mandating the public reporting of all lobbying expenditures, including food and beverages, entertainment, travel, and lodging, and the source of funds for such expenditures—go far beyond what is necessary to serve any legitimate governmental interest. The statute compels lobbyists to reveal the very nature of their political activity, including the identity of their principals and the financial sources behind their advocacy, thereby chilling protected speech.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the right to speak anonymously is a fundamental aspect of the First Amendment.” *Talley v. California*, 362 U.S. 60, 63 (1960). In *McIntyre*, the Court struck down a state law requiring the disclosure of the names of individuals who distribute campaign literature, holding that “the right to remain anonymous may be a necessary condition to the expression of some ideas.” 514 U.S. at 350. The Court emphasized that “the First Amendment protects the right to speak anonymously, and the government may not compel disclosure of one’s identity as a condition of speaking.” Id. at 351.

Here, the Florida statute compels disclosure of not only the identity of the lobbyist and principal but also the precise nature and source of funding for every expenditure related to lobbying. This sweeping mandate creates a chilling effect on political expression. Lobbyists may refrain from engaging in advocacy altogether if they fear public scrutiny, retaliation, or reputational harm. The statute’s requirement that expenditures be categorized—especially in sensitive categories like “entertainment” and “food and beverages”—invites speculation and misinterpretation, further undermining the free exchange of ideas.

Moreover, the statute fails the test of narrow tailoring. The government’s interest in transparency and accountability is legitimate, but it is not served by a regime that compels the disclosure of every minor expenditure. The Supreme Court has held that “a law that burdens speech must be narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest.” *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968). Florida’s disclosure regime is not narrowly tailored; it applies uniformly to all lobbyists, regardless of the nature or impact of their advocacy. The statute sweeps in trivial expenditures—such as a meal with a legislator—while failing to distinguish between lobbying that influences policy and lobbying that merely seeks access. This overbreadth renders the statute facially invalid.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly invalidated broad disclosure laws that lack a sufficient nexus to a compelling state interest. In *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 38–40 (1976), the Court struck down a provision requiring the disclosure of campaign contributors, holding that “the First Amendment protects the right to engage in political speech without being compelled to reveal the source of funding.” The Court emphasized that “the mere fact that a contribution may be used to influence legislation does not justify compelling disclosure of the contributor’s identity.” Id. at 40. The same principle applies here: the mere fact that a lobbyist receives funds to advocate for a policy does not justify compelling disclosure of the source of those funds or the nature of every expenditure.

The Florida statute, by requiring disclosure of all expenditures—including those that are incidental or routine—fails to meet even the minimal standard of proportionality. It is not a “fit” between the means and the end. *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 472 (2010). The statute’s broad reach undermines the very purpose of the First Amendment: to protect the free exchange of ideas, even when those ideas are unpopular or controversial.

II. THE BAN ON CONTINGENCY-FEE LOBBYING IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE IT DIRECTLY RESTRICTS THE CONTENT OF SPEECH AND THE TERMS OF CONTRACTUAL AGREEMENTS, IN VIOLATION OF THE FIRST AMENDMENT.

The prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying—prohibiting lobbyists from receiving compensation contingent on legislative or executive outcomes—constitutes a direct restriction on the content of speech and the terms of private contracts. This restriction is not merely incidental; it is central to the nature of the lobbying activity itself. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the First Amendment protects not only the content of speech but also the manner in which it is communicated.” *Barnes v. Glen Theatre, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 560, 566 (1991).

In *United States v. Nat’l Treasury Employees Union*, 513 U.S. 454, 472 (1995), the Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to engage in expressive conduct, including the right to contract for the performance of such conduct.” The Court emphasized that “the government may not regulate the terms of a contract simply because the contract involves speech.” Id. at 473. The contingency-fee ban, by prohibiting compensation based on outcome, effectively regulates the terms of a contract between a lobbyist and a principal. It forces lobbyists to accept fixed fees regardless of the success of their efforts, thereby distorting the market for political advocacy.

The Supreme Court has recognized that “the right to contract is a fundamental liberty protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.” *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923). While the government may regulate contracts in the public interest, such regulation must be narrowly tailored and serve a compelling interest. The Florida statute fails this test. The state’s interest in preventing corruption or the appearance of corruption is legitimate, but the blanket ban on contingency fees is not narrowly tailored.
