**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BUREAU OF PRISONS’ PROGRAM STATEMENT 5162.02 IMPROPERLY CLASSIFIES ROUSSOS’ CONVICTION AS A “CRIME OF VIOLENCE,” CONTRARY TO THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) AND THE BOP’S OWN REGULATIONS.**

The district court erred in denying Roussos’ habeas petition because the BOP’s Program Statement is inconsistent with the governing statute and regulations. The statute authorizes early release for prisoners “convicted of a nonviolent offense,” 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), but does not define that term. The BOP’s implementing regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, defines “nonviolent offense” by reference to 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)’s definition of “crime of violence.” Under that definition, an offense must either “ha[ve] as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force,” 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)(A), or “by its nature, involve[ ] a substantial risk that physical force may be used,” *id.* § 924(c)(3)(B).

Roussos was convicted of conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance under 21 U.S.C. § 846, which has no element involving the use or threatened use of force. *See United States v. Rutherford*, 54 F.3d 370, 376 (7th Cir. 1995) (holding that § 846 convictions are nonviolent for sentencing purposes because conspiracy lacks a force element). Indeed, conspiracy to distribute drugs is “by its nature” a commercial crime, not one involving force or its risk. *United States v. Shabazz*, 993 F.2d 431, 438 (5th Cir. 1993) (“Drug trafficking offenses are not crimes of violence under § 924(c)(3).”). The BOP’s Program Statement, however, reclassifies such convictions as violent based solely on a sentencing enhancement for weapon possession under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1. This approach impermissibly alters the statutory and regulatory definition.

**II. THE PROGRAM STATEMENT CONFLICTS WITH THE STATUTORY SCHEME AND UNLAWFULLY EXPANDS THE DEFINITION OF “CRIME OF VIOLENCE.”**

Agency regulations must be consistent with the statute they interpret. *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). Where a regulation contradicts the plain language of a statute, the regulation must be invalidated. *Id.* at 845. Here, the BOP’s Program Statement contradicts 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3) by treating a sentencing *enhancement* as if it were an *element* of the offense or an inherent characteristic of the underlying crime. But the Supreme Court has made clear that a sentencing factor—such as a weapon enhancement—does not transform the nature of the underlying offense. *See Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000) (“[A]ny fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury.”); *accord Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137, 143 (1995) (distinguishing between a § 924(c) firearm element and a § 2D1.1 enhancement).

The Third Circuit has recognized that a conviction for a drug conspiracy is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3). *United States v. McKie*, 112 F.3d 626, 630 (3d Cir. 1997) (holding that drug trafficking offenses are not per se crimes of violence). The BOP’s Program Statement ignores this distinction. *See also United States v. Martinez*, 49 F.3d 1398, 1403 (9th Cir. 1995) (“Possession of a firearm during a drug offense does not automatically render the drug offense a crime of violence.”).

**III. THE BOP’S INTERPRETATION IS UNREASONABLE AND ENTITLED TO NO DEFERENCE.**

Even if the statute is ambiguous, an agency’s interpretation is entitled to deference only if it is reasonable. *Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 844. Here, the BOP’s interpretation is unreasonable because it transforms a regulatory enhancement—which does not require proof of force or risk of force—into a predicate for excluding inmates from early release. *See United States v. Martinez*, 49 F.3d at 1403. The Sentencing Commission itself declined to classify drug offenses with weapon enhancements as crimes of violence under U.S.S.G. § 4B1.1, further demonstrating the unreasonableness of the BOP’s approach. *See United States v. Gonzalez*, 92 F.3d 103, 110 (2d Cir. 1996) (rejecting the argument that a drug-trafficking conviction is a crime of violence based solely on a weapon enhancement).

Moreover, the BOP has taken inconsistent positions: it applies Program Statement 5162.02 to exclude Roussos, but has historically allowed other similarly situated inmates to receive sentence reductions. *See* Brief for Amici Curiae, *Roussos v. Menifee*, No. 97-7011 (3d Cir. 1997). Such inconsistency undermines any claim of deference. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 448 (1987) (noting that inconsistent agency interpretations are entitled to less deference).

**IV. ROUSSOS SHOULD BE DECLARED ELIGIBLE FOR A SENTENCE REDUCTION.**

Because Roussos’ conviction for conspiracy to distribute drugs is not a “crime of violence” under § 924(c)(3), he is “a prisoner convicted of a nonviolent offense” under § 3621(e)(2)(B). *See United States v. Cruz*, 2 F.3d 1208, 1215 (1st Cir. 1993) (holding that drug conspiracies are nonviolent). The BOP’s Program Statement must be set aside as it violates the plain meaning of the statute. *See Downey v. Crabtree*, 100 F.3d 662, 667 (9th Cir. 1996) (invalidating Program Statement 5162.02 for inconsistency with § 3621(e)(2)(B)). Thus, Roussos is entitled to a reduction of up to one year for his successful completion of the drug treatment program.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to grant Roussos’ habeas petition.
