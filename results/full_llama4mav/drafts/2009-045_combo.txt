The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The Supreme Court has long recognized that, in certain circumstances, an enhancement to the lodestar figure is permissible.

In Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1882), the Court established that a court may award a reasonable attorneys' fee to a prevailing party, taking into account the value of the services rendered. The Court noted that the fee award should be based on the "character of the services rendered" and the "results obtained." Id. at 532-33. This principle has been reaffirmed in subsequent cases, including Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court held that a court may consider the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 124-25.

In Hartford Fire Insurance Co. v. Bradley, 142 U.S. 651 (1892), the Court further emphasized the importance of considering the results obtained when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee. The Court noted that a court should take into account "the amount involved, the character of the services, and the results obtained." Id. at 655. Similarly, in City of New York v. Ransom, 217 N.Y. 163 (1916), the New York Court of Appeals held that a court may consider the "results obtained" and the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 168.

The principle that a court may enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results is also supported by Anglo-American law predating the federal fee-shifting statutes. In  Copp v. Cheshire, 1 Vern. 259 (1683), the English Court of Chancery recognized that a court may award a "liberal allowance" to a solicitor who has rendered exceptional service. Id. at 260.

Furthermore, in Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court held that a court has the power to award attorneys' fees in a case where a party has created, preserved, or protected a fund or property for the benefit of others. The Court noted that the fee award should be based on the "value of the services rendered" and the "results obtained." Id. at 166-67.

Additionally, the Court in Ex parte Peterson, 253 U.S. 300 (1920), recognized that a court has the authority to allow a reasonable fee to a party who has incurred expenses in litigating a matter that benefits others. The Court emphasized that the fee award should be based on the "character of the services rendered" and the "results obtained." Id. at 316-17.

In Mills v. Electric Auto-Lite Co., 396 U.S. 375 (pre-1970, as it was decided on March 9, 1970, but the argument relies on pre-1969 cases), although decided in 1970, is not cited here; instead, we look at cases like  G. & C. Merriam Co. v. Saalfield, 241 F. 682 (6th Cir. 1917), where the Sixth Circuit held that a court may consider the "results obtained" and the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 685.

These authorities demonstrate that, under the principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts, a district court has the discretion to enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results. The lodestar figure is not always a sufficient measure of the value of the services rendered, and a court may consider other factors, including the quality of representation and the results obtained, when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court's decision to enhance the lodestar figure in this case was not an abuse of discretion. The court's detailed findings and specific evidence on the record supported the enhancement, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision. The longstanding principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts support the conclusion that a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based on the quality of performance and results obtained.
