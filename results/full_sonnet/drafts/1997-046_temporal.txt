# ARGUMENT

## I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Ohio's Clemency Procedure Protected by the Due Process Clause.

### A. Executive clemency has always been understood as an act of grace outside the ordinary machinery of adjudication.

This Court has long recognized that the power to pardon or commute a sentence is fundamentally unlike a judicial proceeding. In *Ex parte Wells*, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307 (1855), the Court described a pardon as "an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual ... from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." *Id.* at 311. That characterization reflects a deliberate constitutional choice to commit clemency to executive discretion rather than to the procedural safeguards attending trial and appeal.

*Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333 (1866), confirmed the breadth of that discretion, holding that the pardoning power "is not subject to legislative control" and "extends to every offence known to the law." *Id.* at 380. If the legislature itself cannot constrain the pardon power, it follows that courts should not import the procedural apparatus of a criminal trial into a proceeding the Constitution places beyond legislative reach altogether.

*Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480 (1927), likewise recognized that a clemency decision serves ends belonging to the public, not to the individual. The Court there held that the executive may commute a sentence without the prisoner's consent because clemency's purpose is "to determine ... whether the public welfare will be better served" by leniency, not to vindicate any personal claim held by the prisoner. *Id.* at 486. An interest that may be conferred or withheld without regard to the beneficiary's own choice is not the kind of interest the Due Process Clause was designed to protect.

### B. No protected liberty interest arises absent a legitimate claim of entitlement recognized by law.

This Court's due process cases have consistently distinguished between interests grounded in law and mere unilateral expectations. In *Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Committee v. McGrath*, 341 U.S. 123 (1951), the Court explained that due process protects only interests that the individual may legitimately claim as his own, not those resting on the grace of another. Similarly, *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886 (1961), held that "the nature of the interest at stake" determines whether — and how much — process is due, *id.* at 895, and that a discretionary privilege calls for markedly less protection than an established right.

Clemency in Ohio, no less than the pardon considered in *Wells*, *Garland*, and *Biddle*, is a matter of grace administered at the unfettered discretion of the Governor. Woodard's judgment of conviction and sentence has already been finally adjudicated through the process the Constitution guarantees — trial, appeal, and collateral review. What remains is not a further stage of adjudication but an appeal to executive mercy that lies wholly outside it. Because Woodard possesses no legitimate claim of entitlement to clemency, he has no life or liberty interest that the Due Process Clause protects, and the Sixth Circuit erred in holding otherwise.

## II. The Option to Participate Voluntarily in a Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.

### A. The privilege against self-incrimination bars only compelled disclosures, not voluntary ones.

The Fifth Amendment, as applied to the States through *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1 (1964), forbids the State from "compelling" a person to give evidence against himself; it does not forbid a person from *choosing* to speak. *Id.* at 8. As this Court explained in *Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293 (1966), the privilege "protects a person only against being incriminated by his own compelled testimonial communications." *Id.* at 302 (emphasis added). Where a defendant freely elects to speak, no constitutional violation occurs merely because his words might later be used against him.

*Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436 (1966), does not compel a different result. Miranda's warnings guard against the "inherently compelling pressures" of custodial interrogation conducted for the purpose of securing evidence for prosecution. *Id.* at 467. Ohio's clemency interview bears no resemblance to that setting: it is not police interrogation, it is not directed at securing a conviction, and Woodard's conviction and sentence are already final. The coercive dynamic that concerned the Court in *Miranda* is simply absent.

### B. Because declining the interview carries no penalty, no unconstitutional condition is imposed.

This Court's "penalty cases" invalidate compelled waivers only where the State threatens to strip the individual of some benefit or status he already possesses if he invokes the privilege. In *Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493 (1967), statements were held compelled because refusal to answer meant forfeiture of public employment. *Id.* at 497–98. *Gardner v. Broderick*, 392 U.S. 273 (1968), and *Uniformed Sanitation Men Ass'n v. Commissioner of Sanitation*, 392 U.S. 280 (1968), applied the same principle to threatened dismissal from public office.

No comparable threat exists here. Woodard loses nothing by declining the interview; he remains exactly where he already stood — a capital inmate whose judgment is final and who retains full access to the clemency process through written submissions, affidavits, and argument of counsel. Because the "option" of an interview subtracts nothing from what Woodard already possesses, it imposes no penalty and therefore no unconstitutional condition.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
