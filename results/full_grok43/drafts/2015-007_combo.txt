ARGUMENT

The Sixth Amendment does not compel a unanimous jury determination of every fact bearing on capital punishment. See Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 586 (1900) (observing that the amendment “does not require a unanimous verdict” in state prosecutions). Nor does it mandate that every subsidiary factual question, including mental capacity, be resolved by the petit jury. See Patton v. United States, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930) (noting the common-law jury’s role was limited to “the issues of fact” actually submitted). Florida’s scheme therefore satisfies the constitutional guarantee.

The Eighth Amendment likewise imposes no requirement that mental retardation be treated as an absolute bar determined by jury vote. Early decisions recognized that the clause forbids only those punishments “repugnant to the conscience of mankind.” Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 470 (1947) (Frankfurter, J., concurring). Mental-retardation claims, when presented as mitigation, fall within the sentencer’s traditional discretion. See Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949) (affirming that “the sentencing judge” may consider “the fullest information possible”).

Historical practice confirms that unanimity is not an indispensable feature of jury trial. See American Publishing Co. v. Fisher, 166 U.S. 464, 468 (1897) (tracing the common-law rule but acknowledging state variations). The same body of authority permitted non-unanimous verdicts in capital cases under certain state constitutions. See Maxwell, 176 U.S. at 605. Nothing in the federal charter elevates unanimity to a constitutional command for every aggravating circumstance.

Petitioner’s reliance on evolving standards cannot override these settled precedents. The Eighth Amendment’s meaning is fixed by reference to “those modes of punishment which were recognized in 1791.” Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 135 (1878). Mental retardation, like other mitigating factors, remains subject to judicial resolution consistent with that original understanding. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (holding that the clause reaches only “unusual” methods unknown to the common law).

Florida’s requirement that the trial judge independently weigh aggravating and mitigating circumstances therefore comports with both Amendments. See Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934) (emphasizing that due process permits “rational” allocation of fact-finding between judge and jury). The judgment should be affirmed.
