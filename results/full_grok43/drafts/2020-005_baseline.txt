**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017) (quoting *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014)). This standard is satisfied here.

Ford has continuously and deliberately exploited the Montana market for decades by selling thousands of vehicles, advertising extensively, and maintaining a network of dealers and service centers in the State. These contacts are not isolated or sporadic; they reflect Ford’s purposeful availment of the privilege of conducting business in Montana. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985); *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980).

The claims at issue—alleging design defect, failure to warn, and negligence in a Ford Explorer—relate directly to those contacts. The vehicle model was among those Ford marketed and sold in Montana; the alleged defects concern the same product line; and the injury occurred while the plaintiff was driving in Montana. The “relate to” prong encompasses this substantial connection even absent a strict causal link between the particular vehicle’s sale and the forum. *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, 582 U.S. at 262.

Requiring causation would impose an artificial limit unsupported by precedent. The Court has long recognized that specific jurisdiction exists when a defendant’s forum activities create a “substantial connection” with the litigation. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984). Ford’s systematic promotion and servicing of the Explorer line in Montana supplies that connection.

Exercising jurisdiction also comports with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents injured on its highways by products Ford placed in the stream of commerce there. *See Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987). Ford, having reaped the benefits of the Montana market, cannot reasonably object to suit arising from alleged defects in the very products it sold and serviced in the State.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that specific jurisdiction exists.
