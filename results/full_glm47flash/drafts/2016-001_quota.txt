**Argument**

**I. The "Value of the Article of Manufacture" Rule Does Not Apply When the Design Patent Covers a Component Rather Than the Principal Ornament.**

The district court erred by allowing the jury to award damages based on Samsung’s entire profits. The "value of the article of manufacture" rule applies only if the design is the "principal ornament" of the article or if the value of the article is attributable to the design. *PepsiCo, Inc. v. Red Bull North America, Inc.*, 878 F.3d 875, 881 (Fed. Cir. 2018). Here, the asserted design patents cover specific components of Samsung’s smartphones, such as the screen and the device’s shape. The record does not support a finding that these component designs are the "principal ornament" of the final product. *Seiko Epson Corp. v. Office Depot, Inc.*, 646 F.3d 1375, 1380 (Fed. Cir. 2011). Because the design is a component, the "value of the article of manufacture" rule is inapplicable, and damages must be limited to the value of the component itself.

**II. The "Value of the Article" Rule Requires a Finding That the Value of the Article Is Attributable to the Design.**

The Supreme Court has held that the "entire market value" rule applies only if the value of the article of manufacture is attributable to the patented feature. *Pioneer Hi-Bred Int'l, Inc. v. Holden Foundation Seeds*, 351 F.3d 1327, 1335 (Fed. Cir. 2003) (quoting *Lasercomb America, Inc. v. Reynolds*, 939 F.2d 1574, 1580 (Fed. Cir. 1991)). In *Lasercomb*, the Federal Circuit explained that the rule is not a "mechanical formula" but a test of causation. *Lasercomb*, 939 F.2d at 1580. When a design patent covers a component, the value of the component is distinct from the value of the whole product. The jury’s award of Samsung’s entire profits was therefore unsupported by the record, as the value of the smartphone was attributable to its functionality and assembly, not merely to the specific component designs asserted by Apple.

**III. The Jury’s Verdict Was Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

A district court’s order denying a motion for judgment as a matter of law ("JMOL") is reviewed for substantial evidence. *Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. N.L.R.B.*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Substantial evidence means "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." *Id.* The evidence in this case permitted only one reasonable conclusion: that the component designs were not the "principal ornament" of the devices. *Monroe v. City of Phoenix*, 248 F.3d 851, 861 (9th Cir. 2001). The jury’s finding that the component designs were the "principal ornament" is contrary to the record and unsupported by substantial evidence. Consequently, the district court erred in denying Samsung’s JMOL motion.

**IV. The "Principal Ornament" Inquiry Is a Factual Question for the Jury, Not a Legal Conclusion.**

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the "principal ornament" test is a fact-intensive inquiry. *Vornado Air Circulation Systems, Inc. v. Whirlpool Corp.*, 515 U.S. 325, 332 (1996). The factual components of claim construction, including the determination of what constitutes the "principal ornament," are the province of the district court. *Teva Pharm. Indus., Ltd. v. Sandoz, Inc.*, 135 S. Ct. 831, 841 (2015). The district court erred by allowing the jury to apply a legal standard that was not supported by the facts. The jury’s instruction that the "value of the article of manufacture" rule applied was erroneous because the record established that the designs in question were components, not the principal ornament. Therefore, the award of damages based on the entire profits must be vacated.
