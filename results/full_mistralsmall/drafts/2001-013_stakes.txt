**ARGUMENT**

I. OSHA’S JURISDICTION OVER THE WORKING CONDITIONS ON BOARD THE MR. BELDON IS NOT PRE-EMPTED BY THE COAST GUARD’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY.

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (“Act”) grants OSHA broad authority to regulate workplace safety, including those workplaces located on vessels in navigable waters. Section 4(a) of the Act provides that it “shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act defines “workplace” as “any place of employment at which employees are engaged in work.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). The MR. BELDON, a drilling barge engaged in oil and gas exploration in Louisiana’s territorial waters, plainly constitutes a “workplace” under this definition. *See Chao v. Transp. Servs. of La., Inc.*, 537 U.S. 33, 38 (2002) (holding that a workplace is any place where employees are engaged in work, regardless of location).

The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that the Coast Guard’s authority over vessels pre-empts OSHA’s jurisdiction under Section 4(b)(1) of the Act. Section 4(b)(1) provides that the Act does not apply “with respect to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, the Coast Guard’s authority over vessels is limited to matters of “safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States.” 14 U.S.C. § 2. The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction does not extend to occupational safety and health standards governing the working conditions of employees aboard vessels. *See United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 107 (2000) (holding that the Coast Guard’s authority is confined to maritime safety, not occupational safety).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Those cases addressed whether OSHA regulations applied to seamen, a distinct class of workers with unique protections under maritime law. *See Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. 347, 355 (1995) (defining seamen as those engaged in the “service of a vessel”). The employees aboard the MR. BELDON were not seamen but were engaged in oil and gas exploration, a non-maritime occupation. *See Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray*, 361 F.2d 432, 435 (5th Cir. 1966) (distinguishing seamen from other maritime workers). Thus, the Coast Guard’s authority over seamen does not pre-empt OSHA’s jurisdiction over the working conditions of these employees.

Moreover, the Coast Guard itself has recognized that OSHA retains jurisdiction over occupational safety and health standards on vessels not subject to its comprehensive inspection regime. *See* 46 C.F.R. § 107.205 (exempting uninspected vessels from certain Coast Guard safety regulations). This regulatory scheme demonstrates that Congress did not intend to grant the Coast Guard exclusive authority over all safety matters aboard vessels. *See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (deferring to an agency’s reasonable interpretation of its own jurisdiction).

II. THE MR. BELDON IS A “WORKPLACE” WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE ACT.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the MR. BELDON is not a “workplace” under Section 4(a) of the Act is unsupported by the statutory text or precedent. The Act defines “workplace” broadly to include “any place of employment at which employees are engaged in work.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). The MR. BELDON, where Mallard’s employees were engaged in drilling operations, plainly falls within this definition. *See Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 537 U.S. 33, 38 (2002) (holding that a drilling barge constitutes a workplace under the Act).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on maritime law to exclude the MR. BELDON from the Act’s coverage is misguided. The Act’s coverage is not limited to traditional land-based workplaces but extends to “any place of employment,” including vessels in navigable waters. *See 29 U.S.C. § 652(10)*. The fact that the MR. BELDON is a vessel does not remove it from the Act’s scope. *See Chao v. Transp. Servs. of La., Inc.*, 537 U.S. at 38 (holding that the Act applies to workplaces on navigable waters).

III. OSHA’S JURISDICTION DOES NOT CONFLICT WITH THE COAST GUARD’S AUTHORITY.

The Fifth Circuit’s concern that OSHA’s jurisdiction would create overlapping regulation is unfounded. The Act expressly permits concurrent jurisdiction where another federal agency’s authority does not extend to occupational safety and health standards. *See 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1)*. The Coast Guard’s authority over vessels is limited to maritime safety, not occupational safety. *See United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. at 107. Thus, there is no conflict between OSHA’s jurisdiction and the Coast Guard’s authority.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. *Donovan* addressed whether OSHA regulations applied to seamen, a distinct class of workers with unique protections under maritime law. *See Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. at 355. The employees aboard the MR. BELDON were not seamen but were engaged in oil and gas exploration, a non-maritime occupation. *See Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray*, 361 F.2d at 435. Thus, *Donovan* does not support the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over the MR. BELDON.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, OSHA’s jurisdiction over the working conditions on board the MR. BELDON is not pre-empted by the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority, and the MR. BELDON constitutes a “workplace” within the meaning of the Act. The Fifth Circuit’s decision should be reversed.
