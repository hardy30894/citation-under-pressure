ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce expert evidence of his mental condition to negate the mens rea element of a crime when the State has chosen to treat mental illness exclusively through an insanity defense. Arizona’s rule is a permissible exercise of its sovereign power to define the elements of crimes and the defenses available to an accused.

**I. States Have Broad Discretion to Define the Insanity Defense and to Limit the Use of Mental-State Evidence**

This Court has long recognized that the States enjoy wide latitude in structuring the insanity defense and in allocating the burden of proof. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that required a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt and that permitted the jury to consider evidence of mental condition only on the issue of insanity, not on the question of premeditation. The defendant in *Leland* argued, as Clark does here, that due process entitled him to have the jury consider his mental condition when determining whether he possessed the requisite criminal intent. This Court rejected that argument, stating: “The state may, however, treat the problem of mental illness as a matter of defense, and may place the burden of proof on the defendant.” *Id.* at 798. The Court further explained that the State’s rule did not deprive the defendant of any constitutional right because he remained free to present his evidence of mental condition in support of his insanity claim. *Id.* at 799.

Similarly, in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), the Court held that the Constitution does not compel a State to recognize a defense based on chronic alcoholism. The Court observed that “the State of Texas has not recognized the condition of chronic alcoholism as a defense to the crime of public drunkenness. This Court has never held that the Constitution requires such a defense.” *Id.* at 536. *Powell* reinforces the principle that a State may choose the boundaries of its affirmative defenses, including those rooted in mental condition.

Arizona has made a policy choice to channel all evidence of mental illness into its insanity defense. That defense permits a defendant to present expert testimony to prove that he lacked criminal responsibility. But the State may also decide that such evidence is not admissible to negate the specific intent element of a crime. This is a substantive rule of law, not an evidentiary restriction that implicates due process. The defendant is not barred from presenting evidence of his mental state; he is merely required to use it within the framework the State has established.

**II. The Defendant Was Not Denied a Meaningful Opportunity to Present a Defense**

Clark was permitted to introduce expert evidence of his paranoid schizophrenia in support of his insanity claim. He was not precluded from presenting other evidence—such as lay testimony or circumstantial evidence—to argue that he lacked the requisite criminal intent. The State’s rule does not foreclose all avenues of proof; it simply limits the use of expert psychiatric testimony to the insanity defense. Such a limitation is a reasonable exercise of the State’s authority to regulate the admissibility of expert evidence. *See, e.g., West v. Louisiana*, 194 U.S. 258, 262 (1904) (holding that the Due Process Clause does not require a State to adopt any particular rule of evidence).

**III. The State May Allocate the Burden of Proof on the Insanity Defense**

In *Leland*, the Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798. Arizona’s rule is consistent with that principle. The defendant bears the burden of proving insanity by a preponderance of the evidence. He cannot complain that the State has limited the use of his expert evidence to that defense, because the defense itself provides a full opportunity to present the evidence. The Due Process Clause does not require the State to allow the same evidence to be used for two different purposes when the State has chosen to treat mental illness as a single, affirmative defense.

**IV. Federal Precedents Do Not Compel a Different Result**

Clark may rely on *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), which established a federal common law rule that once some evidence of insanity is introduced, the government must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt. But *Davis* is not a constitutional decision. As this Court made clear in *Leland*, the States are not bound by the federal rule. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 797–98. The Constitution leaves to each State the choice of how to treat mental illness in its criminal law.

**Conclusion**

Arizona’s rule does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The State may define the insanity defense as the exclusive vehicle for considering mental illness and may preclude the use of expert evidence to negate mens rea. The judgment of the Arizona courts should be affirmed.
