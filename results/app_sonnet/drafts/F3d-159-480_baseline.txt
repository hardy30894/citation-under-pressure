**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1951 Exceeds Congress's Commerce Clause Authority As Applied to This Purely Local Robbery**

The Commerce Clause does not permit Congress to criminalize conduct with only an attenuated connection to interstate commerce. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559 (1995) (requiring a "substantial effect" on interstate commerce for regulation of noneconomic, local activity). While this Court has held that the Hobbs Act's jurisdictional element may be satisfied by a minimal effect on commerce, *United States v. Bolton*, 68 F.3d 396, 398 (10th Cir. 1995), that reasoning cannot survive *Lopez*'s insistence that Congress may not "obliterate the distinction between what is national and what is local." *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 557. The robbery of a single local sandwich shop's cash register is precisely the kind of intrastate, noneconomic-in-effect crime the Commerce Clause leaves to the states. Even under *Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212, 215 (1960), which recognized federal jurisdiction where robbery proceeds "would have been used to buy" out-of-state goods, the connection here—speculative testimony that stolen cash "would have been used" to restock supplies—is too tenuous to constitute the "substantial effect" *Lopez* demands.

**II. The District Court Erred in Excluding Mr. Pearson's Alibi Evidence**

A trial court's power to exclude defense evidence for a discovery violation is not unlimited; preclusion is permissible only where the violation is willful and the sanction proportionate. *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 415 (1988) (upholding preclusion only after finding the defendant's noncompliance "willful and motivated by a desire to obtain a tactical advantage"). Here, nothing in the record suggests Mr. Pearson withheld his alibi in bad faith; the watch-shop receipt and pager records were promptly produced once counsel located them. Under *Michigan v. Lucas*, 500 U.S. 145, 152–53 (1991), preclusion sanctions must be reserved for the "most extreme cases," and a defendant's constitutional right "to present a defense" may not be sacrificed to enforce a technical notice rule. Excluding this corroborated alibi—supported by an independent employee's testimony—deprived Mr. Pearson of his right to present a complete defense.

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support the Convictions**

On sufficiency review, the question is "whether, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The government's identification evidence was fatally weak: Ms. Starks failed to identify Mr. Pearson in a photo array, saw his photograph on television before trial, and identified him in court only because he was the sole young black man present. Her identification of Mr. Martin as the gunman, combined with Mr. Martin's own admission that he fired the fatal shot, undercuts the government's theory of Mr. Pearson's participation. Where identification testimony is this unreliable, and corroborated only by an accomplice with an obvious incentive to cooperate, no rational jury could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319.

**IV. The District Court's Felony-Murder Instruction Misstated the Law**

Liability for a co-felon's act requires that the act be a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the underlying felony agreement. *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 647–48 (1946) (co-conspirator liable only for acts "in furtherance of the conspiracy" that were "reasonably foreseen"). The instruction given here permitted the jury to convict Mr. Pearson of felony murder without any finding that the accidental discharge of Mr. Martin's weapon was foreseeable to him. Moreover, imposing capital-equivalent liability on a non-triggerman absent proof of intent to kill or reckless disregard for life offends the principles articulated in *Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782, 797 (1982), and *Tison v. Arizona*, 481 U.S. 137, 158 (1987), which limit felony-murder culpability for accomplices who neither killed nor intended lethal force.

**V. The District Court Erred in Refusing Mr. Pearson's Lesser-Included-Offense Instructions**

A defendant is entitled to a lesser-included-offense instruction where "the evidence would permit a jury rationally to find him guilty of the lesser offense and acquit him of the greater." *Keeble v. United States*, 412 U.S. 205, 208 (1973). The offense must satisfy the elements test of *Schmuck v. United States*, 489 U.S. 705, 716 (1989), under which a lesser offense is "necessarily included" if its elements are a subset of the greater offense's elements. Mr. Pearson's proposed instructions on simple robbery, without the death-resulting element, met this test: the jury could have credited his alibi and accomplice testimony to find mere peripheral involvement in planning without participation in the fatal robbery itself. Refusing the instruction left the jury an all-or-nothing choice, contrary to *Keeble*'s command that defendants receive the benefit of a rational middle ground supported by the evidence.
