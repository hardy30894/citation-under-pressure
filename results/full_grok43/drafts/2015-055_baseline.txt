ARGUMENT

The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. Johnson held that the Armed Career Criminal Act’s residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges” and is therefore “unconstitutionally vague.” Id. at 2557. That holding is a new substantive rule that “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004).

Under Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new substantive rules apply retroactively on collateral review because they “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal or faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” Schriro, 542 U.S. at 352 (internal quotation marks omitted). Johnson is substantive: it “narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998), removing an entire category of offenses from the definition of “violent felony.” After Johnson, a defendant whose prior conviction qualified only under the residual clause “faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” Schriro, 542 U.S. at 352.

The government’s contrary position—that Johnson is merely procedural—misapprehends the distinction drawn in Schriro. Procedural rules “regulate only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability,” id. at 353, whereas Johnson “modifies the elements necessary to secure a conviction or impose a sentence,” id. at 354. Because the residual clause is void, no defendant may lawfully be sentenced under it. The Eleventh Circuit’s refusal to apply Johnson therefore leaves Welch serving a fifteen-year mandatory minimum that the statute no longer authorizes.

Petitioner’s Florida robbery conviction was counted solely under the residual clause. Johnson’s retroactive application requires that the sentence be vacated.
