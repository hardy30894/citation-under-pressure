**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE STATUTE.**

A criminal defendant may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge a federal statute on federalism grounds. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) ("The [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment."); *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485 (1923) (taxpayer lacked standing to challenge federal spending as violating the Tenth Amendment because "[t]he administration of any federal statute, valid because within the scope of federal power, would be subject to attack by any person who succeeded in showing that the effect of the statute was to increase the cost of his duties on account of the alleged unconstitutional use of the funds."). The Tenth Amendment "is not a source of any federal rights," but instead "states a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It does not confer rights upon individuals, but rather "emphasizes the distribution of power between the States and the Federal Government." *Id.* Thus, only a state whose sovereignty is impaired may raise a Tenth Amendment claim. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998) (citing *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 155–56 (1992)).

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. That case permitted a private party to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, but it did so without analyzing the Supreme Court’s controlling precedent in *Tennessee Electric Power* or *Frothingham*. The Third Circuit correctly recognized the weight of authority rejecting such standing. *Bond v. United States*, 681 F.3d 149, 160–61 (3d Cir. 2012) (citing *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005)). This Court should adhere to the Supreme Court’s settled rule: private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal statutes.

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER AND NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE AUTHORITY.**

Congress may enact legislation to implement a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) ("If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government."); *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 320 (1936) ("The investment of the federal government with the powers of external sovereignty did not depend upon the affirmative grants of the Constitution."). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing it. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 433 ("It is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well-being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.").

Bond’s argument that § 229 lacks a federal interest element is unavailing. The Necessary and Proper Clause permits Congress to regulate intrastate activity if it is "conducive" to the treaty’s enforcement. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 435 ("The treaty in question does not contravene any prohibitory words to be found in the Constitution. The only question is whether it is forbidden by some invisible radiation from the general terms of the Tenth Amendment."). The statute’s application to Bond’s conduct is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority to prevent the use of chemical weapons, regardless of whether the conduct is otherwise local in nature. *Id.* at 434–35.

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE.**

A statute is not void for vagueness if it "define[s] the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited." *Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972) (quoting *United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 7 (1947)). Section 229 provides clear notice: it prohibits the knowing use of toxic chemicals as weapons. *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975) ("[A] statute is not vague if it ‘define[s] the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited.’"). Bond’s conduct—repeatedly spreading toxic chemicals on doorknobs, car handles, and a mailbox—falls squarely within the statute’s prohibition. The statute’s definitional provisions further clarify its scope. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229F (defining "chemical weapon" and "toxic chemical"). There is no merit to Bond’s vagueness claim.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power, and the statute is not unconstitutionally vague.
