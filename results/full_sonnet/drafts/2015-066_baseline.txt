# ARGUMENT

**AN UNCOUNSELED TRIBAL COURT CONVICTION, VALID WHEN ENTERED, MAY BE USED AS A PREDICATE OFFENSE UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)**

Because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings, and because this Court's precedents establish that a valid, uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used to support guilt or enhance punishment in a subsequent proceeding, the Ninth Circuit's judgment should be reversed.

## A. Tribal Courts Are Not Bound by the Sixth Amendment

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is a constraint on the federal and state governments; it does not apply to Indian tribes exercising their retained sovereign authority. *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896). This Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that "the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes." *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008); *see also Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 56 (1978) (tribes retain "attributes of sovereignty over both their members and their territory," including the power to structure their own judicial systems).

Congress, not the Constitution, governs the procedural protections owed to defendants in tribal court. Through the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, Congress imposed a modified due process and right-to-counsel regime tailored to tribal courts—one that does not require appointed counsel for misdemeanants facing sentences of one year or less. Bryant's tribal court convictions, obtained in full compliance with ICRA, were not constitutionally infirm. They were, in the Ninth Circuit's own words, "valid tribal court convictions." *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 675 (9th Cir. 2014). A conviction that violates no constitutional provision cannot logically be deemed to "violate the Sixth Amendment" merely because it lacked counsel.

## B. Nichols v. United States Forecloses the Ninth Circuit's Rule

This Court's decision in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), controls. There, the Court held that "an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott* because no prison term was imposed, is also valid when used to enhance punishment at a subsequent conviction." *Id.* at 746–47 (citing *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). The touchstone of *Nichols* was not whether the prior proceeding resulted in imprisonment, but whether the prior conviction was *valid* under the law governing that proceeding. Because Bryant's tribal convictions were valid under ICRA—the only body of law that could have required counsel—they satisfy *Nichols*'s validity requirement a fortiori.

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on the distinction between sentencing enhancements and substantive elements, *see Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 676, misreads *Nichols* and this Court's recidivism jurisprudence. This Court has "consistently" recognized that recidivist statutes "penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant." *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747; *accord United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) ("When a defendant is given a higher sentence under a recidivism statute... 100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction. None is for the prior convictions."). Section 117(a) does not punish Bryant for his tribal court conduct; it punishes his 2011 domestic assaults, using the prior convictions solely as evidence of recidivist status—precisely the function such convictions serve in every enhancement scheme this Court has approved. The label "element" versus "sentencing factor" does not alter the analysis, because the reliability rationale of *Nichols* turns on the validity of the predicate conviction, not the procedural stage at which it is invoked.

## C. Alabama v. Shelton Confirms That a Valid Misdemeanor Conviction Survives Even When a Sentence of Imprisonment May Not Be Enforced

*Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), reinforces this conclusion. *Shelton* held that a suspended sentence may not be activated based on an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, but it left the underlying conviction itself undisturbed. *Id.* at 662. The Court's remedy was targeted at the punishment, not the conviction's validity for all purposes. If an actual term of imprisonment imposed without counsel in violation of the Sixth Amendment does not vitiate the underlying conviction, then a-fortiori a tribal court conviction—where no Sixth Amendment violation occurred at all—remains valid for all purposes, including use as a § 117(a) predicate.

## D. Principles of Comity and Respect for Tribal Sovereignty Reinforce This Conclusion

This Court has long extended comity to tribal court judgments, recognizing that "comity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts." *Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (extending comity to tribal judgments). The Ninth Circuit's rule—that tribal convictions may be used only if tribal courts provide a right to counsel "coextensive" with the Sixth Amendment—imposes exactly the kind of "judicial paternalism in derogation of tribal self-governance" that this Court has cautioned against. *See Santa Clara Pueblo*, 436 U.S. at 71 (federal courts should not "intrude[] needlessly on tribal self-government").

Congress's plenary authority over Indian affairs, *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004), and its considered judgment in enacting § 117(a) to address the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country, warrant deference. Congress separately addressed the right-to-counsel question where it deemed appropriate, mandating expanded protections only for tribal courts exercising enhanced sentencing authority under the Tribal Law and Order Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)–(c), and under the special domestic-violence jurisdiction of 25 U.S.C. § 1304. Congress did not extend that requirement to the antecedent tribal court misdemeanor convictions used as § 117(a) predicates, and courts should not rewrite that considered legislative judgment under the guise of constitutional interpretation.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
