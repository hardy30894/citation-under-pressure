ARGUMENT

I. The Temporary-Scheduling Provision Sets Forth an Intelligible Principle and Is a Valid Delegation of Authority.

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but that provision does not prevent Congress from enlisting the assistance of the Executive Branch in administering complex regulatory programs, so long as Congress establishes a standard to guide the exercise of delegated authority. This principle traces to Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), where the Court explained that delegation is confined by "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution." Congress satisfies this requirement when it establishes a standard sufficiently definite to guide the delegate's exercise of discretion. J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928).

Section 811(h) meets that standard. Congress required the Attorney General to find that temporary scheduling was necessary to "avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), after weighing factors drawn from § 811(c)(4)-(6) concerning a substance's history and pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. Congress further required Federal Register notice, consideration of the views of the Secretary of Health and Human Services, and a firm outer limit—one year, extendable by only six months—on the duration of any temporary order. These are the kind of confining standards this Court has approved when Congress must act quickly against evolving hazards. See Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944) (delegation upheld where rapid change in the regulated subject made detailed legislative specification impracticable); Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952) (availability of review supports the validity of a delegation); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (delegation upheld in a field long subject to established regulatory standards).

Administrative agencies have long been permitted to apply "the regulatory police power of the State" to "the myriad details" of a regulated field, provided the legislature "enjoin[s] upon it a certain course of procedure and certain rules of decision." Wichita R.R. & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Comm'n, 260 U.S. 48, 59 (1922). Congress did precisely that here.

Nor does the criminal consequence of scheduling call for a different rule. Congress and the States have long possessed broad authority to regulate the "administration, sale, prescription and use of dangerous and habit-forming drugs," a power this Court has called "too firmly established to be successfully called in question." Minnesota ex rel. Whipple v. Martinson, 256 U.S. 41, 45 (1921). This Court sustained the Harrison Act's comprehensive scheme of narcotics regulation in United States v. Doremus, 249 U.S. 86 (1919), notwithstanding its direct bearing on criminal liability. The same deference is due here, where Congress itself defined the offense in § 841 and merely authorized the Attorney General to determine, under confining statutory criteria, whether a particular substance falls within the class of drugs Congress has already resolved to punish.

II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Delegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

The Attorney General's delegation to the DEA Administrator was expressly authorized by 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the CSA to officers within the Department of Justice. This case is unlike Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, 315 U.S. 357 (1942), where the Court declined to imply a power to delegate the subpoena authority because the statute's express grant of delegation power for some functions, and its silence as to others, revealed "a legislative intention to withhold the latter." Id. at 363. Here, by contrast, Congress made no such omission: § 871(a) broadly authorizes delegation of the Attorney General's functions under the Act, and the temporary-scheduling power is among those functions.

Delegation of administrative authority to a subordinate agency created to carry out a national program is a familiar arrangement. Cf. District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co., 346 U.S. 100 (1953) (Congress may vest subordinate bodies with "subordinate legislative powers of a municipal character" to administer matters entrusted to them). The DEA, established within the Department of Justice specifically to administer the CSA, is the natural repository of the Attorney General's scheduling authority, and the 1987 regulation assigning that authority to the DEA Administrator was a straightforward exercise of the delegation power Congress itself conferred.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
