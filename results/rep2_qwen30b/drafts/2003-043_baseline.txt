**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of the Internal Revenue Code and settled precedent. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Government may not collect a partnership’s unpaid taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments or obtaining judgments against them. The Government’s failure to do so, and the resulting expiration of the statute of limitations, bars its claims in bankruptcy. This Court should affirm.

### I. The Statute of Limitations for Assessment Applies Individually to Each Taxpayer

The Government’s claim rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the statutory framework. Section 6501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code establishes a three-year statute of limitations for assessing taxes against a taxpayer. *United States v. Bajakajian*, 524 U.S. 321, 328 (1998) (“The statute of limitations is a jurisdictional bar to assessment.”). The Code defines “taxpayer” broadly, including “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1). But the inclusion of “individual” within the definition of “taxpayer” does not erase the distinction between a partnership and its partners. Each is a separate legal entity with its own taxpayer identification number and independent liability under the Code.

The Government’s argument—that a timely assessment against the partnership automatically extends the limitations period for collecting from individual partners—conflicts with the plain language and structure of the Code. Section 6502(a) permits collection of a tax “only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—(1) within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” (Emphasis added.) The statute refers to “the assessment of the tax,” not “an assessment of a partnership.” The use of the singular “assessment” implies that the assessment must be made against the specific taxpayer from whom collection is sought. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (“[T]he Government does not have any lien” against a partner’s property unless the tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.”).

The Government’s position would render the individual assessment requirement superfluous. If an assessment against the partnership were sufficient to trigger the 10-year collection period for partners, then Congress would have had no reason to require separate assessments against individuals. *See United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562 (7th Cir. 1995) (distinguishing cases where both partnership and partners were assessed). The Government’s theory also contradicts the regulatory requirement that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. An assessment against the partnership fails to identify the individual partners as taxpayers, and thus cannot serve as a valid assessment for purposes of extending the limitations period against them.

### II. The Government Cannot Collect from Partners Without a Judgment or Individual Assessment

The Government’s alternative argument—that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable, allowing it to collect directly from them—fails for the same reason. Under California law, a judgment against a partnership does not constitute a judgment against a partner. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* (“A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.”). To reach a partner’s personal assets, the Government must obtain a separate judgment against the partner. *See 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001)* (“[A] judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”).

The Government has not obtained such a judgment. It has not even attempted to bring an action against the partners within the three-year window. *See 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a)*. The statute of limitations has expired. The Government’s claim is therefore unenforceable under applicable law. *See 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1)* (“A claim is unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor, under applicable law, if such claim is unenforceable under applicable law for a reason other than because such claim is contingent or unmatured.”).

The Government’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the partner had already paid his share of the tax and sought a refund. The court held that he was not entitled to a refund because he was liable under state law. *Id.* at 910. The court did not hold that the Government could collect from a partner without an individual assessment or judgment. Indeed, the district court explicitly stated that “it is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment in order to create liability” *only because the tax had already been paid*. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959). The Government’s failure to assess the partners was irrelevant because the tax had already been collected. The court did not address the Government’s ability to collect in the absence of an assessment or judgment.

### III. The Bankruptcy Court Properly Disallowed the Claims

The bankruptcy court correctly applied the law. A claim is disallowed if it is “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor, under applicable law.” *11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1)*. The Government’s claim is unenforceable because it cannot collect from the partners without an individual assessment or judgment, both of which are barred by the statute of limitations. The Government’s attempt to collect through a proof of claim in bankruptcy is therefore futile.
