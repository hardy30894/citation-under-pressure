**ARGUMENT**

**I. A District Court May Not Certify a Class Under Rule 23(b)(3) Without a Common Method of Proof for Damages.**

A class action may proceed only if "the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 599 (1997). This requirement, known as predominance, necessitates a "common method of proof" for the class’s claims. *General Motors Corp. v. Tracy*, 519 U.S. 278, 291 (1997). The Third Circuit has held that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" to determine whether common questions predominate. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). However, this analysis is limited; a court may not conduct a merits inquiry for any other pretrial purpose. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 355 (2011). Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class based on a damages model that was not admissible evidence and failed to account for individualized issues.

**II. The District Court Relied on Inadmissible Evidence to Find a Common Method of Proof.**

To satisfy Rule 23(b)(3), a plaintiff must demonstrate that damages can be proven through common evidence. *Katz v. Oak Industries, Inc.*, 508 F.2d 134, 138 (3d Cir. 1974). The District Court certified the class based on the testimony of Dr. McClave, whose report was not admissible at trial. The court’s reliance on this inadmissible evidence to find a common method of proof violates the limits on the certification inquiry. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 355. A court cannot certify a class based on speculation or evidence that cannot be used to prove the claim. *Amchem*, 521 U.S. at 623. Because the District Court’s finding of a common method of proof rested on inadmissible expert testimony, its certification decision was erroneous.

**III. Individualized Issues Predominate Over Common Questions.**

Even if the District Court’s findings were reviewed for clear error, the record shows that individualized issues predominate. *Cox v. General Motors Corp.*, 511 F.2d 695, 701 (3d Cir. 1975). The class’s theory of antitrust impact is limited to the deterrence of overbuilder RCN. However, not all class members were affected by this specific conduct. Plaintiffs’ expert model assumes that all class members were harmed by the alleged clustering scheme, but this assumption is not supported by admissible evidence. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. When individualized issues predominate, a class cannot be certified. *Amchem*, 521 U.S. at 623.

**IV. The District Court’s Findings Were Clearly Erroneous.**

This Court reviews a class certification order for abuse of discretion, which occurs if the district court’s decision rests upon a clearly erroneous finding of fact. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). A finding is clearly erroneous if the reviewing court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). Here, the District Court was left with the definite and firm conviction that a common method of proof existed, despite the lack of admissible evidence. The District Court’s reliance on inadmissible expert testimony to find a common method of proof constitutes a clear error of law. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312. Therefore, the District Court abused its discretion in certifying the class.
