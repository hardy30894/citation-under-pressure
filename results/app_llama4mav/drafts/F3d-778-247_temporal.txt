## ARGUMENT

### I. The Search Warrant Failed to Supply a Nexus to the Place Being Searched

The search warrant issued for Joubert's parents' home was defective because it failed to establish a sufficient nexus between the items to be seized and the place to be searched. The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant be supported by probable cause, and that the warrant particularly describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. (Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132, 155-156 (1925)). The affidavit supporting the warrant application must provide a sufficient basis for the issuing judge to conclude that evidence of the crime will be found at the place to be searched. (Dumbra v. United States, 268 U.S. 435, 441 (1925)).

In this case, the affidavit failed to establish a clear connection between Joubert's alleged crimes and his parents' home. While the affidavit detailed Joubert's alleged molestation of various children, including KC, it did not provide specific facts linking the evidence of these crimes to the home. The officer-affiant's conclusory statement that persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of minors often maintain possession of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims is insufficient to establish the required nexus. (Byars v. United States, 273 U.S. 28, 29 (1927)). The search warrant was therefore invalid, and the evidence seized during the search should have been suppressed.

### II. The District Court Abused its Discretion in Admitting Testimony of Uncharged Child Molestation

The district court erred in admitting testimony regarding Joubert's uncharged child molestation. The admission of this testimony was prejudicial and violated Joubert's right to a fair trial. (People v. Zackowitz, 172 N.E. 466, 468 (N.Y. 1930)). The testimony was not relevant to the charged offenses and served only to inflame the jury. The district court's decision to admit this testimony was an abuse of discretion and warrants reversal.

### III. The Commerce Clause Cannot Support Application to Joubert of the Federal Criminal Statutes Under Which He Was Indicted

The federal statutes under which Joubert was indicted, 18 U.S.C. § 2251(a) and 18 U.S.C. § 2252A(a)(5)(B), exceed Congress's authority under the Commerce Clause. (United States v. Dewitt, 76 U.S. 41, 44 (1869)). The Commerce Clause does not permit Congress to regulate non-economic, intrastate activity, such as the production and possession of child pornography that does not involve interstate commerce. The government's attempt to link Joubert's conduct to interstate commerce is tenuous at best, and the statutes as applied to Joubert are unconstitutional.

### IV. Joubert's 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

Joubert's sentence was excessive and substantively unreasonable. (Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910)). The sentence was disproportionate to the offense and failed to take into account Joubert's individual circumstances. The district court's failure to consider mitigating factors and its reliance on improper factors resulted in a sentence that was cruel and unusual. The sentence should be vacated and remanded for resentencing.

In conclusion, the district court's errors warrant reversal of Joubert's convictions and vacatur of his sentence. The search warrant was defective, the admission of uncharged child molestation testimony was prejudicial, the federal statutes under which Joubert was indicted are unconstitutional as applied, and the sentence was substantively unreasonable.
