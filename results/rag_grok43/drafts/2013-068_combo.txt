The Massachusetts statute constitutes a valid time, place, and manner regulation of speech on public sidewalks. States possess authority to control the use of public property and to make reasonable regulations governing the exercise of First Amendment rights on their property. Amalgamated Food Employees Union Local 590 v. Logan Valley Plaza, Inc., 391 U.S. 308, 320-21 (1968). Even where municipal or state property is open to the public generally, the exercise of First Amendment rights may be regulated so as to prevent interference with the use to which the property is ordinarily put by the State. Id. at 321.

The statute is content-neutral on its face. Content-based restrictions on speech are disfavored, Patterson v. Colorado ex rel. Attorney General of Colo., 205 U.S. 454, 462 (1907), yet the Act applies equally to all speakers without regard to viewpoint. The exemptions for clinic employees and certain passersby do not render the law speaker-based, as they serve the neutral purpose of facilitating access rather than favoring particular messages. See Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513, 528-29 (1958).

The buffer zones are narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests in public safety and unobstructed access to medical facilities. Regulations may prohibit picketing in such a manner as to obstruct or unreasonably interfere with free ingress or egress. Logan Valley, 391 U.S. at 321. The fixed thirty-five-foot zones leave open ample alternative channels, including oral speech at conversational distances outside the zones, distribution of literature, and display of signs. See Selover, Bates & Co. v. Walsh, 226 U.S. 112, 123 (1912).

Petitioners' as-applied challenge fails under the law of the case doctrine. An earlier appellate decision in the same case binds a successor panel. Hill v. Smith, 260 U.S. 592, 594 (1923). No dramatic change in controlling authority has occurred that would justify departure. Elder v. Colorado ex rel. Badgley, 204 U.S. 85, 87 (1907). Recent decisions merely restate conventional principles without altering the analysis applicable to sidewalk access regulations.

Petitioners received adequate process. Where a regulation affects a large class, individual hearings are not required before enforcement. Bi-Metallic Investment Co. v. State Board of Equalization, 239 U.S. 441, 445 (1915). The Attorney General's guidance letter does not alter the statute's facial clarity. Weiland v. Pioneer Irrigation Co., 259 U.S. 498, 502 (1922).

The district court's denial of leave to amend was proper. Undue delay supports denial of amendment. Brown v. Colorado, 106 U.S. 95, 98 (1882). Petitioners waited more than two years after the letter issued before seeking to add claims concerning it.

The judgment should be affirmed.
