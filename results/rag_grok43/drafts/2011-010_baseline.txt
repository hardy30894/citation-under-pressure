**ARGUMENT**

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act coverage only when “disability or death of an employee result[s] from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “injury occurring” refers to the location of the injury itself. Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986) (“Only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.”). That construction is consistent with the statute’s geographic premise that “the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage … would be determined principally by locale.” Id. at 219.

Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), confirms the point. The Court there noted that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA,” and that “that statute draws a clear geographic boundary that will be crossed whenever an employee moves between an OCS platform and one on state waters.” Id. at 426-27. An injury sustained on land therefore falls outside the boundary Congress drew.

The Fifth Circuit has applied this rule consistently. Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc) (requiring both a “but for” causal link and that “the injury occurred on an OCS platform or on the waters above the OCS”). The Third Circuit’s contrary “but for” approach in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), cannot be reconciled with the statutory text or with Tallentire’s description of § 1333(b) as preserving an OCSLA “situs requirement.”

Petitioner’s reliance on the absence of an express “on the outer Continental Shelf” clause in § 1333(b) is misplaced. The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” already limits the causal chain to injuries that occur while those operations are being performed on the Shelf. Extending coverage to every land-based consequence would erase the geographic limitation the Court recognized in Tallentire and Herb’s Welding.

The judgment of the Benefits Review Board should be affirmed.
