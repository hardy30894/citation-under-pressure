**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits punishments that are “cruel and unusual.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958). This prohibition is not static; it evolves in tandem with the “progressive development of human thought” and the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that the execution of individuals with intellectual disability constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. The Court’s decision was grounded in a careful analysis of national consensus, the nature of the punishment, and the culpability of the offender. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court, affirming Atkins’ death sentence, must be upheld because it correctly applied the precedent of *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), and because the Eighth Amendment does not require a categorical bar to the death penalty for individuals with intellectual disability.

The Court in *Atkins* did not create a new constitutional rule but rather recognized a long-standing national consensus that had emerged in the decades following *Penry*. The Court observed that “the vast majority of States” had enacted laws prohibiting the execution of individuals with intellectual disability, and that “the number of States that have abolished the death penalty for such individuals has grown steadily.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311. This consensus was not merely legislative; it reflected a moral and practical judgment that individuals with intellectual disability lack the same level of culpability as others due to their diminished capacity for rational thought, impulse control, and understanding of consequences. The Court emphasized that “the execution of individuals with intellectual disability is disproportionate to their personal culpability.” *Id.* at 319.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in rejecting Atkins’ claim. In *Penry*, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment requires that a capital sentencing scheme allow the jury to consider mitigating evidence of intellectual disability. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328. The Court did not hold that such individuals are categorically exempt from the death penalty, but rather that the sentencing process must permit the jury to give meaningful effect to mitigating evidence. The Virginia Supreme Court found that the trial court’s instructions and verdict form allowed the jury to consider Atkins’ intellectual disability as a mitigating factor. The jury was instructed that it could consider “any evidence of mental retardation” in determining whether to impose death. The court further concluded that the jury had the opportunity to weigh the mitigating evidence, including the testimony of the forensic psychologist, and that the verdict was not the result of a constitutional defect in the sentencing process.

The dissenting opinion in *Atkins* correctly noted that the Court’s decision was not based on a “new” constitutional principle but on the “evolving standards of decency” that reflect a maturing society’s understanding of justice. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 327 (O’Connor, J., dissenting). The Court’s reliance on national consensus was consistent with its prior holdings in *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584 (1977), and *Graham v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 48 (2010), where the Court found that the death penalty for certain classes of offenders violated the Eighth Amendment because of the disproportionate nature of the punishment in light of the offender’s diminished culpability. In *Coker*, the Court held that the death penalty for rape was cruel and unusual because it was grossly disproportionate to the offense. *Coker*, 433 U.S. at 592. Similarly, in *Graham*, the Court held that life without parole for juveniles convicted of non-homicide offenses violated the Eighth Amendment because of the “diminished culpability” of youth. *Graham*, 560 U.S. at 60. The reasoning in *Atkins* follows this same trajectory: the punishment must be proportionate to the offender’s moral and personal culpability.

The Virginia Supreme Court’s conclusion that the death penalty may be imposed on individuals with intellectual disability, so long as the sentencing process allows for meaningful consideration of mitigating evidence, is consistent with the Eighth Amendment. The Court in *Atkins* did not hold that the death penalty is categorically impermissible for such individuals, but rather that it is “unconstitutional to execute individuals with intellectual disability.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318. The Court’s opinion was not a command to abolish the death penalty for all such individuals, but a recognition that the execution of those with intellectual disability violates the evolving standards of decency. The Virginia Supreme Court’s decision to affirm the death sentence, after ensuring that the jury had the opportunity to consider Atkins’ intellectual disability, was consistent with the constitutional framework established in *Atkins*.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Eighth Amendment does not require a categorical bar to the death penalty for individuals with intellectual disability, but it does require that the sentencing process be sensitive to the mitigating effects of intellectual disability. The Virginia Supreme Court properly applied *Penry* and upheld the constitutionality of Atkins’ death sentence under the Eighth Amendment.

**WHEREFORE**, the respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court.
