**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE MERIT SYSTEMS PROTECTION BOARD’S DISMISSAL OF PETITIONER’S CLAIM FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION IS A “MIXED CASE” SUBJECT TO REVIEW IN DISTRICT COURT UNDER 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2)**

The jurisdictional dismissal in this case arose directly from Petitioner’s discrimination allegations. The Board dismissed his appeal because it concluded that his retirement and suspension were voluntary, a determination that turned entirely on whether the agency had discriminated against him by coercing the settlement. This is precisely the type of mixed-case dismissal that the Supreme Court held in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S.Ct. 596, 184 L.Ed.2d 433 (2012), must be reviewed in district court.

The Court in *Kloeckner* rejected the Federal Circuit’s categorical rule that all Board dismissals—regardless of their basis—should be reviewed there. Instead, it held that when an employee alleges discrimination and the Board dismisses the appeal, judicial review lies in district court, even if the dismissal is for procedural reasons. *Id.* at 604 (“[T]he proper review forum for a mixed case is district court, whether the MSPB decision is on the merits or on procedural grounds.”). The Court emphasized that the critical factor is whether the employee’s claim “aris[es] from or [is] in any way related to” a discrimination allegation. *Id.* at 605.

Here, Petitioner’s jurisdictional challenge was inextricably intertwined with his discrimination claim. He argued that the agency’s actions—falsely alleging misconduct, threatening termination, and pressuring him to retire—were motivated by discrimination and constituted retaliation for prior EEO activity. The Board’s dismissal rested on its finding that the settlement was voluntary, a conclusion that depended entirely on whether the agency had discriminated against him. This is not a case where the Board lacked jurisdiction over the *type* of claim; rather, it dismissed because it found no discrimination. That is a merits-based conclusion, even if framed as a jurisdictional ruling. *See id.* at 604 (noting that the Board’s dismissal in *Kloeckner* was “on procedural grounds,” yet still requiring district court review).

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary rule in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is inconsistent with *Kloeckner* and has been implicitly rejected by the Federal Circuit. *See Conforto v. Merit Sys. Prot. Bd.,* 713 F.3d 1115, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013) (distinguishing *Powell* and applying *Kloeckner* to a procedurally dismissed mixed case). The Federal Circuit has recognized that *Kloeckner* abrogated any distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases. *Id.* The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to preserve *Powell* by labeling jurisdictional dismissals as fundamentally different is unsupported by the statute or *Kloeckner*.

II. **REVIEW IN THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT WOULD DEPRIVE PETITIONER OF HIS RIGHT TO A JUDICIAL FORUM FOR HIS DISCRIMINATION CLAIMS**

The Federal Circuit has no jurisdiction to adjudicate Petitioner’s discrimination allegations. Section 7703(b)(2) provides that mixed cases “shall be filed in” district court. The Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* confirmed that this provision applies whenever an employee alleges discrimination, regardless of how the Board frames its dismissal. 133 S.Ct. at 604.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. That case predated *Kloeckner* and did not address whether a dismissal based on the voluntariness of a settlement—which is inherently tied to discrimination allegations—should be reviewed in district court. The Federal Circuit has since made clear that *Kloeckner* controls, and that mixed-case dismissals, even those framed as jurisdictional, must be reviewed in district court. *Conforto*, 713 F.3d at 1118.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s approach creates an anomalous result: employees who bring mixed cases are forced into the Federal Circuit simply because the Board dismisses their appeal before reaching the merits, while those who proceed to a merits decision on discrimination claims are entitled to district court review. This arbitrary distinction has no basis in the statute or *Kloeckner*.

III. **THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK AND *KLOECKNER* COMPEL DISTRICT COURT REVIEW**

Section 7702(a)(1) defines a mixed case as one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” Here, Petitioner’s retirement and suspension were actions he could appeal to the Board if involuntary. His allegation that they were coerced by discrimination brings his claim squarely within the mixed-case exception.

The Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* held that this exception applies whenever an employee alleges discrimination, regardless of the Board’s procedural or jurisdictional ruling. 133 S.Ct. at 604. The Court rejected the argument that the forum for review should turn on the Board’s characterization of its dismissal. *Id.* at 605. The same principle applies here: Petitioner’s discrimination allegations are the heart of his claim, and the Board’s dismissal was a function of those allegations.

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* based on the type of dismissal is unavailing. The Supreme Court did not limit its holding to procedural dismissals; it applied the mixed-case exception to all Board decisions in cases involving discrimination allegations. *Id.* at 604. The Federal Circuit has followed this approach, and this Court should as well.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the D.C. Circuit’s judgment and hold that his case is a mixed case subject to review in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).
