ARGUMENT

The conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) cannot stand without proof that the defendant subjectively intended his words as a threat. The statute’s bare reference to a “threat” does not eliminate the element of criminal intent that the common law required for every serious offense. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (“mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent should not be read as eliminating that element from the crimes denounced”).

That requirement is reinforced by the First Amendment. Speech may be punished only when the speaker consciously seeks to produce the harm the statute forbids. In Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), this Court held that words lose protection only when “used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger” that the speaker intends to bring about. The same principle appears in Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616, 627 (1919) (Holmes, J., dissenting) (“the surreptitious publishing of a silly leaflet” is protected absent “specific intent” to obstruct the war effort). An objective standard that convicts on the basis of how a reasonable hearer might react therefore punishes protected speech.

The same mens rea rule governs every analogous federal prohibition. In United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277, 281 (1943), the Court required proof that the defendant “had knowledge of” the facts making his conduct criminal. Without that subjective showing, the statute would reach “a class of cases where intentional wrongdoing must be proved.” Id. Section 875(c) is no different. Its prohibition of communications “containing any threat” is satisfied only when the speaker harbors the specific intent to threaten.

Because the jury was instructed solely on an objective standard, the convictions rest on an impermissible ground and must be reversed.
