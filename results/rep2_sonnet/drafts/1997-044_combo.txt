**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge Only the Value Actually Obtained by Fraud, Not Statutory Penalties Layered on Top of It.**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge a debt "to the extent obtained by" fraud. That phrase is one of limitation, not expansion. This Court has long insisted that exceptions to discharge be measured by their precise statutory terms rather than stretched to reach collateral liabilities. In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1878), the Court confined the predecessor fraud exception to "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong," refusing to read the provision to sweep in liability resting on anything less than the fraud itself. The same interpretive discipline controlled *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), where the Court tied nondischargeability to the debt actually "created by" the fraudulent conduct, not to every liability that might later attach to the same transaction.

*Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), illustrates the point still more directly. There the Court refused to extend the exception for property "obtained by" false pretenses to cover a debt for professional services, holding that the exception reaches only what the statutory words actually describe and no further. The same logic governs here: the tenants' compensatory loss—the $31,382.50 in excess rent—is the value Cohen "obtained by" his conduct. The additional $62,765.00 in treble damages, together with the statutorily mandated attorney's fees and costs, were not obtained by Cohen at all; they are penalties assessed against him by operation of New Jersey statute. Nothing in the text of § 523(a)(2)(A) purports to except such penalties from discharge, and this Court should not supply what Congress omitted.

**II. Exceptions to Discharge Must Be Construed Narrowly to Preserve the Fresh-Start Policy of the Bankruptcy Laws.**

The bankruptcy discharge exists to serve the fundamental purpose of "giv[ing] to the honest but unfortunate debtor... a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That same policy animated *Stellwagen v. Clum*, 245 U.S. 605, 617 (1918), which described the discharge provisions as designed "to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness" and to let him "start afresh." Consistent with that purpose, this Court has repeatedly cabined the discharge exceptions to their narrowest defensible scope. In *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court declined to extend the fiduciary-defalcation exception beyond technical trust relationships, emphasizing that exceptions to discharge are construed strictly against the creditor and in favor of the debtor's fresh start. And in *Perry v. Commerce Loan Co.*, 383 U.S. 392 (1966), the Court refused to enlarge a discharge exception beyond debts "clearly within the purview of the statute," rejecting an interpretation that would have swept collateral obligations into a provision Congress had drafted narrowly.

Treble damages, fees, and costs imposed under a state consumer-fraud statute are, by definition, not compensation for what was "obtained by" the debtor's conduct—they are punitive multipliers designed to deter and punish. Extending § 523(a)(2)(A) to reach them would convert a narrow, targeted exception into a vehicle for importing whatever punitive remedy a State chooses to attach to fraud, defeating the "clear field for future effort" that *Local Loan* and *Stellwagen* identified as the discharge's central purpose.

**III. The Historical Treatment of Fraud Judgments Confirms That Only the Fraud-Created Portion of a Debt Is Nondischargeable.**

Even where this Court has held that a judgment "founded upon" fraud remains nondischargeable notwithstanding its merger into judgment, the Court has tied nondischargeability to the debt the fraud itself created—not to every element a court or legislature later appends. In *Kreitlein v. Ferger*, 238 U.S. 21 (1915), the Court held nondischargeable a judgment for deceit because the judgment represented the debt "created by" the fraud; the decision did not purport to reach damages awarded for reasons independent of the fraud's actual effect on the creditor. Read together with *Gleason* and *Forsyth*, *Kreitlein* confirms the consistent rule: the exception follows the fraud's fruits, not statutory enhancements layered onto those fruits by state legislatures pursuing their own punitive and deterrent objectives.

**IV. Conclusion**

Because § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only the debt "obtained by" fraud, and because this Court's precedents—from *Neal v. Clark* through *Perry v. Commerce Loan Co.*—require narrow construction of discharge exceptions in service of the fresh-start policy recognized in *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt* and *Stellwagen v. Clum*, the Third Circuit erred in holding the entire treble-damages, fee, and cost award nondischargeable. Only the $31,382.50 actually obtained through Cohen's conduct falls within the statutory exception. The judgment below should be reversed insofar as it renders the remainder of the award nondischargeable.
