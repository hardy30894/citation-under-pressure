**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Possess Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims That Merely Implicate Patent Law.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim, which requires proof of a "case within a case" involving patent law, necessarily "arises under" federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. The Supreme Court has long held that federal jurisdiction under § 1338(a) is reserved for cases where federal patent law creates the cause of action or where the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). But a state malpractice claim does not "arise under" patent law merely because a patent issue is an element of the underlying case. *See Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) ("A suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.").

**A. The Patent Issue Here Is Not "Substantial" Within the Meaning of § 1338(a).**

The Supreme Court has consistently required that a federal issue be "substantial" before it can confer federal jurisdiction over an otherwise state-law claim. *Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). A federal issue is not substantial merely because it is "necessary" to the state claim. *Id.* at 112. Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies—is a narrow, fact-bound question that does not implicate the validity or scope of any patent. It is a defense to the on-sale bar, not a question of patent law that requires uniform federal resolution. *See Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199 (1921) (federal jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is "real and substantial," not "merely colorable").

**B. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

The Supreme Court has cautioned that federal courts must not "disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). State legal malpractice claims are traditionally the province of state courts. *See, e.g., Theard v. United States*, 354 U.S. 278, 281 (1957) (recognizing state courts' "peculiar interest" in regulating attorney conduct). Allowing every malpractice claim arising from patent litigation to be removed to federal court would flood the federal docket with state-law claims and undermine the careful balance Congress has struck. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117 (federal jurisdiction must be "selective" and "pick the substantial causes out of the web").

**C. The Federal Circuit's Approach Conflicts with Supreme Court Precedent.**

The Federal Circuit's decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), improperly expand federal jurisdiction by ignoring the substantiality requirement and the federalism concerns articulated in *Grable*. Those decisions treat any patent issue as "substantial" if it is "necessary" to the state claim, but that approach conflates "necessary" with "substantial." *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09. The Supreme Court has never held that a federal issue is substantial simply because it is an element of a state claim. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 112.

**II. The State Court Properly Exercised Jurisdiction Over This Malpractice Claim.**

Because the patent issue here is not substantial and because federal jurisdiction would disturb the balance of state and federal responsibilities, the state court correctly exercised jurisdiction. The experimental use exception is a fact-intensive inquiry that does not require uniform federal resolution. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (experimental use is a question of fact). State courts are fully capable of resolving such factual questions in the context of a malpractice claim. *See Theard*, 354 U.S. at 281.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case remanded for consideration on the merits.
