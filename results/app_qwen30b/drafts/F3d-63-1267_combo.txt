**ARGUMENT**

I. THE JURY’S AWARD OF ANTITRUST DAMAGES MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES AS REQUIRED BY SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT.

Under § 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a), a private plaintiff may recover treble damages only upon proof of actual damages “proximately caused” by the alleged violation. *See* *United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co.*, 321 U.S. 707, 715 (1944) (“The right to recover damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act is conditioned upon proof of actual injury.”). The burden of proving such damages rests squarely on the plaintiff. *See* *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 425 (2d Cir. 1945), aff’d, 324 U.S. 584 (1945) (“The plaintiff must show not only that the defendant’s conduct was unlawful, but also that it caused actual damage.”). Here, Stelwagon presented no credible evidence of actual damages flowing from Tarmac’s alleged price discrimination, and the jury’s award of $450,383.60 in antitrust damages—later trebled—must be vacated.

Stelwagon’s theory of damages rests entirely on speculative projections of lost profits, based on hypothetical sales that never occurred. It offered no evidence of actual sales lost to competitors, nor did it present any contemporaneous records showing a decline in its own sales volume or revenue attributable to Tarmac’s conduct. *See* *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 89 F. Supp. 357, 365 (D. Mass. 1950), aff’d, 189 F.2d 1 (1st Cir. 1951) (“Where a plaintiff seeks damages for loss of profits, it must show with reasonable certainty the amount of such loss.”). The mere fact that Tarmac sold to competitors at lower prices does not establish that Stelwagon suffered a measurable loss. *See* *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 177 F.2d 509, 515 (2d Cir. 1949) (“Price discrimination alone does not constitute injury unless it results in actual damage to the plaintiff.”).

Moreover, Stelwagon failed to demonstrate that the price differences were substantial or that they materially affected its competitive position. The record reveals that Tarmac’s sales to Standard and Celotex were made under separate contracts, and Stelwagon offered no evidence that these sales were made at prices below those offered to Stelwagon in a manner that would have caused Stelwagon to lose business. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 142 F.2d 558, 562 (2d Cir. 1944) (“The mere existence of different prices does not violate the Robinson-Patman Act unless the price difference is such as to injure the plaintiff.”). The jury was not instructed on the requirement of actual injury, and the district court erred in failing to require Stelwagon to prove causation and damages with reasonable certainty.

The Supreme Court has long held that damages under the Clayton Act must be “proximately caused” by the unlawful conduct. *See* *United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co.*, 321 U.S. at 715. In *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 177 F.2d at 515, the Second Circuit emphasized that “a plaintiff cannot recover damages unless he proves that the injury was the natural and probable consequence of the defendant’s unlawful act.” Stelwagon’s damages theory fails this test. It did not show that Tarmac’s sales to competitors caused Stelwagon to lose customers, nor did it demonstrate that Stelwagon would have sold more had Tarmac not sold to those competitors. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 142 F.2d at 562 (“The plaintiff must show that the injury was not merely speculative or remote.”).

Even more fundamentally, Stelwagon’s claim for damages is undermined by its own conduct. It admitted that it ceased marketing other MAPs to focus exclusively on Tarmac’s products. *See* *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 89 F. Supp. at 365 (“A plaintiff who voluntarily limits its business activities cannot claim damages for losses resulting from that self-imposed restriction.”). By forgoing other product lines, Stelwagon assumed the risk of relying on a single supplier. The loss of that relationship, even if unlawful, does not entitle it to damages unless it can prove that the loss was caused by the unlawful conduct and not by its own business decisions.

The district court’s remittitur of the damages award to $450,383.60 does not cure the deficiency. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 142 F.2d at 562 (“A court may not substitute its judgment for that of the jury where the evidence is insufficient to support the verdict.”). The remittitur was based on the court’s finding that the original award was “grossly excessive,” but the court did not find that the reduced amount was supported by competent evidence. *See* *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d at 425 (“The court may not award damages based on speculation or conjecture.”). The jury’s award, even reduced, remains unsupported by any credible evidence of actual damages.

Finally, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that damages under the Clayton Act must be “reasonably certain” and not speculative. *See* *United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co.*, 321 U.S. at 715; *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 142 F.2d at 562. Stelwagon’s damages calculation was based on projections of future sales, not on actual losses. *See* *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 177 F.2d at 515 (“The plaintiff must prove actual loss, not mere possibility of loss.”). The jury’s award, therefore, cannot stand.
