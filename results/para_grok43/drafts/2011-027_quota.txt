The equal-footing doctrine vests a new State with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of statehood. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 228-29 (1845); Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). Title therefore turns on the river's condition on the date the State entered the Union. Oregon ex rel. State Land Bd. v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co., 429 U.S. 363, 370 (1977); Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981).

Navigability for title is determined by whether the river "was used, or was susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce" at that historic moment. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). The inquiry examines the river's natural capacity for trade and travel, not later improvements. The Montello, 87 U.S. 430, 441-42 (1874). When portions of a river differ materially in their navigable characteristics, courts assess each distinct segment separately. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 77, 82-83 (1931).

Evidence of use or susceptibility at statehood may be drawn from contemporaneous records, explorer accounts, and other historical sources that reliably reflect conditions in 1889. Utah v. United States, 403 U.S. 9, 11-12 (1971). Once navigability at statehood is shown for the relevant stretch, title passed to the State and remains with it. Corvallis Sand & Gravel, 429 U.S. at 374; Montana, 450 U.S. at 551-52.

The constitutional test therefore requires a trial court to determine whether the pertinent reach of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. The District Court applied that standard to the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork segments and found sufficient historical evidence of navigability in 1889. That finding satisfies the governing rule.
