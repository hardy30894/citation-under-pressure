**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provisions do not effect an unconstitutional delegation of legislative power. Congress may confer authority upon the Executive when it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized * * * is directed to conform.” J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Act supplies such a principle by enumerating eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, 21 U.S.C. § 811(c), requiring a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services whose negative recommendation is binding, id. § 811(b), and limiting temporary scheduling to substances posing “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” id. § 811(h)(1).

This Court has repeatedly sustained comparable delegations. In Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-693 (1892), the Court upheld authority to suspend tariff provisions upon a factual determination. In Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), it sustained standards governing tea importation. Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364, 386-387 (1907), approved delegation to remove obstructive bridges. United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-517 (1911), upheld regulations governing forest reserves whose violation was made criminal. Arizona v. California, 283 U.S. 423, 435 (1931), confirmed that Congress may authorize the Secretary of the Interior to determine when water deliveries should cease.

More recent pre-1970 decisions confirm the same principle. Sunshine Anthracite Coal Co. v. Adkins, 310 U.S. 381, 397-398 (1940), upheld delegation to fix maximum coal prices. Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator, 312 U.S. 126, 145-146 (1941), sustained wage-and-hour determinations. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 424-426 (1944), approved price-control authority guided by statutory standards. United States v. Rock Royal Co-op., Inc., 307 U.S. 533, 574-575 (1939), upheld marketing orders whose violation carried criminal penalties.

The same authorities establish that the Attorney General may subdelegate. In United States v. Grimaud, supra, the Court recognized that the Secretary of Agriculture could authorize subordinates to issue regulations. Field v. Clark likewise permitted the President to act through designated officers. The 1973 regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely effectuates this established practice.

Because Congress supplied an intelligible principle, required inter-agency consultation, and imposed temporal and procedural limits, the temporary scheduling authority is constitutional. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
