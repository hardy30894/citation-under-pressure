**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF § 1981a DO NOT IMPOSE AN “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT SEPARATE FROM THE STATUTORY MENTAL-STATE STANDARD.**

The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages when the plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” The statute does not add any additional textual element such as “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct. This Court has repeatedly held that “[w]hen the words of a statute are unambiguous, the judicial inquiry is complete.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (citation omitted). Because § 1981a’s text contains no freestanding culpability requirement beyond the statutorily defined mental states, no such requirement should be judicially implied. *See id.* (“[C]ourts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.”) (internal quotation marks omitted).

**II. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 ACT DOES NOT SUPPORT AN “EGREGIOUSNESS” TEST.**

Contrary to petitioner’s suggestion, the legislative history does not compel an “egregiousness” overlay. The Senate Report accompanying § 1981a explained that punitive damages are available when the employer acts “with malice or reckless indifference,” and that “[t]his standard is intended to be identical to the standard for determining whether compensatory damages are available under *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983).” S. Rep. No. 102-21, at 22 (1991). In *Smith v. Wade*, this Court held that punitive damages are appropriate when the defendant’s conduct is “ ‘characterized by evil motive’ or ‘reckless or callous indifference to the rights of others.’ ” 461 U.S. at 32 (quoting *Lake Shore & Mich. So. R.R. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893)). The Senate Report thus equated the § 1981a punitive-damages standard with the *Smith* standard, which focuses exclusively on the defendant’s mental state and does not require any independent showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” acts. *Id.* at 56 (“[P]unitive damages may properly be imposed . . . when the defendant’s actions are shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it is otherwise established that his conduct is characterized by reckless or callous indifference to the rights of others.”).

Petitioner relies on isolated remarks by individual legislators suggesting that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases.” *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). This Court, however, has long treated such floor statements as “ ‘not authoritative indicators of congressional intent’ ” when they conflict with the statute’s text and committee reports. *Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Servs., Inc.*, 545 U.S. 541, 568 (2005) (quoting *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 384 (1968)). Moreover, other legislators expressly rejected the notion that § 1981a imposes a higher threshold. *See* 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1911) (Rep. Edwards) (stating that punitive damages under § 1981a are available “to the same extent and under the same standards” as under 42 U.S.C. § 1981). Given this division, the text and committee report must control. *See Lamie v. U.S. Tr.*, 540 U.S. 50, 60 (2004) (“[W]here the statute’s language is plain, ‘the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.’ ”) (quoting *Hart v. United States*, 519 U.S. 195, 205 (1997)).

**III. THIS COURT’S PRE-1991 PRECEDENTS CONFIRM THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES TURN ON MENTAL STATE, NOT ACTUS REUS.**

Before the 1991 Act, this Court evaluated punitive-damages claims under § 1981 by examining the defendant’s state of mind, not the outrageousness of its conduct. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, the Court upheld a punitive award where the defendant prison officials acted with “reckless indifference” to the plaintiff’s constitutional rights, even though the underlying conditions were not independently “egregious.” *Id.* at 56. Similarly, in *City of Newport v. Fact Concerts, Inc.*, 453 U.S. 247 (1981), the Court permitted punitive damages against a municipality where its policymakers acted with “deliberate indifference” to constitutional violations, without requiring any finding that the municipality’s acts were independently outrageous. *Id.* at 271. These decisions confirm that punitive damages hinge on the defendant’s mental state—“malice” or “reckless indifference”—not on whether the conduct itself crossed some undefined threshold of culpability.

**IV. PETITIONER’S PROPOSED “EGREGIOUSNESS” TEST WOULD CONTRADICT § 1981a’S TEXT AND UNDERMINE CONGRESS’S DETERRENT PURPOSE.**

Adopting petitioner’s “egregiousness” requirement would read a judicially created actus reus element into a statute that defines liability solely by mental state. Such an interpretation would frustrate Congress’s express goal of expanding remedies to deter intentional discrimination. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a) (stating that additional remedies are “needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace”). As this Court has recognized, punitive damages serve a critical deterrent function by “punish[ing] the malicious and reckless” and “deter[ring] future misconduct.” *BMW of N. Am. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 568 (1996). Imposing an extra-textual “egregiousness” threshold would narrow the class of culpable employers who face punitive exposure, thereby diluting the statute’s deterrent force. *See id.* at 586 (Breyer, J., concurring) (“[T]he punitive damages statute’s basic purpose is to encourage responsibility and deterrence.”).

**V. THE COURTS OF APPEALS HAVE OVERWHELMINGLY REJECTED AN INDEPENDENT “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT.**

Every circuit to address the question has held that § 1981a’s punitive-damages standard turns solely on the employer’s mental state. *See, e.g.*, *Farr v. U.S. W. Commc’ns, Inc.*, 151 F.3d 908, 915 (9th Cir. 1998) (“[T]he statute does not require a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct.”); *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 108 F.3d 1436, 1445 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (en banc) (Tatel, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (“[T]he statute’s text and structure . . . do not require a showing of egregious conduct.”), *rev’d on other grounds*, 527 U.S. 526 (1999); *Parker v. Sony Pictures Entm’t*, 260 F.3d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 2001) (“[W]e agree with our sister circuits that § 1981a does not require a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct.”). This uniform consensus supports the conclusion that no additional culpability requirement exists.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment below should be affirmed because § 1981a’s punitive-damages provision requires only a showing that the employer acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference,” and does not impose an independent “egregiousness” or “outrageousness” requirement.
