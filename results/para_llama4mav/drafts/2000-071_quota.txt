The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy, which prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may create a limited public forum, opening its property to certain categories of speech while excluding others. See Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 46-47 (1983). In a limited public forum, the government may impose restrictions on speech that are "reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum" and "respect the viewpoint of all speakers." Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The school's policy of excluding religious worship and instruction is a reasonable restriction, as it is designed to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 394-395 (1993).

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that Milford's exclusion of the Club constituted viewpoint discrimination. The Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, are not merely a religious viewpoint on a secular subject, but rather "quintessentially religious" in nature. See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch., 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). As such, they fall outside the scope of the limited public forum created by the school.

The Supreme Court has recognized that "speech discussing otherwise permissible subjects cannot be excluded from a limited public forum on the ground that the subject is discussed from a religious viewpoint." Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831-832. However, this principle does not apply where the speech at issue is itself religious worship or instruction. See id. at 845-846 (distinguishing between "religious speech" and "speech about religion"). The Club's activities, which are centered around Bible lessons and prayer, are more akin to religious worship than to speech about morals or character from a religious viewpoint.

The school's exclusion of the Club is justified by its concern that permitting the Club's activities would violate the Establishment Clause. The Establishment Clause prohibits government endorsement or promotion of religion, and the school's permission of the Club's activities could be seen as such endorsement. See Lee v. Weisman, 505 U.S. 577, 587 (1992). In Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that a university's exclusion of religious groups from its facilities was justified by Establishment Clause concerns, and that the exclusion did not violate the groups' free speech rights. Similarly, in this case, Milford's exclusion of the Club is justified by its concern that permitting the Club's activities would be seen as endorsing or promoting religion.

The Court has also recognized that the age of the students involved is a relevant factor in Establishment Clause analysis. See Edwards v. Aguillard, 482 U.S. 578, 584 (1987). Here, the Club's activities are directed at elementary school students, who are particularly impressionable and susceptible to the influence of authority figures. See id. at 584-585. The school's exclusion of the Club is therefore justified by its concern that permitting the Club's activities would be seen as endorsing or promoting religion to these young students.

In sum, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a permissible restriction on speech in a limited public forum, and the exclusion of the Club is justified by the school's concern that permitting the Club's activities would violate the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
