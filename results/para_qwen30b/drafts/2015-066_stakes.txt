**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—requires this Court to resolve a conflict among the circuits and to clarify the scope of *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), in the context of tribal sovereignty and federal criminal recidivism statutes. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant, Jr.*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), and its subsequent refusal to rehear the matter en banc, rest on a fundamental misreading of *Nichols*, an erroneous extension of the Sixth Amendment to tribal court proceedings, and a disregard for the principles of comity and federal Indian law. The respondent respectfully submits that the use of a valid, final tribal court conviction—obtained in accordance with the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)—to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment, and that the Ninth Circuit erred in holding otherwise.

### I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT MISREAD *NICHOLS* BY EXTENDING IT BEYOND SENTENCING TO THE GUILT PHASE

The Ninth Circuit’s central error lies in its attempt to apply *Nichols*—a sentencing case—to the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution. In *Nichols*, the Court held that a defendant’s prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, so long as the conviction does not violate the Sixth Amendment. 511 U.S. at 746–47. The Court emphasized that the *reliability* concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment are “accommodated” in the sentencing context because the government need only prove a sentencing enhancement by a *preponderance of the evidence*, not *beyond a reasonable doubt*. Id. at 747–48. Moreover, the defendant may “convince the sentencing court of the unreliability” of the prior conviction. Id. at 748.

Crucially, *Nichols* did not address, and certainly did not hold, that an uncounseled conviction may be used to establish an *element of a crime*. That distinction is constitutionally significant. As the Court has repeatedly held, the government must prove every element of a criminal offense *beyond a reasonable doubt*. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). To permit the use of a potentially unreliable conviction—especially one from a court where the Sixth Amendment does not apply—as a basis for a felony conviction would violate this fundamental principle. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to apply *Nichols* to the guilt phase thus misapplies the precedent and undermines the core protections of *Winship*.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. *Ant* predates *Nichols* and was decided under a different legal framework. More importantly, *Ant* was not a Sixth Amendment case—it was a due process case grounded in the ICRA. The court in *Ant* held that tribal court convictions could not be used to establish guilt in a federal prosecution if the tribal court did not provide an equivalent right to counsel. 882 F.2d at 1396. But *Nichols* explicitly rejected the notion that a prior conviction is constitutionally infirm simply because it was obtained without counsel, so long as the conviction is valid and the defendant was not deprived of due process. 511 U.S. at 746. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to graft *Ant* onto *Nichols* creates a conflict that this Court must resolve.

### II. TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS ARE NOT SUBJECT TO THE SIXTH AMENDMENT, AND THE ICRA PROVIDES ADEQUATE DUE PROCESS

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” because they lack a Sixth Amendment right to counsel is legally unsound. The Sixth Amendment applies only to federal and state courts. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978). The Court has long recognized that Indian tribes possess inherent sovereign authority to govern their members and to adjudicate criminal matters. *Id.* at 324. This sovereignty includes the power to establish their own criminal justice systems, subject only to the limitations imposed by Congress and the Constitution as applied to tribes through the ICRA.

The ICRA, enacted in 1968 and amended by the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010, provides tribal courts with procedural safeguards, including the right to counsel in cases where the defendant faces imprisonment. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a). In this case, Bryant’s prior convictions were obtained in the Northern Cheyenne Tribal Court, which is a court of record with jurisdiction over domestic violence offenses. The record reflects that Bryant was afforded the right to counsel under the ICRA, and that the proceedings were conducted in accordance with tribal law and due process. The fact that the ICRA does not provide a right to counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment does not render the conviction invalid. As the Court has held, “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008).

To hold otherwise would be to impose a federal constitutional standard on tribal courts that Congress has not mandated and that would undermine tribal self-governance. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, by requiring tribal courts to provide a Sixth Amendment-level right to counsel in order for their convictions to be used in federal prosecutions, effectively nullifies the ICRA’s framework and imposes judicial paternalism on tribal nations. This is contrary to the principles of comity and respect for tribal sovereignty. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997).
