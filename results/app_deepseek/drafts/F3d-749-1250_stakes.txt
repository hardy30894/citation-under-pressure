**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court plainly erred by permitting FBI Special Agent Jones to opine on Stanley Hill’s credibility and truthfulness, usurping the jury’s exclusive role.**

Because Stanley did not object to Agent Jones’s testimony at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. *United States v. Gantt*, 679 F.3d 1240, 1245 (10th Cir. 2012). Plain error requires (1) an error; (2) that is clear or obvious; (3) that affected the defendant’s substantial rights; and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* Each element is satisfied here.

**A. The testimony was clearly inadmissible.**

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 permits expert testimony only if it “will help the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” An expert may not, however, “offer an opinion on the credibility of a witness.” *United States v. Adams*, 271 F.3d 1236, 1244 (10th Cir. 2001). This prohibition extends equally to opinions on a defendant’s credibility. *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1266 (10th Cir. 1999). Such testimony “invade[s] the province of the jury” because “the jury is the lie detector in the courtroom.” *United States v. Bedford*, 628 F.3d 1232, 1239 (10th Cir. 2010) (quoting *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 313 (1998)).

Agent Jones’s statements crossed this bright line repeatedly. He testified that many of Stanley’s answers were “not worthy of credence” and that Stanley displayed evasive behaviors “common among the criminal element.” He opined that, based on his training and experience, “when people start bringing faith into validating their statements, that they’re deceptive.” And he declared: “Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” Each of these statements is a direct comment on Stanley’s truthfulness and guilt. This Court has held that an expert’s opinion that a defendant’s conduct is “consistent with someone who is guilty” is inadmissible. *United States v. Avitia-Guillen*, 680 F.3d 1253, 1258 (10th Cir. 2012). Agent Jones’s testimony is indistinguishable—and indeed more harmful, because he explicitly disbelieved Stanley’s exculpatory statements.

Further, Jones’s credentials—training in “special tactics and ways to identify deception”—did not qualify him to opine on credibility. Even if they did, “expert testimony on the credibility of witnesses is not admissible under Rule 702.” *Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1244. The error was clear and obvious.

**B. The error affected Stanley’s substantial rights.**

“To satisfy the third prong of plain error review, the defendant must establish that the error affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Gantt*, 679 F.3d at 1246. Here, the government’s case was largely circumstantial, with no eyewitness identifying Stanley inside the bank. The jury had to decide whether Stanley acted as the getaway driver. Agent Jones’s testimony—clothed in the mantle of an FBI expert—told the jury that Stanley’s statements were deceptive and that no innocent person would say what he said. This directly undermined the only defense: that Stanley was not involved. The jury was likely influenced by an expert who “purported to have special knowledge” that Stanley was lying. *Bedford*, 628 F.3d at 1240. Given the close nature of the case (the first trial ended in a hung jury), the error cannot be deemed harmless.

**C. The error seriously affects the fairness and integrity of the proceedings.**

This Court has repeatedly recognized that “improper expert testimony on credibility undermines the fundamental role of the jury.” *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1266. Allowing a government expert to brand a defendant a liar “corrupt[s] the truth-seeking function of the trial.” *Bedford*, 628 F.3d at 1241. If left uncorrected, such testimony invites future prosecutorial overreach and erodes public confidence in the justice system. Plain error review exists precisely for cases like this, where “the integrity of the judicial proceedings” is at stake. *Gantt*, 679 F.3d at 1247.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
