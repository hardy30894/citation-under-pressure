**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S DENIAL OF ACCESS TO THE GOOD NEWS CLUB WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE BECAUSE IT WAS BASED ON SUBJECT, NOT VIEWPOINT, DISCRIMINATION.**

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club was constitutional subject discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 512-13 (2d Cir. 2000). This Court has long recognized that the government may permissibly exclude speech on certain subjects from a limited public forum, so long as the exclusion is viewpoint neutral. *See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) ("[T]he government must respect both the content and the viewpoint of speech if it chooses to open a nonpublic forum."); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393 (1993) ("The principle that has emerged from our cases is that the government, even when acting in its proprietary capacity, may not regulate speech in ways that discriminate among viewpoints upon any particular issue.").

Milford’s Community Use Policy explicitly excluded "religious purposes" from the limited public forum of its school facilities. *See* 202 F.3d at 507. The Club’s proposed activities—singing religious songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying—were quintessentially religious in nature, not merely discussions of morals from a religious perspective. *Id.* at 512-13. The Club’s own description of its activities confirmed this: it sought to use school facilities for "singing songs, hearing [a] Bible lesson and memorizing scripture." *Id.* at 508. This is not the mere discussion of secular subjects from a religious viewpoint; it is the practice of religion itself. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) ("There is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’").

The Club’s reliance on *Board of Educ. of Westside Cmty. Schs. (Dist. 66) v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is misplaced. In *Mergens*, this Court held that a school’s denial of access to a Christian student group violated the Equal Access Act because the school had created a limited open forum for noncurriculum student groups and could not exclude the Christian group based on the religious content of its speech. *Id.* at 243-44. The Act itself prohibited exclusion based on the religious content of speech, and the Court did not reach the constitutional question. *Id.* at 243. Here, by contrast, Milford’s policy excluded speech on the subject of religion entirely, not based on the viewpoint of the speaker. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512-13.

**II. EVEN IF THE COURT WERE TO FIND THAT MILFORD ENGAGED IN VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION, ITS ACTION WAS JUSTIFIED BY THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.**

Assuming arguendo that Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club constituted viewpoint discrimination, the denial was nonetheless justified by the Establishment Clause. This Court has repeatedly held that the government may exclude religious speech from a limited public forum to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394-95 ("[T]he exclusion of otherwise permissible subject matter from a limited public forum to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is a valid, viewpoint-neutral reason for exclusion."); *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271-72 (holding that the Establishment Clause did not justify a university’s content-based exclusion of religious speech from a forum generally open to student groups).

Milford’s concern that permitting the Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause was reasonable and objective. The Club’s activities—singing religious songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying—are inherently religious in nature. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512-13. Permitting such activities on school grounds would risk the perception that the school was endorsing religion, in violation of the Establishment Clause. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394-95. This Court has recognized that the government may take steps to avoid such perceptions, even if those steps incidentally restrict speech. *See id.*

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), is again misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, this Court held that a university’s denial of funding to a student religious publication violated the Free Speech Clause because the university had created a limited public forum for student publications and could not exclude the religious publication based on its viewpoint. *Id.* at 829-30. The Court did not address the Establishment Clause, and the university’s concern that funding the publication would violate the Establishment Clause was not deemed sufficient to justify the exclusion. *Id.* at 841-42. Here, by contrast, Milford’s concern that permitting the Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause is both reasonable and objective, and this Court has recognized that such concerns may justify exclusion from a limited public forum. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394-95.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club was constitutional subject discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. Even if the Court were to find that Milford engaged in viewpoint discrimination, its action was justified by the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
