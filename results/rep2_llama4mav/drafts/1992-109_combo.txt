The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Constitution demands that a defendant's waiver of constitutional rights be voluntary, knowing, and intelligent. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). To ensure this, a court must be satisfied that the defendant is competent to make such a waiver.

The standard for determining competency to stand trial is not sufficient to ensure that a defendant is competent to waive constitutional rights. As this Court has held, a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960). However, waiving constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning. See Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954) (noting that a defendant's mental competency to stand trial does not necessarily mean they are competent to waive their rights).

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the "reasoned choice" standard, which requires that a defendant have the capacity to make a deliberate and informed decision when waiving constitutional rights. See Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam). This standard is more stringent than the standard for competency to stand trial, as it demands that the defendant be capable of making a reasoned decision among the available alternatives.

In this case, the state court failed to apply the correct standard when evaluating Moran's competency to waive his right to counsel and plead guilty. The court's reliance on psychiatric evaluations that only assessed Moran's competency to stand trial was insufficient to determine whether he was competent to waive his constitutional rights. See Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385-86 (1966) (holding that a trial court's failure to hold a competency hearing when there was substantial evidence of a defendant's incompetence violated due process).

The state court's error was not cured by the post-conviction hearing, which also applied the wrong legal standard. See Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 314 (1963) (holding that a federal court may not presume that a state court made the necessary factual findings to support its legal conclusion if there is evidence that the state court applied an incorrect legal standard).

In light of the foregoing, it is clear that the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when determining a defendant's competency to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed.

The heightened standard is rooted in the principle that a defendant's waiver of constitutional rights must be voluntary, knowing, and intelligent. See Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942). The application of this standard ensures that a defendant's waiver is not the result of mental incompetence or other factors that might impair their decision-making capacity.

By applying the "reasoned choice" standard, courts can ensure that defendants are competent to waive their constitutional rights and that their waivers are valid. See Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708, 723-24 (1948) (plurality opinion) (emphasizing the importance of ensuring that a defendant's waiver of their right to counsel is intelligent and understanding).
