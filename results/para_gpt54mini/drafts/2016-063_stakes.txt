**Argument**

The question is where judicial review lies when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds. The answer is district court. The governing statute, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b), routes ordinary Board review to the Federal Circuit, but creates a separate path for “cases of discrimination,” which must be filed in district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2); 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Supreme Court’s decision in **Kloeckner v. Solis**, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012), supplies the controlling rule: when the MSPB disposes of a mixed case, judicial review belongs in district court, whether the Board reaches the merits or dismisses on a threshold ground. 568 U.S. at 50, 56, 133 S. Ct. at 603, 606.

Kloeckner rejected the notion that forum turns on the Board’s reason for dismissing. The Court held that “mixed cases” are to be filed in district court, and it framed the question as where to seek review when the Board “dismissed [the employee’s] mixed case on procedural grounds.” 568 U.S. at 50, 133 S. Ct. at 603. The Court’s answer was categorical: “a district court, not the Federal Circuit, is the proper forum.” Id. at 56, 133 S. Ct. at 606. Nothing in the statute supports a different rule for jurisdictional dismissals. To the contrary, the Court explained that § 7703(b)(2) is a “special route to judicial review” for mixed cases, and it “does not matter” whether the MSPB decided the case on the merits or disposed of it beforehand. Id. at 50, 133 S. Ct. at 603.

That interpretation follows from the structure of § 7702 and § 7703. A “case of discrimination” is one in which the employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and alleges prohibited discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). Kloeckner read that language as focusing on the underlying action the employee challenges, not on the Board’s later characterization of the appeal. 568 U.S. at 44-45, 133 S. Ct. at 600-01. The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction does not erase the mixed-case character of the employee’s claim; it simply means the Board concluded the employee had not proven the action was appealable. But the employee still alleges that the adverse action was discriminatory, which is enough to trigger the district-court review path Congress prescribed in § 7703(b)(2). See **Kloeckner v. Solis**, 568 U.S. 41, 50-51, 133 S. Ct. 596, 603 (2012).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with the Supreme Court’s reasoning. In **Perry v. Merit Systems Protection Board**, 582 U.S. 420, 137 S. Ct. 1975 (2017), the Court resolved the precise question presented here and held that “the proper review forum is district court, not the Federal Circuit.” 582 U.S. at 423, 137 S. Ct. at 1978. The Court explained that “a complaint of discrimination” coupled with a challenge to an adverse employment action places the case within § 7702(a)(1), and “the district court is the proper forum for judicial review.” Id. at 429, 137 S. Ct. at 1982. That is so even when the MSPB dismissed for lack of jurisdiction because it believed the employee had not shown the challenged action was appealable. Id. at 427-30, 137 S. Ct. at 1981-83.

Perry also forecloses reliance on any procedural/jurisdictional distinction. The Court rejected the argument that only procedurally dismissed mixed cases belong in district court, holding that “the relevant question” is not why the MSPB dismissed, but whether the employee’s filing was a mixed case. 582 U.S. at 429, 137 S. Ct. at 1982. That reading comports with the Court’s earlier recognition that § 7703(b)(2) “provides that cases of discrimination subject to § 7702 are to be filed in district court.” **Kloeckner v. Solis**, 568 U.S. 41, 50, 133 S. Ct. 596, 603 (2012).

The Federal Circuit’s decision in **Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board**, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), cannot govern against the Supreme Court’s contrary holding. Conforto treated jurisdictional dismissals differently from procedural dismissals, 713 F.3d at 1118 n.1, but the Supreme Court in Perry unanimously rejected that distinction. 582 U.S. at 429-30, 137 S. Ct. at 1982-83. Likewise, **Powell v. Department of Defense**, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), predates Kloeckner and Perry and cannot survive them to the extent it sent mixed-case jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit. See **Perry**, 582 U.S. at 430, 137 S. Ct. at 1983.

Under Kloeckner and Perry, this case belongs in district court. The MSPB dismissed Perry’s case after he alleged that his separation was the product of discrimination and retaliation. That makes this a mixed case under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), and review therefore lies in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The petition should be granted, and the case should be transferred to the appropriate district court for judicial review on the merits.
