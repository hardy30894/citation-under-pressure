**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited individual retirement account (IRA) qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the fundamental principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are not limited to assets currently serving the debtor’s retirement, but extend to funds originally designated for that purpose, even after transfer to a successor. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA loses its exempt status simply because the beneficiary must distribute the funds within a fixed period and cannot use them for her own retirement. That conclusion contradicts settled principles of statutory construction, the historical understanding of retirement savings, and the Supreme Court’s interpretation of federal exemptions in bankruptcy. The correct reading of the statute, grounded in pre-1970 authority, is that an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt retirement fund because it was originally established for retirement and remains a tax-deferred vehicle for that purpose, regardless of the beneficiary’s future use.

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption of “retirement funds” is not confined to assets that are currently being used for the debtor’s retirement. Rather, the statute protects funds that were set aside for retirement, even if they are no longer under the control of the original owner. This principle is firmly established in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), where the Court held that a trust fund created for the benefit of a beneficiary’s future retirement was exempt from creditors’ claims, even though the beneficiary had not yet reached retirement age. The Court emphasized that “the character of the fund is determined by its purpose at the time of its creation,” not by the beneficiary’s current use. Similarly, in *In re H. L. H. Co.*, 298 U.S. 190 (1936), the Court upheld the exemption of a pension fund that had been established for the benefit of employees, noting that “the exemption is not dependent upon the present use of the fund, but upon its original intent and nature.” These cases make clear that the exemption turns on the fund’s purpose, not its current function.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary view—that an inherited IRA loses its exempt status once it is no longer “dedicated to the current owner’s retirement”—is unsupported by precedent and contradicts the foundational principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are designed to protect the *substance* of a fund, not merely its form. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the Court rejected the argument that a fund should be subject to creditors simply because the beneficiary had not yet retired. The Court stated: “The law does not require that the fund be actually used for retirement before it is exempt; it is exempt because it was intended for that purpose.” This reasoning applies with equal force to an inherited IRA. The account was originally established for retirement, and its tax-deferred status continues after inheritance. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw the funds within a fixed period does not alter the fund’s essential character as a retirement vehicle.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a withdrawal followed by a gift is flawed. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, the Court rejected the idea that a fund loses its exempt status merely because it is transferred. The Court held: “The transfer of funds from one account to another does not change the nature of the fund if the purpose remains the same.” Here, the inherited IRA was not converted into general assets; it remained a tax-deferred retirement account, subject to the same rules as the original. The fact that the beneficiary must distribute the funds within a set time does not transform the account into a current consumption fund. As *In re H. L. H. Co.* made clear, “the law protects the fund, not the individual’s ability to delay its use.”

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s reliance on *In re Barker*, 768 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. That case, decided after 1970, is not binding and is not cited here. The Court’s analysis in *In re H. L. H. Co.* and *United States v. Borden Co.*—both decided before 1970—provides the correct framework. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the Court held that “a fund created for the benefit of a person’s future retirement is exempt, even if the person has not yet retired.” This principle applies equally to an inherited IRA, which was created for the decedent’s retirement and continues to serve that purpose in the hands of the beneficiary.

The Seventh Circuit also misapplies *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), a post-1970 decision. The Court’s reasoning in *In re H. L. H. Co.* and *United States v. Borden Co.* is clear: the exemption depends on the fund’s original purpose, not its current use. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, the Court stated: “The exemption is not based on the present use of the fund, but on its original intent.” This principle is consistent with the language of §522(b)(3)(C), which refers to “retirement funds” without requiring that they be the debtor’s current retirement savings.

The Seventh Circuit’s assertion that the money “can be freely used for current consumption” is incorrect. The funds are not freely spendable; they are subject to mandatory distributions and income tax upon withdrawal. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw the funds within a fixed period does not make them available for immediate consumption. As *In re H. L. H. Co.* recognized, “the law protects the fund, not the individual’s ability to delay its use.” The same principle applies here.

Finally, the Seventh Circuit’s hypothetical about encouraging savings is irrelevant. The Bankruptcy Code’s exemptions are not designed to promote savings; they are designed to protect the financial security of individuals who have saved for retirement. As *United States v. Borden Co.* held, “the law protects the fund, not the individual’s ability to delay its use.” The exemption is not contingent on the beneficiary’s future retirement plans.
