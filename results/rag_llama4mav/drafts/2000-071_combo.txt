The respondent, Milford Central School, respectfully submits that its exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at the school did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. Even if it did, the exclusion was justified by Milford's concern that permitting the Club's activities would violate the Establishment Clause.

The Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, are "quintessentially religious" and not merely the teaching of morals from a religious perspective. As the Second Circuit held, the Club's activities "fall outside the bounds of pure 'moral and character development'" (202 F.3d 502, 509 (2nd Cir. 2000)). The subject matter of the Club's meetings is not merely morals and character, but rather religious instruction and worship.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may restrict speech in a limited public forum to the extent that it is reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum (Perry Ed. Assn. v. Perry Local Educators' Assn., 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983)). Here, Milford's community use policy explicitly prohibits use of school facilities for "religious purposes" (App. at 34). This restriction is viewpoint-neutral and reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum, which is to provide a limited public space for community activities.

The Club's argument that its activities constitute a permissible "religious viewpoint" on the subject of morals and character is unpersuasive. While the Supreme Court has recognized that a religious perspective can constitute a separate viewpoint on a wide variety of seemingly secular subject matter (Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993)), the Club's activities go beyond merely expressing a religious viewpoint on morals and character. Rather, they involve explicit religious instruction and worship.

Furthermore, the Establishment Clause prohibits government endorsement or promotion of religion (Abington School Dist. v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222 (1963)). By permitting the Club to meet at the school, Milford would be seen as endorsing or promoting the Club's religious activities, which could be perceived as government sponsorship of religion (Illinois Ex Rel. McCollum v. Board of Ed. of School Dist. No. 71, Champaign Cty., 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948)). The risk of such perception is particularly high given the age of the children involved and the fact that the Club's activities are not merely a "religious viewpoint" on a secular subject, but rather explicit religious instruction and worship.

In Zorach v. Clauson, the Supreme Court upheld a "released time" program that allowed students to leave school premises for religious instruction, emphasizing that the program did not involve the expenditure of public funds or the use of public school classrooms for religious instruction (343 U.S. 306, 308-309 (1952)). In contrast, the Club's proposed use of Milford's facilities involves the use of public school premises for explicit religious instruction and worship.

The Court has also recognized that the government has a legitimate interest in avoiding the appearance of endorsing or promoting religion (Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236, 243 (1968)). By excluding the Club from meeting at the school, Milford is avoiding the risk of being seen as endorsing or promoting the Club's religious activities.

In Fowler v. Rhode Island, the Court held that a state may not prefer one religious group over another (345 U.S. 67, 69 (1953)). Here, Milford's exclusion of the Club is not a preference for one religious group over another, but rather a neutral application of its community use policy.

In conclusion, Milford's exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at the school did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. Even if it did, the exclusion was justified by Milford's concern that permitting the Club's activities would violate the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
