**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not bar use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act, not the Bill of Rights. See Seymour v. Superintendent of Washington State Penitentiary, 368 U.S. 351, 358 (1962) (recognizing that federal law treats tribal jurisdiction as distinct). Because the tribal proceedings complied with the law applicable to them, the convictions remain valid for recidivist purposes.

This Court has long held that a recidivist statute “penalizes only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Carlesi v. New York, 233 U.S. 51, 57 (1914). The prior convictions serve merely as historical facts that increase the penalty for the new crime; they are not elements of that crime requiring identical constitutional protections. Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948); Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 452 (1962). Consequently, the government need not prove the validity of each predicate under the full panoply of rights applicable to the current federal prosecution. Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554, 565 (1966).

The same principle governs challenges to the manner in which a prior conviction was obtained. In Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), the Court required that a prior conviction used for enhancement be “constitutionally valid,” yet it did not impose on the earlier proceeding every procedural safeguard that would apply in the later case. See also Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196, 201-02 (1948) (due process requires only that the defendant receive notice and an opportunity to contest the recidivist allegation). Where, as here, the tribal court afforded the rights required by the governing statute, the conviction satisfies that standard.

Nor does the absence of appointed counsel in the tribal proceedings render the convictions infirm for enhancement. Chandler v. Warden Fretag, 348 U.S. 3, 9 (1954), and Price v. Henkel, 216 U.S. 488, 494 (1910), confirm that the right to counsel is measured by the law applicable at the time and place of the prior proceeding. Tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment; their procedures are measured by the Indian Civil Rights Act. Sigler v. Losieau, 396 U.S. 988, 989 (1969) (recognizing that collateral attacks on prior convictions turn on whether those convictions were valid under the law then controlling). Because Bryant’s tribal convictions were valid when rendered, they may be used to establish the § 117(a) predicates.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule would require federal courts to import Sixth Amendment standards into every tribal proceeding—an intrusion this Court has never sanctioned. The judgment should be reversed.
