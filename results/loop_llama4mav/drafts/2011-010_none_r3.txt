ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Workers’ Compensation Provision Requires a Situs-of-Injury Test

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) to injuries occurring “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). A plain reading of this provision indicates that the injury must be connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Supreme Court has long recognized the importance of locality in determining the applicability of workers' compensation statutes, as seen in Southern Pac. Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917), where the Court emphasized that the locality of the injury is a critical factor.

The LHWCA, which provides the framework for OCSLA's workers' compensation provision, requires that the injury occur on “navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); see also Swanson v. Marra Bros., 328 U.S. 1 (1946); Nogueira v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co., 281 U.S. 128 (1930). The consistent application of a situs requirement in the LHWCA, as seen in cases such as State Industrial Comm’n v. Nordenholt Corp., 259 U.S. 263 (1922), and London Guarantee & Accident Co. v. Indus. Accident Comm’n, 279 U.S. 109 (1929), supports the conclusion that a similar requirement should apply to OCSLA claims. This interpretation is further reinforced by the principle that workers' compensation statutes should be construed to effect their remedial purpose, while also respecting their statutory boundaries.

II. La Conchita is Not a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA requires that the injury occur on a maritime situs, defined as “navigable waters of the United States, including any adjoining pier, wharf, dry dock, terminal, building way, marine railway, or other adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). La Conchita does not meet this definition, as it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks and is not used for maritime purposes. The facility's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity, which further supports the conclusion that it is not a maritime situs. See T. Smith & Son, Inc. v. Taylor, 276 U.S. 179 (1928).

The Ninth Circuit's "functional relationship" test, as applied in Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978), is used to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs. Under this test, La Conchita is not a maritime situs because it lacks a sufficient connection to navigable waters and is not used for maritime purposes. The test considers factors such as the site's suitability for maritime uses and its proximity to the waterway, further supporting the conclusion that La Conchita does not qualify as a maritime situs.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the BRB's denial of benefits under the OCSLA and LHWCA.
