# ARGUMENT

**THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT RECOGNIZE A "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION ABSENT ALLEGATIONS OF MEMBERSHIP IN A CLASS OR GROUP**

## I. The Text and History of the Equal Protection Clause Require Class-Based Discrimination

The Equal Protection Clause commands that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has consistently construed the Clause as guarding against discrimination directed at *classes* of persons, not as a general guarantee against all arbitrary government action. In *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202 (1982), this Court explained that equal protection analysis requires courts to examine whether "some fundamental right is involved" or whether legislation "burdens a suspect class" — language presupposing that the constitutional inquiry begins with class identification.

The doctrinal architecture this Court has built for equal protection claims—strict scrutiny for suspect classifications, intermediate scrutiny for quasi-suspect classifications, and rational basis review for ordinary classifications, *see Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985)—makes sense only if there exists an actual class against which to compare treatment. As this Court stated in *Cleburne*, "the Equal Protection Clause is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike." 473 U.S. at 439. This formulation necessarily requires identification of a comparator class; without one, there is no meaningful way to assess whether "similarly situated" persons received disparate treatment based on class membership rather than individualized, case-specific factors.

## II. Recognizing a "Class of One" Theory Would Constitutionalize Ordinary Municipal Land-Use and Contract Disputes

The Seventh Circuit's holding—permitting suit whenever government action is motivated by "a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective"—would transform every disputed exercise of municipal discretion into a potential constitutional tort. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against converting garden-variety state-law disputes into federal constitutional claims. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), this Court rejected an equal protection challenge to an allegedly arbitrary and discriminatory denial of a certificate of election, holding that "the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." *Id.* at 8. Critically, this Court emphasized that mere arbitrariness, without discriminatory purpose tied to some classification, does not offend the Clause. *Id.*

Permitting a single property owner to sue based on a claim of differential treatment from *other* individual owners—without any allegation that the plaintiff belongs to an identifiable class subject to a pattern of discrimination—would eliminate any principled limit on federal court oversight of state and local decisionmaking. Municipal governments daily negotiate easements, set permit conditions, and exercise discretion in myriad transactions with individual citizens. Every such decision that treats one citizen differently from another, for whatever site-specific or negotiation-specific reason, could be recast as a "class of one" equal protection claim if the Seventh Circuit's rule stands.

## III. Respondent's Allegations Sound in State Contract and Tort Law, Not Constitutional Discrimination

Respondent's complaint alleges that the Village demanded a 33-foot easement rather than the 15-foot easement allegedly required of other property owners. This is, at bottom, a dispute about the terms on which a municipality will provide a service—a matter traditionally governed by state contract and administrative law principles, not the Fourteenth Amendment. This Court's decision in *Village of Belle Terre v. Boraas*, 416 U.S. 1 (1974), confirms that municipalities retain substantial "police power to zone" and structure land-use requirements, and that such determinations receive deferential rational-basis review rather than heightened constitutional scrutiny.

Respondent's own characterization of her claim—that the Village's demand was "wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective"—is simply an allegation of arbitrariness. But as *Snowden* makes clear, arbitrary state action, without more, is not a constitutional violation. 321 U.S. at 8. If every citizen who believes she received a worse deal than her neighbor in negotiating with a municipal entity can state a federal constitutional claim, the practical effect will be to federalize local governance and open federal courthouse doors to a flood of individualized grievances that state courts and state law are far better equipped to adjudicate.

## IV. This Court Should Reaffirm That Equal Protection Claims Require Class-Based Allegations

The rational basis standard, as articulated in cases such as *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. 432, exists precisely to accommodate the reality that government must draw lines and make individualized judgments. That standard presupposes a comparison between defined groups—not an atomized, person-by-person comparison untethered to any classification scheme. Respondent has not alleged she is a member of any suspect or quasi-suspect class, nor has she alleged a pattern of discrimination against similarly situated *groups* of property owners. She alleges only that she, individually, received different treatment than certain other individuals in easement negotiations.

This Court should hold that such allegations, without an accompanying assertion of class or group membership, fail to state a cognizable equal protection claim. The Fourteenth Amendment protects against class-based discrimination; it was never intended to serve as a roving constitutional cause of action for every disappointed negotiator with local government.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
