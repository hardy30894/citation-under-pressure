# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. This Court has recognized that the word "shall" ordinarily imposes an obligatory duty rather than a discretionary option, absent some clear indication elsewhere in the statute that Congress intended otherwise. *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). Nothing in Section 1407(a) contains any such contrary indication, still less one that would excuse remand whenever a transferee judge invokes the wholly separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a) to conclude that retention would be convenient.

This Court's approach to statutory construction confirms that such mandatory language must be enforced according to its terms. As Chief Justice Marshall explained, courts must depart only reluctantly from the plain meaning of words a legislature has chosen. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95–96 (1820). And it remains "emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is"—not to rewrite a statute's terms to accommodate the convenience of the parties or the preferences of a particular court. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803). Section 1407(a)'s command is unambiguous: remand must occur "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings—not "unless the transferee court prefers otherwise."

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute, and courts have no license to nullify a mandatory command because they judge that departure serves efficiency.

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is the general venue-transfer provision applicable to ordinary civil litigation. This Court has recognized that Section 1404(a) reposes discretion in the transferor court to weigh considerations of convenience and the interest of justice in deciding whether transfer to another proper venue is warranted—a discretion exercised within, not in defiance of, the statute's own terms. *Norwood v. Kirkpatrick Co.*, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955). Section 1404(a)'s remedial purpose has likewise been understood as one of general applicability to ordinary venue disputes between districts where an action might properly have been brought, not as a freestanding grant of authority to reassign a case held under some other, separately limited statutory mandate. *Olberding v. Illinois Central R. Co.*, 346 U.S. 338, 340–41 (1953); *see also United States v. National City Lines, Inc.*, 337 U.S. 78, 84 (1949) (discussing the scope of the discretion Congress conferred by Section 1404(a)).

Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the mandatory remand command. Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. This Court has explained that "general language of a statutory provision, although broad enough to include it, will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment." *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902). That principle controls here: Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer, and that specific command cannot be nullified by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a), a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all.

Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance is inconvenient.
