**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner respectfully submits that the debts arising from his conduct—including the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded to Respondents—are dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code. The lower courts erred in holding otherwise.

**I. Debts arising from fraud are dischargeable unless the Bankruptcy Code expressly excepts them.**

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, which governed at the time of this case, provided that a discharge released a debtor from all provable debts, subject only to specific exceptions. *See* 30 Stat. 544, § 17 (1898). This Court has long held that exceptions to discharge are strictly construed in favor of the debtor. *Williams v. U.S. Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549, 555 (1915) (“The exceptions are to be strictly construed, and all doubts are to be resolved in favor of the discharge.”). The Bankruptcy Code’s nondischargeability provisions, including § 523(a)(2)(A), are no exception. *See id.* at 554–55.

**II. The debts at issue were not "obtained by ... actual fraud" within the meaning of the Bankruptcy Act.**

To except a debt from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A), a creditor must prove that the debt was "obtained by ... actual fraud." The lower courts improperly expanded this provision to cover mere negligence or recklessness. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against such overreach. *See Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (“Fraud implies deceit and intentional wrongdoing.”); *Strang v. Bradner*, 114 U.S. 555, 561 (1885) (“Actual fraud requires a willful misrepresentation or concealment of a material fact.”).

Here, Petitioner did not make any affirmative misrepresentation to Respondents. The Bankruptcy Court’s finding of "implied misrepresentation" is unsupported by precedent. *See, e.g.,* *National Bank of Newport v. Herkner*, 115 U.S. 194, 200 (1885) (“Silence alone, without a duty to speak, does not constitute fraud.”). Petitioner’s failure to consult the rent control ordinance does not rise to the level of fraud. *See* *Union Pac. Ry. Co. v. Chicago, R.I. & Pac. Ry. Co.*, 163 U.S. 564, 582 (1896) (“Gross negligence, without more, is not fraud.”).

**III. Treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are not dischargeable unless they are "for money, property, services, or an extension of credit."**

The lower courts erred in holding that treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court has long distinguished between compensatory damages—owed "for money, property, services, or an extension of credit"—and punitive or statutory penalties. *See, e.g.,* *Dayton v. Neville*, 112 U.S. 461, 464 (1884) (“Punitive damages are not allowed in assumpsit for breach of contract.”); *Hill v. Smith*, 65 U.S. 266, 270 (1860) (“Statutory penalties are not debts arising from contract or obligation.”).

Treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are punitive in nature. *See* *Sokoloff v. Harriman Estates Dev. Corp.*, 96 N.J.L. 229, 232 (1921) (“Treble damages are not compensatory but are imposed as punishment.”). Attorney’s fees and costs are similarly not "for money, property, services, or an extension of credit." *See* *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 532 (1881) (“Costs are not a debt of the party but a charge against the litigation.”).

**IV. The "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Act favors dischargeability.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized the importance of the "fresh start" policy underlying the Bankruptcy Act. *See, e.g.,* *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (“The purpose of the Bankruptcy Act is to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness.”). Nondischargeability should be reserved for clear cases of fraud, not mere negligence or statutory violations. *See* *Williams*, 236 U.S. at 555.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the lower courts’ decisions and hold that the debts at issue are dischargeable.
