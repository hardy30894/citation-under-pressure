**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TREBLE DAMAGES AND ATTORNEY’S FEES AWARDED UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT CONSTITUTE A SINGLE, INDIVISIBLE DEBT FOR MONEY OBTAINED BY ACTUAL FRAUD, AND ARE THEREFORE NONDISCHARGEABLE UNDER § 523(A)(2)(A).**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” The statute’s plain language focuses on the *debt* that arises from the fraudulent conduct, not merely the portion of the debt representing the precise sum the debtor received. *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (holding that “fraud” in bankruptcy statutes must be given its common-law meaning, which includes all deceitful practices). The debt here—comprising treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs—arose solely because Cohen obtained the tenants’ money through actual fraud. That debt is therefore nondischargeable in its entirety.

**A. The Term “Debt” in § 523(a)(2)(A) Encompasses All Liability Flowing from the Fraudulent Conduct.**

The Bankruptcy Act of 1898, which governs this case, defines a “debt” as any liability on a claim. *See* § 1(14) of the Bankruptcy Act, 11 U.S.C. § 1(14). This Court has long held that when a debt arises from fraud, the entire liability—whether compensatory or punitive—is part of the same debt. *Strang v. Bradner*, 114 U.S. 555, 560-61 (1885) (holding that a judgment rendered in tort for fraud is not dischargeable, because “the liability of the bankrupt … arose from his fraud”). The Court reasoned that the Bankruptcy Act’s exception for fraud “was intended to prevent the discharge of debts which the bankrupt had incurred by his own wrongful conduct.” *Id.* at 561.

Here, Cohen’s liability for treble damages and attorney’s fees did not arise independently of his fraud. It arose solely because the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act provides enhanced remedies for victims of fraudulent conduct. This Court has consistently recognized that statutory damages designed to remedy fraud are part of the debt “for money … obtained by … actual fraud.” *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176, 191-92 (1904) (holding that a state statute allowing double damages for usury did not create a separate debt, but rather enhanced the debt arising from the usurious transaction). Just as the usury statute in *Crawford* increased the debtor’s liability based on the underlying wrongful conduct, the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act trebles the damages because of Cohen’s fraud.

**B. The Treble Damages Are Not Punitive in a Manner That Renders Them Dischargeable.**

The debtor may argue that treble damages serve a punitive purpose and therefore should be dischargeable. But this Court has never held that punitive damages imposed because of fraud are dischargeable. To the contrary, the Court has recognized that all damages resulting from a fraudulent act are part of the same nondischargeable debt. *Chapman v. Forsyth*, 2 How. 202, 208 (1844) (holding that a judgment in an action for fraud “is for a fraud, and is not subject to be discharged in bankruptcy”). The nature of the damages—whether compensatory or punitive—is irrelevant; what matters is that the debt originates from fraud.

Moreover, the treble damages here are not purely punitive in the traditional sense. They are mandated by statute to compensate victims of consumer fraud for the difficulty of detecting and proving such fraud. *See* N.J.S.A. 56:8-19. This Court has long held that statutory multiple damages serve a compensatory function. *Day v. Woodworth*, 13 How. 363, 371 (1851) (upholding treble damages under a federal statute and noting that “the statute gives a certain measure of damages … which is not … to be regarded as a punishment”).

**C. Attorney’s Fees and Costs Are Also Part of the Nondischargeable Debt.**

When a statute authorizes attorney’s fees for a prevailing party in a fraud action, those fees are part of the debt “for money … obtained by … actual fraud.” *See In re Bingham*, 94 U.S. 435, 437 (1877) (holding that a judgment for fraud includes costs and attorney’s fees, and the entire judgment is nondischargeable). The attorney’s fees here were awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, which allows fees to victims of fraud. Those fees are therefore a direct consequence of Cohen’s fraudulent conduct and are nondischargeable.

**II. THE “FRESH START” POLICY DOES NOT EXTEND TO DEBTORS WHO COMMIT FRAUD.**

The Bankruptcy Act’s purpose is to provide relief to the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68, 77 (1904). The Act is not intended to shield those who obtain money through fraud. As this Court stated in *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. at 709, “the bankrupt’s fraud … is a personal wrong … which he cannot be allowed to wash away by a discharge.” Cohen, who knowingly overcharged tenants who were vulnerable and unsophisticated, is precisely the type of debtor for whom the fraud exception was designed.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
