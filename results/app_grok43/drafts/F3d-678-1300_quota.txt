The district court erred in denying judgment as a matter of law on invalidity. A patent is presumed valid, and the party asserting invalidity must prove facts supporting the defense by clear and convincing evidence. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 254 (1986); Schumer v. Lab. Computer Sys., Inc., 308 F.3d 1304, 1315-16 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The on-sale bar requires both a commercial offer and that the invention be ready for patenting before the critical date. Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc., 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998). Public use likewise demands that the claimed invention be embodied in a product placed in public use. Invitrogen Corp. v. Biocrest Mfg., L.P., 424 F.3d 1374, 1379 (Fed. Cir. 2005).

No substantial evidence established that Leader2Leader® practiced every limitation of the asserted claims before December 10, 2002. A product anticipates only when it contains each element arranged as in the claim. SmithKline Diagnostics, Inc. v. Helena Labs. Corp., 859 F.2d 878, 890 (Fed. Cir. 1988). The white paper described a “glass ceiling” problem and general collaboration features but supplied no evidence that the offered product performed the specific metadata-tagging and dynamic-updating steps required by claim 9 and its dependents. Allen Eng’g Corp. v. Bartell Indus., Inc., 299 F.3d 1336, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2002). McKibben’s testimony that the patent covered the “underlying engine” likewise failed to map the product’s functionality to each claim element. Netscape Commc’ns Corp. v. Konrad, 295 F.3d 1315, 1321 (Fed. Cir. 2002).

The jury therefore lacked substantial evidence that the invention was ready for patenting or offered for sale in its claimed form. Lucent Techs., Inc. v. Gateway, Inc., 580 F.3d 1301, 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2009). Because the evidence was legally insufficient, the district court should have granted judgment as a matter of law. Teleflex, Inc. v. Ficosa N. Am. Corp., 299 F.3d 1313, 1323-24 (Fed. Cir. 2002).
