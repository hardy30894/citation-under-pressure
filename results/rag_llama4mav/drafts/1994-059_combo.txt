The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2, and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate, respectively. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements that a person must meet to be eligible for congressional office, and it is well-established that states may not add to or alter these qualifications.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a constitutional limitation on the power of states to regulate congressional elections. In Wiley v. Sinkler, 179 U.S. 58 (1900), the Court held that the right to vote for a member of Congress is derived from the Constitution, and that states, in prescribing the qualifications of voters for their own legislatures, do not thereby prescribe the qualifications for voters for Congress. This principle is equally applicable to the qualifications for congressional officeholders themselves.

The Court has also emphasized the importance of uniformity in the qualifications for congressional office. In United States v. Gradwell, 243 U.S. 476 (1917), the Court noted that the Constitution has established a uniform standard for the election of members of Congress, and that this standard is not subject to variation by the states.

The framers of the Constitution deliberately chose to establish specific qualifications for congressional office, and to preclude states from adding to or modifying these qualifications. As the Court observed in Hawke v. Smith (No. 1), 253 U.S. 221 (1920), the Constitution's provisions regarding the election of members of Congress are designed to ensure that the selection of these officials is governed by a uniform national standard, rather than by varying state laws.

By denying ballot access to candidates who have served a certain number of terms, Amendment 73 effectively adds a new qualification for congressional office, one that is not authorized by the Constitution.

The Court has struck down state laws that have attempted to do so, holding that such laws are unconstitutional because they conflict with the Qualifications Clauses. For example, in Newberry v. United States, 256 U.S. 232 (1921), the Court considered the validity of a federal statute regulating primary elections for congressional office, and noted that the Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are a limitation on the power of Congress, as well as the states, to regulate congressional elections.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are not merely a limitation on the power of states to regulate congressional elections, but also a fundamental aspect of the constitutional structure. In Barry v. United States Ex Rel. Cunningham, 279 U.S. 597 (1929), the Court observed that the Senate's power to judge the elections, returns, and qualifications of its members is a constitutional power that is essential to the Senate's ability to perform its constitutional functions.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that Amendment 73's attempt to impose term limits on Arkansas's congressional delegation is unconstitutional. The Qualifications Clauses establish the exclusive qualifications for congressional office, and states may not add to or alter these qualifications.
