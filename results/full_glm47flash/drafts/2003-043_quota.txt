**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Statute of Limitations for Collection Extends Only to the Taxpayer Whose Tax Has Been Assessed.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires that a tax be assessed against a specific taxpayer to trigger the ten-year limitations period for collection. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a).  Here, the IRS assessed the tax liability solely against the partnership, Marina Cabrillo Partners.  Because the partners are distinct taxpayers, the assessment against the partnership did not extend the collection period to them.  26 U.S.C. § 6203; 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

Section 6203 defines an assessment as the recording of the liability of the taxpayer.  26 U.S.C. § 6203.  The implementing regulations further require that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer."  26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.  The partners are separate "persons" and "taxpayers" under the Code, each possessing their own identification number.  26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1).  Consequently, the IRS’s failure to assess the partners individually means the ten-year clock did not start for them.

This interpretation is supported by precedent.  In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the Ninth Circuit held that a tax lien could not attach to an individual’s property without an assessment against that individual.  The court stated, "this court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203."  *Id.* at 676.  Similarly, the Seventh Circuit in *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), noted that the only relevant question was whether the statute of limitations applicable to the partners should be tolled while the period was tolled with respect to the partnership.  *Id.* at 562.  Because the IRS did not assess the partners, the limitations period for them remained the standard three years, which had expired.

**II. State Law Does Not Override the Federal Statute of Limitations Regarding the Assessment Requirement.**

The IRS argues that California’s partnership laws, which make partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allow the government to bypass the federal assessment requirement.  This argument is foreclosed by controlling precedent.  In *United States v. W. Prods., Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d 84 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), the court held that the government was entitled to collect withholding taxes from a general partner under state partnership laws, but only because the government had obtained a judgment against the partner.  *Id.* at 91.  The court rejected the argument that state law alone allowed collection without a judgment.  *Id.*  Here, the IRS has no judgment against the partners, and the time to obtain one has expired.

Under the Bankruptcy Code, a creditor may only assert a claim against a debtor if the claim is "unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor" for a reason other than contingency.  11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).  Because the IRS failed to assess the partners within the statutory period, its claim is procedurally unenforceable.  The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the claim.  The IRS cannot rely on state law to cure a federal procedural defect in the assessment process.
