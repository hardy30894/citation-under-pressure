**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE STATUTE**

A criminal defendant may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge a federal statute on federalism grounds. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) (“[T]he [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment.”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed this principle. *See, e.g., Orr v. Orr*, 440 U.S. 268, 271–72 (1979) (“[T]he Court has consistently refused to allow individuals to raise generalized grievances about the conduct of government.”); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004) (“[P]rivate parties lack standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to federal legislation.”).

Bond’s reliance on dicta in *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703–04 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. The Seventh Circuit’s observation was expressly limited to a facial challenge under the Commerce Clause, not the Tenth Amendment. *Id.* at 704 n.10. Moreover, the weight of authority rejects private standing to assert Tenth Amendment violations. *See, e.g., Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006).

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER**

Congress may enact legislation to implement a valid treaty, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (“If [a] treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is its necessary and proper implementation. *See United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004) (upholding federal prosecution under the Indian Major Crimes Act as a valid exercise of the treaty power).

Bond’s contention that § 229 lacks a federal nexus is irrelevant. *Holland* expressly rejected such a requirement. 252 U.S. at 433 (“It is obvious that there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well-being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could.”). The statute’s nationwide reach is a permissible means of effectuating the treaty’s obligations. *See United States v. Lue*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998) (holding that treaty-implementing legislation need not contain a federal interest element).

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

A statute is void for vagueness only if it fails to provide “fair notice” of the conduct it proscribes. *United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). Section 229 clearly prohibits the knowing use of toxic chemicals as weapons. 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1). Bond’s conduct—repeatedly applying toxic chemicals to another person’s property—falls squarely within its terms. The statute’s scienter requirement ensures that it does not criminalize innocent or accidental conduct. *See United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994) (requiring scienter to avoid vagueness concerns).

**IV. THE STATUTE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE TENTH AMENDMENT**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992). However, the treaty power is an enumerated federal power, and Congress may legislate to implement treaties without infringing on state sovereignty. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432. The statute does not commandeer state governments or commandeer state officials. *Compare Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997) (invalidating commandeering of state officials under the Necessary and Proper Clause).

**CONCLUSION**

Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, and § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power. The judgment below should be affirmed.
