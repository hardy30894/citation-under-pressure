# ARGUMENT

## I. THE COMPETENCY STANDARD FOR STANDING TRIAL IS THE SAME STANDARD APPLICABLE TO PLEADING GUILTY OR WAIVING COUNSEL

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard finds no footing in this Court's precedents and should be rejected.

### A. Dusky Supplies the Universal Competency Standard

In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court held that the test for competency to stand trial is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. This Court has never suggested that a different, higher standard governs particular decisions made during trial. To the contrary, competency to stand trial necessarily encompasses the competency to make the range of decisions that trial entails—including whether to plead guilty and whether to accept the assistance of counsel. A defendant who is competent to stand trial, by definition, is competent to determine how to conduct his defense at that trial.

The Ninth Circuit's holding that "[c]ompetency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than that required to stand trial," 972 F.2d at 265, has no support in this Court's decisions. Its cited authority—*Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny—represents a lower-court gloss unmoored from *Dusky*. Nothing in *Dusky*, *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), or *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), draws the distinction the Ninth Circuit invented.

### B. Waiver of the Right to Counsel Requires Only That the Waiver Be Knowing and Voluntary, Not a Heightened Competency Showing

This Court's waiver jurisprudence separates two distinct inquiries: (1) whether the defendant is competent, and (2) whether the waiver was knowing and voluntary. In *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), this Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to represent himself, requiring only that he be made aware of the "dangers and disadvantages of self-representation" so that the record establishes he "knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open." *Id.* at 835. Faretta himself was competent to stand trial; the *knowing-and-voluntary* inquiry, not a heightened competency inquiry, addressed whether his self-representation waiver was valid.

Similarly, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), held that waiver of counsel must be "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, but did not create a competency standard distinct from fitness to stand trial. The inquiry into whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary is functionally and analytically separate from the inquiry into mental competency. Conflating the two, as the Ninth Circuit did, improperly imports a heightened mental-capacity requirement into what is otherwise an assessment of adequate information and volition.

The same reasoning governs guilty pleas. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), this Court required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and knowing before it may be accepted, given that a plea "is not just a confession... it also is a waiver" of the rights to a jury trial, confrontation, and self-incrimination protection. *Id.* at 243. The *Boykin* Court's concern was information and voluntariness, not a separate, elevated competency threshold.

### C. The Ninth Circuit's "Reasoned Choice" Standard Is Unworkable and Inconsistent With the Structure of Competency Doctrine

The Ninth Circuit derived its "reasoned choice" standard from a line of its own cases—*Sieling*, 478 F.2d 211; *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884 (9th Cir. 1986)—none of which is grounded in a decision of this Court articulating such a standard. The court below acknowledged as much when it characterized competency to waive constitutional rights as demanding a mental capacity beyond that required by *Dusky*. 972 F.2d at 265.

This bifurcated approach produces an unworkable and illogical result: a defendant adjudicated competent to stand trial—and therefore to face the full panoply of decisions inherent in a trial, including complex strategic and evidentiary judgments made through counsel—would nonetheless be deemed incompetent to make the simpler, more discrete decision to plead guilty or to represent himself. If a defendant has, per *Dusky*, "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him," 362 U.S. at 402, and can consult with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding, there is no principled basis for concluding he lacks the capacity to decide whether to continue exercising the right to counsel or to enter a plea.

### D. Due Process Requires Only That Courts Ensure Waivers Are Knowing, Intelligent, and Voluntary—A Separate Inquiry Satisfied Here

The Due Process Clause protects against the conviction of an incompetent defendant, see *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. at 385, and against the involuntary or unknowing waiver of constitutional rights, see *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 242–43. These are the applicable safeguards. Once the *Dusky* competency threshold is met, the constitutional inquiry shifts to whether the waiver itself—of counsel, of trial, of the privilege against self-incrimination—was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary under the totality of circumstances, as required by *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464, and *Faretta*, 422 U.S. at 835.

The record here reflects that the trial court engaged Moran in exactly this inquiry, and the state courts, applying the psychiatric findings that Moran was competent under *Dusky*'s framework, were entitled to rely on those findings. The Ninth Circuit erred by superimposing an atextual, extra-constitutional "reasoned choice" standard onto a competency determination that this Court's precedents do not require.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
