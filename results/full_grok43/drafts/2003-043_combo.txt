ARGUMENT

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment identify the specific taxpayer whose liability is recorded. 26 U.S.C. § 6203. Because each partner is a distinct taxpayer, United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961), an assessment against the partnership alone does not extend the ten-year collection period to the partners. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960); Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959) (“[T]he only reason for naming such individual … is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.”).

This Court has long held that assessment operates as the predicate for collection against the person assessed. Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 260 (1935); Phillips v. Commissioner, 283 U.S. 589, 596-97 (1931). An assessment naming only the partnership therefore leaves the ordinary three-year period applicable to the individual partners. United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489, 494 (1930); Burnet v. Willingham Loan & Trust Co., 282 U.S. 437, 439 (1931).

State-law joint-and-several liability does not dispense with the federal assessment requirement. Lewis v. Reynolds, 284 U.S. 281, 283 (1932). A creditor seeking to reach a partner’s personal assets must first obtain a judgment against that partner. The government’s failure to assess or sue within three years bars both avenues. Helvering v. Mitchell, 303 U.S. 391, 401 (1938); Commissioner v. Sunnen, 333 U.S. 591, 598 (1948).

The same principle appears in earlier decisions requiring precise identification of the assessed party. United States v. Rindskopf, 105 U.S. 418, 422 (1881). Because no timely assessment or suit was made against respondents, the claims are time-barred.
