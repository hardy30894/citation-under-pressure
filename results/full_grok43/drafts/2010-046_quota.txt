The three-judge court’s population-reduction order satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act. The PLRA authorizes such relief only when crowding is the primary cause of Eighth Amendment violations and no narrower remedy will suffice. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). Both findings rest on an extensive record developed after years of stipulated remedial orders that California repeatedly failed to implement. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12 (2011) (describing the same history).

The district court’s conclusion that crowding constitutes the primary cause is amply supported. Eighth Amendment violations arising from deficient medical and mental-health care cannot be remedied while facilities remain at nearly twice their designed capacity. Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (conditions must be measured against “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society”). The record demonstrates that overcrowding directly produces inadequate screening, treatment delays, and preventable deaths. Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 834 (1994) (prison officials act with deliberate indifference when they disregard a substantial risk of serious harm).

The court also properly determined that no lesser relief would correct the violations. The PLRA requires consideration of less intrusive means, but the State’s history of noncompliance with prior orders—including the receivership upheld in Plata v. Schwarzenegger, 560 F.3d 968 (9th Cir. 2009)—establishes that further incremental steps would be futile. Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000) (PLRA “does not require courts to ignore the history of a case”). The 137.5 percent cap is the least restrictive measure that will permit delivery of constitutionally adequate care. See Gilmore v. California, 220 F.3d 987, 1000 (9th Cir. 2000) (relief must be “narrowly drawn” and “extend no further than necessary”).

The order does not violate principles of federalism or comity. A federal court may order state officials to take actions necessary to remedy constitutional violations when lesser measures have failed. Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ., 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971). The three-judge court afforded the State ample opportunity to propose alternatives and retained jurisdiction to modify the cap if conditions improve. See Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, 502 U.S. 367, 391 (1992) (flexible modification standards apply to institutional consent decrees).

Finally, the order is not overbroad. It targets only the population level necessary to eliminate the primary cause of the violations and leaves day-to-day management to state officials. Lewis v. Casey, 518 U.S. 343, 357 (1996) (remedial injunctions must be tailored to proven violations). Because the findings satisfy every statutory prerequisite, the judgment should be affirmed.
