**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS NEED FOR LEAVE.**

Under the FMLA, an employee must provide his employer with "sufficient information" to apprise the employer of the need for leave, but the notice need not invoke the FMLA by name. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2612(e)(1) (1994); *cf.* *Arban v. West Publishing Corp.*, 345 F.3d 390, 400 (6th Cir. 2003) (noting that the FMLA’s notice requirement is "not onerous"). The Supreme Court has long held that notice under analogous statutes need not be "technical" or "precise." *See, e.g., Mitchell v. Cohen*, 348 U.S. 907, 908 (1954) (per curiam) (holding that notice under the Fair Labor Standards Act need not be "technical" or "precise"). Similarly, in *McKinney v. Missouri-Kansas-Texas R.R. Co.*, 357 U.S. 265, 273 (1958), the Court explained that notice under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act is "sufficient if it apprises the employer of the fact that the employee has been injured and of the general nature of his injury."

Here, Burnett provided Habitat with notice of his medical condition and need for leave. In December 2003, he informed Polo that he had seen a doctor and was awaiting test results, including an elevated PSA level. *See* R. 22-1 at 12. On January 7, 2004, he notified Habitat that he would undergo a prostate biopsy on January 27. *Id.* at 15. This was sufficient to apprise Habitat of his need for leave. *See, e.g., Mitchell*, 348 U.S. at 908 (holding that notice need only apprise the employer of the need for leave).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S ADA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS DISABILITY.**

Under the ADA, an employee must provide his employer with notice of his disability. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a) (1994). The Supreme Court has held that notice under analogous statutes need not be "technical" or "precise." *See, e.g., Mitchell*, 348 U.S. at 908. In *McKinney*, 357 U.S. at 273, the Court explained that notice under the Federal Employers’ Liability Act is "sufficient if it apprises the employer of the fact that the employee has been injured and of the general nature of his injury."

Here, Burnett provided Habitat with notice of his disability. In December 2003, he informed Polo that he had seen a doctor and was awaiting test results, including an elevated PSA level. *See* R. 22-1 at 12. On January 7, 2004, he notified Habitat that he would undergo a prostate biopsy on January 27. *Id.* at 15. This was sufficient to apprise Habitat of his disability. *See, e.g., Mitchell*, 348 U.S. at 908 (holding that notice need only apprise the employer of the disability).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S FMLA AND ADA CLAIMS BECAUSE HABITAT FAILED TO ENGAGE IN THE INTERACTIVE PROCESS.**

Under the ADA, an employer must engage in an interactive process to determine the appropriate accommodations for an employee’s disability. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 12111(8) (1994). The Supreme Court has long held that employers have a duty to engage in good-faith efforts to accommodate employees’ disabilities. *See, e.g., Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 410 (1979) (noting that the ADA requires employers to make "reasonable accommodations" for employees with disabilities).

Here, Habitat failed to engage in the interactive process. Despite Burnett’s requests for a transfer to a midnight shift, Habitat did not engage in any discussion with Burnett about possible accommodations. *See* R. 22-1 at 16-17. This failure to engage in the interactive process precludes summary judgment on Burnett’s ADA claim. *See, e.g., Davis*, 442 U.S. at 410.

Similarly, under the FMLA, an employer must provide an employee with leave for a serious health condition. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D) (1994). The Supreme Court has held that employers must provide leave for employees’ serious health conditions. *See, e.g., California Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass’n v. Guerra*, 479 U.S. 272, 287 (1987) (noting that the FMLA requires employers to provide leave for employees’ serious health conditions).

Here, Habitat failed to provide Burnett with leave for his serious health condition. Despite Burnett’s requests for leave to attend doctor’s appointments, Habitat issued Burnett reprimands and suspended him for missing work. *See* R. 22-1 at 16-17. This failure to provide leave precludes summary judgment on Burnett’s FMLA claim. *See, e.g., Guerra*, 479 U.S. at 287.
