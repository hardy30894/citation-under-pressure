**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges only that she was treated arbitrarily and spitefully, without claiming membership in any identifiable class or group. The Clause prohibits discrimination that is based on an impermissible classification, not merely unequal treatment of a single individual.

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Requires Discrimination Against a Class or Group**

From its earliest interpretations, this Court has held that the Equal Protection Clause “does not require that all persons be dealt with identically, but it does require that a classification bearing a reasonable relation to the purpose of the legislation be not arbitrary.” *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885). The Clause is “designed to prevent any person or class of persons from being singled out as a special subject for discriminating and hostile legislation.” *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155 (1897). A plaintiff must show that she has been “subjected to an invidious discrimination” because of her membership in a class. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944). In *Snowden*, this Court rejected an equal protection claim where the plaintiff alleged only that state officials arbitrarily refused to certify his nomination, because he did not “allege that he was a member of a class from which the state action singled him out for discriminatory treatment.” *Id.* at 9. The Clause does not “guarantee against all arbitrary action by the state,” but only against “discrimination that is based on some invidious classification.” *Id.* at 11 (Frankfurter, J., concurring).

**II. The “Class of One” Theory Would Transform the Clause Into a General Guarantee of Rational Government Action**

The Seventh Circuit’s holding—that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective”—would effectively make the Clause a catchall remedy for any arbitrary government conduct. This Court has consistently held that the Clause does not require that all governmental action be rational; it requires only that classifications be reasonable. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911) (“The classification must be reasonable, not arbitrary, and must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation.”). A law that treats one person differently from others without any class-based distinction may be arbitrary, but it does not violate equal protection. *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (upholding regulation that exempted some vehicles from advertising ban because “the classification has relation to the purpose of the ordinance”). The Clause “does not forbid discrimination as such; it forbids only invidious discrimination.” *Williamson v. Lee Optical*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955). An individual’s claim of spiteful treatment, without more, does not allege invidiousness.

**III. The Seventh Circuit’s “Spiteful Effort” Standard Has No Basis in Equal Protection Precedent**

This Court has never recognized a cause of action for a “class of one” based solely on a government official’s ill will. In *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962), the Court held that selective enforcement of a law violates equal protection only if the selection was “deliberately based upon an unjustifiable standard such as race, religion, or other arbitrary classification.” The Court did not suggest that a single instance of arbitrary enforcement, motivated by personal animus, would suffice. Similarly, in *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425 (1961), the Court applied rational-basis review to a Sunday closing law, emphasizing that the “presumption of constitutionality” applies to economic regulations. A “spiteful” motive does not overcome that presumption; the plaintiff must show that the state action was based on an impermissible classification. *Morey v. Doud*, 354 U.S. 457, 463 (1957) (striking down classification that singled out one company, but only because the classification was “wholly arbitrary” and lacked a rational basis). The “class of one” theory would allow any disgruntled citizen to challenge routine administrative decisions, flooding courts with claims that the Clause was never intended to address.

**IV. The Proper Remedy for Arbitrary Individual Treatment Lies in State Tort Law or Due Process**

The Constitution does not guarantee that every government action be free from spite or malice. The Due Process Clause provides a remedy for arbitrary deprivations of life, liberty, or property, but the Equal Protection Clause addresses only discriminatory classifications. *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940) (“The equal protection clause does not take from the States the power to classify, but it does take from them the power to classify arbitrarily.”). A plaintiff who alleges that a municipality demanded an easement out of personal animus may have a claim under state tort law for abuse of process or malicious prosecution, but not under the Equal Protection Clause. *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners*, 330 U.S. 552, 556 (1947) (upholding classification based on nepotism because it was not “wholly arbitrary”). The Clause is not a “general insurance against arbitrary official action.” *Hartford Steam Boiler Inspection & Insurance Co. v. Harrison*, 301 U.S. 459, 462 (1937).

**Conclusion**

Because Grace Olech did not allege that she was a member of any class or group, her claim does not state an equal protection violation. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
