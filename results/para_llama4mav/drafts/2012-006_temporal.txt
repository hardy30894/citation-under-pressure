## Argument

The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. Withdrawing from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period does not shift this burden to the defendant.

The Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. In conspiracy cases, this includes proving that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government must prove the defendant's participation in the conspiracy within the limitations period (See Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946); Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 396-397 (1957)).

In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court held that the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member of it during that time. The Court emphasized that "the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the conspiracy is complete, and that the conspiracy is not complete until its purpose is accomplished or abandoned" (Id. at 369-370).

The burden of proof remains with the government to establish the defendant's membership in the conspiracy during the relevant period. The government's failure to meet this burden cannot be cured by the defendant's failure to prove withdrawal from the conspiracy.

In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court reversed the convictions of several defendants because the government failed to prove that they were part of a single conspiracy. The Court held that the government's evidence was insufficient to establish that the defendants were part of a single conspiracy, and that the variance between the indictment and the proof was prejudicial (Id. at 773-774).

Similarly, in the present case, the government failed to prove that the defendants were part of a single conspiracy that continued into the relevant statute of limitations period. The fact that the leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the relevant period does not necessarily mean that the conspiracy ended. However, it is the government's burden to prove that the conspiracy continued and that the defendants were members of it during the relevant period.

The D.C. Circuit erred in affirming the convictions because it failed to properly allocate the burden of proof. The government, not the defendants, bears the burden of proving the defendants' membership in the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period.

## Conclusion

The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed because the government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants were members of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The burden of proof remains with the government, and the defendants are entitled to a new trial with proper instructions on the burden of proof.
