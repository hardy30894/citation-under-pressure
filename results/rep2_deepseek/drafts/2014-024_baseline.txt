**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below must be reversed because 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat. The Third Circuit’s objective “reasonable speaker” standard criminalizes speech that the speaker never intended as a threat, violating both the statute’s text and the First Amendment.

**I. The Text and Common Law of § 875(c) Demand Subjective Intent**

Section 875(c) punishes anyone who “transmits in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat.” The word “threat” is a term of art that, at common law, required the speaker to intend his words to be taken as a serious expression of purpose to inflict harm. *See Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (holding that “political hyperbole” is not a threat because the speaker did not intend to communicate a serious threat). The statute’s silence on mens rea does not eliminate this requirement; “the existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence.” *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 436 (1978). Because § 875(c) is a general intent crime, the government must prove that the defendant acted with “knowledge of the facts that constitute the offense.” *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994). A speaker cannot “knowingly” transmit a “threat” unless he understands that his communication is threatening. The objective standard—which asks only what a reasonable speaker would foresee—dispenses with this knowledge requirement and criminalizes innocent conduct.

**II. *Virginia v. Black* Mandates a Subjective Intent Standard**

In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), this Court defined “true threats” as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The phrase “means to communicate” unambiguously requires a subjective intent to threaten. The Court explained that the true-threats exception to the First Amendment is “a specific category of speech” that is “not protected” because it “encompasses those statements where the speaker *intends* to place the victim in fear of bodily harm or death.” *Id.* at 359-60 (emphasis added). The Third Circuit erred by reading *Black* as merely adding an “intent to intimidate” element to cross-burning cases, rather than as announcing a general subjective-intent requirement for all true threats. *Black*’s reasoning applies equally to § 875(c): without proof that the defendant intended his words to be threatening, the statute sweeps in protected speech—such as rap lyrics, political hyperbole, or emotional outbursts—that the speaker never meant as a threat.

**III. The Objective Standard Chills Protected Speech and Violates the First Amendment**

The First Amendment “demands that the government prove that the speaker intended to communicate a threat.” *United States v. Cassel*, 408 F.3d 622, 633 (9th Cir. 2005) (citing *Black*). An objective standard, by contrast, punishes speech based on how a reasonable listener might interpret it, regardless of the speaker’s intent. This “chills a substantial amount of protected speech” because speakers cannot know in advance whether their words will be deemed threatening by a jury. *Id.* at 631. As this Court recognized in *Watts*, even “vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp attacks” are protected unless the speaker intends them as threats. 394 U.S. at 708. The Third Circuit’s standard would criminalize the lyrics of Eminem, the rants of a distraught spouse, or the dark humor of a comedian—all of which lack the subjective intent to threaten. The Constitution does not permit such overbreadth.

**IV. The Government’s Policy Concerns Do Not Justify Diluting Mens Rea**

The government argues that a subjective-intent requirement would make it too difficult to prosecute threats, because defendants will claim they were joking or engaging in artistic expression. But this Court has consistently rejected such utilitarian arguments when they undermine fundamental mens rea protections. *See, e.g., Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723, 740 (2015) (holding that negligence is insufficient for § 875(c) because “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal”). The government can still prove subjective intent through circumstantial evidence—the context, the speaker’s history, the nature of the communication, and the reaction of the recipient. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 367-68. What the government cannot do is dispense with intent altogether and convict based on a reasonable-person standard that turns on the vagaries of listener perception.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury must find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
