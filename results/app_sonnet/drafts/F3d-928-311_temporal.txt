**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sentence-Enhancing Statutes Must Be Construed Strictly and in Furlow's Favor**

Both the ACCA and the career-offender provision impose enhanced punishment beyond that otherwise authorized by Congress for the offense of conviction. Where, as here, the government seeks to invoke such enhancements, the settled canon of strict construction governs. As Chief Justice Marshall explained nearly two centuries ago, "[t]he rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself," for "it is the legislature, not the Court, which is to define a crime, and ordain its punishment." *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). That principle has never been abandoned. Where the reach of a penal statute is genuinely uncertain, "the ambiguity should be resolved in favor of lenity." *Ladner v. United States*, 358 U.S. 169, 178 (1958). This Court should therefore resolve any doubt about whether Furlow's prior convictions fall within the ACCA's and the Guidelines' enumerated categories against the government and in favor of the defendant. *See Bell v. United States*, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955) (the Court's task is "to determine what Congress meant by the words it used, and, if that meaning is not clear, to resolve doubts in favor of the defendant").

**II. Furlow's South Carolina Conviction for Distribution of Crack Cocaine Is Not a Proper "Serious Drug Offense" or "Controlled Substance Offense"**

Congress did not intend the ACCA's "serious drug offense" definition, or the Guidelines' parallel "controlled substance offense" definition, to depend upon the idiosyncratic labels a State chooses to attach to its criminal statutes. Where Congress uses a term of art in a federal enhancement provision, courts must determine whether the prior state offense's elements match the federal, generic definition of that term — not simply accept the state label. This principle is not new. In interpreting the word "stolen" in the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act, the Supreme Court held that Congress intended the term to carry its "generic" meaning, independent of the common-law technicalities that individual States might employ, because "in the absence of a plain indication of a contrary purpose," federal statutes are not read to make their application "dependent on state law." *United States v. Turley*, 352 U.S. 407, 411 (1957); *see also Jerome v. United States*, 318 U.S. 101, 104 (1943) ("federal statutes are generally intended to have uniform nationwide application"). Similarly, in construing "extortion" under the Travel Act, the Court refused to let the outcome "turn on the label" that a particular State happened to use for its offense, and instead required that the state offense's elements correspond to the generic definition of the enumerated federal term. *United States v. Nardello*, 393 U.S. 286, 293–95 (1969).

The same method applies here. The South Carolina distribution statute under which Furlow was convicted reaches conduct broader than the "distributing" or "possessing with intent to ... distribute" language that Congress used in 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii) and that the Sentencing Commission used in USSG § 4B1.2(b). Where a state statute's elements sweep more broadly than the generic federal offense — for example, by criminalizing an offer or solicitation to sell rather than an actual distribution or possession with intent to distribute — the conviction cannot categorically qualify as a predicate, regardless of the facts underlying Furlow's particular case. Under *Wiltberger* and *Ladner*, any doubt on this score must be resolved against expanding Furlow's sentence beyond what Congress plainly authorized. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1) is unambiguous that the predicate must be "an offense ... involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute" — nothing broader — and this Court should not stretch that language merely because South Carolina has chosen to punish additional, non-generic conduct under the same statutory label.

**III. Furlow's Georgia Conviction for First-Degree Arson Does Not Match the Generic Definition of "Arson" Enumerated in the ACCA and the Guidelines**

The same generic-offense methodology governs the enumerated-offense clause. Both 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii) and USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2) list "arson" without further definition, which means this Court must ask whether the elements of the Georgia offense correspond to the generic, contemporary meaning of arson as Congress and the Commission understood that term — not whether Georgia simply affixed the word "arson" to its statute. *Turley*, 352 U.S. at 411; *Nardello*, 393 U.S. at 293–95. Congress cannot be presumed to have delegated to the Georgia legislature the power to define, through its own local statute, what conduct triggers a mandatory federal fifteen-year minimum sentence. *See Jerome*, 318 U.S. at 104.

The record does not establish that Georgia's first-degree arson offense requires the elements traditionally associated with the generic crime — namely, the malicious burning of a dwelling or occupied structure. If the Georgia statute instead permits conviction on a broader range of conduct, such as reckless or non-malicious burning, or the burning of property other than a dwelling, the conviction is not categorically "arson" within the generic meaning Congress intended to enumerate. Because that threshold question remains unresolved on this record, and because penal enhancement statutes must be read narrowly, *Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. at 95; *Ladner*, 358 U.S. at 178, the government has not carried its burden of establishing that this conviction is a proper ACCA or Guidelines predicate.

**IV. Conclusion**

Because neither the South Carolina drug conviction nor the Georgia arson conviction has been shown to satisfy the generic definitions Congress and the Sentencing Commission intended, and because any doubt must be resolved in Furlow's favor, this Court should vacate the sentence and remand for resentencing without the ACCA and career-offender enhancements.
