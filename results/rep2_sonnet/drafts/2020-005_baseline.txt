# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Permits Specific Jurisdiction Whenever a Claim "Arises Out of *or* Relates To" a Defendant's Forum Contacts, and Ford's Contacts with Montana Satisfy That Standard.

This Court has never held that a defendant's forum contacts must be the mechanical *cause* of a plaintiff's injury before specific jurisdiction may attach. In *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), the Court asked only whether the defendant maintained contacts of such a nature that maintaining the suit does not offend "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice," *id.* at 316, and whether the "obligation which is sued upon" has a connection to activities within the forum, *id.* at 319. The Court has repeatedly restated the test disjunctively: jurisdiction is proper when the litigation "results from alleged injuries that 'arise out of or relate to' those activities." *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985) (quoting *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984)). Even in *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), which rejected jurisdiction over out-of-state plaintiffs' claims with no forum connection at all, the Court preserved and applied the same two-part formulation, requiring only "a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue," *id.* at 1781—precisely the connection the Montana Supreme Court found here.

Ford's contacts with Montana are not incidental. Ford advertises, markets, and sells Ford Explorers—the very model at issue—throughout Montana; it maintains dealer networks that sell and service Explorers; and it solicits an ongoing stream of Montana consumers to purchase, finance, and repair its vehicles in the State. These are the "continuous and systematic" commercial activities that *International Shoe* identified as sufficient to support jurisdiction over claims connected to those activities. 326 U.S. at 320. Ford has thus purposefully availed itself of "the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State," *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958), and has intentionally cultivated a Montana market for the exact product that caused Ms. Gullett's death. Under *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286 (1980), a defendant that "delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State" may fairly be sued there, *id.* at 297–98. Ford's expectation was not speculative—it built a market for Explorers in Montana through direct sales, advertising, and authorized dealer servicing.

## II. This Case Does Not Present a *Walden*-Style Absence of Forum Connection; Ford's Own Purposeful Conduct Links It to Montana.

Petitioner's reliance on cases limiting jurisdiction based on a plaintiff's unilateral contacts misapprehends the record. In *Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277 (2014), the defendant's only "contact" with the forum was the plaintiffs' own residence there; the defendant himself had done nothing to create a connection with Nevada. *Id.* at 289–91 ("the plaintiff cannot be the only link between the defendant and the forum"). Here, by contrast, Ford—not Gullett—created the Montana connection: Ford chose to advertise, sell, and service Explorers throughout the State, cultivating exactly the sort of ongoing commercial relationship that *Walden* distinguished from a plaintiff's unilateral forum contacts. *Id.* at 284. Nor is this a case, as in *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873 (2011), where a foreign manufacturer's product reached the forum through the independent acts of a third party with no showing that the manufacturer "purposefully avail[ed] itself of the market" in that State, *id.* at 882 (opinion of Kennedy, J.). Ford does not merely place vehicles into a national stream of commerce and hope they wash up in Montana—it deliberately markets, sells, and services this vehicle model there, satisfying even the narrower purposeful-availment formulations discussed in *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987) (plurality op.).

## III. Exercising Jurisdiction Comports with Fair Play and Substantial Justice.

Once minimum contacts are shown, jurisdiction may be defeated only by a "compelling case" that other considerations render it unreasonable. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 477. No such showing exists here. Montana has a manifest interest in "providing effective means of redress for its residents" injured within its borders, *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 776 (1984), and in adjudicating a products-liability action arising from a crash on a Montana highway involving a Montana decedent. Ford, a sophisticated multinational manufacturer that markets vehicles nationwide and specifically in Montana, faces no unfair burden in defending a suit in a State where it has systematically sought out business for the very product line at issue.

## CONCLUSION

Because Ford's purposeful, continuous contacts with Montana—advertising, selling, and servicing the Explorer model that injured Markkaya Gullett—relate directly to Respondent's claims, the Montana Supreme Court correctly exercised specific personal jurisdiction consistent with *International Shoe* and its progeny. The judgment below should be affirmed.
