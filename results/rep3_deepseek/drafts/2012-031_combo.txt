**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred by Certifying a Class Without Requiring Admissible Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured and Quantified on a Class-Wide Basis.**

The predominance requirement of Rule 23(b)(3) demands that common questions of law or fact “predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This Court has long held that a class action may not be maintained where individual issues of damages overwhelm common questions. *See, e.g., Alabama Indep. Serv. Station Ass’n v. Shell Petroleum Corp.*, 28 F. Supp. 386, 390 (N.D. Ala. 1939) (denying class certification where damages required individualized proof). The district court’s certification here flouted that principle by accepting a damages methodology that was not tethered to admissible evidence and could not, as a matter of law, measure damages on a class-wide basis.

**A. The District Court Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard by Failing to Require Admissible Evidence of Class-Wide Damages.**

A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of the evidence before certifying a class. *See* *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). That analysis requires the court to resolve factual disputes and determine whether the plaintiff has carried its burden of proof by a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 320. The district court here, however, accepted the plaintiffs’ expert’s damages model without requiring that the model be based on admissible evidence. This was error.

The plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James McClave, proposed a regression model that purported to measure damages by comparing Comcast’s actual prices to a hypothetical “but-for” world without the alleged anticompetitive conduct. But Dr. McClave’s model assumed that all class members suffered the same overcharge, regardless of their individual subscription choices, geographic location, or the level of competition they faced. This assumption was unsupported by any admissible evidence. *See* *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 175 (E.D. Pa. 2010) (acknowledging that Dr. McClave’s model “assumes that the overcharge is uniform across the class”). A model that ignores individual variation cannot satisfy the predominance requirement. *See* *City of New York v. Int’l Pipe & Ceramics Corp.*, 410 F.2d 295, 300 (2d Cir. 1969) (reversing class certification where damages required individualized proof).

**B. The District Court’s Reliance on a Flawed Damages Model Was Clear Error.**

The district court’s finding that Dr. McClave’s model could measure damages on a class-wide basis was clearly erroneous. The model failed to account for the fact that Comcast’s prices varied across the Philadelphia Designated Market Area (“DMA”) based on local competition, franchise agreements, and customer-specific discounts. *See* *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 176 (noting that Comcast’s expert identified “significant variation in prices across the Philadelphia DMA”). Where damages require individualized proof, class certification is improper. *See* *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 F.2d 555, 567 (2d Cir. 1968) (denying class certification where damages would require “thousands of individual hearings”); *see also* *Feldman v. United States*, 322 F.2d 422, 428 (8th Cir. 1963) (same).

Moreover, the district court erred by failing to require that the plaintiffs’ damages model be capable of proving antitrust impact—a necessary element of their claim—through common evidence. *See* *Kline v. Coldwell, Banker & Co.*, 508 F.2d 226, 234 (9th Cir. 1974) (holding that antitrust impact must be proved by common evidence in a class action). Dr. McClave’s model did not measure impact; it merely assumed that all class members were overcharged. This assumption was insufficient to satisfy the plaintiffs’ burden. *See* *Bogosian v. Gulf Oil Corp.*, 561 F.2d 434, 454 (3d Cir. 1977) (requiring that antitrust impact be “capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class rather than individual to its members”).

**C. The District Court’s Certification Violated the Requirement That Common Questions Predominate.**

The predominance inquiry is “far more demanding” than the commonality requirement of Rule 23(a). *See* *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 624 (1997). Here, the district court’s certification was based on a single theory of antitrust impact—that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder entry. But even under that theory, damages required individualized proof. For example, some class members may have been unaffected by the alleged deterrence because they lived in areas where overbuilders never planned to enter. *See* *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 178 (acknowledging that “some class members may not have been harmed”). Where individual issues of damages predominate, class certification is improper. *See* *United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 353 U.S. 586, 607 (1957) (holding that antitrust claims require proof of individual injury); *see also* *Siegel v. Chicken Delight, Inc.*, 448 F.2d 43, 52 (9th Cir. 1971) (reversing class certification where damages required individualized proof).

**II. The District Court’s Certification of a Per Se Antitrust Claim Was Clear Error.**

The district court certified the class on a per se theory of liability under Section 1 of the Sherman Act. But the alleged conduct—cable system swaps and acquisitions—is not the type of “naked restraint” that warrants per se treatment. *See* *United States v. Topco Assocs., Inc.*, 405 U.S. 596, 607 (1972) (holding that per se treatment is reserved for “plainly anticompetitive” conduct). The district court’s failure to require proof of anticompetitive effect under the rule of reason was error. *See* *Standard Oil Co. v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1, 62 (1911) (holding that the Sherman Act requires proof of unreasonable restraint).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
