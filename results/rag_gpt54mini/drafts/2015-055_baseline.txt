**Argument**

Johnson announced a new substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review. Under *Teague v. Lane*, new substantive rules “generally apply retroactively,” while new procedural rules do not. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). A rule is substantive if it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Id.* at 353. Johnson fits that definition because it narrowed the class of offenders subject to the ACCA’s enhanced punishment by invalidating the residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). Before Johnson, a defendant could receive ACCA’s fifteen-year minimum if his prior conviction “otherwise involve[d] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” After Johnson, that clause cannot be used at all. That is a classic substantive change. See *Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1265 (2016).

This Court’s retroactivity precedents compel that conclusion. In *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620–21 (1998), the Court held that decisions narrowing a criminal statute’s scope are substantive and therefore retroactive. Likewise, in *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351–52, the Court explained that substantive rules “include decisions that narrow the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms.” Johnson did exactly that. It did not merely regulate how courts decide whether a prior conviction qualifies under ACCA; it struck down one of ACCA’s operative definitions of “violent felony.” Once the residual clause fell, the government could no longer rely on it to increase punishment. That “necessarily carrie[d] a significant risk that a defendant” sentenced under the clause “faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him.” *Id.* at 352.

The government may argue Johnson is merely procedural because it changed the method courts use to classify prior convictions. But this Court has rejected that formalism. In *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 200–01 (2016), the Court reiterated that substantive rules apply retroactively because they place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe” or forbid “a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense.” Johnson did the latter: it removed ACCA’s enhanced mandatory minimum from a class of prisoners whose only basis for enhancement was the residual clause. That is substantive even though courts must apply it by reviewing prior convictions.

Nor does *Tyler v. Cain*, 533 U.S. 656 (2001), help the government. Tyler addressed when this Court has “made” a rule retroactive for purposes of second or successive habeas petitions under 28 U.S.C. § 2244(b)(2)(A). *Id.* at 663–64. It did not alter the substantive/procedural distinction governing ordinary retroactivity on initial collateral review. And the retroactivity analysis under *Teague* turns on whether the rule is substantive, not whether it was previously announced in a collateral case. *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 198–99.

Johnson is also unlike the procedural rules the Court has declined to apply retroactively. In *Teague*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989), the Court explained that procedural rules “merely raise the possibility that someone convicted with use of the invalidated procedure might have been acquitted otherwise.” Johnson did not merely create a new procedure for identifying violent felonies; it eliminated punishment authorized only by the residual clause. That is why the rule in Johnson is more like the statutory-interpretation decisions in *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620–21, than the trial-procedure rule in *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353–54.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary approach cannot stand. A rule is substantive when it “necessarily carry[ies] a significant risk that a defendant ... faces a punishment that the law cannot impose.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 352. Johnson did exactly that for prisoners sentenced under ACCA’s residual clause. Because Welch’s fifteen-year sentence was enhanced under a clause that Johnson invalidated, his sentence exceeds the punishment the law authorizes unless another ACCA predicate independently supports the enhancement. See *Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1265 (2016).

The judgment below should be reversed.
