**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF THE MENTALLY RETARDED**

The State respectfully submits that the execution of a mentally retarded individual is not categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The controlling precedent in this Circuit is *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989). In *Penry*, the Supreme Court explicitly rejected the argument that the execution of the mentally retarded is per se unconstitutional. The Court held that "the Eighth Amendment does not bar the execution of a mentally retarded person." *Penry* established that the Constitution does not require a special jury instruction on mental retardation, and that the consideration of such mitigating evidence is sufficient to satisfy constitutional requirements.

**II. MENTAL RETARDATION IS DISTINGUISHABLE FROM INSANITY**

The State further argues that the Eighth Amendment distinguishes between the insane and the mentally retarded. In *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), the Supreme Court held that the execution of the insane is prohibited because they lack a rational understanding of the punishment they face. However, mental retardation is not synonymous with insanity. The Court in *Ford* noted that the prohibition on executing the insane "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." The State contends that the execution of the mentally retarded does not violate these evolving standards, as the mentally retarded retain the rational understanding necessary to appreciate the nature and consequences of their actions.

**III. THE JURY WAS PROPERLY INSTRUCTED TO CONSIDER MITIGATING EVIDENCE**

Finally, the State argues that the jury was properly instructed to consider Atkins' mental retardation as a mitigating factor. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586 (1978), requires that the sentencer consider any mitigating evidence. *Penry* addressed this exact issue, holding that the Eighth Amendment does not require a special jury instruction on mental retardation, and that the consideration of such evidence is sufficient to satisfy constitutional requirements. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* by allowing the jury to consider Atkins' mental retardation as a mitigating circumstance. Therefore, the execution of Atkins does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment.
