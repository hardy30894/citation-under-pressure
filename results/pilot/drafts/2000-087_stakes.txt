**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001), the Court held that *§ 1231(a)(6)* of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not authorize indefinite detention. Rather, the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas has been detained for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. No country will accept him. Germany and Lithuania have both rejected his claims to citizenship. No repatriation treaty exists with any nation capable of receiving him. The INS has made no meaningful effort to locate a third country willing to accept him. Under these circumstances, the continued detention of Zadvydas is not reasonably necessary to secure removal. It is, instead, a form of indefinite, punitive confinement—precisely the kind of detention the Constitution forbids.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that the statute’s reach is not unlimited. The Court rejected the notion that Congress intended to permit “indefinite detention” of removable aliens. Id. at 2498. It emphasized that “the statute, read in light of the Constitution’s demands, limits an alien’s post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien’s removal.” Id. The Court further explained that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. This is not a mere suggestion—it is a constitutional mandate.

The Court also established a framework for judicial review. It held that “the habeas court must ask whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” Id. at 2504. The court must measure reasonableness “primarily in terms of the statute’s basic purpose, namely assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” Id. If removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court must conclude that continued detention is unreasonable and no longer authorized. Id.

Here, the district court correctly applied this standard. It found that Zadvydas “will never be deported because there is no place to send him.” *Zadvydas v. Caplinger*, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997). That finding, while not explicitly labeled “reasonably foreseeable,” necessarily implies that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized that “the government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut” a showing that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. 533 U.S. at 2505. The INS has offered no such evidence. It has not identified any country willing to accept Zadvydas. It has not demonstrated any ongoing, good-faith efforts to secure removal. It has not even attempted to pursue his Lithuanian citizenship application, which remains “apparently still pending,” as the Court noted. Id. at 2496.

The Fifth Circuit’s initial opinion, which upheld Zadvydas’s detention, relied on a standard that the Supreme Court explicitly rejected: that the government need only show “good faith efforts” and that the alien must prove “impossibility” of removal. 185 F.3d at 294. The Supreme Court made clear that this standard “would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—[which] demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” 533 U.S. at 2505. The burden is not on the alien to prove impossibility. It is on the government to show that removal remains reasonably foreseeable. The government has failed to meet that burden.

The Court also rejected the idea that detention is justified simply because an alien is removable. Id. at 2499. The Court emphasized that the statute applies broadly to “aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations.” Id. This underscores that the government cannot use the machinery of removal to justify indefinite confinement for non-dangerous, non-flight-risk individuals. The Court noted that “the choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large’... It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” Id. at 2501. That is precisely the remedy the district court ordered: supervised release with conditions, including regular reporting, employment, and a bond. These conditions are reasonable, enforceable, and consistent with the Court’s directive.

The Fifth Circuit’s subsequent reversal of its prior opinion, in light of *Zadvydas*, was correct. It recognized that Zadvydas had “provided good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future,” and that the INS had not rebutted that showing. The court properly concluded that continued detention was no longer authorized by statute. The modification it permitted—allowing the INS to seek re-detention if removal becomes reasonably foreseeable—complies with the Court’s instruction that “the alien may be returned to custody upon a violation of those conditions.” 533 U.S. at 2504.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court must be affirmed. The Constitution does not permit the government to detain an individual indefinitely when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. The post-removal-period statute does not authorize such detention. The government has not met its burden of showing that removal remains reasonably foreseeable. The district court’s order for supervised release is consistent with the Constitution and with this Court’s holding in *Zadvydas v. Davis*. The judgment below should be affirmed.
