**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act, as amended in 1984, does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, nor does it permit an impermissible delegation to the Drug Enforcement Administration. The challenged provisions of 21 U.S.C. § 811(h) satisfy the constitutional requirements of the non-delegation doctrine, which demands only that Congress establish an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. This standard has been consistently upheld by this Court for nearly a century, and the statute before us meets it with precision.

**I. Congress Has Established an Intelligible Principle for Temporary Scheduling**

The non-delegation doctrine, rooted in Article I, § 1 of the Constitution, does not prohibit all delegations of legislative power. Rather, it forbids Congress from surrendering its legislative function to another branch without providing a clear standard to govern the exercise of that power. As this Court held in *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), “Congress may delegate authority to an administrative officer, provided it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the officer is directed to conform.” This principle is not a rigid requirement of detailed regulation, but a general standard that channels discretion and prevents arbitrary action.

In *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), the Court emphasized that “the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution” require that Congress not abdicate its legislative role. Yet the Court has long recognized that Congress may delegate authority to coordinate branches when the delegation is guided by a clear policy objective. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 519 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of the Interior to regulate forest reserves, noting that “the power to make rules and regulations for the government of the public lands is not a legislative power in the sense of the Constitution, but a delegated administrative power.” The Court found that Congress had provided sufficient guidance through the mandate to “preserve the forests and their products,” a standard that was “intelligible” and “sufficiently definite” to prevent arbitrary action.

Similarly, in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 265 U.S. 652, 666 (1924), the Court upheld a delegation to the Interstate Commerce Commission to regulate interstate trucking, stating that “the power to regulate commerce among the several States is a legislative power, but it may be exercised by administrative officers under standards laid down by Congress.” The Court rejected the argument that the delegation was invalid because the standards were “general,” observing that “the Constitution does not require that the standards be so specific as to leave no room for administrative discretion.”

The temporary scheduling provision of § 811(h) is no different. Congress has prescribed a clear and intelligible principle: the Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only if it poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety.” This standard is not vague or amorphous. It is grounded in the same factors that govern permanent scheduling—abuse potential, medical use, public health risk, and pattern of abuse—set forth in § 811(c). These factors are not mere suggestions; they are mandatory criteria that must be considered before any scheduling action is taken. The Attorney General is not free to act on whim or political whim. He must evaluate the substance in light of these well-established, objective standards.

Moreover, the statute requires notice and comment procedures under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 553, and mandates that the Secretary of Health and Human Services provide a binding evaluation if he opposes scheduling. These procedural safeguards further ensure that the Attorney General’s discretion is not unfettered. As this Court held in *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 88 (1921), “the mere fact that a statute confers discretion upon an administrative officer does not render it unconstitutional, if the discretion is limited by standards which are reasonably clear and definite.”

**II. The Attorney General May Lawfully Subdelegate Authority to the DEA**

The question of whether the Attorney General may delegate his authority to the DEA is governed by settled precedent. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 265 U.S. 652, 666 (1924), the Court held that “Congress may authorize the exercise of legislative power by administrative officers, provided the standards are sufficiently definite.” The Court further recognized that “the power to administer a statute may be delegated to subordinate officers,” so long as the delegation is consistent with the legislative intent and the standards are not abandoned.

In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 519 (1911), the Court upheld the delegation of authority to the Secretary of the Interior to issue regulations governing forest reserves, noting that “the Secretary is not a mere agent, but an officer charged with the duty of administering the law.” The Court emphasized that “the power to make rules and regulations for the government of the public lands is not a legislative power in the sense of the Constitution, but a delegated administrative power.” This principle applies with equal force here: the Attorney General, as the head of the executive branch, may assign the performance of his statutory duties to subordinate officers, including the DEA Administrator, so long as the standards are preserved.

The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which delegates the functions of the Attorney General under the CSA to the DEA Administrator, is consistent with this principle. The regulation does not expand the scope of the authority; it merely assigns the operational responsibility for implementing the statutory scheme. The DEA Administrator acts under the Attorney General’s supervision and is bound by the same statutory standards. As this Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “the power to administer a statute may be delegated to subordinate officers, provided the standards are not abandoned and the delegation is not so broad as to amount to a surrender of legislative power.”
