# ARGUMENT

**A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be understood as a threat.**

## I. The Presumption of Scienter Requires This Court to Read a Subjective Intent Element Into Section 875(c).

This Court has long held that criminal statutes silent on mens rea are not to be read as dispensing with intent altogether. In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), the Court explained that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion" but is instead "as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." *Id.* at 250. The Court refused to read the statute before it as eliminating the traditional requirement that a crime be "generally constituted only from concurrence of an evil-meaning mind with an evil-doing hand." *Id.* at 251. Section 875(c) contains no textual signal that Congress meant to abandon this settled presumption, and nothing in its history suggests Congress intended to create the kind of narrow public-welfare offense recognized in *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922), where liability without fault was tolerated only because the regulated conduct posed inherent dangers to public health and safety and carried modest penalties. Threatening speech, transmitted through ordinary channels of communication and punishable by years of imprisonment, is nothing like the strict-liability regulatory offenses at issue in *Balint*.

Where a criminal statute is ambiguous as to intent, this Court has not hesitated to construe it to require a heightened mental state, particularly where a lesser standard would sweep in constitutionally protected conduct. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court construed the word "willfully" in a federal civil-rights statute to require "a specific intent" rather than mere generalized bad conduct, precisely to avoid constitutional infirmity that would otherwise attend a broader reading. *Id.* at 101–04. The same interpretive course is required here: Section 875(c) should be read to require that the defendant know, or specifically intend, that his statement will be understood as a true threat.

## II. This Court's True-Threat Jurisprudence Confirms That Subjective Intent Is the Touchstone.

In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), this Court reversed a conviction for threatening the President, holding that the government must distinguish a "true threat" from protected expression, and that "the language of the political arena, like the language used in labor disputes . . . is often vituperative, abusive, and inexact." *Id.* at 708. The Court examined not merely how a reasonable listener might perceive Watts's statement, but the actual character of the statement and the surrounding reaction, concluding it amounted only to "a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition to the President." *Id.* The Third Circuit's purely objective "reasonable speaker" test, rooted in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), cannot be squared with *Watts*'s insistence that courts look to what the speech actually communicated and was meant to communicate, not merely how an outside observer might construe isolated words torn from context.

## III. An Objective Standard Impermissibly Chills Protected Speech.

The First Amendment does not tolerate criminal liability premised on negligent misunderstanding of one's own words. In *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147 (1959), this Court struck down an obscenity ordinance that dispensed with scienter, explaining that liability without knowledge would cause distributors to "restrict the books they sell" to only the most anodyne material, thereby imposing "a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter." *Id.* at 153. The same chilling dynamic threatens speakers under an objective standard for Section 875(c): a speaker who does not intend to threaten, but fears a jury might later deem his words objectively threatening, will self-censor emotionally charged, hyperbolic, or artistic expression—precisely the kind of expression this Court protected in *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1 (1949), where it held that speech "may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger." *Id.* at 4.

This Court has likewise cautioned that statutes touching expression must be construed with precision because "the objectionable quality of vagueness and overbreadth" in the First Amendment context creates a "danger of tolerating . . . a penal statute susceptible of sweeping and improper application." *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Only "well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech" fall outside constitutional protection, *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–72 (1942), and true threats are properly excluded only when the speaker himself means to threaten—not whenever a jury, viewing words in hindsight, believes a reasonable person might have taken fright.

Finally, due process itself resists criminal punishment untethered from personal culpability. As this Court observed in *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225 (1957), "engrained in our concept of due process is the requirement of notice," *id.* at 228, and punishment for conduct the defendant did not know was wrongful offends that principle. Elonis's Facebook posts—rap-style, hyperbolic, and addressed to no one—cannot support a felony conviction absent proof that he subjectively intended them as threats.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
