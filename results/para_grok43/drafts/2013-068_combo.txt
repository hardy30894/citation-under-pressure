The Massachusetts statute is a content-neutral regulation of the place and manner of expressive activity on public sidewalks. Such measures have long been sustained when they serve significant governmental interests in public safety and the orderly use of streets without suppressing speech. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (recognizing “the authority of a municipality to impose regulations in order to assure the safety and convenience of the people in the use of public highways”); Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949) (upholding restrictions on sound amplification that left “other methods of communication” open).

The fixed buffer zones advance the State’s interest in unobstructed access to medical facilities and the prevention of congestion. Regulations of this character have been upheld even when they incidentally limit the preferred methods of sidewalk advocacy. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (noting that the State may “regulate the times, places and manner of” solicitation “in the interest of public safety”); Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942) (affirming that “fighting words” and conduct that invades privacy or obstructs passage may be proscribed).

The exemptions for clinic employees and emergency personnel do not render the statute viewpoint-based; they are tailored to the distinct functions those persons perform inside the regulated area. Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 99 (1940) (distinguishing valid place restrictions from those that “picket or patrol” in a manner that blocks ingress). Petitioners retain ample alternative channels, including signs, literature, amplified speech from outside the zone, and one-on-one conversation at its perimeter. Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953) (sustaining a permit system that left “other methods of reaching the public” available); Breard v. Alexandria, 341 U.S. 622, 631 (1951) (upholding a ban on door-to-door solicitation that preserved “other means of communication”).

Historical precedent confirms that narrowly drawn spatial restrictions on public ways do not abridge the freedom of speech when they leave open “the opportunity to disseminate” ideas. Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966) (rejecting a claim that exclusion from a nonpublic portion of jail grounds violated the First Amendment); Cameron v. Johnson, 390 U.S. 611, 617 (1968) (sustaining an anti-picketing statute aimed at preventing obstruction of courthouse entrances). Because the Act is “narrowly drawn” to address documented problems of blockage and harassment, it satisfies the requirements long applied to time, place, and manner rules. Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 293 (1951).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
