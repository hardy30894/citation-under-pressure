# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Exemption 2 Limits Its Reach to Agency Personnel Rules and Practices, and the Ninth Circuit's Atextual "Predominantly Internal" Standard Cannot Be Reconciled With That Limit

Congress enacted Exemption 2 to shield matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The word "solely" is not surplusage; a court's duty is to give effect to every operative word Congress chose. *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883). When a statute's terms are plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *United States v. American Trucking Assns., Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The Ninth Circuit's rule—exempting anything "predominantly" internal—substitutes a judicially invented threshold for the statutory command that the material relate "solely" to personnel rules and practices. That substitution cannot stand, because courts may not read qualifying language out of a statute, nor may they add a standard that loosens Congress's chosen limitation. *See FCC v. American Broadcasting Co.*, 347 U.S. 284, 296 (1954) (courts must apply the words Congress used according to their ordinary import rather than a judicially crafted substitute).

ESQD arc data has nothing to do with "personnel." It does not regulate Navy employees' conduct, discipline, or working conditions; it describes the physical consequences of an explosion at a fixed geographic location—information of direct concern to the civilian public living near Indian Island, not an internal employment rule. Because the statute's own words foreclose treating such data as "personnel" material at all, the inquiry should have ended there, without resort to any "predominantly internal" gloss.

## II. FOIA's Exemptions Must Be Construed Narrowly, With Doubts Resolved in Favor of Disclosure

Courts confronting the Act in its earliest years recognized that its exemptions are narrow exceptions to a broad rule of disclosure and must be read accordingly. In *Consumers Union of United States, Inc. v. Veterans Administration*, 301 F. Supp. 796 (S.D.N.Y. 1969), the district court examined a claim that agency testing procedures fell within Exemption 2 and held that the exemption reaches only genuinely internal administrative matters, not information bearing on the agency's dealings with the outside world. Likewise, in *American Mail Line, Ltd. v. Gulick*, 411 F.2d 696 (D.C. Cir. 1969), the D.C. Circuit construed a FOIA exemption narrowly, explaining that exemptions from the Act's disclosure mandate are to be given the narrowest construction consistent with their language, lest the exception swallow the rule of disclosure that Congress enacted.

That interpretive caution reflects why Congress passed FOIA in the first place. Agencies had previously relied on the old "housekeeping" statute to withhold records from the public on the theory that the records concerned internal agency management. The D.C. Circuit's decision in *Boeing Airplane Co. v. Coggeshall*, 280 F.2d 654 (D.C. Cir. 1960), illustrates the abuse Congress sought to correct: an agency's characterization of records as being for internal use does not itself justify withholding them from persons with a legitimate interest in their contents. FOIA was designed to end exactly the sort of open-ended "internal use" rationale that the Ninth Circuit has now revived through its "predominantly internal" standard. A rule permitting withholding whenever an agency affidavit asserts the material is chiefly for internal planning—precisely the showing the Navy made through Commander Whitbred's declaration—reintroduces the same unbounded secrecy rationale FOIA was written to abolish.

## III. FOIA Is a Remedial Disclosure Statute, and Its Exemptions Must Be Interpreted to Effectuate, Not Defeat, That Remedial Purpose

It is a settled principle that remedial legislation is to be interpreted so as to advance, rather than restrict, the object Congress sought to achieve. *SEC v. C.M. Joiner Leasing Corp.*, 320 U.S. 344, 350–51 (1943) (a statute enacted to remedy a recognized evil should be construed to effectuate that remedial purpose); *Phelps Dodge Corp. v. NLRB*, 313 U.S. 177, 193–94 (1941) (statutory purpose guides construction of ambiguous terms in remedial legislation). FOIA's remedial purpose is public access to information about government operations affecting the public. Allowing an agency to withhold safety-related data—information bearing directly on the risks that Navy operations pose to surrounding civilian communities—simply because the agency also uses the data for internal planning purposes inverts that remedial purpose. Nearly every agency record has some internal planning use; if that fact alone sufficed to trigger exemption, Exemption 2 would engulf records Congress plainly intended the public to see.

## IV. Conclusion

The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" standard has no footing in the text Congress enacted, contradicts the narrow-construction principle that has governed FOIA since its infancy, and revives the very administrative secrecy practices FOIA was designed to end. The ESQD arc data are not personnel rules or practices, and the decision below should be reversed.
