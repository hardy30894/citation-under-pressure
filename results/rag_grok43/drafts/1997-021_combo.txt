The National Labor Relations Act confers upon employers the right to test employee sentiment regarding an incumbent union through polling when objective indications warrant doubt as to continued majority support. La Crosse Telephone Corp. v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Board, 336 U.S. 18, 24 (1949). The Board's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard, by requiring evidence sufficient to justify outright withdrawal of recognition, renders polling a nullity and contravenes the statutory scheme. United Mine Workers of America v. Arkansas Oak Flooring Co., 351 U.S. 62, 73-74 (1956).

This Court has recognized that the Board's authority to develop labor policy is bounded by the Act's text and structure. Federal Communications Commission v. Allentown Broadcasting Corp., 349 U.S. 358, 362-63 (1955). The standard at issue imposes an evidentiary threshold that effectively eliminates the polling option Congress preserved, thereby disrupting the balance between stable bargaining relationships and employee free choice. Smith v. Evening News Assn., 371 U.S. 195, 197-98 (1962). Where an employer possesses substantial objective evidence of diminished union support, the Act permits polling under procedural safeguards without first satisfying the withdrawal threshold. Retail Clerks International Ass'n, Local Unions Nos. 128 & 633 v. Lion Dry Goods, Inc., 369 U.S. 17, 28-29 (1962).

The Board's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. Marine Engineers Beneficial Ass'n v. Interlake Steamship Co., 370 U.S. 173, 180 (1962). Multiple employees expressed dissatisfaction with union representation during hiring interviews and in direct statements to management. Brotherhood of Railway & Steamship Clerks, Freight Handlers, Express & Station Employees v. Association for the Benefit of Non-Contract Employees, 380 U.S. 650, 667 (1965). The Board improperly discounted these statements on grounds unrelated to their probative value. Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 180-81 (1967).

The record further demonstrates that the successor unit inherited a presumption of continuing majority status whose factual foundation was attenuated by the reduction in workforce and absence of prior certification. Boire v. Greyhound Corp., 376 U.S. 473, 477-78 (1964). Statements from employees outside the unit on the critical date and equivocal expressions cannot supply the requisite objective basis for the Board's conclusion. McLeod v. General Electric Co., 385 U.S. 533, 535 (1967). The Board's order therefore rests on an erroneous application of the substantial-evidence standard and must be set aside.
