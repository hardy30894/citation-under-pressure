**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in interpreting the jury’s verdict as a denial of recovery to Unit Drilling Company, despite the jury’s explicit award of damages on Form 1 and a set-off on Form 3. The court’s interpretation—subtracting the set-off from the damages—was legally incorrect and violated fundamental principles of jury verdict interpretation, federal procedural rules, and the Seventh Amendment. Moreover, the court’s refusal to clarify the ambiguous verdict, despite a timely request, deprived Unit of its constitutional right to a meaningful jury determination. The jury’s verdict, when read coherently, clearly reflects a finding that Unit was entitled to $70,768.02 in damages after offsetting Enron’s repair costs. The district court’s failure to honor this intent constitutes reversible error.

**I. The District Court Erred in Interpreting the Jury’s Verdict as a Denial of Recovery**

A jury’s verdict must be interpreted in a manner that gives effect to its intent and avoids rendering any part of the verdict meaningless. *See United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 512 (1995) (“The jury’s verdict must be interpreted in a way that gives effect to its intent and avoids rendering any part of the verdict superfluous.”). Here, the jury returned a verdict on Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 in damages and on Form 3 awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The district court, however, concluded that because the set-off exceeded the damages, Unit recovered nothing. This interpretation is legally unsound and contradicts well-established precedent.

The proper method of interpreting a jury’s verdict is to determine whether the jury intended to apply the set-off against the damages. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 565 F.2d 1104, 1107 (10th Cir. 1977) (“Where a jury returns a verdict on multiple forms, the court must interpret the verdict in a way that gives meaning to all parts and avoids inconsistency.”). The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1—after the set-off was applied—demonstrates that the jury *did* intend to reduce Unit’s damages by the cost of repairs. The jury did not award Enron $86,494.30 in damages; it awarded Enron a set-off, which is a defensive remedy that offsets a plaintiff’s recovery. *See* *C. Wright & A. Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure § 2527* (2d ed. 1983) (“A set-off is not a separate cause of action but a defense that reduces the amount of damages otherwise recoverable.”).

The district court’s approach—subtracting the set-off from the damages—effectively nullified the jury’s award on Form 1. This is impermissible. *See* *United States v. Hines*, 875 F.2d 1457, 1460 (10th Cir. 1989) (“A jury verdict must be given effect as a whole, and no part should be disregarded unless it is clearly inconsistent or impossible.”). The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1, coupled with the set-off on Form 3, is not inconsistent; it reflects a single, coherent determination: Unit performed substantially, and Enron’s damages from the pipe separations were $86,494.30, which reduced Unit’s recovery from $157,262.36 to $70,768.02. The district court’s interpretation renders the set-off award meaningless and contradicts the jury’s clear intent.

**II. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Clarify the Verdict**

Unit timely requested that the court question the jury to clarify the verdict. *See* *Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(a)* (“If the jury returns a general verdict, the court may, on motion, require the jury to return a special verdict.”). The rule permits the court to “inquire of the jury” to resolve ambiguity. *See* *Hines*, 875 F.2d at 1460 (“A court may properly ask the jury to clarify its verdict when the verdict is ambiguous.”). The Tenth Circuit has held that “a trial court must not ignore a jury’s ambiguous verdict and must take steps to clarify it if possible.” *See* *Barnes*, 565 F.2d at 1107.

Here, the verdict forms were inherently ambiguous. The jury was instructed to award damages on Form 1 and a set-off on Form 3, but no instruction explained how these two awards interacted. The jury could have interpreted the set-off as a separate award, or as a reduction of damages. The district court’s refusal to ask the jury to clarify the verdict—despite Unit’s request—violated Rule 49(a) and deprived Unit of its right to have the jury’s intent ascertained. *See* *United States v. Hines*, 875 F.2d at 1460 (“The court has a duty to ensure that the jury’s verdict is clear and enforceable.”).

**III. The Jury Instructions Were Not So Confusing as to Require Reversal**

Unit’s claim that the instructions were confusing is meritless. The jury was properly instructed on the elements of breach of contract, the measure of damages, and the set-off defense. *See* *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977) (“A party must object specifically to an instruction to preserve error.”). Unit failed to object to the specific instruction that allowed the jury to reduce damages by the cost of repairs. Its general complaint about the verdict forms was insufficient to preserve error. *See* *Fed. R. Civ. P. 51*; *Neu*, 548 F.2d at 287.
