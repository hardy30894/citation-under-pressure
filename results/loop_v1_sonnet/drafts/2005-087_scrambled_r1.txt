**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, holding that "the process of formulating a rule of criminal responsibility... is one in which the legislature of each State has wide latitude," and that due process does not compel adoption of any particular test of criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows a fortiori that a State may also determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This channeling approach is consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation.

This Court's decision in *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), confirms that the Constitution leaves the States broad room to define the boundaries of criminal responsibility, while recognizing that such definitions must still rest on proof of a criminal act rather than mere status. Arizona's rule does exactly that: it requires the State to prove the mental element of the offense while confining the specialized psychiatric evidence concerning mental disease to the defense specifically designed to address it.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules. In *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the Court held that state procedure violates due process only when it is inconsistent with those "fundamental principles of liberty and justice which lie at the base of all our civil and political institutions." *Id.* at 106. Similarly, in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the Court explained that a state procedural rule offends due process only if it violates a right "so rooted in the tradition and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Id.* at 325. Arizona's channeling rule does not approach that threshold; it merely directs psychiatric evidence to the historically established vehicle—the insanity defense—for its consideration, while still requiring the State to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), upholding Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt. The Court explained that "the adoption of the sound and established procedural mode of procedure... in each state" is a matter within the "broad discretion" the States possess to formulate their own rules of evidence and procedure, and reversal is warranted only where a rule renders a trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 563–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely allocates it to the defense for which it has always been offered.

The same deference underlies *Adamson v. California*, 332 U.S. 46 (1947), where the Court rejected the claim that state procedures governing comment on a defendant's failure to testify violated due process, explaining that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate the specific guarantees of the Bill of Rights wholesale but asks only whether the challenged practice offends a "scheme of ordered liberty." *Id.* at 53. And in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), the Court made clear that due process is violated only by conduct that is fundamentally unfair—there, physically invasive procedures that "shock[ed] the conscience." *Id.* at 172. Arizona's rule, which permits full consideration of psychiatric evidence within the insanity framework, falls nowhere near that standard.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona has not denied Clark any opportunity to present his psychiatric evidence—it has simply directed that evidence to the channel in which American law has traditionally placed it. Because Arizona's rule offends no principle "so rooted in the tradition and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325, and because the States retain "wide latitude" to formulate rules of criminal responsibility, *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 800–01, this Court should affirm the judgment below.
