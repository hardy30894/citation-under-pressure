ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN REFUSING TO SEEK CLARIFICATION OF THE JURY'S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT BEFORE DISCHARGING THE JURY.

When a jury returns a verdict that is capable of two conflicting interpretations, the trial court has a duty to resolve the ambiguity while the jury is still available to explain itself, rather than guessing at the jury's intent after the fact. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 49(b) authorizes a trial court, when a jury's answers are inconsistent with each other, to "return the jury for further consideration of its answers." Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(b). The purpose of this rule is to ensure that the verdict actually reflects what the jury decided, not what the court later infers from an incomplete record.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that reviewing courts, and trial courts before them, bear a "duty of harmonizing the answers" returned by a jury whenever a fair reading of those answers permits it. Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963). In Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd., 369 U.S. 355 (1962), the Court held that where a jury's findings can be reconciled on any fair reading of the record, the trial court must adopt that reading rather than declare a conflict that requires unraveling the verdict after the jury has dispersed. Id. at 364. That principle applies with even greater force where, as here, the party seeking clarification specifically asked the court to resubmit the question to the jury before it was discharged.

Here, Unit timely and specifically requested that the trial court question the jury about the relationship between the Form 1 damage award and the Form 3 set-off award before the jury was released. The court refused. That refusal deprived Unit of the one opportunity to eliminate the ambiguity at its source — the jury itself — and left the district court to speculate, after the fact, about what the jury meant. Courts confronting similarly ambiguous verdicts have recognized that a trial judge's failure to resubmit the question to the jury, when timely requested, is reversible error precisely because post-hoc judicial reconstruction of a verdict cannot substitute for the jury's own clarification. See Domeracki v. Humble Oil & Refining Co., 443 F.2d 1245, 1247-48 (3d Cir. 1971) (trial court should resubmit ambiguous or inconsistent verdict to jury rather than resolve the ambiguity itself). The district court's failure to do so here was error.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY'S VERDICT TO DENY UNIT ANY RECOVERY.

Even without resubmission, the verdict forms themselves, read together and in light of the instructions the jury was given, compel the conclusion that the jury intended Unit to recover $70,768.02 — not zero. A court's obligation, when construing a verdict, is to adopt the interpretation that gives coherent effect to the jury's evident intent rather than one that renders the jury's deliberate arithmetic meaningless. See Gallick, 372 U.S. at 119.

The jury was instructed that if it found Unit had only substantially performed, it was to award "the amount of the unpaid invoices, less the cost to Enron of correcting any defects that Unit caused." The unpaid invoices totaled $157,262.36. The jury awarded $70,768.02 on Form 1 — a figure that is not random, but is precisely $157,262.36 minus $86,494.30, the very set-off figure the jury separately wrote on Form 3. That arithmetic identity is not coincidental; it demonstrates that the jury had already performed the subtraction the instructions directed it to perform when it filled out Form 1, and that Form 3 was simply the jury's mechanism for recording the amount it had already deducted — not an instruction to deduct that same amount a second time.

The district court's interpretation — subtracting the Form 3 figure from the Form 1 figure yet again — assumes the jury intended a double deduction that the instructions never authorized and that no rational jury, having just performed the subtraction once, would have intended to perform twice. A trial court is not free to adopt an interpretation of a verdict that produces an internally inconsistent or irrational result when a fair and consistent reading is available. Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, 369 U.S. at 364. Here, the consistent reading — that the jury already netted the set-off into its Form 1 award — was readily available and was the interpretation Unit urged below. The district court's contrary interpretation should be reversed, and judgment should be entered for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed. This Court should either remand for entry of judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02, consistent with the only reading of the verdict that harmonizes Forms 1 and 3, or, in the alternative, remand for a new trial with instructions requiring the jury to clarify any future ambiguous verdict before discharge, consistent with the trial court's obligations under Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(b) and this Court's precedent.
