ARGUMENT

I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges.

The ADEA excludes from its definition of “employee” any “appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan fall squarely within this exception. As the First Circuit correctly held, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Missouri Supreme Court decisions establish common law for the entire state, Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and resolve questions with far-reaching public-policy implications, Cruzan v. Harmon, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), cert. granted, 109 S. Ct. 3240 (1989). Judges also exercise supervisory authority over the judicial system itself. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4.

Congress did not manifest a clear intent to intrude upon this core state function. In traditionally sensitive areas affecting the federal balance, “the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Absent such an unequivocal statement, the ADEA does not displace Missouri’s constitutional provision. See also EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d at 54; Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141 (1988).

II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Rational-Basis Review.

Age is not a suspect classification, and the right to public employment is not fundamental. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam); San Antonio Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973). Missouri’s rule therefore need only bear a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest. The provision eliminates the “unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980), reduces delays caused by death or disability, Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979), and promotes public confidence by ensuring that judges share experiences with the majority of adult citizens, Apkin, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Because these rationales are sufficient, the Equal Protection Clause is not offended.
