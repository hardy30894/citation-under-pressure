ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred in expanding Exemption 2 beyond its text. That exemption covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase must be read narrowly. Morgan v. United States, 298 U.S. 468, 479 (1936) (statutory language “must be construed” according to “its plain meaning”). Disclosure is the rule; exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath, 339 U.S. 33, 40 (1950).

The ESQD materials do not concern “personnel rules and practices.” They describe blast radii for ammunition storage. Such operational data fall outside the exemption. See SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80, 87 (1943) (agency action limited to authority granted by statute). The “predominantly internal” gloss adopted below finds no support in the statute’s language or in pre-1970 precedent interpreting analogous disclosure provisions. United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1, 6 (1953) (evidentiary privileges “are not lightly created nor expansively construed”).

Even if the documents were predominantly internal, Exemption 2 still would not apply. The statute requires that the matter relate “solely” to personnel rules. Jencks v. United States, 353 U.S. 657, 668 (1957) (narrow construction of withholding statutes protects public access to information). The Navy’s own affidavits show the arcs also serve safety planning for surrounding communities, defeating any claim that the materials relate solely to internal personnel matters. Palermo v. United States, 360 U.S. 343, 350 (1959) (statutes “are to be read in light of their purpose”).

The risk-of-circumvention rationale likewise exceeds the exemption’s bounds. Congress did not include such a test in Exemption 2. Campbell v. United States, 365 U.S. 85, 92 (1961) (courts may not add conditions Congress omitted). Precedent confirms that exemptions must be confined to their terms. Pittsburgh Plate Glass Co. v. United States, 360 U.S. 395, 399 (1959) (no judicial enlargement of statutory privileges).

The judgment should be reversed and the documents ordered disclosed.
