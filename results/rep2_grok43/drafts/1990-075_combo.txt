ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General satisfies the intelligible-principle requirement. In J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928), this Court held that Congress may delegate authority when it lays down “an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized * * * is directed to conform.” The Act supplies such a principle by enumerating eight specific factors the Attorney General must consider, 21 U.S.C. § 811(c), requiring a binding scientific evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, id. § 811(b), and limiting temporary scheduling to substances posing “an imminent hazard to the public safety,” id. § 811(h)(1). These constraints mirror those upheld in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420, 426 (1944), and Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

The same standards confirm that the Attorney General may subdelegate the function. In United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc., 272 U.S. 1, 12 (1926), the Court recognized that an officer vested with statutory power may “delegate to subordinates the performance of duties not requiring the exercise of his own judgment.” The temporary-scheduling power is ministerial once the statutory criteria are met, and the Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), merely assigns its execution to the DEA Administrator. See also United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911); Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364, 385 (1907).

Petitioners’ reliance on the two 1935 decisions is misplaced. Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495, 537 (1935), involved statutes containing “no criterion to govern” the delegatee’s course. Here the Act supplies concrete factors, time limits, and an independent scientific check. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), and Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), confirm that Congress may entrust fact-finding and application of statutory standards to executive officers without violating Article I.

The availability of judicial review of the resulting criminal prosecution further satisfies separation-of-powers concerns. Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542 (1952). Because the delegation is constitutional, the convictions should be affirmed.
