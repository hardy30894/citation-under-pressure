**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because § 1333(b) does not extend Longshore Act coverage to every land-based injury that can be traced back to offshore production. Congress extended benefits only for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That language limits coverage to injuries that arise from operations on the Shelf itself; it does not transform OCSLA into a general compensation statute for any employee whose work happens to relate to offshore drilling.

The Court has long required fidelity to statutory text. “Where the words of a law are plain, and the meaning clear, we have no right to speculate upon the intentions of the legislature.” **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**. Likewise, **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**, teaches that courts must apply the words Congress enacted, and **United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820)** warns that a statute is not to be enlarged by construction. Congress knew how to write a broader causal statute if it wanted one; it did not do so here.

The Court has also long recognized that statutory compensation schemes turn on the jurisdictional limits Congress established. In **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932)**, the Court upheld the Longshore Act by enforcing the statute’s defined factual boundaries. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court likewise respected the statutory line Congress drew rather than substituting a broader equitable theory of coverage. Those cases confirm that courts may not replace Congress’s location-based trigger with an open-ended “substantial nexus” test untethered to the statute’s words.

Respondent’s reading also conflicts with the principle that even remedial statutes may not be rewritten under the banner of liberal construction. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**, rejected the notion that courts may depart from statutory text because a broader reading seems more consistent with statutory purpose. **Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 416 (1932)**, likewise teaches that courts must enforce the limits Congress chose, not the limits a claimant would prefer. And **Ex parte McNiel, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 236, 242 (1871)** holds that statutory conditions are not dispensed with by implication.

The statutory words here matter. Congress required an injury “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That formulation does not say “any injury caused in fact by offshore employment.” It requires a meaningful connection to operations conducted on the Shelf. An onshore forklift accident at La Conchita, an oil-processing facility on the California coast, is too attenuated. The facility processed crude oil slurry after it had already been brought ashore from the platforms. Mr. Valladolid was not injured on the Shelf, on the water above the Shelf, or even during transport to or from the platforms. Extending coverage to that accident would sever the statute from the geographic and operational limitation Congress chose.

The Court’s older compensation and maritime decisions reinforce that point. In **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 281 U.S. 574, 580-81 (1930)**, the Court recognized that compensation coverage depends on the legislative boundary enacted. In **Baltimore Mail S.S. Co. v. Fawcett, 269 U.S. 247, 251-52 (1925)**, the Court declined to expand a maritime compensation regime beyond its statutory design. And **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**, together with **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**, reflects the settled rule that statutory reach is not enlarged by implication. If Congress had intended to cover every shoreside injury connected to offshore operations, it could have said so expressly. It did not.

Respondent’s theory would also make the phrase “on the outer Continental Shelf” largely surplusage. Courts avoid constructions that render statutory words superfluous. See **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**; **United States v. Goldenberg, 168 U.S. 95, 102-03 (1897)**. Reading § 1333(b) to cover any downstream land-based injury would detach the statute from the offshore situs Congress specified and convert a targeted extension of Longshore benefits into a far broader compensation program.

Accordingly, the Ninth Circuit correctly declined to impose a categorical situs-of-injury rule, but the respondent’s claim still fails because the statute does not reach an onshore injury at a processing facility merely because the employee’s work related to offshore drilling. Section 1333(b) covers injuries arising from operations on the Outer Continental Shelf itself, and this one did not. The judgment should be affirmed.
