**ARGUMENT**

The Board erred in holding that the intervening ’838 and ’768 applications sufficiently incorporated the ’579 application by reference. That holding contravenes settled patent law—predating 1970—requiring that incorporation by reference be effected through language that is clear, unequivocal, and unambiguous as to the identity of the incorporated document.

**I. The Board Applied an Erroneous Legal Standard.**

This Court’s prior decision in *Harari I* articulated a narrow rule for evaluating incorporation statements at the “initial filing stage”—whether “the identity of the incorporated reference is clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented.” *Harari I*, 602 F.3d at 1352-53. That rule, however, was tailored to the unique circumstances of an applicant who copies an ambiguous statement from a parent application into a new continuation. It does not govern the separate inquiry into whether *intervening* applications satisfy the strict continuity requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 120. For intervening applications, traditional law applies: incorporation by reference must be explicit and unmistakable. *See In re Hawkins*, 247 F.2d 956, 959 (C.C.P.A. 1957) (“[I]ncorporation by reference requires a clear and unequivocal statement.”); *In re Hession*, 296 F.2d 930, 933 (C.C.P.A. 1961) (“The language of incorporation must be such that the incorporated material is as completely described as if it were set forth in the application itself.”); *In re Moore*, 155 F.2d 379, 381 (C.C.P.A. 1946) (“[A] reference incorporated by reference must be identified with sufficient particularity that it can be ascertained without the exercise of speculation.”).

**II. The Incorporation Language in the Intervening Applications Was Fatally Ambiguous.**

The ’566 application’s incorporation language stated that the ’579 application was “filed on the same day as the present application.” J.A. 1204. When that language was copied verbatim into the ’838 and ’768 applications, the phrase “the present application” referred—at each filing—to a different application: the ’838 or the ’768, respectively. The ’579 application was *not* filed on the same day as either of those intervening applications. Thus, the literal statement was false and misleading.

Pre-1970 law uniformly rejects such ambiguous language. In *In re Schechter*, 205 F.2d 185, 188 (C.C.P.A. 1953), this court held that an incorporation statement which “does not clearly and unequivocally point to the specific document” is insufficient. *Accord In re Stempel*, 241 F.2d 755, 759 (C.C.P.A. 1957) (incorporation must “leave no doubt” as to the identity of the referenced document). The language here, copied without adaptation, creates precisely the doubt that the rule forbids. A reasonable person reading the ’838 or ’768 application would have to guess whether “the present application” meant the original ’566 application, the ’838 application, or something else. Such speculation is impermissible. *See In re Wiggins*, 397 F.2d 356, 359 (C.C.P.A. 1968) (“[M]ere reference to a prior application, without more, does not satisfy the requirement of a definite identification.”).

**III. The Purpose of Section 120 Requires Clarity in the Chain of Priority.**

35 U.S.C. § 120 conditions the benefit of an earlier filing date on a “written description” that is “contained in the application or in an application previously filed” and that complies with the “requirement … for an earlier application.” This is a *per se* requirement of continuity. *See generally* *The Incandescent Lamp Patent*, 159 U.S. 465, 472-73 (1895) (emphasizing that a patent must “describe the invention … in such full, clear, concise, and exact terms as to enable any person skilled in the art to make and use the same”). A chain of priority must be unbroken and unambiguous. *United States v. Dubilier Condenser Corp.*, 289 U.S. 178, 187-88 (1933) (noting that the patent system “requires exactness and definiteness in the description of the invention”). The ambiguous incorporation here breaks the chain. Because the ’838 and ’768 applications did not clearly identify the ’579 application, the ’880 application is entitled only to the filing date of the ’708 application (December 20, 1996)—which postdates Hollmer’s conception date.

**IV. The Board’s Decision Also Conflicts with the Written Description Requirement.**

Even if the incorporation were otherwise sufficient, the copied statement fails to provide adequate written description of the ’579 application’s subject matter. The statement merely references a co-pending application by a title and a filing date that is inaccurate. *Schriber-Schroth Co. v. Cleveland Trust Co.*, 311 U.S. 211, 218 (1940) (“The patentee must describe his invention in such a way that one skilled in the art may clearly understand it.”). A title alone does not describe the technical content. *United Carbon Co. v. Binney & Smith Co.*, 317 U.S. 228, 236 (1942) (“[T]he inventor must define his invention with particularity.”). The Board thus erred in concluding that the intervening applications provided any substantive disclosure of the ’579 application.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the Board’s judgment against Hollmer should be reversed. The intervening applications failed to incorporate the ’579 application by reference under the clear, pre-1970 legal standards this Court has long applied. The ’880 application is not entitled to the benefit of the ’566 application’s filing date, and judgment should be entered in favor of Hollmer.
