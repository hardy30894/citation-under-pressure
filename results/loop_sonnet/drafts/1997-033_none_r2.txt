# ARGUMENT

## I. The mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses use of Section 1404(a) to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent trial venue.

Section 1407(a) commands that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. That word choice is not incidental. Congress's use of "shall" ordinarily signals a mandatory duty rather than a discretionary option, and courts have consistently treated such directives as binding rather than advisory. *See Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). Where Congress speaks in mandatory terms, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 1407(a) contains no exception permitting continued retention where a transferee judge independently concludes, under the discretionary convenience standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be more efficient. That silence is itself significant, for "there is no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to do precisely what Section 1407(a) forbids: retain permanent custody of a case that had been assigned to it solely "for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings." Congress did not leave the duration of a multidistrict transfer to the discretion of the transferee court. It fixed that duration by statute. A transferee judge's belief that continued retention serves efficiency cannot override a command phrased in mandatory terms.

## II. Section 1407(a)'s specific limitation on the duration of multidistrict transfer controls over the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a).

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) address different problems and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is "a federal judicial housekeeping measure, dealing with the placement of litigation in the federal courts and generally intended, subject to the transferee district court's discretion, to protect litigants, witnesses, and the public against unnecessary inconvenience and expense." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 636–37 (1964). It authorizes transfer only "for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice," and only to a district in which the action might properly have been brought in the first instance. *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960); *see Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960) (refusing to relax that limitation even where the parties themselves preferred a different forum). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating overlapping pretrial proceedings, subject to the Panel's express and continuing duty to remand once those proceedings conclude.

Where Congress enacts a specific provision governing a particular subject, that provision is not displaced by a general statute addressing the same broad area. As this Court held in *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer. That specific command cannot be "controlled or nullified" by resort to Section 1404(a)—a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict proceedings and supplies no textual basis for an exception to Section 1407(a)'s remand mandate. *Fourco Glass*, 353 U.S. at 228–29.

Nor does Section 1404(a)'s underlying purpose support such a use. That statute exists to protect litigants from inconvenient forums among venues where the plaintiff already could have sued, *Van Dusen*, 376 U.S. at 636, not to permit a court that acquired a case solely by operation of Section 1407(a) to entrench itself as the permanent trial forum. Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its chosen forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s discretionary convenience calculus authorizes the transferee court to displace that choice by transferring the case to itself.

## III. The court of appeals erred in treating the statutory conflict as unsettled.

The Ninth Circuit acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate," yet excused noncompliance based on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and prior circuit practice adopted without independent analysis of the statutory text. But an internal Panel rule cannot rewrite the words Congress enacted, and accumulated practice cannot substitute for statutory authority Congress never granted. Under *American Trucking*, 310 U.S. at 543, and *Caminetti*, 242 U.S. at 485, a court's task when statutory text is plain is to enforce it—not to weigh the policy trade-offs Congress already resolved when it drafted Section 1407(a)'s remand command. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois "at or before" the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, and because Section 1404(a) furnishes no authority to override that specific and mandatory command, the judgment below should be reversed.
