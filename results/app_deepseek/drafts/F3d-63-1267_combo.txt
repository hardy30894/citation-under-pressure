## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law because Stelwagon failed to prove actual antitrust damages caused by the alleged price discrimination. A plaintiff seeking treble damages under section 4 of the Clayton Act must prove that the defendant’s violation directly and proximately caused injury to its business or property. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). Mere proof of a statutory violation, without evidence of actual loss, is insufficient. *Keogh v. Chicago & Northwestern Ry. Co.*, 260 U.S. 156, 163 (1922). Stelwagon’s damages evidence was speculative and failed to satisfy this fundamental requirement.

**I. Stelwagon’s Proof of Damages Was Speculative and Insufficient**

The jury’s original award of $2,272,000 was unsupported by the evidence, as the district court itself recognized when it ordered a remittitur. *Stelwagon Manufacturing Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Systems, Inc.*, 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994). Even after remittitur, the reduced antitrust damages of $450,383.60 lacked a causal nexus to Tarmac’s pricing. Under *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931), damages must be proven with reasonable certainty, not left to speculation. Stelwagon’s expert testimony—based on assumptions about lost sales to customers who never purchased from Tarmac—failed to isolate the effect of price discrimination from other market factors. *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 377 (1927), requires that damages be “the natural and proximate consequence” of the unlawful conduct. Here, Stelwagon’s lost profits could have resulted from its own business decisions, including its voluntary cessation of marketing other product lines, rather than from Tarmac’s pricing.

Moreover, Stelwagon did not prove that it actually paid higher prices than its competitors. In *Bruce’s Juices, Inc. v. American Can Co.*, 330 U.S. 743, 757 (1947), the Supreme Court held that a Robinson-Patman plaintiff must show that the discrimination caused it to lose sales or profits. Stelwagon presented no evidence that it lost specific customers or contracts because of Tarmac’s lower prices to Standard and Celotex. *Krug v. International Telephone & Telegraph Corp.*, 142 F.2d 768, 771 (3d Cir. 1944), reinforces that antitrust damages cannot be based on “mere conjecture.” The district court’s remittitur did not cure this fundamental defect; it merely reduced an already speculative award to a still-unsupported figure.

**II. Stelwagon Failed to Establish a Prima Facie Violation of the Robinson-Patman Act**

Even if damages were proven, Stelwagon did not establish the requisite competitive injury. Section 2(a) requires that the discrimination “may be substantially to lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly.” *FTC v. Morton Salt Co.*, 334 U.S. 37, 46 (1948). While *Morton Salt* allows an inference of competitive injury from sustained price differentials between competing purchasers, that inference is rebuttable. Here, Tarmac presented evidence that its lower prices to Standard and Celotex were cost-justified or made in good faith to meet competition. *Corn Products Refining Co. v. FTC*, 324 U.S. 726, 741 (1945), requires that the plaintiff show actual or likely injury to competition, not merely a price difference. Stelwagon’s evidence showed only that it paid more than some competitors, not that competition in the Philadelphia roofing market was harmed. *FTC v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 363 U.S. 536, 549 (1960), clarifies that the Act targets discrimination that injures competition, not individual competitors. Stelwagon’s own market share remained stable, and it continued to sell Tarmac products throughout the alleged discrimination period.

**III. The Breach of Contract Claim Also Fails**

The oral exclusive distributorship agreement was unenforceable under the statute of frauds because it could not be performed within one year. *Standard Oil Co. v. FTC*, 340 U.S. 231, 248 (1951), though not directly on point, underscores the need for clear contractual terms in antitrust contexts. Stelwagon’s claim for lost profits on the contract was duplicative of its antitrust claim and equally speculative.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court should have granted Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law. The judgment must be reversed.
