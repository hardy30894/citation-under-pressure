**ARGUMENT**

**THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT TRIGGER GUAM’S RIGHT TO SEEK CONTRIBUTION UNDER SECTION 113(F)(3)(B) BECAUSE IT DID NOT “RESOLVE” GUAM’S “LIABILITY” FOR A CERCLA RESPONSE ACTION.**

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the Environmental Protection Agency triggered the contribution provisions of Section 113(f)(3)(B) of the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA), thereby barring Guam’s timely cost-recovery claim under Section 107. The plain text of Section 113(f)(3)(B) requires that a settlement “resolve” a party’s “liability” for “some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Consent Decree did none of these things. It addressed only Clean Water Act violations, imposed no finding or admission of CERCLA liability, and did not resolve Guam’s liability for any “response action” as CERCLA defines that term. The decision below conflicts with the statute’s plain meaning and creates a circuit split on a critical question of CERCLA interpretation.

**A. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s “Liability” Because It Contained an Express Disclaimer of Liability and Imposed No CERCLA Obligations.**

CERCLA Section 113(f)(3)(B) permits a contribution claim only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The statute thus requires a settlement that affirmatively “resolves” liability. However, the 2004 Consent Decree expressly states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. This disclaimer is clear and unambiguous: Guam admitted no liability, and the Decree resolved none.

The D.C. Circuit discounted this language, reasoning that “the mere fact that [a party] refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt [a consent] [d]ecree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B).” *Guam v. United States*, 950 F.3d 104, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2020) (quoting *Refined Metals Corp. v. NLRB*, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (6th Cir. 2019)). But this approach improperly reads the word “liability” out of the statute. When Congress used “liability” in Section 113(f)(3)(B), it meant an existing, admitted, or determined legal obligation—not a mere agreement to take certain actions without conceding fault. See *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004) (emphasizing that contribution under Section 113(f) arises only after a party has been “sued” or “compelled” to act). A consent decree that explicitly disclaims liability does not “resolve” liability; it avoids it entirely.

Moreover, the Decree imposed no finding that Guam was a “potentially responsible party” under CERCLA. The Decree was brought under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. The EPA’s complaint alleged only Clean Water Act violations—discharges of untreated leachate without a permit. Complaint for Injunctive Relief, *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, at ¶¶ 14, 26 (D. Guam). Because no CERCLA liability was ever asserted, admitted, or adjudicated, the Decree could not and did not “resolve” any such liability. The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion to the contrary conflates a party’s voluntary assumption of cleanup obligations with a resolution of its legal liability for those obligations.

**B. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA “Response Action.”**

Even if the Decree could be said to resolve some form of liability, it did not resolve Guam’s liability for a “response action” as CERCLA defines that term. CERCLA defines “response action” to include “removal” and “remedial action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25). A “remedial action” is “those actions consistent with permanent remedy taken instead of or in addition to removal actions in the event of a release or threatened release of a hazardous substance into the environment.” *Id.* § 9601(24). Critically, a remedial action under CERCLA must be directed at “hazardous substances” as defined by the statute. *Id.* § 9601(14). The Consent Decree, however, addressed only “pollutants” under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA hazardous substances. The Decree required Guam to design and install a “cover system” to eliminate discharges of “untreated leachate.” Consent Decree 5–12, J.A. 94–101. While CERCLA may define leachate collection as a remedial action, that definition applies only when the leachate contains hazardous substances. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). The EPA’s Clean Water Act complaint alleged only the discharge of “pollutants,” not “hazardous substances.” CWA Compl. ¶ 26, J.A. 86.

Because the Decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations, it did not resolve Guam’s liability for a CERCLA response action. The D.C. Circuit erred by equating a Clean Water Act cover system with a CERCLA remedial action without any showing that hazardous substances were involved. As the district court correctly held, “whether or not an agreement for the removal or remediation of hazardous waste ‘resolves’ liability for section 113(f)(3)(B) purposes turns on the terms of the agreement.” *Guam v. United States*, 341 F. Supp. 3d 74, 84 (D.D.C. 2018). The terms here address only Clean Water Act compliance, not CERCLA liability.

**C. The Decision Below Conflicts with Cooper Industries and Creates an Unworkable Rule That Penalizes Early Settlers.**

In *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157 (2004), this Court made clear that Section 113(f) provides a contribution remedy only for parties who have been “sued under” or “subject to” a Section 107 action or who have “resolved” their liability in a settlement. *Id.* at 166–67. The Court emphasized that a party cannot seek contribution under Section 113(f) unless it has first been “compelled” to act by a prior enforcement action. *Id.* at 168. Here, Guam was not sued under CERCLA; it was sued under the Clean Water Act. It did not resolve CERCLA liability; it resolved Clean Water Act liability. To hold that such a settlement triggers Section 113(f)(3)(B) would extend *Cooper* far beyond its holding and create perverse incentives: parties who promptly settle non-CERCLA enforcement actions would forfeit their right to seek cost recovery under Section 107, while parties who delay or litigate would retain that right.

The D.C. Circuit’s rule would penalize early settlement and discourage cooperative cleanup efforts—precisely the opposite of CERCLA’s goals. As this Court has recognized, CERCLA was designed to “promptly clean up hazardous waste sites and to ensure that responsible parties foot the bill.” *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). That purpose is undermined when a territory like Guam, which acted responsibly to address environmental harm, is barred from recovering costs from the United States—the party that created the contamination.

**CONCLUSION**

The 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for a CERCLA response action. Guam should be permitted to proceed with its timely Section 107 cost-recovery claim against the United States. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
