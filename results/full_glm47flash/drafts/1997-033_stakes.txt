**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Statutory Text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Original District for Trial**

The district court erred by retaining the case for trial under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The text of § 1407(a) is unambiguous: it requires the remand of transferred actions to their original districts for trial. The dissent correctly observes that the statute contains "a clear statutory command." Specifically, § 1407(a) provides that after pretrial proceedings are completed, "[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel." This mandate is not merely permissive; it is a mandatory instruction that the Multidistrict Litigation Panel must execute before a case proceeds to trial. By ignoring this command and retaining the case, the district court violated the plain language of the statute.

**II. The Scope of § 1407 Authority is Strictly Limited to Pretrial Proceedings**

The authority conferred upon the transferee court by § 1407(a) is limited to the duration of pretrial proceedings. The dissent correctly identifies that the purpose of § 1407 is to facilitate coordinated discovery and pretrial management, not to determine the forum for trial. As the dissent notes, "Pretrial proceedings under 1407 are obviously limited to those raising issues common to other cases, primarily discovery; that's the entire point of the multidistrict process." Because the statute explicitly limits the transferee court’s jurisdiction to pretrial matters, that court lacks the authority to retain the case for trial. When it comes to conducting a trial, plaintiffs retain their ancient right to select the forum. The district court’s attempt to retain the case under § 1404(a) effectively usurps this right and exceeds the scope of its statutory authority.

**III. The Practice of Retaining Cases Under § 1404(a) Subverts the Statutory Scheme**

The practice of transferee courts retaining transferred cases for trial under § 1404(a) is an illicit subversion of the statutory scheme. The dissent correctly characterizes this practice as "widely ignored" and "consistently wrong." Courts have long recognized that the statute does not authorize a transferee judge to transfer the action to his district for purposes of trial. By enshrining this unlawful practice into Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b), the Panel has exacerbated the problem. The time has come to put a stop to this illicit practice by saying loud and clear that the Multidistrict Panel assigns cases only for pretrial proceedings; it doesn't grant proprietary rights in them. The district court’s decision to retain the case for trial was a clear error of law that must be corrected.
