**ARGUMENT**

**Due process does not entitle a defendant to a freestanding right to use mental-disease evidence for any purpose he chooses; States retain broad authority to structure how such evidence is used in the criminal process.**

This Court has long recognized that “the definition of the elements of a criminal offense is entrusted to the legislature, particularly in the case of state crimes.” *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 210 (1977). Due process demands only that the prosecution “prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged.” *Id.* at 210 (quoting *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970)). Beyond that irreducible floor, this Court has consistently deferred to the States’ authority to allocate burdens of proof, define affirmative defenses, and channel the evidentiary uses of particular categories of proof. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952) (upholding Oregon’s requirement that insanity be proved beyond a reasonable doubt because the Due Process Clause does not put “a constitutional strait-jacket” on the States’ criminal law); *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 201–02. Arizona’s rule confining Clark’s expert testimony to his insanity defense falls squarely within that traditional latitude.

**A. This Court has already rejected the claim that due process guarantees a right to use mental-state evidence to negate intent apart from an insanity defense.**

In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the defendant sought to introduce psychiatric evidence of a mental abnormality short of legal insanity to negate premeditation and reduce a murder charge. This Court held that the jurisdiction’s refusal to recognize such a “partial responsibility” doctrine did not violate due process, explaining that the question of how mental-disease evidence bears on intent is “a question of legislative policy and not one of constitutional law.” *Id.* at 476. The Court left to the jurisdiction the choice whether to allow psychiatric evidence to operate outside the insanity framework at all.

*Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), confirms that States may categorically exclude a class of exculpatory mental-state evidence from the mens rea inquiry without offending due process. There, the plurality upheld a Montana statute barring evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate the mental state element of a crime, observing that “the near-unanimous rejection of [the] view” that due process requires admission of such evidence “is a strong indication that the [contrary] view is not . . . a fundamental principle of justice.” *Id.* at 48 (plurality opinion). The same logic controls here: Arizona’s decision to channel mental-disease evidence into the insanity inquiry, rather than allowing it to negate mens rea directly, reflects a permissible legislative judgment about how unreliable and diffuse psychiatric testimony should be weighed against the State’s interest in secure verdicts.

**B. Arizona’s channeling rule does not relieve, and does not purport to relieve, the State of its burden to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt.**

The due process inquiry asks only whether a state procedural rule “offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445 (1992) (internal quotation marks omitted). Arizona did not prevent the jury from hearing about Clark’s paranoid schizophrenia; it merely confined the use of that evidence to the insanity defense, on which Clark bore the burden by clear and convincing evidence. The jury remained free to consider observable evidence bearing on intent—Clark’s conduct before, during, and after the shooting—and the State remained bound to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt using that evidence. Nothing in this arrangement shifts the burden on any element of the offense; it simply reflects a rule of evidentiary organization, the sort of rule this Court has said is “the province of the States.” *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 201–02; see also *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535–36 (1968) (plurality opinion) (states have wide latitude to decide how psychiatric evidence bears on culpability).

**C. *Ake v. Oklahoma* does not support a right to use psychiatric evidence for whatever purpose the defendant prefers.**

*Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68 (1985), held that an indigent defendant is entitled to psychiatric assistance to prepare an insanity defense when sanity will be a significant factor at trial. *Id.* at 83. *Ake* concerned access to expert assistance, not the constitutional necessity of admitting that expert’s testimony for every legal purpose the defense might invent. Clark had, and used, precisely the access *Ake* guarantees: he received psychiatric evaluation and presented that evidence fully in support of his insanity defense. *Ake* says nothing about a right to redeploy that same evidence to negate mens rea once the insanity claim has been rejected as insufficiently proven.

**Conclusion**

Arizona’s channeling rule reflects a permissible line-drawing choice, consistent with *Fisher*, *Leland*, *Patterson*, and *Egelhoff*. The Due Process Clause does not create a separate, freestanding right to introduce mental-disease evidence to negate intent outside the insanity framework the State has chosen to adopt. The judgment below should be affirmed.
