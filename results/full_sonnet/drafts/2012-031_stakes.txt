**ARGUMENT**

**A district court may not certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) without admissible evidence establishing that damages are susceptible of measurement on a class-wide basis consistent with the class's liability theory.**

**I. Rule 23 demands rigorous, evidence-based proof, not mere assertion, that common questions predominate.**

This Court has made clear that "Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading standard." *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). A plaintiff seeking certification "must affirmatively demonstrate his compliance with the Rule," and "actual, not presumed, conformance" with Rule 23's requirements "remains ... indispensable." *Id.* at 350-51 (quoting *Gen. Tel. Co. of the Sw. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982)). This obligation necessarily "entail[s] some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim," because certification cannot rest on the pleadings alone. *Id.* at 351. The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) is no exception: it "tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation." *Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). Even the Third Circuit recognized below that "[t]he district court must conduct a 'rigorous analysis' of the evidence and arguments in making the class certification decision." *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). That analysis is not satisfied by an expert's bare assurance that a "common methodology" exists; it requires the court to determine, based on admissible evidence, that the methodology actually fits the case.

**II. Respondents' damages model was not tied to the sole theory of antitrust impact certified for class treatment, and therefore could not supply the common proof predominance requires.**

The courts below narrowed the class's theory of antitrust impact to a single ground: that Comcast's "anticompetitive clustering conduct" had "the effect ... to deter the entry of overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA." App. 00032. Yet respondents' expert, Dr. McClave, produced a regression model that calculated supra-competitive pricing resulting from *all four* of respondents' original theories of anticompetitive conduct—not damages flowing solely from deterred overbuilder entry. A model that cannot distinguish damages attributable to the certified theory from damages attributable to theories the court rejected is not "common evidence" of anything; it is evidence of an amalgam that no longer corresponds to the cause of action before the court.

This mismatch is fatal under Rule 23(b)(3). Predominance requires that the *same* evidence that proves classwide liability also be capable of proving classwide damages on that liability theory. Where, as here, the offered methodology does not isolate damages caused by the certified theory of impact, individual inquiries into which portion of a class member's alleged overcharge is attributable to the surviving theory—as opposed to the discarded ones—will overwhelm any common questions. A methodology that fails to measure damages "consistent with its liability case," rather than some broader or different theory, does not establish that damages can be proven on a classwide basis; it merely establishes that *some* damages number can be generated, without regard to whether that number bears any relationship to the wrong the class is permitted to litigate.

**III. Because the model was not admissible evidence tailored to the certified theory, the district court's finding of predominance was clearly erroneous.**

A finding of fact "completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility" cannot stand. *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972). Here, the only evidence offered to satisfy the predominance requirement as to damages was a model that Dr. McClave himself acknowledged did not isolate the overbuilder-deterrence theory from the three theories the district court had rejected. A model untethered to the certified theory of liability is not "evidentiary support" for the proposition that damages are measurable on a classwide basis with respect to *that* theory; it is evidence of a different case altogether. The district court therefore lacked an adequate evidentiary basis for its conclusion that "there is a common methodology available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis." *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010).

The Third Circuit compounded this error by treating the adequacy of the damages methodology as a question to be resolved with a lenient thumb on the scale in favor of certification, rather than subjecting it to the same "rigorous analysis" this Court demanded in *Wal-Mart*. But admissibility and fit are not merits questions to be deferred to trial; they are precisely the kind of threshold, fact-intensive determinations that *Falcon* and *Wal-Mart* require district courts to resolve, by a preponderance of admissible evidence, before certifying a class. 564 U.S. at 350-51; 457 U.S. at 161.

**Conclusion**

Because respondents failed to present admissible evidence that damages attributable to their sole surviving theory of antitrust impact are measurable on a classwide basis, the district court abused its discretion in certifying the class, and the Third Circuit erred in affirming. The judgment below should be reversed.
