**Argument**

### I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date.

Congress did not clearly provide that the reinstatement statute applies to aliens whose illegal reentry was completed before April 1, 1997. Section 241(a)(5) authorizes reinstatement of a prior removal order against an alien who “has reentered the United States illegally” and provides that the prior order “is reinstated from its original date and is not subject to being reopened or reviewed.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). But the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that courts must not give statutes retroactive effect absent clear congressional intent. Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994); INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 316-17 (2001).

The statute’s text speaks in the present perfect tense, but that grammatical choice does not clearly resolve whether Congress meant to reach completed pre-enactment reentries. In St. Cyr, the Court held that “the antiretroactivity principle” requires a clear statement before a statute is construed to attach new legal consequences to completed conduct. 533 U.S. at 316. Likewise, in Fernandez-Vargas’s case, the relevant conduct was his illegal reentry in 1982 and his ensuing decade-plus reliance on the legal regime then in force. Congress did not clearly state that such completed reentries would be swept into the new regime. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70.

That reading is reinforced by the statute’s structure. Section 241(a)(5) does not merely speed removal; it extinguishes eligibility for all INA relief. See Fernandez-Vargas v. Ashcroft, 394 F.3d 881, 884-86 (10th Cir. 2005). That is the kind of severe legal disability that courts do not impose retroactively without unmistakable direction. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321-24. Here, the government seeks to use the statute to foreclose adjustment of status based on marriage to a United States citizen—relief that was at least potentially available when Fernandez-Vargas reentered and lived openly in the United States for years. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255; St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 325.

### II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.

Even if the statute reaches pre-enactment reentries as a class, its application here is retroactive because it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 270. The question is whether the new statute impairs rights, increases liability, or imposes new duties with respect to past conduct. Id. at 280. It does.

Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry occurred in 1982, long before Congress enacted § 241(a)(5) in 1996. For nearly fifteen years after reentry, he remained in the United States under a legal regime that did not categorically foreclose adjustment and other forms of relief. He then married a United States citizen and sought adjustment of status, conduct that would have been materially meaningful under prior law. The government’s use of § 241(a)(5) to bar that relief is retroactive because it eliminates relief that was available, or at least potentially available, to him based on a past course of conduct. See St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321-25.

The Tenth Circuit erred in reasoning that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” of adjustment. Fernandez-Vargas v. Ashcroft, 394 F.3d at 889. Retroactivity analysis does not turn on a subjective entitlement or settled expectation alone; it turns on whether the statute changes the legal consequences of prior acts. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70. In St. Cyr, the Court held that repeal of discretionary relief was impermissibly retroactive when applied to aliens who had pleaded guilty before the change, because they had entered their plea in reliance on the availability of relief. 533 U.S. at 321-24. The same principle applies here. Fernandez-Vargas remained in the country for years under one legal regime and then pursued adjustment in reliance on the possibility of relief that Congress later extinguished. See Ponnapula v. Ashcroft, 373 F.3d 480, 492-95 (3d Cir. 2004).

The government may argue that Fernandez-Vargas’s continued illegal presence after 1997 makes the statute prospective. But the relevant past event is not merely his continued presence; it is his completed illegal reentry, which triggered a legal status that the new statute later converted into a permanent bar to relief. Applying the statute here therefore imposes a new disability on completed conduct. Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70; St. Cyr, 533 U.S. at 321.

Because Congress did not clearly make § 241(a)(5) applicable to pre-enactment reentries, and because applying it here would be impermissibly retroactive, the petition should be granted and the reinstatement order vacated.
