**Argument**

The judgment should be reversed because Feltmann failed to prove that Plaza Motors’ legitimate business decisions were a pretext for unlawful sex discrimination or retaliation. Under Title VII and the MHRA, the ultimate burden remained on Feltmann to show intentional discrimination or retaliatory motive. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); *Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253-56 (1981). Where the employer articulates lawful reasons for its actions, the plaintiff must prove those reasons were false and that discrimination was the real reason. *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 507-08, 515-17 (1993). The evidence here does not satisfy that standard.

### I. The record does not support a finding that Plaza’s sales-management decisions were sex discrimination.

Plaza’s proof of legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons was substantial. Rich testified that after the incentive program ended and overall sales declined, he increased scrutiny of the consultants and focused on sales technique and profitability. Feltmann’s own sales numbers deteriorated in average gross profit from December 1992 through April 1993, and Rich explained that her low profits reflected her failure to thoroughly explain vehicle features, which reduced the selling price. That is a business judgment the courts do not second-guess absent evidence of unlawful motive. See *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 259; *Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters*, 438 U.S. 567, 578 (1978).

The isolated female-related incidents do not establish discriminatory intent. Feltmann was the only female consultant, but that fact alone does not prove bias. See *Hazelwood Sch. Dist. v. United States*, 433 U.S. 299, 307-08 (1977). Her complaint about Anzalone was promptly reported upward, investigated, and addressed: the owners met with Feltmann, asked whether she wanted him fired, reprimanded him, and warned that a repeat offense would result in termination. An employer that promptly responds to a complaint does not thereby admit discrimination. See *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 807-08 (1998) (recognizing the significance of prompt remedial action).

Nor does the evidence of a “personal memo” about work ethic and guest drives show sex discrimination. Rich had observed declining sales and believed Feltmann was not a team player and was using guest drives too early and too often. Those are facially neutral concerns tied to sales performance. Under Title VII, the question is not whether Rich was correct, but whether the stated reasons were a cover for sex bias. *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 515. Feltmann offered no evidence that similarly situated male consultants with comparable sales and profitability were treated more favorably on these points. See *St. Mary’s Honor Ctr.*, 509 U.S. at 510-11.

The Chicago sales-competition decision likewise does not establish disparate treatment. Rich offered the trip to the top two consultants; when they declined, he did not then offer it to Feltmann or to other male consultants who wanted to go. That sequence defeats any inference that the decision singled out women for worse treatment. See *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 258. At most, Feltmann showed dissatisfaction with Rich’s discretionary allocation of a sales incentive, not sex discrimination. See *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 515.

The demo-car restriction is equally lawful on this record. Rich restricted demo use to the St. Louis area after becoming concerned about dealership costs. Feltmann admitted she continued driving to Marion weekly despite the restriction and despite a follow-up memo. An employer may enforce a neutral business restriction against an employee who violates it. See *McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804. The fact that no male consultants received the memo does not establish discrimination absent proof that similarly situated men were actually violating the restriction and were spared discipline. See *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 258.

### II. The retaliation claim fails because the evidence does not show a materially adverse, causally linked retaliatory action.

To establish retaliation, Feltmann had to prove protected activity, an adverse action, and a causal connection. *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006); *Brooks v. Woodline Motor Freight, Inc.*, 852 F.2d 1061, 1064-65 (8th Cir. 1988). Her complaint about Anzalone was protected activity, but the alleged retaliatory acts are insufficient.

The January memo, the probation, the demo restriction, and the Chicago trip decision were all tied to performance and business concerns. Temporal proximity alone does not establish retaliation where the employer has legitimate reasons for its conduct. See *Kipp v. Missouri Highway & Transp. Comm’n*, 280 F.3d 893, 897 (8th Cir. 2002). Rich had already begun evaluating consultants more closely because sales had fallen. Feltmann’s own low average gross profit, admitted violation of the demo-car policy, and declining sales furnished independent bases for management action.

Further, the owners’ response to the Anzalone complaint undercuts any inference of retaliation. They investigated, reprimanded the offender, and told Feltmann to report future incidents. Where an employer promptly addresses a complaint, a later adverse-action theory requires stronger proof than mere suspicion. See *Faragher*, 524 U.S. at 806-08. Feltmann presented no such proof.

### III. The verdict cannot stand because the evidence permits only a business-dispute, not unlawful motive.

A plaintiff must prove more than that the employer made hard or even mistaken decisions; she must show intentional discrimination. *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 511. On this record, Plaza offered legitimate reasons for each challenged action, and Feltmann did not carry her burden of proving those reasons were pretextual and that sex or retaliation was the real motive. The judgment should therefore be reversed.
