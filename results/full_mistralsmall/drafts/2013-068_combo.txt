**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Content-Neutral, Viewpoint-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation That Satisfies the First Amendment.**

The First Circuit correctly held that the Massachusetts statute is a permissible regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (citing *Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness*, 452 U.S. 640, 648 (1981)). Such regulations are valid if they (1) are content-neutral, (2) are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Clark v. Cmty. for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984) (citing *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968)).

The statute is content-neutral because it does not discriminate based on the subject matter of speech or the speaker’s viewpoint. *Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95 (1972) (holding that a ban on picketing near schools was content-neutral where it applied regardless of the message). The law applies equally to all speakers, whether pro-life or pro-choice, and does not single out any particular message for disfavored treatment. *Forsyth Cty. v. Nationalist Movement*, 505 U.S. 123, 134 (1992) (invalidating a permit fee that varied based on the content of speech). The statute’s exemptions—covering patients, employees, and municipal agents—are viewpoint-neutral because they are based on function, not message. *Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 651 (upholding a content-neutral permit requirement for fairgrounds).

The statute is narrowly tailored to serve the Commonwealth’s significant interest in public safety and orderly access to medical facilities. *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 86 (1949) (upholding a ban on sound trucks to prevent street congestion and disorder). The 35-foot buffer zone is no broader than necessary to protect patients and clinic staff from harassment, intimidation, and interference. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 554 (1965) (upholding a breach-of-peace conviction where protesters obstructed access to a courthouse). The statute does not prohibit all speech in the vicinity of clinics, only targeted conduct within the buffer zone. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966) (upholding a trespass conviction where protesters refused to leave a jail driveway).

Ample alternative channels for communication remain available. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939) (holding that a ban on leafleting in public streets was invalid where no adequate alternatives existed). The plaintiffs may still speak, distribute literature, and engage in prayer outside the buffer zone. *Martin v. City of Struthers*, 319 U.S. 141, 148 (1943) (invalidating a ban on door-to-door canvassing where alternatives were inadequate). The statute does not prevent the plaintiffs from reaching their intended audience; it merely requires them to do so at a reasonable distance. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (holding that a permit requirement for religious solicitation was valid where it did not foreclose all communication).

**II. The First Circuit Correctly Relied on *Hill v. Colorado* and That Decision Should Not Be Overruled.**

The First Circuit properly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which upheld a similar buffer-zone statute. *Hill* is controlling precedent and should not be overruled. *Smith v. Allwright*, 321 U.S. 649, 665 (1944) (stating that settled precedent should not be disturbed absent compelling reasons). The plaintiffs’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* is unavailing. *Hill* expressly rejected the argument that buffer zones violate the First Amendment, holding that they are permissible time-place-manner regulations. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719 (citing *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791).

The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Schneider*, *Martin*, and *Cantwell* is misplaced. Those cases involved outright bans on speech in quintessential public forums, not narrowly tailored buffer zones. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 163; *Martin*, 319 U.S. at 148; *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308. In contrast, the Massachusetts statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

**III. The First Circuit Did Not Err in Upholding the Statute as Applied.**

The plaintiffs’ as-applied challenge fails because the statute does not prevent them from engaging in effective speech. *Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 654 (upholding a permit requirement where alternatives remained available). The plaintiffs may still speak, distribute literature, and pray outside the buffer zone. *Martin*, 319 U.S. at 148. The statute does not foreclose all communication; it merely regulates the manner in which speech occurs. *O’Brien*, 391 U.S. at 377.

The plaintiffs’ claim that the buffer zone impedes their ability to communicate is speculative. *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47 (upholding a trespass conviction where protesters obstructed access). The statute does not prevent the plaintiffs from reaching their intended audience; it merely requires them to do so at a reasonable distance. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 308.

**Conclusion**

The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a valid time-place-manner regulation that satisfies the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld it, and *Hill v. Colorado* remains controlling precedent. The judgment below should be affirmed.
