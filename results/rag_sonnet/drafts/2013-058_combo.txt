ARGUMENT

I. The Exemption for "Retirement Funds" Turns on the Present Character of the Account, Not Its Origin

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds" from the claims of creditors. That exemption, like every exemption Congress has written into the Code, protects a defined category of property because of what the property presently is and does—not because of what it once was in someone else's hands. This Court has consistently held that entitlement to a bankruptcy exemption depends on the debtor's own relationship to the property at the time the statutory conditions must be satisfied. In Myers v. Matley, 318 U.S. 622 (1943), the Court examined whether a debtor's spouse could claim a homestead exemption, focusing on when the homestead declaration was filed and whether the property functioned as the claimant's home at the relevant time—not merely on the property's historical use by the couple. The inherited IRA here fails that test: by the time the Clarks filed their petition, the account no longer functioned as retirement savings for anyone. It was, in the Seventh Circuit's words, "an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings."

This Court's decision in Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), confirms that exemption analysis must attend to the actual character of the property claimed, because erroneously admitting non-qualifying property into the protected category works an "irremediable" harm to creditors by removing assets from the estate that should have been available to satisfy claims. The inverse is equally true: erroneously exempting an inherited IRA—an account that must be drained within five years and that generates ordinary taxable income upon withdrawal rather than deferred retirement growth—would work exactly the harm Lockwood warned against, shielding a pool of freely spendable cash from the creditors who are otherwise entitled to reach it.

II. Bankruptcy Exemptions and Related Statutory Categories Are Construed According to Substance and Purpose, Not Labels

This Court has repeatedly refused to let a statutory label control where the underlying economic reality diverges from the purpose the label is meant to signal. In Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States, 391 U.S. 224 (1968), the Court held that employer contributions to a "welfare fund" were not entitled to priority because the payments "did not satisfy the manifest purpose" of the priority provision, even though the fund carried the name and trappings of an employee-benefit account. The same logic controls here. The inherited IRA carries the label "IRA," but the label does not satisfy the purpose behind §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—shielding funds "set aside for retirement," not funds that federal tax law affirmatively requires be distributed and consumed within a fixed, short period regardless of the recipient's age or proximity to retirement.

This Court's decision in Kaufman v. Tredway, 195 U.S. 271 (1904), likewise instructs that Congress conditions favorable bankruptcy treatment on the genuine presence of the purpose the statute is designed to serve, not on the formal characteristics of a transaction. There, the Court explained that the preference provisions required that transferred property "in fact become a part of the debtor's estate," rejecting arrangements that satisfied the letter of the statute while defeating its object. So too here: an inherited IRA satisfies the letter of the Internal Revenue Code's IRA provisions, but it does not serve the object of the Bankruptcy Code's retirement exemption, because the beneficiary cannot hold the funds until retirement even if she wished to.

Similarly, in McKee v. Paradise, 299 U.S. 119 (1936), this Court recognized that a fund's claim to special treatment in bankruptcy depends on whether the fund can still be identified and traced as bearing the character that entitles it to that treatment, not merely on its remote origin. Once Ruth Heffron's IRA passed to her daughter, it lost the identifying attributes of a retirement account—no further contributions, no discretion to defer withdrawal past five years, no connection to the beneficiary's own working life—just as surely as commingled funds in McKee lost their traceable trust character.

Finally, this Court's decisions in Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358 (1964), and Boteler v. Ingels, 308 U.S. 57 (1939), confirm that provisions relieving debtors of ordinary obligations are not stretched beyond their text merely because a broader reading would further the general policy of debtor relief. Bruning declined to treat post-petition tax interest as discharged despite the Bankruptcy Act's general solicitude for debtors, and Boteler construed the discharge provision according to its terms rather than an asserted underlying purpose. The same restraint applies here: whatever savings incentive might be served by exempting inherited IRAs, "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs," and §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) does not exempt assets that are not, in the debtor's own hands, retirement funds at all.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
