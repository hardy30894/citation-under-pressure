Respondent has no need to dispute the Third Circuit’s standing analysis on the merits of a Tenth Amendment claim. Under controlling Supreme Court authority, a private defendant may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to contest a federal criminal prosecution absent a state objection. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-144, 59 S. Ct. 366, 83 L. Ed. 543 (1939), the Court rejected a private utility’s attempt to challenge federal action as an invasion of state authority, explaining that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144. That principle forecloses Bond’s attack.

The same conclusion follows from the Court’s broader federalism cases. The Tenth Amendment “is but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered,” United States v. Darby, 312 U.S. 100, 124, 61 S. Ct. 451, 85 L. Ed. 609 (1941), and it operates as a structural limit on federal-state relations, not as a personal defense to criminal liability. In Asbell v. Kansas, 209 U.S. 251, 256, 28 S. Ct. 507, 52 L. Ed. 778 (1908), the Court sustained a state health regulation against federal-preemption arguments and recognized that federalism doctrines concern “the powers of the States” and the distribution of authority among sovereigns. Likewise, in Champion v. Ames, 188 U.S. 321, 357-363, 23 S. Ct. 321, 47 L. Ed. 492 (1903), the Court upheld federal criminal legislation regulating interstate commerce notwithstanding objections that it intruded into local police powers. These decisions confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether Congress acted within its enumerated authority, not whether an individual defendant prefers state prosecution.

Bond’s contrary theory would convert the Tenth Amendment into a roving defense for any criminal defendant who believes Congress should have left the matter to the States. That is not the law. The Court has long held that where Congress legislates within a delegated power, federal statutes are valid even when they incidentally affect matters traditionally handled by the States. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421, 4 L. Ed. 579 (1819) (“Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate” are constitutional). And in Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432-433, 40 S. Ct. 382, 64 L. Ed. 641 (1920), the Court upheld treaty-implementing legislation notwithstanding federalism objections, emphasizing that if a treaty is valid, Congress may enact legislation as a “necessary and proper” means to execute it. Bond’s claim is therefore doubly defective: the federal statute under which she was convicted was enacted to implement a treaty obligation, and the Tenth Amendment does not afford a private litigant standing to resist that enactment.

Nor can Bond avoid Tennessee Electric by recasting her argument as a challenge to the scope of federal criminal power. The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to invalidate federal action merely because it reaches subjects ordinarily within state regulation. In Hoke v. United States, 227 U.S. 308, 320-323, 33 S. Ct. 281, 57 L. Ed. 523 (1913), the Court upheld a federal criminal statute against claims that it invaded state police power, explaining that when Congress acts pursuant to a constitutional grant, “the States are not prohibited from action” but federal law remains supreme within its sphere. Similarly, in Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 491-497, 37 S. Ct. 192, 61 L. Ed. 442 (1917), the Court sustained federal criminal enforcement even though the conduct was morally and locally regulated, reinforcing that Congress may punish conduct Congress has validly reached.

Bond’s reliance on the Tenth Amendment also fails because the Amendment restrains governmental power, not private litigants’ strategic preferences. As the Court explained in Frothingham v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447, 486-489, 43 S. Ct. 597, 67 L. Ed. 1078 (1923), a litigant may not assert generalized grievances about federal action that do not invade a personal legal right. Bond has no personal Tenth Amendment right to demand state rather than federal prosecution. At most, she complains that Congress should have left the matter to the States. That is a structural objection that only a State may press.

Because Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds, the Court should affirm. Tennessee Electric Power Co., 306 U.S. at 144. Even if the Court were to reach the merits, the conviction should still be sustained because Congress may enact legislation necessary and proper to execute a valid treaty. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. at 432-433. The judgment should be affirmed.
