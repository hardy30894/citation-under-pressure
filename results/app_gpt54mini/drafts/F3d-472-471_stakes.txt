**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Viewed in Burnett’s favor, the record would allow a reasonable jury to find that Habitat had notice sufficient to trigger its obligations under both the FMLA and the ADA, and that Habitat’s subsequent discipline and constructive discharge were unlawful. Summary judgment is proper only when, drawing all reasonable inferences for the nonmovant, no genuine dispute of material fact exists. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248–52 (1986).

I. **A reasonable jury could find that Burnett gave Habitat notice sufficient to invoke the FMLA.**

An employee need not invoke the statute or use magic words to request FMLA leave. The employee need only provide enough information for the employer to reasonably understand that the employee may need leave for a potentially FMLA-qualifying condition. *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d 471, 478–79 (7th Cir. 2006); *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d 471, 478 (7th Cir. 2006) (recognizing that notice may be informal and that the employee need not expressly cite the FMLA). The question is whether the employer had enough information to place it on notice of a possible serious health condition. *Id.*; *Pagel v. TIN Inc.*, 695 F.3d 622, 629 (7th Cir. 2012).

Here, Burnett repeatedly told Habitat that he was “sick,” that he had a “weak bladder,” that he was seeing doctors, and that he was being evaluated for a prostate problem. He told Polo he had undergone testing, that his PSA was elevated, that he had upcoming urology appointments, and that he was scheduled for a prostate biopsy. He also gave Habitat documentation describing the biopsy and stating that the procedure was indicated by an abnormal exam or elevated PSA. A jury could readily infer that this information signaled a possible serious health condition involving continuing treatment and invasive diagnostic procedures. *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d at 479–80; *Cavin v. Honda of America Manufacturing, Inc.*, 346 F.3d 713, 723–24 (6th Cir. 2003) (notice sufficient where employee communicated hospitalization and ongoing treatment).

The district court’s contrary conclusion improperly required Burnett to diagnose himself and to prove FMLA eligibility before requesting leave. But the FMLA’s notice regime is designed to avoid that trap. *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d at 478–80; *Stevenson v. Hyre Electric Co.*, 505 F.3d 720, 726–27 (7th Cir. 2007). Once Burnett informed Habitat of his recurring prostate-related problems, medical appointments, and biopsy, the issue of whether he in fact had a “serious health condition” was for the jury, not summary judgment. *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d at 480.

II. **The ADA claim likewise survived because Habitat had notice of Burnett’s impairment and need for accommodation.**

The ADA requires only that the employer know of the employee’s disability and need for accommodation; the employee need not utter the word “accommodation” if the circumstances reasonably alert the employer to the need. *Hedberg v. Indiana Bell Telephone Co., Inc.*, 47 F.3d 928, 934–35 (7th Cir. 1995); *Jovanovic v. In-Sink-Erator Division of Emhart Industries, Inc.*, 201 F.3d 894, 899–900 (7th Cir. 2000). An employer cannot avoid liability by ignoring obvious signs of impairment. *Hedberg*, 47 F.3d at 934.

Burnett told Polo that he had a “weak bladder” and declined a transfer because it would reduce restroom access. He later explained that he was seeing a doctor because of a prostate-related condition, that he had elevated PSA results, and that he would need additional medical appointments and a biopsy. A jury could conclude that these statements, together with the requested shift transfer and his need for restroom access, put Habitat on notice that Burnett had a medical impairment affecting a major life activity and needed workplace accommodation. See *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d at 480–81; *Squibb v. Memorial Medical Center*, 497 F.3d 775, 783–84 (7th Cir. 2007).

The district court’s ruling effectively imposed a pleading requirement on the employee’s oral communications. That is not the law. The ADA asks whether the employer had enough information to understand that assistance may be required. *Jovanovic*, 201 F.3d at 899–900; *EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co.*, 417 F.3d 789, 804–05 (7th Cir. 2005). On this record, Habitat could not reasonably claim ignorance.

III. **Because notice was a disputed factual issue, summary judgment was improper.**

This Court has already recognized in Burnett’s own case that the evidence of notice must be evaluated in the light most favorable to him, and that a jury could credit his testimony about what he told Habitat concerning his prostate condition and need for leave. *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d at 478–81. The same evidence supports the inference that Habitat’s escalating reprimands and suspension occurred after it learned of Burnett’s medical problems. At minimum, disputed issues of intent, causation, and credibility preclude summary judgment. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255; *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d at 481–82.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
