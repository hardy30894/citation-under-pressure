### ARGUMENT

#### I. Riddle was denied due process because the elected prosecutor became a witness for the State and then continued to direct the prosecution.

The Due Process Clause guarantees a fundamentally fair trial. When the government’s lead advocate personally injects himself into the proof as a witness, the danger is not merely impropriety; it is the collapse of the adversarial process itself. See *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935); *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 7–8 (1985). The Fifth Circuit has recognized that prosecutorial conduct must be assessed in light of whether it “so infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process.” *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974); *Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 181 (1986).

That standard was violated here. The chief prosecutor, Danny Hill, was not a peripheral witness; he was the elected district attorney and chief trial prosecutor. Riddle testified that Hill told him he would do his “damnest” to see that Riddle received the death penalty. Hill then called witnesses to rebut that testimony and took the stand himself. Yet after becoming a witness, he continued to prosecute the case. The assistant prosecutor then used Hill’s testimony in closing argument to portray Riddle as a liar. This is precisely the type of official dual role condemned by the Supreme Court’s due process cases. See *Young*, 470 U.S. at 9–10; *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88.

The unfairness is heightened because Hill’s testimony went directly to credibility, a central issue at trial. Riddle’s defense depended in substantial part on his account of the events inside Bennett’s home. By testifying and then resuming his role as the State’s advocate, Hill placed the prestige of his office behind the State’s version of the facts and gave the jury a reason to credit the prosecution’s narrative. That is incompatible with the “rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935); *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959). At minimum, the use of Hill as both witness and prosecutor created a constitutionally intolerable risk that the verdict was influenced by the prosecutor’s personal credibility rather than the evidence. See *Young*, 470 U.S. at 18–19.

The state courts’ rejection of this claim was an unreasonable application of clearly established federal law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), habeas relief is warranted where the state court unreasonably applies Supreme Court precedent to the facts. *Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 362, 407–09 (2000). No fair-minded application of *Berger*, *Young*, and *Darden* could uphold a capital conviction obtained after the chief prosecutor testified about the defendant’s credibility and then remained in charge of the case.

#### II. The district court erred in denying an evidentiary hearing on Riddle’s constitutional claims.

A habeas petitioner is entitled to a federal evidentiary hearing when he alleges facts that, if proven, would entitle him to relief and the state court did not afford a full and fair hearing. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312–13 (1963); *Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes*, 504 U.S. 1, 11–12 (1992). Although AEDPA limits hearings in some circumstances, a hearing remains appropriate where the petitioner shows diligence and the record does not resolve the factual dispute. *Williams*, 529 U.S. at 437; *Cullen v. Pinholster*, 563 U.S. 170, 185–86 (2011) (recognizing the centrality of the record before the state court, while preserving the rule that relief depends on the adequacy of existing factfinding).

Riddle repeatedly sought a hearing below and supported his request with a supplemental memorandum. The record, however, does not resolve critical issues bearing on prejudice: what Hill said before testifying, why he remained in the case afterward, what role he played in closing argument, and how the jury perceived his dual role. Those matters are material because the constitutional error turns on the effect of the prosecutor’s conduct on the fairness of the trial. See *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 313; *Schriro v. Landrigan*, 550 U.S. 465, 474 (2007) (hearing unnecessary only when the record conclusively forecloses relief).

The district court’s summary judgment ruling therefore rested on an incomplete factual record. In a capital case, where reliability is paramount, federal courts must ensure that constitutional claims are not rejected without the development of facts essential to their adjudication. See *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 411–12 (1986); *Miller-El v. Dretke*, 545 U.S. 231, 240 (2005). Riddle was entitled to present evidence on the prosecutor’s improper dual role and the resulting prejudice.

#### III. Relief is required because the constitutional error was not harmless.

Even constitutional error may be deemed harmless only if it did not have a “substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury’s verdict.” *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 623 (1993). The State cannot satisfy that standard here. The prosecutor’s testimony and continuing participation struck at the heart of the case by attacking Riddle’s credibility in a capital murder trial where his account of consent, entry, and intent mattered greatly. Given the centrality of the disputed facts and the State’s reliance on Hill’s personal rebuttal, the error cannot be dismissed as harmless. See *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946); *Brecht*, 507 U.S. at 637.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment below, remand for an evidentiary hearing, and grant habeas relief.
