The Ohio Adult Parole Authority's clemency procedure does not implicate a protected life or liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause. As the Supreme Court held in Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), a state's clemency procedure does not create a federally protected liberty interest merely because it is a part of the state's system for determining the severity of punishment. Id. at 464-65. The Court in Dumschat rejected the argument that a prisoner's liberty interest is implicated when a state parole board denies commutation without explanation, holding that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." Id. at 464.

Respondent's argument that Ohio's clemency procedure is an "integral part" of the state's adjudicatory process, and therefore subject to due process scrutiny under Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), is unpersuasive. Evitts involved a state's appellate procedure, which is fundamentally different from a clemency proceeding. The Supreme Court has never extended Evitts to clemency proceedings, and doing so would be inconsistent with the historical understanding that clemency decisions are a matter of executive discretion. See Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 464.

Furthermore, the Ohio clemency procedure does not violate Respondent's Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination. The regulation governing the clemency interview explicitly states that the prisoner is not required to answer any questions. Ohio Rev.Code § 2967.07; see also Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 317-18 (1976) (holding that a state may draw adverse inferences from a prisoner's silence in a disciplinary hearing). Respondent's claim that the clemency procedure presents a "Hobson's choice" between participating in the interview and asserting his Fifth Amendment privilege is unfounded, as the regulation does not compel him to incriminate himself.

The Court of Appeals' remand for further consideration of Respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is premature, as any potential self-incrimination is speculative and may never occur. See Baxter, 425 U.S. at 324-28 (holding that a district court's order requiring a state to provide immunity to a prisoner was premature). The district court should be instructed to dismiss Respondent's complaint, as Ohio's clemency procedure does not implicate a protected life or liberty interest and does not violate Respondent's Fifth Amendment rights.
