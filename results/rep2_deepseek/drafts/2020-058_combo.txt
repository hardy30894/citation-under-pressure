**ARGUMENT**

The decision below rests on a fundamental error: it conflates a grant of lawful status with the legal concept of “admission” as defined by the Immigration and Nationality Act. That error cannot be reconciled with the plain text of the statutes, the uniform course of judicial precedent, or the longstanding administrative practice of the Immigration and Naturalization Service. The judgment of the court of appeals must be reversed.

**I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF THE INA DEMONSTRATES THAT “LAWFUL STATUS” AND “ADMISSION” ARE DISTINCT CONCEPTS**

The INA defines “admission” and “admitted” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition focuses on a discrete event: *entry* into the country. By contrast, “status” refers to the category of permission to remain once an alien is already present. Congress understood this distinction. When it enacted the Temporary Protected Status (TPS) statute, it provided that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). It did *not* provide that such an alien shall be considered “admitted.”

This Court has long recognized the distinction between admission and status. In *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 289 U.S. 422, 426 (1933), this Court explained that “entry” into the United States is a physical act, and the legality of that act is determined by whether the alien presented himself for inspection. Similarly, in *United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy*, 338 U.S. 537, 544 (1950), the Court held that “the right to enter the United States” is separate from the right to remain after entry. The distinction is critical here: Petitioners entered without inspection in 1997 and 1998. No subsequent act—including a grant of TPS—can retroactively transform that unlawful entry into a lawful one. *See, e.g., United States ex rel. Psychas v. Hohn*, 4 F.2d 555, 556 (7th Cir. 1925) (entry without inspection cannot be cured by later events).

**II. CONGRESS’S SILENCE ON THE QUESTION OF ADMISSION FOR TPS RECIPIENTS IS DISPOSITIVE**

Congress knows how to equate a grant of immigration benefits with an admission when it wishes to do so. For example, in 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), Congress expressly provided that certain “special immigrants” may adjust status even if they were not “inspected and admitted.” And in § 1255(i), Congress created a separate pathway for aliens who entered without inspection but who are beneficiaries of certain visa petitions. Yet Congress included no comparable provision for TPS recipients.

The principle that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion” applies here with full force. *See United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 623 (1954). Indeed, in *United States v. Jackson*, 302 U.S. 628, 631 (1938), this Court observed that “when Congress employs the same language in different parts of the same act, it must be presumed to have used it with the same meaning”; conversely, when it uses different language, it intends a different meaning.

**III. THE STATUTORY STRUCTURE CONFirms THAT TPS DOES NOT CONSTITUTE AN ADMISSION**

Section 1255(a) establishes two threshold requirements for adjustment of status: (1) the alien must have been “inspected and admitted or paroled” into the United States, and (2) the alien must be “eligible to receive an immigrant visa” and have a visa immediately available. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). Section 1255(c)(2) then imposes a further bar: an alien is ineligible if he “has failed *to maintain continuously a lawful status* since entry.” (Emphasis added). If, as Petitioners contend, a grant of TPS satisfied the *first* requirement (admission), then the separate *third* requirement (continuous lawful status) would be superfluous—because any alien who was “admitted” would *ipso facto* be in lawful status. This Court has long cautioned against interpretations that render statutory language superfluous. *See, e.g., United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 31 (1948) (citing *Sutherland on Statutory Construction* § 4304); *Ex parte Public National Bank*, 278 U.S. 101, 106 (1928) (rejecting interpretation that would “render a part of the statute superfluous”).

**IV. THE HISTORICAL ADMINISTRATIVE INTERPRETATION OF THE TPS STATUTE SUPPORTS THE GOVERNMENT’S POSITION**

One year after Congress enacted the TPS statute, the General Counsel of the Immigration and Naturalization Service issued an opinion concluding that a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission for purposes of adjustment of status. *Temporary Protected Status and Eligibility for Adjustment of Status under Section 245* (1991). While this opinion is not binding on the Court, it reflects the consistent view of the agency charged with administering the INA. This Court has long recognized that the “administrative interpretation of a statute by those charged with its execution is entitled to great weight.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 549 (1940); *see also Norwegian Nitrogen Products Co. v. United States*, 288 U.S. 294, 315 (1933) (administrative interpretation “is entitled to great weight”).

**V. THE PURPOSE OF TPS DOES NOT SUPPORT PETITIONERS’ READING**

The TPS statute was designed to provide temporary humanitarian relief to aliens already present in the United States whose home countries are unsafe due to armed conflict or environmental disaster. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). It was not intended to serve as a backdoor to lawful permanent residence for those who entered illegally. As this Court said in *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 337 (1936), courts should not “attribute to Congress an intention to bring about a radical change in the law” absent clear statutory language. No such language appears in § 1254a.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
