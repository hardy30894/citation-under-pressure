ARGUMENT

I. The Confusion Created By This Court’s Recall Order and the Clerk’s Statement to Buckles’s Sister Warrants Treating Buckles’s Certiorari Petition as Timely.

A federal conviction becomes final, for purposes of computing the one-year limitations period under 28 U.S.C. § 2255(f)(1), when “the time for filing a certiorari petition expires.” Clay v. United States, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003). Ordinarily that period is ninety days from denial of rehearing. See Sup. Ct. R. 13(1), (3). Buckles does not dispute that, absent tolling, his petition — filed September 5, 2007 — came more than sixty days after the July 2 deadline calculated from this Court’s April 3 denial of rehearing.

But Buckles reasonably relied on information from this Court itself. On June 4, 2007, an employee of the Clerk’s office told Buckles’s sister that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” That representation, combined with this Court’s subsequent order recalling the mandate on June 12 and reissuing it on June 20, gave Buckles good reason to believe that the certiorari clock ran anew from the recall. While this Court has since made clear that a Rule 4-1(e) motion does not itself extend the certiorari deadline, the rule was not so unambiguous in 2007 that a pro se, incarcerated litigant should be charged with divining otherwise once he had been told the opposite by the Clerk’s own staff. Cf. Bowles v. Russell, 551 U.S. 205, 214 (2007) (recognizing that clarity in appellate deadlines matters precisely because litigants and courts must be able to rely on them). Buckles relied on the clarity he was given — even though it later proved wrong.

II. In the Alternative, Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling of the § 2255 Limitations Period.

If this Court concludes that the certiorari petition, and therefore the § 2255 motion, was untimely as a matter of calculation, Buckles is nonetheless entitled to equitable tolling. A habeas petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling if he shows “(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way and prevented timely filing.” Holland v. Florida, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010) (quoting Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005)). Equitable tolling is available even against the government where a claimant has been “induced or tricked by his adversary’s misconduct into allowing” a filing deadline to pass. Irwin v. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990).

Buckles satisfies both parts of the Holland test.

First, extraordinary circumstances impeded timely filing. Buckles’s retained counsel was obligated under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e) to “assist the client in preparing and filing a motion for appointment of counsel,” yet counsel told Buckles only that he would “check into the procedure” and never followed up despite Buckles’s repeated attempts to reach him. An attorney’s failure to perform the basic services the client engaged him to perform — indeed, services a court rule specifically obligated him to provide — is the kind of misconduct that courts have found sufficient to toll a filing deadline. See Spitsyn v. Moore, 345 F.3d 796, 800–01 (9th Cir. 2003) (attorney’s failure to prepare and file a petition despite being retained to do so was “sufficiently egregious” to warrant equitable tolling); Baldayaque v. United States, 338 F.3d 145, 152 (2d Cir. 2003) (attorney conduct that is “far outside the range of behavior that could be expected” from counsel may constitute an extraordinary circumstance). Layered atop counsel’s abandonment was the Clerk’s office’s own misstatement to Buckles’s sister about how the certiorari clock would run. This Court has recognized that a habeas petitioner’s reliance on erroneous information from an official source can itself amount to an extraordinary circumstance justifying equitable tolling. See Stillman v. LaMarque, 319 F.3d 1199, 1203 (9th Cir. 2003) (equitable tolling appropriate where prison official’s conduct was “the cause of” the petitioner’s failure to timely file). The combination of counsel’s abandonment and the Clerk’s inaccurate representation is at least as compelling as the circumstances in Spitsyn and Stillman.

Second, Buckles diligently pursued his rights. He wrote to counsel on the twenty-first day after rehearing was denied — the very deadline set by Rule 4-1(e) — asking for help preparing a certiorari-counsel motion. When counsel did not respond, Buckles sent this Court his own “Supplemental Motion for Appointment of Counsel” within days, and had his sister call the Clerk’s office to check on the motion’s status. He then filed his certiorari petition, pro se, promptly after this Court denied his motion and the mandate reissued. This is precisely the sort of persistent effort to protect one’s rights, notwithstanding obstacles not of his own making, that satisfies the diligence prong of the equitable-tolling standard. See Miranda v. Castro, 292 F.3d 1063, 1066 (9th Cir. 2002) (equitable tolling turns on whether “extraordinary circumstances” beyond the prisoner’s control made it impossible to file on time despite diligence).

Because Buckles has alleged facts that, if true, satisfy both prongs of Holland, this Court should, at minimum, remand for an evidentiary hearing to resolve the factual disputes concerning counsel’s conduct and the Clerk’s office’s representation to Buckles’s sister, rather than affirm dismissal of his § 2255 motion as untimely on the present record.
