The petitioners argue that their constitutional challenge to the school policy is not moot because they seek nominal damages. However, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a claim for nominal damages alone is insufficient to save an otherwise moot constitutional challenge.

The mootness doctrine is rooted in the principle that federal courts may only decide cases or controversies. As the Supreme Court held in Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. The Court further explained in Barker v. Wise, 75 U.S. 729 (1869), that a court's decision should not be rendered unnecessary by a change in circumstances.

Here, the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot because the school revised its policies, rendering the challenged provisions no longer in effect. The petitioners' graduation from the school also moots their claims for prospective relief, as they are no longer subject to the school's policies. See Jones v. Montague, 194 U.S. 147 (1904) (holding that a case becomes moot when the plaintiff is no longer affected by the challenged law or policy).

The petitioners' claim for nominal damages does not save their otherwise moot claims. As the Court held in Mills v. Green, a claim for damages is not sufficient to maintain a suit when the underlying controversy has become moot. The Court has long recognized that a claim for damages must be tied to a live controversy, and when the controversy becomes moot, the claim for damages is insufficient to keep the case alive. See Barker v. Wise.

The petitioners' attempt to distinguish their case from others is unavailing. The fact that they brought an as-applied challenge, in addition to a facial challenge, does not change the analysis. The Court has not drawn a distinction between facial and as-applied challenges in the context of mootness.

The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the petitioners leave to amend their complaint. The petitioners failed to properly request leave to amend, instead burying their request in a response to the respondents' motion to dismiss. See United States v. Newbury, 123 F.2d 453 (2d Cir. 1941) (holding that a request to amend must be made by motion, not by implication or inference).

For these reasons, the Eleventh Circuit's decision should be affirmed, and the petitioners' claims should be dismissed as moot.
