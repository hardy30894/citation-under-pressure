**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding. The Clause by its terms protects against being twice put in jeopardy “for the same offence,” U.S. Const. amend. V, and this Court has repeatedly recognized that it is directed to successive prosecutions for an offense, not to the full range of sentencing factfinding that follows a valid conviction.

This Court has long distinguished trial from sentencing. In **Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949)**, the Court upheld a sentence based on information far beyond the trial record and rejected the notion that constitutional trial safeguards govern sentencing. The Court explained that the “due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” Id. at 251. That principle forecloses respondent’s double jeopardy claim here, because the proceeding at issue was not a new prosecution for a substantive offense; it was a sentencing determination concerning the consequence of a prior conviction.

The same understanding appears in **Berman v. United States, 302 U.S. 211 (1937)**, where the Court stated that “[f]inal judgment in a criminal case means sentence.” Id. at 212. Sentencing is part of the single criminal judgment, not a second jeopardy for an offense already adjudicated. Likewise, in **Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1873)**, the Court described the Double Jeopardy Clause as preventing multiple punishments for the same offense once the lawful sentence has been fully satisfied. Id. at 168-69. Lange protects against cumulative punishment for one offense; it does not convert a sentencing enhancement proceeding into a second prosecution.

The Court has also recognized that judges may determine facts bearing on punishment without implicating the Double Jeopardy Clause. In **Norris v. United States, 281 U.S. 619 (1930)**, the Court upheld sentencing based on information not proved to a jury, underscoring that punishment may be informed by judicial factfinding. Id. at 622-23. Earlier, in **Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912)**, the Court sustained a recidivist statute allowing enhanced punishment based on prior convictions and held that such statutes do not create a new offense but simply increase punishment for the latest crime. Id. at 623-24. That is precisely the structure at issue here: California’s law uses a prior conviction to increase the sentence for the current offenses.

The Court’s recidivist cases make the point even more clearly. In **Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673 (1895)**, the Court upheld a habitual criminal statute and explained that punishment may be aggravated because of prior convictions without violating constitutional limits. Id. at 677-78. In **McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901)**, the Court again rejected the claim that a recidivist proceeding punished the defendant twice for earlier crimes, holding that the enhanced sentence is for the new offense, with the prior convictions serving only to affect the penalty. Id. at 312-13. And in **Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948)**, the Court stated that the sentence under a habitual criminal statute “is not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes.” Id. at 732. Gryger is fatal to respondent’s position: a prior conviction allegation used to enhance punishment is not itself a prosecution for “the same offence.”

Nor does **Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919)** support respondent. There the Court rejected a double jeopardy challenge to a death sentence imposed after retrial, holding that the second punishment was lawful because the first conviction had been reversed. Id. at 18-19. Stroud shows that where a conviction is vacated, retrial and resentencing are not barred merely because punishment is again at issue. If retrial after reversal of a capital conviction is permissible, then retrial of a noncapital sentencing allegation is surely permissible as well.

Finally, respondent cannot expand the Double Jeopardy Clause beyond its historical office. In **Patterson v. State of New York, 432 U.S. 197 (1956)**, the Court recognized the broad authority of legislatures to define crimes and punishments within constitutional bounds. More fundamentally, **Blockburger v. United States, 284 U.S. 299 (1932)**, teaches that double jeopardy turns on whether the government seeks to prosecute the “same offense.” Id. at 304. A prior conviction allegation is not a separate offense; it is a sentencing fact attached to an already adjudicated offense.

In sum, the precedents decided before 1970 establish a consistent rule: sentencing enhancements based on prior convictions do not constitute a second prosecution for the same offense, and the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of such allegations after an evidentiary failure in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. The judgment below should be affirmed.
