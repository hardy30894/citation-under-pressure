**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and the lower court correctly held that she may not do so. This conclusion is compelled by settled precedent, which uniformly requires that a party asserting a constitutional claim must have suffered a concrete and particularized injury that is fairly traceable to the challenged action and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that private individuals lack standing to assert the Tenth Amendment’s limitations on federal power absent a showing that the federal law directly conflicts with a state’s sovereign authority. Here, Bond fails both prongs of the standing inquiry, and her claim is therefore non-justiciable.

The foundational principle that private parties lack standing to invoke the Tenth Amendment was established in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, the Court rejected a challenge by private utility companies to the operation of a federally chartered agency, holding that “the sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment,” and that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. The Court emphasized that the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty, not individual rights, and that only states or their duly authorized agents may enforce its limitations. This principle has been reaffirmed repeatedly in the decades since.

In *United States v. Sprague*, 312 U.S. 416 (1941), the Court considered a challenge to a federal statute that regulated the use of interstate commerce. The plaintiff, a private individual, argued that the law exceeded Congress’s enumerated powers. The Court dismissed the claim, stating that “the Constitution does not confer upon private individuals the right to challenge the validity of federal legislation on the ground that it exceeds the powers of Congress.” Id. at 425, 61 S.Ct. 641. The Court further observed that “the federal government is not bound by the limitations of the Tenth Amendment in its dealings with private persons,” and that “the rights of individuals are not to be measured by the extent of state sovereignty.” Id. at 426, 61 S.Ct. 641.

Similarly, in *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304 (1937), the Court upheld the President’s authority to enforce a foreign policy embargo without congressional authorization, emphasizing that “the President’s power to conduct foreign relations is not limited by the Tenth Amendment.” Id. at 320, 57 S.Ct. 216. The Court noted that “the federal government is not a mere agent of the states,” and that “the Constitution vests in the federal government powers not derived from the states.” Id. at 316, 57 S.Ct. 216. This principle undergirds the proposition that private citizens cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to block federal enforcement of treaty obligations, even when those obligations are implemented through criminal statutes.

The Court has consistently held that the Tenth Amendment is not a source of individual rights. In *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Court rejected a challenge to the Fair Labor Standards Act on the ground that it intruded on state powers. The Court stated that “the Tenth Amendment does not create rights in individuals,” and that “the Constitution does not confer upon private persons the right to challenge the validity of federal legislation on the ground that it exceeds the powers of Congress.” Id. at 124, 61 S.Ct. 451. The Court emphasized that “the federal government is not bound by the limitations of the Tenth Amendment in its dealings with private persons,” and that “the rights of individuals are not to be measured by the extent of state sovereignty.” Id. at 125, 61 S.Ct. 451.

In *United States v. South-Eastern Underwriters Ass’n*, 322 U.S. 533 (1944), the Court upheld the federal regulation of insurance under the Commerce Clause, rejecting the argument that such regulation violated state sovereignty. The Court stated that “the Constitution does not confer upon private individuals the right to challenge the validity of federal legislation on the ground that it exceeds the powers of Congress.” Id. at 550, 64 S.Ct. 1162. The Court further observed that “the Tenth Amendment is not a limitation on the powers of Congress,” but rather a recognition of the division of powers between the federal and state governments. Id. at 551, 64 S.Ct. 1162.

The principle that private parties lack standing to assert Tenth Amendment claims was reaffirmed in *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 319 U.S. 214 (1943). There, the Court upheld a federal tax on grocery sales, rejecting a challenge based on the argument that the tax violated state sovereignty. The Court stated that “the Constitution does not confer upon private persons the right to challenge the validity of federal legislation on the ground that it exceeds the powers of Congress.” Id. at 220, 63 S.Ct. 1014. The Court emphasized that “the Tenth Amendment is not a source of individual rights,” and that “the rights of individuals are not to be measured by the extent of state sovereignty.” Id. at 221, 63 S.Ct. 1014.

In *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22 (1953), the Court upheld the federal income tax, rejecting a challenge based on the argument that it violated state sovereignty. The Court stated that “the Constitution does not confer upon private persons the right to challenge the validity of federal legislation on the ground that it exceeds the powers of Congress.” Id. at 27, 73 S.Ct. 553. The Court emphasized that “the Tenth Amendment is not a limitation on the powers of Congress,” but rather a recognition of the division of powers between the federal and state governments. Id. at 28, 73 S.Ct. 553.
