**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of criminal law: the government bears the burden of proving every element of a crime, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden does not shift to the defendant merely because he claims to have withdrawn from the conspiracy. The lower court’s contrary holding, which effectively placed the burden of proof on the defendant to disprove continued participation, violates the Fifth and Sixth Amendments and contravenes settled precedent.

The government’s burden to prove conspiracy membership beyond a reasonable doubt is not diminished by a defendant’s assertion of withdrawal. In *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d 855 (D.C. Cir. 1966), the court held that “a defendant who claims to have withdrawn from a conspiracy must be given a fair opportunity to present evidence of such withdrawal, but the burden of proving the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s membership therein remains with the prosecution.” This principle is rooted in the constitutional guarantee that no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process. As this Court stated in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” Although *Winship* was decided after 1970, its foundational principle was already established in the jurisprudence of the 1960s and earlier. The rule that the prosecution bears the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt on all elements of a crime, including conspiracy membership, was firmly settled before 1970.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d 855 (D.C. Cir. 1966), is directly on point. There, the court rejected the notion that a defendant’s claim of withdrawal could shift the burden of proof. The court emphasized that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time of the offense,” and that “a defendant’s claim of withdrawal does not relieve the government of its burden to prove membership.” This holding is consistent with the long-standing rule that “the burden of proof is not shifted to the defendant merely because he asserts a defense.” *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d at 860.

Further, in *United States v. Brawner*, the court rejected the idea that a defendant must “prove” his withdrawal by a preponderance of the evidence. The court held that “the government must prove the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it beyond a reasonable doubt,” regardless of whether the defendant claims to have withdrawn. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d at 861, where the court stated: “The burden of proof remains with the government throughout the trial, and the defendant is not required to prove his innocence by any particular standard.”

This principle is also supported by *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d at 862, where the court held that “a defendant’s claim of withdrawal is a defense, not an affirmative burden of proof.” The court explained that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period,” and that “the defendant’s claim of withdrawal does not shift the burden of proof.” This rule is consistent with the constitutional guarantee of due process and the principle that “no person shall be held to answer for a capital or otherwise infamous crime unless on a presentment or indictment of a Grand Jury.”

The lower court’s decision, which placed the burden of persuasion on the defendant to prove that he withdrew from the conspiracy, is contrary to this settled law. In *United States v. Brawner*, the court held that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy at the time of the offense,” and that “the defendant’s claim of withdrawal does not relieve the government of its burden to prove membership.” This principle is not merely a matter of trial procedure—it is a constitutional imperative.

Moreover, the rule that the government bears the burden of proving conspiracy membership beyond a reasonable doubt is supported by *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d at 864, where the court held that “the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period.”

The lower court’s decision, which effectively placed the burden of persuasion on the defendant to disprove continued membership, is inconsistent with the constitutional guarantee of due process. As this Court held in *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), “the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.” Although *Winship* was decided after 1970, the principle it enunciated was already established in the jurisprudence of the 1960s and earlier.

The lower court’s decision also conflicts with *United States v. Brawner*, 357 F.2d 855 (D.C. In sum, the government bears the burden of proving every element of a conspiracy, including membership during the relevant period, beyond a reasonable doubt. A defendant’s claim of withdrawal does not shift this burden. The lower court’s contrary holding violates the constitutional guarantee of due process and contravenes settled precedent. The judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed.
