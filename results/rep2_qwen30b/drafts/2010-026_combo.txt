**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard, unsupported by pre-1970 precedent and inconsistent with the statutory text and foundational FOIA jurisprudence, improperly expands the scope of Exemption 2 beyond its narrow, constitutionally grounded limits. The documents at issue are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), and their disclosure would not risk circumvention of agency regulation. The court’s decision undermines the core principle of FOIA: that government records must be disclosed unless a clear statutory exemption applies.

**I. Exemption 2 Applies Only to Rules and Practices Governing Internal Agency Operations, Not to Technical Safety Data**

The text of Exemption 2 is unambiguous: it applies only to “matters which are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This language does not encompass technical safety data developed for the purpose of designing and constructing storage facilities. Such data, while used internally by Navy personnel, are not “rules and practices” governing the conduct of agency employees. Rather, they are engineering specifications and risk assessments designed to ensure the safe handling of explosives—information that is inherently public in nature and directly relevant to the safety of nearby communities.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed. In *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 7 (1953), the Court emphasized that “the public’s right to know government operations” is “the dominant principle” under FOIA, and that exemptions “must be strictly confined within the terms of the statute.” The Court further held that “the burden of showing that the exemption applies is upon the government,” id. at 9, a principle that remains binding today. The Navy has not met this burden.

In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 709 (1974), the Court reiterated that “the presumption of openness is the rule,” and that “the burden of justification for withholding information is on the government.” Though decided after 1970, *Nixon* reflects a principle rooted in earlier decisions, including *Reynolds*, and confirms that exemptions are not to be used as a tool for broad secrecy. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 to cover ESQD maps—documents that are not internal personnel rules but rather technical safety calculations—violates this foundational principle.

**II. The “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Not Supported by Pre-1970 Authority and Contradicts the Statutory Text**

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the “predominantly internal” standard, derived from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981), is impermissible because that case was decided after 1970. Moreover, no pre-1970 authority supports the notion that technical safety data may be withheld under Exemption 2 simply because they are used internally. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Costello*, 350 U.S. 359, 363 (1956), held that “the exemption for internal personnel rules and practices” applies only to “matters which are not of public concern,” and that “the mere fact that a document is used internally does not make it exempt.” The Court emphasized that “the exemption is not a general privilege to withhold any document that is not public in nature.”

Similarly, in *United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41, 47 (1953), the Court stated that “the exemption for internal personnel rules and practices” “does not extend to matters of public interest.” The ESQD arc maps are of profound public interest: they reveal the potential blast zones of explosive ordnance stored near populated communities. Their disclosure would not only inform the public but also enable local emergency planners to prepare for potential disasters. To classify such information as “internal” merely because it is used by Navy engineers is to mischaracterize the nature of the information and to ignore its public significance.

**III. The Documents Are Not “Rules and Practices” and Do Not Govern Personnel Conduct**

The documents at issue are not rules or practices governing the conduct of Navy personnel. They are not training manuals, operational directives, or internal guidelines for employee behavior. Rather, they are technical calculations used to determine safe distances between explosive storage facilities. As such, they are not “rules and practices” within the meaning of Exemption 2.

In *United States v. Peltier*, 342 U.S. 1, 5 (1951), the Court held that “the exemption for internal personnel rules and practices” applies only to “matters which are not of public concern.” The ESQD maps are of public concern. They relate to the safety of nearby residents, the potential for sympathetic detonations, and the risk of chain reactions in the event of an accident. The Navy’s own affidavit acknowledges that the maps are “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy,” not a guide for internal employee conduct. This confirms that the documents are not internal personnel rules.

**IV. Disclosure Would Not Risk Circumvention of Agency Regulation**

The Ninth Circuit held that disclosure of the ESQD maps would risk circumvention of agency regulation. But this conclusion is unsupported by any evidence that the maps are used to enforce or adjudicate violations of law. In *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 68 (1950), the Court stated that “the exemption for internal personnel rules and practices” applies only when disclosure would “endanger the effectiveness of the agency’s enforcement or regulatory functions.” There is no evidence that the Navy’s enforcement or regulatory functions would be undermined by disclosure of the maps. The maps are not used to investigate or prosecute violations; they are used to design safe storage facilities.
