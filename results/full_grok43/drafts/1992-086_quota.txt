The District’s denial of access to its school facilities for the exhibition of films addressing family values and child-rearing from a religious perspective violated the First Amendment. The facilities constituted a limited public forum. In such a forum, restrictions must be “viewpoint neutral” and “reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum.” Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 46, 49 (1983); Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). The District permitted after-hours use by numerous community groups for lectures, performances, and discussions on comparable secular subjects, including family-related topics. It nevertheless barred Lamb’s Chapel solely because its presentation would occur “from a Christian perspective.” This distinction turns on the speaker’s viewpoint, not on any subject-matter classification the forum itself had adopted.

Viewpoint discrimination is prohibited even in limited public forums. Police Department of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92, 96 (1972); Carey v. Brown, 447 U.S. 455, 461-62 (1980). The District’s rule against “religious purposes” operated as a viewpoint restriction because it permitted secular speech on family values while excluding the identical subject when presented through a religious lens. See Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269 (1981) (university’s exclusion of religious speech from generally open facilities “discriminates against student groups . . . solely because of the religious content of their speech”). The same principle applies to after-hours access by nonstudent community groups. Board of Education of the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990).

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation does not justify the exclusion. Permitting religious speech on equal terms with secular speech in a limited public forum does not constitute an establishment of religion. Widmar, 454 U.S. at 271-73; see also Mueller v. Allen, 463 U.S. 388, 398-99 (1983). The District’s policy and practice of opening the facilities to a wide range of community expression already created the requisite forum; selective exclusion of religious viewpoints was therefore neither necessary nor narrowly tailored to any compelling interest. Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989).

Because the denial rested on the religious character of Lamb’s Chapel’s message rather than on any neutral, reasonable limitation applicable to the forum, it abridged the First Amendment freedom of speech.
