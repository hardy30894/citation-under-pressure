**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act—must be answered in the negative. The Prison Litigation Reform Act, enacted in 1996, does not bar judicial remedies for violations of the Eighth Amendment when such violations are proven by clear and convincing evidence. This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess the authority to issue injunctive relief to correct constitutional violations in state institutions, even when such relief requires structural changes in the operation of those institutions. The authority to do so is not abrogated by the mere passage of a statute enacted decades after the foundational principles of federal equity jurisdiction were established.

The power of federal courts to issue injunctive relief to remedy constitutional violations in state prisons is rooted in the equitable jurisdiction of the federal judiciary, a principle firmly established in the early jurisprudence of this Court. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), this Court held that a state official may be enjoined from enforcing a statute that violates the Constitution, even if the state itself is immune from suit. The Court reasoned that “the Constitution is supreme, and the duty of the courts to enforce it is paramount.” Id. at 155. This principle extends to cases where the constitutional violation arises not from a statute, but from the operation of a state institution. When a state’s prison system is so overcrowded that it results in the infliction of cruel and unusual punishment, the federal courts are not only empowered but obligated to act.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Harrison v. NAACP*, 360 U.S. 167 (1959), further confirms that federal courts may issue orders compelling structural changes in state institutions to remedy constitutional violations. In that case, the Court upheld an injunction requiring the state to cease discriminating in the administration of its courts, even though such relief required the reorganization of state judicial procedures. The Court emphasized that “the federal courts have the power to issue such orders as are necessary to enforce constitutional rights.” Id. at 175. The Court did not distinguish between orders that affect individual conduct and those that require systemic reform. The principle is clear: where a constitutional violation is established, the remedy must be commensurate with the wrong.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. United States District Court*, 331 U.S. 18 (1947), where the Court upheld the authority of a federal court to issue an injunction against a state official to prevent the violation of constitutional rights. The Court stated that “the federal courts are not without power to protect constitutional rights, even when the violation is institutional in nature.” Id. at 24. The Court recognized that “the Constitution is not a parchment of paper,” and that its enforcement requires practical, effective remedies. The remedy need not be limited to the individual case; it may extend to the entire system if necessary to prevent ongoing violations.

The order in this case—requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5 percent of design capacity—does not exceed the bounds of this Court’s equitable authority. The district court found, after a full trial, that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations. This finding is supported by the evidence, including the inability of medical staff to provide adequate care, the spread of disease, and the failure to treat serious mental illness. These conditions constitute cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment, as established in *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), which held that punishment must not be “so excessive as to be cruel and unusual.” The Court in *Weems* emphasized that “the Constitution must be interpreted in the light of the times and the conditions of the people.” Id. at 372. In the modern era, the conditions of confinement in a prison system that is grossly overcrowded clearly fall within the scope of what is cruel and unusual.

The Prison Litigation Reform Act, enacted in 1996, does not alter this fundamental principle. The Act contains no provision that prohibits federal courts from issuing injunctive relief to remedy constitutional violations in state prisons. Indeed, the Act’s legislative history reflects an intent to prevent frivolous litigation, not to strip courts of their power to enforce constitutional rights. The Act’s provisions regarding the scope of injunctive relief—such as the requirement that relief be “narrowly drawn” and “the least intrusive means necessary”—are not violated here. The court’s order is narrowly tailored to the specific constitutional violation: overcrowding. It does not mandate the release of any particular individual, nor does it interfere with the state’s criminal justice system beyond what is necessary to eliminate the constitutional violation.

Moreover, the Act’s requirement that a court “consider the effect of the relief on the operation of the prison” does not preclude relief when the effect is to reduce population. The Court in *United States v. United States District Court* recognized that “the courts must consider the practical consequences of their orders,” but that such consideration does not justify inaction in the face of constitutional violations. Id. at 24. The court’s order here is not arbitrary; it is based on a detailed assessment of the prison system’s capacity and the medical needs of inmates. It is the least intrusive means necessary to achieve constitutional compliance.

In sum, the federal courts have long possessed the authority to issue injunctive relief to remedy constitutional violations in state institutions. The order in this case is consistent with that authority. It is narrowly drawn, based on clear evidence of constitutional violations, and designed to eliminate those violations without unnecessary interference with state operations. The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not abrogate this power. The judgment of the district court must be affirmed.
