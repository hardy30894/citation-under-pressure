ARGUMENT

The district court erred in dismissing the complaint. This Court reviews such dismissals de novo, construing well-pleaded facts in the light most favorable to the plaintiff and drawing all reasonable inferences in his favor. Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014).

I. Foley’s allegations state claims under Massachusetts law.

The complaint alleges that the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement required Wells Fargo to consider Foley for HAMP before any foreclosure steps and, if ineligible, to apply the MAP2R waterfall process. Foley pleaded that Wells Fargo ignored these obligations, failed to provide required written explanations, and proceeded to foreclosure. These facts support breach of the servicing commitments, which were intended to benefit class members such as Foley. See, e.g., Quinn v. City of Boston, 325 F.3d 18, 28 (1st Cir. 2003) (recognizing enforceable obligations arising from settlement agreements). The same conduct states a claim under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244 for improper foreclosure practices and under chapter 93A for unfair or deceptive acts. See, e.g., Hershenow v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co. of Boston, 840 N.E.2d 526, 533 (Mass. 2006).

II. The claims are not preempted.

Federal law governing home-mortgage lending does not displace Foley’s state-law causes of action. The settlement obligations and Massachusetts foreclosure statutes regulate conduct of general applicability and do not impose requirements that conflict with federal authority. See Barnett Bank of Marion County, N.A. v. Nelson, 517 U.S. 25, 33 (1996) (state laws not preempted unless they “prevent or significantly interfere with” federal objectives). Wells Fargo’s servicing commitments arise from its voluntary agreement; enforcing them does not regulate the content of loan disclosures or impose new federal requirements. See, e.g., Watterson v. Page, 987 F.2d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 1993) (courts may consider settlement documents attached to the complaint when assessing legal sufficiency).

Because the pleaded facts, accepted as true, state plausible claims under state law that are not preempted, the judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
