ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). To trigger preemption, the other federal agency must have exercised its statutory authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue. See Guss v. Utah Labor Relations Board, 353 U.S. 1, 10-11 (1957); Bethlehem Steel Co. v. NLRB, 120 F.2d 641, 645-46 (D.C. Cir. 1941).

The Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is limited to specific areas, such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation of engine and fuel tank compartments. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 391a). While the Coast Guard has exercised its authority in these areas, its regulations do not comprehensively cover all occupational safety and health issues on uninspected vessels. See Kelly v. Washington, 302 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1937); The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558, 579-80 (1874).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Coast Guard's authority is not necessarily exclusive, and that other federal agencies may regulate aspects of maritime employment not covered by the Coast Guard. See Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47-48 (1942); NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 214-15 (1940); International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50, 52-53 (1926). In Southern S.S. Co., the Court held that the National Labor Relations Act applied to maritime employers, despite the Coast Guard's authority to regulate certain aspects of maritime employment. 316 U.S. at 47-48.

The Court of Appeals' reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Those cases involved inspected vessels, which are subject to a more comprehensive regime of Coast Guard regulation. See United States v. Andrews, 207 U.S. 229, 241-42 (1907); The Pennsylvania, 86 U.S. 125, 136-37 (1873). In contrast, uninspected vessels are subject to a more limited regime of Coast Guard regulation, and OSHA's jurisdiction is not preempted.

The distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels is significant. The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific safety equipment and practices, whereas inspected vessels are subject to a broader range of regulations. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102; United States v. Hana, 153 F.2d 536, 539-40 (7th Cir. 1946); The Silvia, 171 U.S. 462, 464-65 (1898). In this context, OSHA's jurisdiction is not preempted by the Coast Guard's authority.

In conclusion, OSHA has jurisdiction to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
