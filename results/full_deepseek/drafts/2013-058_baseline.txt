The court of appeals correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt as “retirement funds” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” so the phrase must be given its ordinary meaning. *Perrin v. United States*, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979). In ordinary usage, retirement funds are assets set aside to provide income during a person’s retirement years. An inherited IRA does not have that character in the hands of a non-spousal beneficiary. The Internal Revenue Code prohibits contributions to the account, 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C), and requires the beneficiary to begin receiving distributions within one year of the owner’s death—and to complete those distributions on a schedule tied to the beneficiary’s life expectancy, 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B). The beneficiary cannot postpone distributions to retirement age; indeed, the account must be distributed even if the beneficiary is young. An inherited IRA is therefore, as the court below observed, “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013).

The text of the exemption turns on the present function of the assets, not their historical source. A fund is exempt only if it is a “retirement fund” in the debtor’s hands. When Ruth Heffron died, the funds in her IRA lost their status as retirement funds; they became assets subject to mandatory distribution. The Seventh Circuit correctly concluded that “when she died they became no one’s retirement funds.” *Id.* at 562. That conclusion is confirmed by the court’s hypothetical: if Ruth had withdrawn the $300,000, paid the taxes, and given the money to Heidi, the cash unmistakably would not be exempt. *Id.* The transfer of assets at death should not yield a different result.

The contrary decisions of the Fifth Circuit and the Eighth Circuit Bankruptcy Appellate Panel are unpersuasive. *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012); *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010). Those courts focused on the label “IRA” and the fact that the funds were once someone’s retirement funds. But the statutory exemption asks whether the assets currently are “retirement funds,” not whether they are held in a retirement-named account. As the Seventh Circuit explained, the inherited IRA’s tax benefits do not make the funds retirement savings; the mandatory distribution schedule ensures that the funds will be consumed before the beneficiary’s retirement. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562.

The structure of §522 reinforces this reading. Other exemptions, like the homestead exemption, are tied to the debtor’s current use of the property. A house inherited from a parent and rented out is not a “domicile” under §522(b)(3)(A), even though it was the parent’s home. *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. Likewise, funds that were previously dedicated to a parent’s retirement are not “retirement funds” unless they are actually dedicated to the debtor’s retirement. The omission of the phrase “the debtor’s” from §522(b)(3)(C) does not suggest a contrary result. That omission is best understood as a temporal feature: it allows contributions to retirement funds made after the bankruptcy petition is filed to be exempt. *Id.* It does not transform an asset that is not a retirement fund at any point during the bankruptcy into one.

Congress’s purpose in creating the exemption was to preserve assets truly reserved for retirement. Inherited IRAs are not such assets. They are windfalls that the original owner could have removed from her retirement account and spent during her lifetime. If she had done so, the funds would be fully available to creditors. There is no indication that Congress intended to shield those funds when they pass by inheritance. The bankruptcy exemption should be applied according to its plain terms, and the Seventh Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
