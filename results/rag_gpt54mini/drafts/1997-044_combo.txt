**Argument**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by” fraud. That text limits nondischargeability to the debt obtained by the fraudulent conduct itself. A bankruptcy discharge does not wipe away liabilities generally; it bars only those obligations the Code makes dischargeable, and exceptions are construed against the debtor only when the statute clearly reaches the debt at issue. See *Chapman v. Forsyth*, 43 U.S. (2 How.) 202, 208-09 (1844); *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709-10 (1878); *Scott v. Ellery*, 142 U.S. 381, 385-86 (1892).

Under that settled approach, the compensatory rent overcharge here is plainly nondischargeable because it is the money Cohen obtained by actual fraud. But the treble-damage, fee, and cost components are different. They are not money or property “obtained by” Cohen; they are later-imposed statutory consequences. The Court of Appeals erred by treating those consequential liabilities as though they were themselves “obtained” by fraud. The statutory phrase “to the extent obtained by” must have work to do. See *Miller v. Irving Trust Co.*, 296 U.S. 256, 267-68 (1935) (bankruptcy statutes must be applied according to their terms); *Moore v. Bay*, 284 U.S. 4, 5-6 (1931) (Bankruptcy Act governs distribution according to federal rule, not general notions of equity); *Bluthenthal v. Jones*, 208 U.S. 64, 69-70 (1908) (discharge analysis turns on the debt within the bankruptcy statute).

The phrase “actual fraud” likewise does not expand the statute to reach every legal consequence that follows from a fraudulent transaction. This Court has long distinguished actual fraud from constructive or implied fraud. *Neal* held that the 1867 Act’s reference to “fraud” meant “positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong,” not “fraud in law.” 95 U.S. at 709. That narrower understanding fits § 523(a)(2)(A), but it does not authorize the court of appeals’ further leap that all statutory penalties flowing from fraud are themselves “debts ... obtained by” fraud. A discharge exception must be tied to the statutory object—here, the money actually acquired through the fraud—not to every later-added remedy. See *Chapman*, 43 U.S. at 208-09; *Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709-10.

The Court of Appeals also placed too much weight on the supposed breadth of the word “debt.” Whatever “debt” may include, Congress narrowed § 523(a)(2)(A) with the limiting clause “to the extent obtained by.” That language prevents the exception from swallowing punitive or multiplied damages that were not obtained from the creditor. The older cases likewise reflect that bankruptcy law reaches only the liability the statute specifies. *Scott*, 142 U.S. at 385-86; *Miller v. Guasti*, 226 U.S. 170, 171-72 (1912); *Bruning v. United States*, 376 U.S. 358, 360-61 (1964) (postpetition interest on an otherwise surviving tax debt is treated as part of that debt only because the underlying obligation remains; the case does not enlarge exceptions beyond statutory text).

Nor does the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act change the federal discharge question. Whether a charge is labeled “punitive,” “statutory,” or “treble” under state law cannot control the meaning of a federal bankruptcy exception. The dischargeability inquiry is one of federal law. See *Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709-10; *Miller v. Irving Trust Co.*, 296 U.S. at 267-68. State-law labels cannot convert post-fraud penalties into money “obtained by” fraud.

At most, treble damages and attorney’s fees are collateral consequences of the underlying fraud, not funds Cohen “obtained” from the tenants. Under the plain text of § 523(a)(2)(A), only the amount actually procured by fraud falls within the exception. That reading best respects the Bankruptcy Code’s fresh-start policy while still denying a discharge as to the fraudulent overcharge itself. See *Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709-10; *Scott*, 142 U.S. at 385-86; *Bluthenthal*, 208 U.S. at 69-70.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed insofar as it treats treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs as nondischargeable.
