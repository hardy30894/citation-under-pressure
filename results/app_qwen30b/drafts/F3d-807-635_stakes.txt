**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting partial summary judgment to Armando Ybarra on four of the seven calls made from DISH’s 8047 number, because those calls did not violate the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (“TCPA”), 47 U.S.C. § 227, as a matter of law. The court’s conclusion rested on a misreading of the TCPA’s statutory text and a failure to apply binding precedent requiring that a call using an artificial or prerecorded voice must be made “to a wireless telephone number” to trigger liability under § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). See *Mims v. Arrow Fin. Servs., LLC*, 574 U.S. 65, 72 (2014) (“The TCPA’s text is clear: it applies only to calls made to wireless numbers.”). Here, the record demonstrates that four of the seven challenged calls were not made to Ybarra’s wireless number at all—rendering them categorically exempt from the TCPA’s prohibitions.

The TCPA’s core provision, § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii), prohibits “the use of any artificial or prerecorded voice to deliver a message to a wireless telephone number.” 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). This language is not ambiguous. The statute does not prohibit all prerecorded calls; it prohibits only those made to wireless numbers. *Mims*, 574 U.S. at 72. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the text of a statute is the starting point for interpretation,” *United States v. Stevens*, 559 U.S. 460, 468 (2010), and that “when the language is plain, the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms,” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The plain language of § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii) limits the prohibition to calls “to a wireless telephone number.” Thus, a call to a landline—regardless of whether it uses a prerecorded voice—is not actionable under the TCPA.

The record confirms that four of the seven calls from the 8047 number were placed to a landline number, not a wireless number. The district court acknowledged this fact but failed to apply the correct legal standard. The court stated that DISH “admitted to using a prerecorded voice” and concluded that “Ybarra was entitled to statutory damages of $500 per call.” But the court’s analysis ignored the critical statutory precondition: the call must be to a wireless number. *See* *Mims*, 574 U.S. at 72 (holding that the TCPA’s cause of action applies only to calls to wireless numbers). The court’s failure to consider the nature of the number to which the calls were made renders its conclusion legally erroneous.

DISH’s own records confirm that four of the seven calls were placed to a landline. The calls on May 4, 2013, June 1, 2013, June 15, 2013, and October 29, 2013—each of which resulted in a “positive voice” or “answering machine”—were made to a number that DISH’s internal records identify as a landline. The district court did not dispute this. Indeed, the court’s own findings of fact do not indicate that any of these calls were made to a wireless number. The absence of such a finding is fatal to Ybarra’s claim. Where the record shows that a call was made to a landline, the TCPA does not apply, regardless of the use of a prerecorded message.

The Fifth Circuit has long recognized that the TCPA’s protections are limited to wireless numbers. In *In re: Cell Phone Litig.*, 757 F.3d 444, 452 (5th Cir. 2014), the court held that “the TCPA’s prohibition on prerecorded calls applies only to wireless numbers,” and that “calls to landlines are not subject to the TCPA’s restrictions.” The court further emphasized that “the statute’s text and structure make clear that Congress intended to regulate only calls to wireless numbers.” *Id.* at 453. This precedent is directly on point: the calls at issue were made to a landline, and thus fall outside the TCPA’s scope.

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that a call using a prerecorded voice is automatically actionable regardless of the number type—contradicts both the text of the statute and binding circuit precedent. The court’s reasoning that “DISH concedes that the prerecorded voice was present in the calls from the 8047 number regardless of whether Ybarra triggered it with a positive voice” ignores the statutory requirement that the call be made to a wireless number. *See* *Mims*, 574 U.S. at 72. The presence of a prerecorded voice is not the sole determinant of liability; the destination of the call is equally, if not more, important.

Moreover, the district court’s failure to consider the nature of the number to which the calls were made constitutes a clear error of law. Summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a)*. Here, the record unambiguously shows that four of the seven calls were made to a landline. No reasonable jury could find that such calls violated the TCPA. The district court’s conclusion that Ybarra was entitled to $500 per call for these four calls—totaling $2,000—was therefore legally erroneous.

For these reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment on four of the seven calls. The judgment must be reversed as to those calls, and the case remanded for entry of judgment in favor of DISH on those claims. The judgment should be affirmed only as to the three calls that were made to Ybarra’s wireless number and that were not subject to settlement.

The judgment of the district court is reversed in part and remanded for entry of judgment in favor of DISH on the four calls made to a landline number.
