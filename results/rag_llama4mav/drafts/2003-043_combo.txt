The government's argument that assessing the partnership is sufficient to extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from individual partners is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, an "individual" is a separate "taxpayer"1" within the meaning of the statute, and the assessment against the partnership does not automatically extend the statute of limitations for the individual partners. See Heiner v. Mellon, 304 U.S. 271 (1938) (distinguishing between partnership and individual income for tax purposes).

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. The district court's holding in Young was limited to the proposition that a partner who pays taxes assessed against a partnership is not entitled to a refund simply because the assessment did not name him individually. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D.Cal.1959). The court acknowledged that the IRS would have had to resort to court action or individually assess the taxes against the partner to collect the tax. Id. at 76,050. Our affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson further supports the respondent's position. In Coson, the court held that an assessment against a partnership is insufficient to create a valid lien against an individual partner's property. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir.1961). The district court in Coson noted that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer," and the assessment at issue named only the partnership and "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers." Coson v. United States, 169 F.Supp. 671, 675 (S.D.Cal.1958).

Under California law, a creditor may not automatically collect from a general partner a debt that the partnership owes to the creditor. To the contrary, the creditor must first obtain a judgment against the partner holding the partner liable for the partnership's debt. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c); 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp.2001). This is consistent with the Supreme Court's long-standing recognition of the distinction between partnership and individual liability. See Francis v. McNeal, 228 U.S. 695 (1913) (discussing the administration of partnership and individual estates in bankruptcy); Meek v. Centre County Banking Co., 264 U.S. 499 (1924) (same); Forsyth v. Woods, 78 U.S. 484 (1871) (distinguishing between partnership debts and individual debts of partners).

The government's argument that it can collect taxes from individual partners without assessing them individually is also inconsistent with the Supreme Court's decisions in Lusthaus v. Commissioner, 327 U.S. 293 (1946), and Commissioner v. Tower, 327 U.S. 280 (1946), which emphasize the importance of recognizing the separate identities of partners for tax purposes. See also Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 303 U.S. 493 (1938) (distinguishing between partnership and individual income for tax purposes); Meek v. Centre County Banking Co. Dale v. Same. Breeze v. Same, 268 U.S. 426 (1925) (discussing the bankruptcy of partnerships and individual partners); Commissioner v. Culbertson, 337 U.S. 733 (1949) (recognizing the importance of the parties' intent in determining the validity of a partnership for tax purposes); Liberty Nat. Bank of Roanoke, Va. v. Bear, 276 U.S. 215 (1928) (discussing the administration of partnership and individual estates in bankruptcy).

In conclusion, the government's argument that assessing the partnership is sufficient to extend the statute of limitations for collecting taxes from individual partners is without merit. The respondent is entitled to prevail on this issue.
