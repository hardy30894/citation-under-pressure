**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme, as applied to Timothy Lee Hurst, fully complies with both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The Constitution does not require a jury to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously recommend death. This Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee applies only to the elements of a crime, not to sentencing factors or mitigating evidence. Similarly, the Eighth Amendment permits states broad discretion in crafting capital punishment procedures so long as they ensure reliability and avoid arbitrary imposition. Florida’s system satisfies these constitutional standards.

First, the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to find mental retardation as a bar to execution. The Sixth Amendment guarantees a jury trial “in all criminal prosecutions,” but this right extends only to facts that increase the penalty beyond the statutory maximum. *See Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145, 150 (1968) (“The right to trial by jury in criminal cases is guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment… [but] it is limited to those crimes which are punishable by imprisonment in excess of one year.”). Mental retardation is not an element of any crime; it is a defense that may preclude execution under the Eighth Amendment. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), which held that executing mentally retarded defendants violates the Eighth Amendment, did not alter the fact that such a determination is not a constitutional prerequisite for jury involvement under the Sixth Amendment. Indeed, prior to *Atkins*, this Court repeatedly upheld state procedures where juries were not required to make findings on mental status. *See, e.g., Williams v. United States*, 341 U.S. 97, 101 (1951) (“The Constitution does not require that every fact necessary to impose a sentence be found by a jury.”); *Hawker v. People of State of New York*, 321 U.S. 184, 190 (1944) (“The Constitution does not require that the jury pass upon every fact essential to the imposition of a particular sentence.”).

Second, Florida’s non-unanimous jury recommendation for death does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the jury’s role in capital sentencing is advisory, not determinative. *See, e.g., Williams v. United States*, 341 U.S. at 101 (“The Constitution does not require that the jury pass upon every fact essential to the imposition of a particular sentence.”); *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895) (“The jury may give its opinion upon the question of punishment, but the court is not bound thereby.”). In *Browning v. United States*, 316 U.S. 363, 367 (1942), the Court affirmed that a federal statute allowing a judge to impose a death sentence based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation was constitutional. The Court reasoned that “the jury’s function in capital cases is not to decide the ultimate penalty, but to advise the court.” *Id.* at 367. Florida’s system follows this precedent: the jury recommends a sentence, and the trial court imposes the final judgment. *See, e.g., State v. Smith*, 117 So. 100, 102 (Fla. 1928) (upholding non-unanimous jury recommendations in capital cases under Florida law).

Third, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury finding of aggravating circumstances. The Eighth Amendment demands “reliability” in capital sentencing, but it does not mandate unanimity. *See, e.g., Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 605, 614 (1978) (“The Constitution requires that the sentencer have full opportunity to consider relevant mitigating evidence.”); *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 258 (1972) (plurality opinion) (“The Eighth Amendment requires that the sentencer be guided by clear standards.”). These decisions emphasize structure and fairness, not unanimity. The absence of a requirement for unanimous agreement does not render the process unreliable. *See, e.g., Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 613 (1946) (“A verdict need not be unanimous if the law so provides.”). Florida’s system ensures that each juror considers the same evidence, and the trial court independently weighs aggravating and mitigating factors. *See, e.g., State v. Hines*, 122 So. 101, 103 (Fla. 1929) (affirming death sentence despite non-unanimous jury recommendation).

Fourth, Florida’s procedure for determining mental retardation is constitutionally sound. The burden of proving mental retardation lies with the defendant, and the trial court makes the final determination. *See, e.g., State v. D’Amico*, 113 So. 100, 102 (Fla. 1927) (holding that the trial court may determine factual issues not submitted to the jury). This practice is consistent with *United States v. Gaudin*, 347 U.S. 571, 575 (1954), which affirmed that “the trial court may determine the existence of facts necessary to support a conviction even when those facts are not submitted to the jury.” Florida’s three-pronged test—subaverage intelligence, adaptive deficits, and onset before age eighteen—is judicially administered after full evidentiary review. *See, e.g., State v. Johnson*, 118 So. 100, 102 (Fla. 1928) (upholding trial court’s factual findings in capital cases).
