The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. When a defendant withdraws from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial, as the government met its burden.

The Supreme Court has long held that the government must prove a defendant's participation in a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt. In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), the Court stated that "the burden is on the Government to prove that the conspiracy... continued into the period covered by the statute of limitations." (Id. at 216). Similarly, in Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391 (1957), the Court held that the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, and that the defendant's participation in it was not limited to a period outside the limitations period. (Id. at 396-397).

The Court has also emphasized the importance of proving a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period. In United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205 (1940), the Court held that mere knowledge of a conspiracy is not enough to prove membership, stating that "one who without more furnishes supplies to one who intends to use them in committing a crime is not guilty of a conspiracy." (Id. at 210-211).

In Blumenthal v. United States, 332 U.S. 539 (1947), the Court held that a defendant's participation in a conspiracy must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, and that the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Id. at 556-557). The Court has also noted that a defendant's withdrawal from a conspiracy does not necessarily mean that they are not liable for the conspiracy, but the government must still prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912).

Furthermore, the Court has established that a conspirator is liable for the acts of their co-conspirators. As noted in Marino v. United States, 91 F.2d 691 (1937), cert. denied, 302 U.S. 764 (1937), and referenced in a similar context in Pinkerton v. Commissioner, 8 T.C. 910 (1947), although not a Supreme Court case, a relevant earlier case is Lott v. United States, 205 F. 28 (1913), where the court discussed the principles of conspiracy liability.

The government's burden to prove a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period is not alleviated by the defendant's withdrawal from the conspiracy. As the Court stated in Lutwak v. United States, 344 U.S. 604 (1953), the government must prove that the conspiracy continued and that the defendant was a member of it during the period covered by the indictment.

In this case, the government met its burden by presenting evidence that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial. The government's evidence was sufficient to prove that the defendants were members of the conspiracy during the relevant period, and the defendants' withdrawal from the conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period does not alleviate the government's burden.

In conclusion, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial, as the government met its burden. The Supreme Court's precedents, including Fiswick, Grunewald, Falcone, Blumenthal, Hyde, and Lutwak, support the government's burden to prove a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period.
