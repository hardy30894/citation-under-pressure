**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether the Internal Revenue Service may invoke the ten-year extended collection period of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) against individual partners when it has assessed only the partnership, and never assessed—or obtained a judgment against—the partners themselves. Under long-settled principles of this Court and the Ninth Circuit, the answer is no. An assessment fixes liability, and extends the limitations period, only as to the person actually assessed. The Gallettis and the Briguglios were never assessed, and the time to do so has passed.

**I. An Assessment Extends the Collection Period Only as to the Taxpayer Actually Identified in the Assessment.**

Assessment is the mechanism by which the government converts a tax claim into something with the functional force of a judgment, entitling it to invoke summary remedies—liens, levies, and distraint—without first obtaining a judicial judgment. *Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935). Because assessment substitutes for the ordinary judicial process by which liability is adjudicated against a named defendant, the assessment must identify the particular taxpayer whose property may be reached. This is precisely why, in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the district court held that a lien could not attach to an alleged partner's individual property where the assessment named only the partnership and unidentified "unknown" taxpayers: "such a lien does not exist against a particular individual's property pursuant to §[§] 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203. Since plaintiff never was assessed and no lien exists without such an assessment, it follows that the Government does not have any lien." *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958), *aff'd*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). The Ninth Circuit's affirmance did not disturb this reasoning; it simply rested affirmance on an independent, alternative defect in the lien. 286 F.2d at 458, 462-63. The principle Coson articulates—that identification of the taxpayer is indispensable to a valid assessment, and that no derivative collection remedy exists absent that identification—controls here. The Partnership was assessed; Debtors were not. The extended ten-year period of § 6502(a) therefore runs only against the Partnership.

*Young v. Riddell* is not to the contrary. There, a partner who had voluntarily paid tax assessed against his partnership sought a refund on the theory that he personally had never been assessed. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D. Cal. 1959), *aff'd*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). The district court denied a refund because the partner was substantively liable under state law for a debt he had actually paid—but the court was careful to note that an assessment would nonetheless have been necessary had the government sought to *collect* the tax from him rather than retain a voluntary payment: absent an assessment, the government could reach the partner only "by resorting to court action." Id. at 76,050. This Court's own affirmance rested on the same narrow footing—that the partner, "[h]aving been found a general member of the partnership, . . . is personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership, including its tax liability." 283 F.2d at 910. Substantive liability under partnership law was never disputed here either. The question is procedural: whether the government may enforce that liability, after the ordinary limitations period has run, without ever assessing the individual or securing a judgment against him. Young answers that it may not.

**II. Statutes Governing the Government's Time to Collect Taxes Are Strictly Construed, and Exceptions Extending the Collection Period Are Not to Be Enlarged by Implication.**

This Court has consistently held that provisions extending the government's otherwise-applicable time to collect taxes must be strictly construed and confined to their terms. In *Bowers v. New York & Albany Lighterage Co.*, 273 U.S. 346 (1927), the Court refused to read the statute of limitations governing tax collection expansively, emphasizing the taxpayer's interest in the "assurance of security" that finality provides once a limitations period has run. Similarly, in *Lucas v. Pilliod Lumber Co.*, 281 U.S. 245 (1930), the Court declined to extend, by implication, an exception to the ordinary limitations period beyond what its terms plainly authorized. And in *United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489 (1930), the Court held that the government's extended period to collect from a taxpayer whose liability had been assessed did not carry over to permit collection, after the ordinary period had expired, from a different person—there, a transferee—who had never himself been assessed within the applicable time. The same logic governs partners. A partnership and its individual partners are separate taxpayers, each assigned separate identification for tax purposes; an assessment naming the Partnership no more automatically extends the collection period as to the Gallettis and Briguglios than an assessment against a corporation extended the period against its transferees in *Updike*.

**III. The Government's Remedy Was to Assess Debtors or Obtain Judgments Against Them Within the Ordinary Limitations Period, Which It Failed to Do.**

Nothing in these authorities leaves the government without recourse against partners generally liable for partnership debts. It need only assess the partners individually, or timely sue to reduce the partnership's liability to a judgment against them, within the three years allowed by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The government did neither here. Having slept on that right, it cannot now invoke an extended limitations period that, under *Bull*, *Coson*, *Young*, *Bowers*, *Lucas*, and *Updike* alike, attaches only to the taxpayer actually assessed. The judgment below should be affirmed.
