**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DELEGATION TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE CONGRESS PROVIDED AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TO GUIDE HIS DISCRETION.**

The Constitution vests “[a]ll legislative Powers herein granted . . . in a Congress of the United States.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 1. Yet this Court has long recognized that Congress may “obtain assistance from [other] coordinate branches” so long as it supplies an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate’s discretion. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision satisfies this test.

In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), the Court struck down a delegation because Congress failed to provide “any standard or rule” to guide the President’s discretion. *Id.* at 415. By contrast, § 811(h) directs the Attorney General to schedule a substance only if it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and meets three specific criteria: (1) the history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of such abuse; and (3) the risk to the public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). These standards are “sufficiently definite and precise” to cabin the Attorney General’s discretion. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944).

The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress prescribed “a specific legislative goal” and “definite criteria” to guide the delegate. *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932). Section 811(h) does precisely that. It requires the Attorney General to act only when a substance threatens imminent public harm and to consider well-defined factors in making that determination. This is a far cry from the “unfettered discretion” condemned in *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 542 (1935).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUBDELEGATION TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED BY CONGRESS AND CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE.**

The Attorney General has long delegated his administrative functions under the Controlled Substances Act to the DEA Administrator. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989). This subdelegation is valid because Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “perform all functions vested in him” by the Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) (1988). The Court has held that “delegation of discretionary authority to an administrative agency” is permissible where the statute confers such authority. *Federal Crop Ins. Corp. v. Merrill*, 332 U.S. 380, 384 (1947).

Moreover, the subdelegation doctrine permits executive officials to “assign their duties to subordinates” so long as the original delegation is valid. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911). Here, the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator is a permissible exercise of executive authority, not an unlawful transfer of legislative power.

**III. THE STATUTE’S JUDICIAL REVIEW BAR DOES NOT PRECLUDE CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGES.**

Section 811(h)(6) provides that a temporary scheduling order “is not subject to judicial review.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6). However, this Court has held that “when constitutional questions are at issue, the availability of judicial review is presumed.” *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). The statute’s bar does not apply to facial constitutional challenges, as the Third Circuit correctly recognized. *See also United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988).

**CONCLUSION**

The delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional because § 811(h) provides an intelligible principle to guide his discretion. The subdelegation to the DEA is authorized by Congress and constitutionally permissible. The judicial review bar does not preclude constitutional challenges. The convictions should be affirmed.
