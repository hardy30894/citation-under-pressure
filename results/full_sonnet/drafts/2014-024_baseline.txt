# ARGUMENT

## A CONVICTION UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS COMMUNICATION AS A THREAT

### I. The Common-Law Presumption of Scienter Requires a Subjective Mental State

This Court has long recognized that "the existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence." *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251 (1922); *see also Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (crime requires "a concurrence of an evil-meaning mind with an evil-doing hand"). Section 875(c) criminalizes transmission of "any communication containing any threat," but is silent as to mental state. This Court's precedent instructs that such silence does not "dispense with a mens rea requirement." *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994). Rather, courts must "read into the statute" the mental state "necessary to separate wrongful conduct from otherwise innocent conduct." *Carter v. United States*, 530 U.S. 255, 269 (2000) (quoting *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 72).

The Third Circuit's negligence-like "reasonable person" standard collapses this distinction. Under that standard, a defendant may be convicted though he never intended his words to threaten anyone—precisely the "otherwise innocent conduct" *Carter* forbids criminalizing without proof of intent. The presumption favoring scienter applies with special force here because §875(c) is a felony punishable by up to five years imprisonment; this Court has emphasized that "the more severe the punishment, the more likely some form of scienter is required." *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 618.

### II. The First Amendment Requires Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten

The "true threat" doctrine excludes certain speech from First Amendment protection, but this Court has consistently anchored that exception in the *speaker's* intent, not the listener's reaction. In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), this Court defined true threats as statements where "the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence." *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). This formulation—"the speaker means"—is inherently a subjective inquiry into the defendant's state of mind, not an objective inquiry into how a reasonable listener would perceive the statement.

*Black* further held that a state may not presume intent to intimidate merely from the act of cross burning itself, because doing so "ignores all of the contextual factors that are necessary to decide whether a particular cross burning is intended to intimidate." *Id.* at 366. If the State cannot presume intent from an act as historically freighted with menace as cross burning, it certainly cannot dispense with intent altogether based on an objective reasonable-listener standard for ambiguous Facebook posts.

This Court's broader First Amendment jurisprudence confirms that speech regulation premised on content requires attention to the speaker's purpose. In *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46 (1988), the Court demanded a showing of "actual malice" before permitting liability for offensive speech about public figures, recognizing the constitutional peril of chilling protected expression through negligence-based liability. An objective "reasonable speaker" standard, as the Third Circuit applied below, creates precisely this chilling effect: individuals who communicate in hyperbole, rap lyrics, or dark humor—forms of expression this Court has recognized as protected, *see Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam) (distinguishing "political hyperbole" from unprotected threats)—risk felony conviction based on how an ill-defined "reasonable person" might construe their words, regardless of what the speaker actually meant.

*Watts* itself confirms the subjective focus: this Court reversed a conviction because the statement, considered "in context," was "a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition to the President," not a "true 'threat.'" 394 U.S. at 708. The inquiry in *Watts* necessarily examined what the speaker meant to convey, not merely how the words might land on an undifferentiated audience.

### III. The Objective Standard Applied Below Sweeps in Protected Speech

The Third Circuit relied on its earlier decision in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), holding that a statement is a true threat when "a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted . . . as a serious expression of intent to inflict bodily harm." *Kosma*, 951 F.2d at 557. But this negligence standard asks only what a reasonable person *should have known*—not what the defendant *intended*. The instruction given below—that Elonis need only "intentionally make a statement" that a reasonable person would find threatening, App. 24—permits conviction even where the defendant subjectively believed, however reasonably or unreasonably, that he was engaged in venting, joking, or artistic expression, as Elonis testified he was doing when he composed rap-styled lyrics modeled on performers like Eminem.

This objective approach cannot be reconciled with *Black*'s repeated emphasis that the true-threats exception targets speakers who "mean to communicate" a threat. 538 U.S. at 359. Nor can it be squared with the settled principle that "government may not regulate [speech] based on hostility—or favoritism—towards the underlying message expressed," *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 386 (1992), without careful attention to what the speaker intended.

### IV. The Proper Remedy Is Reversal and Remand for a New Trial

Because the jury was instructed under an erroneous objective standard, and because a subjective-intent instruction—asking whether Elonis intended his statements to be understood as threats—might have produced a different verdict, the conviction cannot stand. This Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and remand for further proceedings consistent with the correct subjective-intent standard required by §875(c) and the First Amendment.
