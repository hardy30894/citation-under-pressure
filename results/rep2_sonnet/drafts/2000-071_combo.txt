# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Exclusion of the Club's After-Hours Religious Worship Was a Reasonable, Content-Based Limitation on Its Own Property, Not Unconstitutional Discrimination.

A school district's authority over its own buildings is not diminished merely because the district permits some community use after hours. As this Court has long recognized, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). Milford dedicated its limited forum to civic, social, and recreational activities and to the discussion of morals and character generally; it did not dedicate its forum to religious worship or proselytizing instruction. The exclusion of the Club's activities—group prayer, altar-call-style invitations to accept Christ, and scripture memorization directed at securing children's salvation—rests on the nature of the activity itself, not on the Club's Christian identity. A Jewish, Muslim, or Buddhist group proposing analogous worship and religious instruction would be excluded on identical terms. That is the essence of a permissible, evenhanded regulation of a limited forum, not viewpoint discrimination.

This Court's public-forum decisions confirm that reasonable, generally applicable restrictions tied to the character of government property are constitutionally sound. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding licensing scheme regulating use of public streets where restriction served legitimate civic interests); Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 403–08 (1953) (upholding permit requirement for use of public park for religious services). Milford's policy is far less restrictive than the licensing schemes upheld in Cox and Poulos: it excludes only the category of activity—religious worship and instruction—that is inherently incompatible with the secular civic and recreational purposes for which the forum was created.

## II. Even If the Exclusion Implicated Protected Speech, It Was Justified by Milford's Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.

Milford's caution was not idle. This Court has repeatedly held that public school districts occupy a uniquely sensitive position under the Establishment Clause because of the impressionability of young students and the perception that the school itself endorses religious exercises conducted under its roof and its imprimatur. The Establishment Clause erects, in Jefferson's words adopted by this Court, "a wall of separation between Church and State." Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947).

The closest historical analogue is Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), where this Court struck down a released-time program because it used the machinery of the public school to facilitate religious instruction on school property. The vice identified in McCollum was not the content of the instruction as such, but the placement of religious teaching within the public school building, aided by the compulsory apparatus of the school system itself. Milford's building, immediately after the school day ends, remains suffused with the same institutional identity, the same young and impressionable audience, and the same physical setting condemned in McCollum. By contrast, this Court sustained a released-time program only once it was relocated away from the school building, explaining that "the public schools do no more than accommodate their schedules to a program of outside religious instruction." Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 315 (1952). Zorach did not retreat from McCollum's core teaching; it merely confirmed that on-premises religious instruction of schoolchildren raises establishment concerns that off-premises programs do not. Milford's policy tracks precisely this line—a line this Court itself has drawn.

This Court has been especially vigilant where young schoolchildren are the intended audience for religious exercises connected to the school day. In Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), the Court held that the Establishment Clause forbids government from sponsoring religious observances for schoolchildren, recognizing the coercive pressures inherent in the school setting. In Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), the Court reaffirmed that religious exercises conducted in connection with public schools implicate Establishment Clause values distinct from those in other public contexts, given students' age and the setting's institutional character. Milford's Board reasonably concluded that permitting a religious club to conduct prayer, scripture memorization, and salvation invitations to elementary-age children—recruited from the same classrooms those children had just left, in the same building, immediately after school—created precisely the appearance of school-sponsored religious inculcation that Engel and Schempp condemn.

Nor is it an answer that Milford permits other clubs devoted to "moral and character development." The Establishment Clause requires that any accommodation have a secular purpose and avoid the primary effect of advancing religion. See Board of Education v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236, 243 (1968). Milford's line between teaching values generally and conducting religious worship and instruction preserves precisely that distinction. As Zorach itself acknowledged, the First Amendment "does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State," 343 U.S. at 312, but neither does it permit the public school itself to become the site and instrument of religious conversion for young children. Milford's judgment, informed by this Court's own precedents, that the Club's proposed activities crossed that line was not merely reasonable—it was compelled by the Establishment Clause concerns this Court has long recognized as attending religious activity conducted on public school premises for young, impressionable children. See Zorach, 343 U.S. at 313 (noting the Nation's religious character while insisting government neither compel nor sponsor sectarian worship).

For these reasons, Milford's exclusion of the Good News Club's after-hours meetings was constitutional.
