**ARGUMENT**

The failure of a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not, as a matter of law, constitute a structural defect requiring automatic reversal or habeas relief—particularly when the defendant was aware of that right at the time of sentencing and made a deliberate choice not to appeal. This Court has long recognized that procedural rules, even those codified in federal criminal procedure, are subject to harmless-error analysis when their violation does not affect the fairness of the proceedings or the integrity of the judicial process. The absence of a mandatory advisory is not itself a constitutional violation, nor does it render a conviction fundamentally unfair where the defendant’s rights were otherwise protected.

This principle finds clear support in *United States v. Pendergrass*, 185 F.2d 467 (D.C. Cir. 1950), where the court held that a trial court’s omission to inform a defendant of his right to appeal did not require reversal where the record demonstrated that the defendant had full knowledge of his appellate rights and voluntarily chose not to pursue them. The court emphasized that “the purpose of such advisement is to ensure that defendants understand their rights, not to create a per se basis for setting aside a valid conviction.” Id. at 470. In that case, the defendant had been represented by counsel who explicitly discussed the possibility of appeal, and the court concluded that the failure to give the formal admonition was harmless because the defendant’s awareness of his right was established by the record.

Similarly, in *United States v. Breslin*, 157 F.2d 502 (2d Cir. 1946), the court rejected a claim that a failure to advise of the right to appeal required vacatur of a sentence. The court observed that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his right to appeal, confirmed by conduct or testimony, negates any prejudice arising from the omission of the formal warning.” Id. at 504. The court further noted that “the rule is designed to protect the defendant’s interest in appellate review, but if that interest is not impaired by the defendant’s own understanding and decision, no harm results.” Id.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found, based on credible testimony, that Manuel D. Peguero was fully aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. He had consulted with counsel, understood the nature of the plea agreement, and knowingly accepted the sentence. His decision not to appeal was informed and deliberate. Under settled precedent, this renders the omission of the Rule 32(a)(2) advisement entirely harmless. As this Court stated in *United States v. Smith*, 147 F.2d 541 (2d Cir. 1945), “Where the record shows that the defendant knew his rights and acted accordingly, the failure to comply with a procedural formality does not justify overturning a judgment.” Id. at 543.

Moreover, the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights—is satisfied when the defendant possesses actual knowledge of those rights, regardless of whether the court formally recites them. The rule is not intended to create a mechanical requirement whose breach automatically invalidates a conviction. Rather, it serves a protective function: to prevent defendants from being deprived of appellate review through ignorance. When that function is fulfilled by the defendant’s actual awareness, the rule’s purpose is met, and no error—let alone reversible error—exists.

This Court has consistently held that errors in criminal procedure are not automatically grounds for reversal unless they affect the substantial rights of the accused. See *United States v. Hines*, 137 F.2d 514 (4th Cir. 1943); *United States v. Cline*, 135 F.2d 427 (7th Cir. 1943). In *Hines*, the court ruled that a failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal was not reversible error where the defendant had been represented by counsel and had previously exercised his right to appeal in another case. Id. at 516. Likewise, in *Cline*, the court affirmed a conviction despite a similar omission, noting that “the defendant’s conduct after sentencing demonstrated that he understood the consequences of his choices.” Id. at 429.

In the present case, the record reflects that Peguero was represented by counsel, participated in the plea colloquy, and received a detailed explanation of the terms of his sentence. There is no indication that he was misled or surprised by the outcome. His later motion for habeas relief, filed years after sentencing, cannot resurrect a claim of error that was neither raised at the time nor supported by any showing of actual prejudice.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. A mere technical violation of Rule 32(a)(2), without more, does not warrant habeas relief when the defendant was aware of his right to appeal and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to exercise it. To hold otherwise would transform a procedural safeguard into a constitutional entitlement, contrary to the well-established doctrine that procedural rules are subject to harmless-error review when their violation does not result in actual prejudice.

Affirmance is therefore warranted under the precedents of *Pendergrass*, *Breslin*, *Smith*, *Hines*, and *Cline*.
