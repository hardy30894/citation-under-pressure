ARGUMENT

I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Procedure Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee protects the defendant's interest in having a jury resolve the question of guilt; it has never been understood to require that every fact bearing on the *severity* of punishment be found by a jury under a unanimity rule. This Court held in *Duncan v. Louisiana*, 391 U.S. 145 (1968), that trial by jury is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," id. at 149, but that holding rested on the historic function of the jury as the body that decides "issues of fact in criminal cases," a role this Court traced in *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276 (1930), and reaffirmed in *Singer v. United States*, 380 U.S. 24 (1965), where the Court explained that trial by jury has traditionally been regarded as the "normal and ... preferable mode of disposing of issues of fact in criminal cases." Neither *Patton* nor *Singer* suggests that the jury's fact-finding role must be replicated, unanimity requirement and all, at every subsequent stage of a criminal proceeding.

This Court's long-standing sentencing jurisprudence confirms that States retain wide latitude to structure the factfinding that determines the ultimate punishment. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a capital sentence imposed by a judge who considered information never submitted to the jury that convicted the defendant, reasoning that the constitutional guarantees governing the guilt phase do not automatically transfer to sentencing determinations. Likewise, in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), the Court sustained a Texas recidivist procedure permitting the judge, rather than the jury, to resolve facts materially affecting the length of a defendant's sentence, holding that such procedural allocation offended neither due process nor the right to trial by jury. Florida's scheme — under which a jury renders an advisory recommendation and the trial judge makes the ultimate findings regarding aggravation, mitigation, and any claim of mental retardation — is no more constitutionally suspect than the procedures approved in *Williams* and *Spencer*.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment impose a unanimity requirement on capital sentencing recommendations. Where this Court has required unanimous jury findings in a capital-sentencing context, it has done so as a matter of statutory construction, not constitutional command. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court read the federal Kidnapping Act's unanimity requirement into the jury's death recommendation because Congress had so provided, not because the Sixth Amendment itself demanded it. Where, as here, the legislature has not imposed such a requirement, nothing in the Sixth Amendment supplies one. Florida's judgment that a supermajority jury recommendation is sufficient to inform the court's sentencing determination is a permissible legislative choice of the kind approved in *Spencer*.

II. Florida's Capital Sentencing Procedure Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is directed at the substance of the punishment imposed, not at the particular procedural mechanism — such as jury unanimity — by which a State chooses to allocate factfinding responsibility. This Court's Eighth Amendment cases have measured punishments against "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), and against the historic principle that the Amendment forbids the "unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain" in carrying out a sentence, see *Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459 (1947). Neither inquiry turns on whether a jury's sentencing recommendation was unanimous or whether a jury, rather than a judge, resolved a defendant's claim of mental retardation. The Eighth Amendment concern identified in *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), was the substantive imposition of punishment for a status rather than an act; it says nothing about which institutional actor within the State's system must resolve subsidiary factual disputes.

This Court's decision in *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510 (1968), illustrates the proper scope of Eighth Amendment scrutiny in the capital-sentencing context. There, the Court addressed the composition of the capital jury itself, holding that the State could not systematically exclude jurors merely because they voiced general objections to the death penalty. But *Witherspoon* did not suggest that the Eighth Amendment dictates the structure of the sentencing determination beyond ensuring an impartial jury; it left the balance of the sentencing procedure — including the respective roles of judge and jury — to the States. Florida's procedure, which allows the trial judge to determine mental retardation as a bar to execution while permitting the jury to weigh the same evidence in mitigation, and which does not require unanimity for the jury's advisory recommendation, reflects precisely the kind of legislative choice that *Williams*, *Spencer*, and *Witherspoon* leave undisturbed.

Because Florida's capital sentencing scheme allocates factfinding responsibility in a manner consistent with this Court's Sixth and Eighth Amendment precedents, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
