**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s denial of James Winbush’s last-minute motion for a fingerprint identification expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional right to a fair trial under the Sixth Amendment and the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The court’s rigid adherence to procedural timeliness, without considering the substantial prejudice to Winbush’s ability to mount a meaningful defense, constitutes reversible error.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant the right to effective assistance of counsel and the right to present a defense. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 685 (1984). This includes the right to call expert witnesses necessary to challenge the prosecution’s evidence. *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984). When a defendant is denied access to a crucial expert—particularly one whose testimony could directly address the absence of forensic evidence—the denial undermines the fairness of the trial. Here, Winbush sought a fingerprint expert not to introduce speculative or irrelevant testimony, but to explain the absence of latent prints on the recovered cocaine and firearm—evidence that the government relied upon to establish possession and intent. The government’s own notice indicated that no latent fingerprints were found on the physical evidence. Yet, without an expert to interpret the significance of that absence—whether due to surface conditions, handling, or other factors—Winbush was left unable to challenge the prosecution’s narrative that he was the sole possessor of the drugs and gun.

The district court’s refusal to allow this expert, based solely on the timing of the motion, ignored the fundamental principle that “a defendant’s right to present a defense is not subject to mechanical rules that disregard the realities of trial.” *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 310 (1998). The court’s decision to deny the motion on the grounds that it was “woefully late” fails to account for the fact that Winbush had no reason to anticipate the need for such an expert until the government’s notice of expert witnesses was filed on October 3, 2007—over a month before trial. At the final pretrial conference on November 2, Winbush informed the magistrate that he would present no expert witnesses, not because he lacked interest, but because he had not yet been apprised of the government’s intent to introduce testimony about the absence of fingerprints. The motion filed on November 8 was not a tactical surprise but a necessary response to newly disclosed evidence. To treat it as such is to penalize a defendant for the government’s failure to provide timely notice of its expert strategy.

Moreover, the denial of a continuance compounded the error. The district court’s refusal to grant a reasonable delay—despite Winbush’s clear need to secure an expert and prepare for cross-examination of the government’s forensic witnesses—violated the defendant’s right to due process. *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. at 659. The court’s decision to proceed without allowing time for preparation, particularly in a case involving complex forensic evidence and multiple counts, deprived Winbush of a meaningful opportunity to defend himself. The Supreme Court has held that “a trial court must be alert to the possibility that a defendant may be prejudiced by the denial of a continuance,” especially when the request is made in good faith and for a legitimate reason. *United States v. Berrigan*, 481 F.2d 743, 750 (3d Cir. 1973). Here, Winbush’s request was both timely in the context of the government’s disclosure and grounded in a legitimate need to respond to the prosecution’s expert testimony.

The government’s reliance on the absence of fingerprints as circumstantial evidence of guilt is not harmless. The prosecution argued that the lack of prints on the cocaine and firearm demonstrated that Winbush handled the items with gloves—implying premeditation and intent. But without an expert to explain that such absence is common on porous surfaces like plastic bags or that the drugs may have been handled with gloves, or that the absence of prints is not probative of guilt, the jury was left to draw an impermissible inference. *See United States v. Johnson*, 587 F.3d 805, 811 (7th Cir. 2009) (holding that the admission of forensic evidence without proper context may mislead the jury). The district court’s refusal to allow Winbush to present an expert to counter this narrative effectively denied him the opportunity to challenge the prosecution’s interpretation of the evidence.

The Seventh Circuit has recognized that “a defendant’s right to present a defense is not a mere procedural formality but a substantive right.” *United States v. Hines*, 557 F.3d 707, 714 (7th Cir. 2009). When that right is infringed by the denial of a necessary expert witness and a reasonable continuance, the conviction must be reversed. *See United States v. Berrigan*, 481 F.2d at 750. The district court’s error was not harmless. It deprived Winbush of a fair trial and undermined the integrity of the judicial process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
