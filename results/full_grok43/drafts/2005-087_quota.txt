ARGUMENT

Arizona’s evidentiary limitation does not offend the Due Process Clause. A state may restrict the purposes for which mental-disease evidence may be introduced without denying a defendant the opportunity to contest the prosecution’s case. Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 56 (1996) (plurality opinion); see also id. at 58 (Ginsburg, J., concurring in the judgment). The Clause “does not require the States to conform to any particular model” for allocating the burden of proof on mental incapacity. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952). Arizona therefore may channel such evidence exclusively to its insanity defense, on which the defendant bears the burden. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 207 (1977).

The prosecution’s burden to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt, In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), is not enlarged by the Due Process Clause into an affirmative right to rebut mens rea with any evidence the defendant chooses. Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 U.S. 684, 700–01 (1975), invalidated only a presumption that relieved the State of its burden; it did not constitutionalize particular categories of defense evidence. Likewise, Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228, 233 (1987), upheld a requirement that self-defense be proved by the defendant, confirming that the State may define the contours of its affirmative defenses without creating a separate constitutional avenue for the same evidence.

States enjoy “broad latitude” to adopt rules excluding evidence “from criminal trials.” Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 324 (2006) (quoting United States v. Scheffer, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998)). Such rules violate due process only when they are “arbitrary” or “disproportionate to the purposes they are designed to serve.” Id. at 325 (quoting Scheffer, 523 U.S. at 308). Arizona’s rule is neither. It rationally prevents the insanity defense from being circumvented and avoids the confusion that would arise from dual use of the same evidence. Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 445–46 (1992) (state procedural rules carry a presumption of constitutionality).

The right to present a defense recognized in Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973), and Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986), remains subject to “the usual rules of evidence.” Clark therefore had no Fourteenth Amendment entitlement to introduce expert testimony on mental state for any purpose other than the insanity defense Arizona provided. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
