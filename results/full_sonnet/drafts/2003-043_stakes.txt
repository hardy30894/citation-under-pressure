# ARGUMENT

**I. Because Debtors Are Separate "Taxpayers" Under the Code, the IRS's Assessment Against the Partnership Did Not Extend the Limitations Period Applicable to Debtors.**

The Internal Revenue Code imposes a three-year limitations period for the IRS to assess a "taxpayer" for unpaid taxes. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Only where "the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto" does the ten-year collection period of § 6502(a) apply, and it applies only to "the taxpayer" so assessed. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The Code defines "taxpayer" broadly to mean "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and defines "person" to include "an individual" and "a . . . partnership" as distinct categories. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14). An individual partner and the partnership itself are therefore separate taxpayers, each entitled to the protection of an independently running limitations period.

This textual structure is not a technicality; it is the mechanism by which Congress balanced the government's collection interests against the repose interests of individual citizens. An assessment is not a mere bookkeeping notation—this Court has held that "the assessment . . . is given the force of a judgment." Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 260 (1935). A judgment against one party does not, by operation of law, become a judgment against another. Because the IRS never assessed Debtors individually, it never obtained the equivalent of a judgment against them, and the ten-year extension of § 6502(a) never attached to Debtors' separate three-year limitations period under § 6501(a).

**II. This Court's Decisions in *Young* and *Coson* Confirm That an Assessment Must Be Made Against the Specific Taxpayer From Whom Collection Is Sought.**

The Ninth Circuit's own precedent forecloses the government's position. In *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), this Court affirmed a district court decision recognizing that a partner's liability for partnership taxes assessed only against the partnership survives solely because the tax was already collected; the district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that to *collect* an unassessed tax from a partner, "the IRS would have had to either resort to court action or individually assess the taxes against the partner." *Young*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff'd, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). Nothing in this Court's affirmance disturbed that holding.

*United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), reinforces the point. There, the district court held that a tax lien could not attach to an individual partner's property where the assessment named only the partnership and "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers," because 26 U.S.C. § 6203 and its implementing regulations require that "a valid assessment 'shall provide identification of the taxpayer.'" *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 675 (S.D. Cal. 1958), aff'd, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). This Court's affirmance did not reject that reasoning. Together, *Young* and *Coson* establish that identification of, and assessment against, the specific person to be charged is a precondition of enforceable collection.

**III. California Partnership Law Does Not Substitute for a Judgment Against the Individual Partners.**

The government's fallback theory—that state law joint-and-several liability permits collection directly from partners—fails on its own terms. Under California law, "[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The government obtained no judgment—federal or state—against Debtors. A bankruptcy claim may be disallowed where it "is unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor . . . under . . . applicable law." 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Because California law bars collection from a partner's separate assets absent an individual judgment, and because the time to obtain such a judgment expired under § 6501(a), the IRS's claim was properly disallowed.

**IV. *United States v. Wright* Does Not Support the Government's Position.**

The government's reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed *both* the partnership and the individual partners, extending the six-year collection period as to each. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the partners' independently-running limitations period should be tolled during the partnership's bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562–63. The Seventh Circuit never considered whether an assessment against the partnership alone could extend the limitations period for the partners, because that predicate assessment against the partners already existed. *Wright* therefore offers no support for dispensing with individual assessment altogether.

**CONCLUSION**

Because Debtors were never individually assessed, and because California law required a separate judgment before their assets could satisfy the Partnership's tax debt, the ten-year extension of § 6502(a) never applied to them. The ordinary three-year period of § 6501(a) governs, and it expired before the IRS filed its claims. The judgment below should be affirmed.
