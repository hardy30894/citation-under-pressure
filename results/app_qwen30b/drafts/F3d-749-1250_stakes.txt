**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones as an expert on deception and truthfulness, thereby violating Stanley Hill’s constitutional right to a fair trial under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The admission of Agent Jones’s opinion testimony—based on subjective, unscientific, and inherently prejudicial observations of demeanor and speech—constituted plain error under Federal Rule of Evidence 103(d) and violated the fundamental principles of due process established in *United States v. Scheffer*, 526 U.S. 493 (1999), and *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). Because the error was both plain and affected substantial rights, reversal is required.

Agent Jones testified as an expert on “identifying deception in statements and truths in statements,” claiming to have been trained in “special tactics” to detect lies. He opined that Stanley’s answers “did not make sense” and were “not worthy of credence,” and that Stanley’s evasive behaviors were “common among the criminal element.” He further asserted, without foundation, that “never in my career have I seen [a person] say they’d rather die than face charges” from an innocent person. Most egregiously, he testified that invoking God to affirm truthfulness is a “deceptive” behavior, stating: “When people start bringing faith into validating their statements, that they’re deceptive.” These statements were not based on any scientific methodology, peer-reviewed research, or established behavioral science. Rather, they reflected the subjective, anecdotal, and stereotypical judgments of a law enforcement officer with no recognized expertise in psychology, linguistics, or lie detection.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that expert testimony must be grounded in scientific validity and reliability. In *Daubert*, the Court emphasized that “the trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.” 509 U.S. at 592. The Court identified several factors to assess reliability, including whether the theory or technique has been tested, whether it has been subjected to peer review, and whether it has a known or potential rate of error. Id. at 593–94. Agent Jones’s testimony failed each of these criteria. His “methodology” was not tested, not peer-reviewed, and not subject to any known error rate. His conclusions were based on personal experience and anecdotal observation—precisely the kind of subjective impressionism the Court rejected in *Daubert*.

Moreover, the Court in *United States v. Scheffer* held that the admission of polygraph evidence—despite its widespread use in law enforcement—was improper because it lacked scientific reliability. 526 U.S. at 501. The Court noted that “the scientific community has not accepted polygraphy as a reliable method of detecting deception.” Id. at 500. Agent Jones’s testimony was no more reliable than polygraphy. It relied on the same unproven assumptions: that certain behaviors—such as evasiveness, emotional distress, or religious references—correlate with deception. Yet, as the Court recognized in *Scheffer*, “the mere fact that a technique is used by law enforcement does not make it reliable.” Id. at 501. The same is true here: the fact that FBI agents may use such observations in interviews does not render them admissible as expert testimony.

The admission of such testimony is particularly prejudicial in a criminal trial. The jury was left with the impression that Agent Jones, a government expert, had a “scientific” basis for concluding that Stanley was lying—despite the absence of any such basis. This testimony effectively allowed the government to present a “character” or “guilt” inference based on demeanor, which is precisely what the Supreme Court has warned against. In *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667 (1985), the Court held that “the admission of evidence that tends to suggest guilt based on demeanor or conduct, rather than on facts, may be so prejudicial as to violate due process.” Id. at 677. Here, the testimony went beyond mere observation and crossed into the realm of impermissible opinion on guilt. By stating that Stanley’s invocation of God was “deceptive,” the government invited the jury to equate religious faith with dishonesty—a dangerous and constitutionally suspect inference.

The error was not only plain but also affected substantial rights. The government’s case rested heavily on the credibility of Stanley’s statements during interrogation. Agent Jones’s testimony directly undermined Stanley’s credibility, suggesting that his emotional responses and religious references were signs of guilt. This testimony was not cumulative; it was central to the government’s narrative. Without it, the jury might have viewed Stanley’s statements as consistent with a person under stress, not deception. The error was therefore “so obvious and substantial as to affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993).

Because the district court admitted expert testimony that was neither reliable nor relevant under *Daubert* and *Scheffer*, and because the error was plain and affected substantial rights, reversal is required. The judgment of the district court must be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial free from the taint of this improper expert testimony.

**WHEREFORE**, appellant Stanley Eugene Hill respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and remand for a new trial.
