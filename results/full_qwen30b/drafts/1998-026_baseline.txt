**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief—particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to appeal.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires a district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal following a guilty plea. *See Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2)*. This requirement is designed to ensure that defendants are fully informed of their appellate rights, thereby promoting the integrity of the sentencing process and safeguarding against unknowing forfeiture of appellate remedies. *See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 60 (2002)* (“The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal.”). However, the rule’s purpose is not served by a mechanical application that grants relief whenever the court fails to recite the required language—especially when the defendant has actual knowledge of his right and has exercised it.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that not every violation of a procedural rule warrants reversal or collateral relief. *See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993)* (“A reviewing court may not reverse a conviction for a nonconstitutional error unless the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights.”). The Court has further held that Rule 32(a)(2) violations are subject to harmless-error review. *See Vonn, 535 U.S. at 60–61*. In *Vonn*, the Court emphasized that “the failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is not a structural error” and that “the defendant’s actual knowledge of the right to appeal is a critical factor in determining whether the error was harmless.” *Id. at 61*. The Court explained that “if the defendant knew of his right to appeal, the error is harmless.” *Id.* at 62.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found, based on credible testimony, that Manuel D. Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The record reflects that Peguero had been represented by counsel, who had advised him of his appellate rights, and that Peguero himself had previously appealed a prior conviction. *See App. 12–13*. This evidence establishes that Peguero was not only aware of his right to appeal but had exercised it in the past. Under *Vonn*, this renders the failure to advise a harmless error.

The Court of Appeals correctly applied *Vonn* and held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review. *See Peguero v. United States, 124 F.3d 457, 460 (3d Cir. 1997)*. The court concluded that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to ensure that defendants are informed of their right to appeal—had been satisfied because Peguero was, in fact, informed. *Id.* This conclusion is consistent with the principle that “the law does not require a defendant to be told what he already knows.” *See United States v. Bynum, 155 F.3d 1250, 1254 (11th Cir. 1998)*.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that procedural rules like Rule 32(a)(2) create per se grounds for collateral relief. *See United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 166 (1982)* (“[T]he mere violation of a procedural rule does not automatically entitle a defendant to relief.”). To hold otherwise would invite abuse of the habeas process and undermine finality in criminal convictions. *See id.* at 167 (“The availability of habeas relief is not to be expanded to cover every procedural error.”). The Court has also cautioned against treating Rule 32(a)(2) as a “safety net” for defendants who fail to appeal for strategic reasons. *See Vonn, 535 U.S. at 62*.

Peguero’s claim is not merely a procedural oversight—it is a claim that a court’s failure to recite a formality, when the defendant already knew his rights, should nullify a final conviction. Such a rule would transform the habeas corpus statute into a vehicle for second-guessing trial strategy and would encourage defendants to delay appeals and then seek relief based on technical violations. *See id.* at 63* (“[T]he defendant’s decision not to appeal must be respected if he knew of his right to do so.”)*.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless error because he was aware of that right at the time of sentencing and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to appeal. The purpose of the rule was fulfilled, and no prejudice resulted. The Court should not expand habeas relief to cover such technical violations when the defendant’s actual knowledge and informed choice are clear.

**For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.**
