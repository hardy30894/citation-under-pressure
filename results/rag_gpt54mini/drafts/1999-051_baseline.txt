**Argument**

Under this Court’s precedents, a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may not serve as “cause” to excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was properly exhausted and not itself barred. The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule is irreconcilable with *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), and *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255 (1989).

1. **Cause must be a claim the state courts had a fair opportunity to consider.**  
This Court has held that a habeas petitioner generally must present his federal claim to the state courts before obtaining federal relief. *O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 844-45 (1999). That exhaustion requirement applies with full force to an ineffective-assistance claim asserted as cause for another defaulted claim. In *Murray v. Carrier*, the Court explained that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” 477 U.S. at 489. The reason is straightforward: a state court should have the first opportunity to adjudicate the constitutional adequacy of counsel’s performance. *Id.* at 488-89.

That principle controls here. Carpenter’s asserted cause is appellate counsel’s ineffectiveness. But the state courts dismissed that ineffectiveness claim as untimely. A claim rejected by the state courts on procedural grounds is itself defaulted and cannot automatically supply cause for a separate defaulted claim.

2. **A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot bootstrap federal review of another defaulted claim.**  
In *Coleman v. Thompson*, the Court held that when a petitioner defaults a claim in state court pursuant to an independent and adequate state procedural rule, federal habeas review is barred unless the petitioner demonstrates cause and prejudice or a fundamental miscarriage of justice. 501 U.S. at 750. The Court further made clear that “cause” must itself be an external impediment, not merely a second defaulted federal claim. *Id.* at 753-54. If the underlying ineffectiveness claim was not properly presented to the state courts, it cannot serve as cause unless the petitioner can independently excuse that default. *Id.* at 752-54.

This Court’s treatment of related default questions confirms the point. In *Harris v. Reed*, the Court explained that federal habeas review yields to a state court judgment that rests on an independent and adequate state ground. 489 U.S. at 262-63. Here, the state courts’ timeliness ruling was such a ground. The federal courts may not disregard it simply because the petitioner recasts the same issue as “cause.”

3. **Allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse another default would erase the procedural-default doctrine.**  
The doctrine of procedural default is designed to enforce comity, finality, and orderly federal-state litigation. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750-51. If a petitioner could always rely on an unexhausted or defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to resurrect any other defaulted claim, the state courts’ procedural rules would be rendered largely meaningless. A petitioner could bypass state review by defaulting both the substantive claim and the ineffectiveness claim, then asking federal court to treat one default as the excuse for the other. *Murray* and *Coleman* do not permit that result. See *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488-89; *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-54.

Nor does *Terrell v. Morris, Superintendent, Southern Ohio Correctional Facility*, 493 U.S. 1 (1989), help Carpenter. There, the Court addressed whether a state could apply a new procedural bar retroactively to an ineffective-assistance claim; the case did not hold that a defaulted ineffectiveness claim may serve as cause for a different defaulted claim. *Id.* at 2-3. Likewise, *Stewart v. LaGrand*, 526 U.S. 115 (1999), reflects that default and cause are separate inquiries; a petitioner must establish a valid basis for excusing the default. *Id.* at 119-20.

4. **Carpenter’s claim fails because he has not shown cause for the default of his ineffectiveness claim.**  
To excuse the sufficiency-of-the-evidence default, Carpenter relies on appellate counsel’s ineffectiveness. But that ineffectiveness claim was itself procedurally defaulted in Ohio as untimely. Under *Murray* and *Coleman*, Carpenter therefore must show cause and prejudice for that default too. He has not done so. Without a valid, exhausted, and nondefaulted ineffectiveness claim, there is no cause to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
