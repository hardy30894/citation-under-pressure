ARGUMENT

I.   THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO WORKERS INJURED AS A DIRECT RESULT OF OPERATIONS CONDUCTED ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct causal link between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted this provision to require a "situs-of-injury" test, holding that an employee must be injured on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it to be eligible for OCSLA benefits. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc).

The Supreme Court's dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), supports this interpretation. Although the Court did not directly address the issue, it noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." Id. at 219. This statement suggests that the location of the injury is a critical factor in determining OCSLA coverage.

In contrast, the Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test, adopted in this case, is overly broad and ignores the statutory language requiring a direct causal link between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP, 693 F.3d 6936 (9th Cir. 2010). The Third Circuit's "but for" test, adopted in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is similarly flawed, as it would extend OCSLA coverage to employees injured in circumstances only tangentially related to outer Continental Shelf operations.

II.  THE BRB CORRECTLY DENIED BENEFITS UNDER THE LHWCA

The LHWCA requires that an employee be injured on a maritime situs to be eligible for benefits. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Benefits Review Board ("BRB") correctly determined that La Conchita, the onshore oil-processing facility where Valladolid was killed, is not a maritime situs. The facility is not adjacent to navigable waters, is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. See Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982).

The Ninth Circuit's "functional relationship" test, applied in Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978), supports the BRB's determination. La Conchita's primary purpose is to process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, a non-maritime activity. Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb's Welding I), 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985). Although the facility is used to store scrap metal from the platforms, this use is incidental to its primary purpose and does not convert it into a maritime situs.

The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it. The BRB's denial of benefits under the LHWCA should be affirmed, as La Conchita is not a maritime situs.
