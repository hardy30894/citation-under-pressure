The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the claims are moot. When a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence, a case becomes moot. Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc., 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013); Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000); County of Los Angeles v. Davis, 440 U.S. 625, 631 (1979). Georgia Gwinnett College repealed the speech zones and code provisions, replacing them with policies permitting campus speech without permits except in narrow circumstances. The district court found no reasonable basis to expect reversion, and appellants do not contest that finding on appeal.

A request for nominal damages does not preserve a live controversy once the underlying conduct has ended. Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248, 1263-64 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc). Nominal damages are a symbolic remedy that does not redress an ongoing injury. Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43, 71 (1997); Farrar v. Hobby, 506 U.S. 103, 114 (1992). Where, as here, the complaint alleges only the abstract violation of constitutional rights without pleading concrete, compensable harm, the claim for nominal damages cannot avert mootness. Slicker v. Jackson, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230-31 (11th Cir. 2000).

The voluntary-cessation exception does not apply. The college’s policy change was complete, unambiguous, and not a tactical maneuver timed to evade review. Already, 568 U.S. at 93; DeFunis v. Odegaard, 416 U.S. 312, 317 (1974). Nor does the capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review doctrine rescue the case, because the policies have been superseded and appellants face no continuing threat. United States v. Munsingwear, Inc., 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950).

Appellants’ request for leave to amend was properly denied. A request embedded in an opposition memorandum does not constitute a proper motion under Rule 15. Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). The district court correctly declined to permit amendment after judgment. Jacobs v. Tempur-Pedic Int’l, Inc., 626 F.3d 1327, 1344 (11th Cir. 2010).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
