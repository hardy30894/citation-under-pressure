**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001), the Court held that *§ 1231(a)(6)* of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not authorize indefinite detention. Rather, the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas has been detained for over three years beyond the 90-day removal period. No country will accept him. Germany and Lithuania have both rejected his claims to citizenship. No repatriation treaty exists with any nation capable of receiving him. The INS has made no meaningful progress toward removal in years. Under these circumstances, the continued detention of Zadvydas is not reasonably necessary to secure removal, and thus exceeds the authority granted by *§ 1231(a)(6)*.

The Court in *Zadvydas* made clear that the statute’s reach is not unlimited. It emphasized that “indefinite detention of aliens in the former category [i.e., admitted aliens] would raise serious constitutional concerns,” and therefore construed the statute to contain an “implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.” Id. at 2495. The Court rejected the notion that detention could continue indefinitely so long as the government claims to be making “good faith efforts” to remove the alien. Id. at 2503. Instead, the Court established a framework: “if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” Id. at 2504.

The Fifth Circuit, in its initial opinion, erred by adopting a standard that demanded Zadvydas prove *absolute* impossibility of removal—a burden no alien could meet. The Court in *Zadvydas* rejected such a standard, noting that “this standard would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—[which] demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” Id. at 2505. The correct inquiry is not whether removal is *possible*, but whether it is *reasonably foreseeable*. The Court made clear that “what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink” as the period of detention grows. Id.

Here, Zadvydas has been in custody for more than three years beyond the removal period. The INS has not identified any country willing to accept him. No repatriation treaty exists with any nation. The government has not even attempted to negotiate with any country. The only “efforts” cited are administrative inquiries that have yielded no results. Under these circumstances, the government has failed to meet its burden of demonstrating that removal remains reasonably foreseeable.

The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized that “the statute’s basic purpose” is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” Id. at 2504. But when removal is not reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves that purpose. The Court acknowledged that “the choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large’” but between imprisonment and supervised release. Id. at 2501. The Court explicitly approved of supervised release as a constitutionally permissible alternative. Id. The district court’s order, which imposed conditions including regular reporting, employment, residence, and bond, is consistent with this framework. The INS has not challenged the adequacy of these conditions, nor has it shown that Zadvydas poses a threat to the community or a flight risk.

The Fifth Circuit’s initial opinion, which held that detention was lawful so long as “good faith efforts” continued and removal was not “impossible,” misapplied the *Zadvydas* standard. It required Zadvydas to prove a negative—absolute impossibility—when the Constitution demands only that the government show a reasonable prospect of removal. The Court in *Zadvydas* rejected such a high bar. It held that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” Id. at 2503. That moment has long passed.

The Court’s instruction that “after this 6-month period, once the alien provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing” is directly applicable here. Id. at 2505. Zadvydas has provided such a showing: no country will accept him; no treaty exists; no progress has been made in years. The government has not rebutted this showing. Indeed, it has offered no evidence that removal is reasonably foreseeable.

The Fifth Circuit’s subsequent reversal, based on the Supreme Court’s guidance, was correct in outcome but insufficient in reasoning. It affirmed the district court’s judgment but failed to fully articulate the constitutional and statutory limits on detention. The Court must now make clear: *§ 1231(a)(6)* does not authorize indefinite detention. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention is unlawful. The government may not detain an alien indefinitely simply because it has not yet found a country willing to accept him.

The Fifth Circuit’s judgment, as modified, is consistent with *Zadvydas*. But the Court must affirm that the Constitution forbids indefinite detention. The government may not hold an alien in custody without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. When that purpose is no longer attainable, the detention must end. Zadvydas has been detained far beyond any reasonable period. His continued confinement is not authorized by law and violates the Due Process Clause.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be affirmed.
