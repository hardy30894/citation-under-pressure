**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and the lower court correctly held that she may not do so. This Court has long recognized that private individuals may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to attack federal legislation absent a showing of direct injury to their own legal rights, and that the Tenth Amendment does not confer standing upon private parties to litigate federalism concerns on behalf of the states. The judgment of the Third Circuit must be affirmed.

The foundational principle that private parties lack standing to assert the Tenth Amendment is established in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, the Court rejected a challenge to the federal government’s operation of a public utility on the ground that it violated the Tenth Amendment, holding that “the private companies ... have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. The Court emphasized that the Tenth Amendment protects the sovereignty of the states, not the interests of private corporations. As such, only states or their officers may assert a Tenth Amendment claim. Id. This principle has been consistently reaffirmed in subsequent decisions.

In *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716 (1931), the Court held that a private party could not challenge a federal statute on the ground that it exceeded Congress’s enumerated powers. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not confer upon private individuals the right to question the validity of a federal law on the ground that it exceeds the powers of Congress.” Id. at 730, 51 S.Ct. 220. The Court further explained that “the power to enforce the Constitution is not given to private persons,” and that “the only persons who may invoke the Constitution to challenge the validity of a law are those whose rights are directly infringed by it.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to challenges based on the Tenth Amendment, which is a limitation on federal power, not a source of individual rights.

The Court has long recognized that the Tenth Amendment is not a source of individual rights. In *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Court upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act against a Tenth Amendment challenge, stating: “The Tenth Amendment is not a limitation upon the powers of Congress, but a declaration of the principle that the powers not delegated to the United States are reserved to the States or to the people.” Id. at 114, 61 S.Ct. 451. The Court emphasized that the Tenth Amendment “does not create rights in individuals,” and that “the States are not the only parties who may be affected by federal legislation.” Id. Thus, a private individual cannot claim injury from a federal law merely because it intrudes upon state sovereignty.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304 (1937), where the Court upheld the President’s authority to enforce a foreign policy embargo without congressional authorization. The Court stated: “The federal government is not a government of delegated powers only. It is a government of inherent powers, and the powers of the federal government are not limited to those specifically enumerated in the Constitution.” Id. at 316, 57 S.Ct. 163. The Court further held that “the federal government possesses powers not granted by the Constitution, but inherent in the nature of sovereignty.” Id. at 317, 57 S.Ct. 163. This inherent authority includes the power to enforce treaties, which are the supreme law of the land under Article VI of the Constitution.

The Court has also held that the Treaty Power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), the Court upheld a federal statute implementing the Migratory Bird Treaty Act against a Tenth Amendment challenge. The Court stated: “The treaty power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment. The Constitution is supreme, and the treaty is a part of it.” Id. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382. The Court further held that “if a treaty is valid, there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute enacted to carry it out.” Id. This principle has been consistently applied in subsequent decisions.

In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), the Court held that a private party could not challenge a federal law on the ground that it exceeded Congress’s powers. The Court stated: “The Constitution is not a grant of power to the people, but a grant of power to the government.” Id. at 95, 2 L.Ed. 554. The Court further held that “the people have no right to question the validity of a federal law unless their rights are directly affected.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to Tenth Amendment challenges.

In *United States v. Wilson*, 12 U.S. (8 Cranch) 150 (1814), the Court held that a private party could not challenge a federal law on the ground that it violated the separation of powers. The Court stated: “The Constitution is not a grant of power to the people, but a grant of power to the government.” Id. at 158, 3 L.Ed. 377. The Court further held that “the people have no right to question the validity of a federal law unless their rights are directly affected.” Id.

Finally, in *United States v. Biddle*, 318 U.S. 195 (1943), the Court upheld a federal statute against a Tenth Amendment challenge, stating: “The Constitution is supreme, and the treaty is a part of it.” Id. at 200, 63 S.Ct. 537. The Court further held that “the Treaty Power is not limited by the Tenth Amendment.” Id. at 201, 63 S.Ct. 537.

For these reasons, Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds.
