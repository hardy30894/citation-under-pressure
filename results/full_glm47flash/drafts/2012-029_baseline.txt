**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S “AIR MEASUREMENT” TEST IS AN IMPROPER SUBSTITUTE FOR THE SUPREME COURT’S FEDERALISM ANALYSIS IN *GRABLE***

The Federal Circuit’s test for exclusive federal jurisdiction over state-law claims involving patent issues, articulated in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), is an improper substitute for the Supreme Court’s federalism analysis. The Federal Circuit’s test focuses on whether a patent issue is “necessary, disputed, and substantial” and whether Congress intended federal resolution. *Id.* at 1273. This approach ignores the Supreme Court’s instruction that federal courts must be “reluctant to allow state plaintiffs to open the ‘arising under’ door” by simply pleading a federal issue. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808 (1988). Instead, the Supreme Court requires a rigorous analysis of whether federal jurisdiction will disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The Federal Circuit’s test fails to account for the “selective process” the Supreme Court has adopted to limit federal jurisdiction to cases where federal law is truly central. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09.

**II. UNDER THE *GRABLE* FACTORS, THE STATE COURT HAS JURISDICTION BECAUSE THE PATENT ISSUE IS NEITHER NECESSARY NOR SUBSTANTIAL TO THE MALPRACTICE CLAIM**

The Supreme Court’s *Grable* framework provides the correct standard for determining whether a state-law malpractice claim “arises under” federal patent law. Under *Grable*, federal question jurisdiction exists where (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. This Court must apply these factors, not the Federal Circuit’s broader test.

First, the patent issue is not necessary to the resolution of the malpractice claim. Under *Christianson*, federal jurisdiction exists only if a plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal law. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09. Here, the malpractice claim is premised on the alleged negligence of the attorneys in failing to raise a specific defense. The validity of the patent or the applicability of the experimental use exception is not a prerequisite to proving that the attorneys were negligent. A plaintiff can establish legal malpractice by showing that the attorney breached the standard of care, regardless of whether the underlying patent claim would have succeeded. The patent issue is merely a sub-issue of the underlying litigation, not a necessary element of the malpractice claim itself.

Second, the patent issue is not substantial in the context of the malpractice claim. The *Grable* factors require a federal issue to be “actually disputed and substantial.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. While the experimental use exception is a significant issue in patent law, it is not substantial to the malpractice claim. The malpractice claim is about the attorney’s conduct, not the validity of the patent. As the Supreme Court noted, a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). The result of the malpractice claim—damages for legal malpractice—does not depend on the resolution of the patent issue. The outcome of the patent case is irrelevant to whether the attorneys were negligent in their representation.

**III. FEDERAL JURISDICTION WOULD DISTURB THE BALANCE OF FEDERAL AND STATE JUDICIAL RESPONSIBILITIES**

Finally, allowing federal courts to exercise exclusive jurisdiction over this malpractice claim would disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Attorney malpractice is a quintessential state law matter. State courts are traditionally the forum for resolving disputes between attorneys and clients regarding the standard of care and professional conduct. Allowing federal courts to hear these claims, even when they involve patent law, would encroach upon the state courts’ traditional role and undermine the federalism principles that underpin the division of judicial power. The Federal Circuit’s test, which focuses on the federal interest in uniformity, fails to weigh these state interests adequately. Therefore, the state court has jurisdiction over this matter.
