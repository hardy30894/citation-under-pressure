**Argument**

**Johnson announced a new substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review.**

Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules generally do not apply on collateral review unless they are substantive or are watershed procedural rules. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). A rule is substantive if it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Id.* at 353. Substantive rules include decisions that “narrow[] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” as well as rules that place “particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish.” *Id.* at 351–52; *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620–21 (1998).

*Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), is substantive under those settled principles. The Court held that ACCA’s residual clause is void for vagueness because it “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges.” *Id.* at 595, 597. That holding did not regulate the procedures used to find facts; it eliminated one statutory basis for imposing an enhanced sentence. Where “Congress has no authority to impose a punishment that exceeds what the statute allows,” a decision invalidating a sentencing provision is substantive. *Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1264–65 (2016); *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353.

The logic of *Johnson* fits squarely within the Court’s retroactivity precedents. In *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346–47 (1974), the Court held that collateral relief is available when a decision shows that the petitioner “stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal.” Likewise, in *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620–21, the Court applied retroactively an interpretation narrowing a criminal statute because the decision “involved a substantive change in the law.” *Johnson* likewise narrows ACCA by “alter[ing] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353.

The government’s contrary position confuses vagueness doctrine with procedure. A procedural rule “regulates only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability.” *Id.* at 353. But *Johnson* did not address how courts decide ACCA cases; it removed from the statute a category of offenses that could not constitutionally support punishment. After *Johnson*, a defendant whose sentence depended on the residual clause “is not a category of offenders subject to ACCA.” *Welch*, 136 S. Ct. at 1265. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule.

This Court’s decisions confirm the point. In *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 550 (1982), the Court explained that where a decision “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe,” the rule is substantive. The same is true when a decision “narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute.” *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351–52. *Johnson* does both: it strips force from ACCA’s residual clause and narrows the class of offenders subject to the Act’s fifteen-year minimum. See *Welch*, 136 S. Ct. at 1265–66.

Nor does the “void for vagueness” label make *Johnson* procedural. The Court has retroactively applied decisions resting on constitutional invalidation of sentencing provisions where those decisions “necessarily carried a significant risk that a defendant” was punished under a provision the law no longer authorizes. *See*, e.g., *Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460, 465, 470 (2012); *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 198–99 (2016). ACCA’s residual clause is no different. Once the clause is excised, sentences enhanced solely by that clause exceed the punishment the statute permits. *Welch*, 136 S. Ct. at 1265.

Finally, retroactivity here serves the core purpose of collateral review: to correct “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice.” *Davis*, 417 U.S. at 346. A prisoner serving a sentence imposed under an unconstitutional clause is serving a sentence Congress never validly authorized. That is precisely the sort of substantive error *Teague* permits courts to correct. *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 351–53; *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620–21.

The judgment below should be reversed.
