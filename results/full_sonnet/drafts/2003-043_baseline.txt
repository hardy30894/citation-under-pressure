# ARGUMENT

## I. The Internal Revenue Code Requires a Separate Assessment Against Each "Taxpayer" Before the Government May Invoke the Ten-Year Collection Period Against That Taxpayer.

The three-year limitations period of 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) requires the Commissioner to assess a tax "against the taxpayer" within three years of the filing of the taxpayer's return. Only if that timely assessment is made does § 6502(a) extend the government's time to collect "by levy or by a proceeding in court" to ten years "after the assessment of the tax." 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The extension is keyed expressly to "the assessment," not to any generalized notion of liability.

Congress defined "taxpayer" to mean "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and defined "person" to include, separately, "an individual" and "a partnership." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (a)(14). A partnership and its individual partners are therefore distinct taxpayers under the Code's own terms. The regulation implementing § 6203 confirms that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer," 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1—meaning the assessment must identify the particular person against whom the extended collection period is to run. Because the Partnership and each Debtor are separate taxpayers, an assessment naming only the Partnership cannot, by its terms, extend the limitations period applicable to Petitioners individually. As the court below correctly held, "[t]he assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership," leaving "unaltered the limitations period applicable to" the individual partners.

This reading gives effect to the structural design of the assessment-and-collection scheme: assessment is the mechanism by which the government converts a tax liability into something functionally equivalent to a judgment, entitling it to proceed directly against the assessed taxpayer's property without further judicial process. See 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 6502(a). That extraordinary self-help remedy is properly confined to the taxpayer actually assessed. Nothing in the statute permits the government to bootstrap an assessment against one taxpayer into an open-ended collection right against a different taxpayer who was never assessed.

## II. This Court's Precedents Confirm That Assessment—or a Timely Judgment—Against the Individual Is a Prerequisite to Direct Collection From That Individual.

This Court's decision in *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not support the government's contrary position. The district court in *Young* expressly recognized that "[i]t is the government's contention that where an assessment names an entity such as in the instant case, that it is unnecessary to name the individual members of the entity in order to establish individual liability," but that assessment naming the entity alone would not permit the government to *collect* from the individual "without resorting to court action." *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959), *aff'd*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). This Court's affirmance did not disturb that limitation; it held only that a partner who had *already paid* the assessed tax could not obtain a refund merely because the assessment failed to name him individually. 283 F.2d at 910. *Young* thus confirms, rather than undermines, the rule that the government's remedies to compel payment from an unwilling individual partner require either an individual assessment or a court judgment against that partner.

*United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), reinforces the same principle. There, the district court held that a tax lien "does not exist against a particular individual's property pursuant to §[§] 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203." *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This Court's affirmance rested on a different, narrower ground, but nothing in its opinion repudiated that holding. The consistent thread running through this Court's precedent is that the government's coercive collection remedies attach to a particular taxpayer only when that taxpayer has been assessed or reduced to judgment.

## III. State Partnership Law Does Not Excuse the Government's Failure to Obtain a Judgment Against the Individual Partners Within the Applicable Limitations Period.

The government's fallback theory—that state law joint-and-several liability permits it to collect the Partnership's tax debt directly from Petitioners—fares no better. Under California law, "[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner," and such a judgment "may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). A creditor holding a claim against a partnership must still obtain a judgment against the individual partner before reaching that partner's separate assets. The government never obtained such a judgment against Petitioners, and the three-year period in which it might have brought a timely collection action against them under § 6501(a) has long since expired.

Because a bankruptcy claim may be disallowed when it "is unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor," 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), and because the government's claims against Petitioners are unenforceable under both federal assessment law and California partnership law, the courts below correctly disallowed the IRS's proofs of claim.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
