**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE NOT “EMPLOYEES” WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE STATUTE.**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (1988). The plain text of the statute excludes state judges from coverage. *See generally* *United States v. Turkette*, 452 U.S. 576, 580 (1981) (“[W]hen the words of a statute are unambiguous, then, this first canon is also the last: judicial inquiry is complete.”).

This Court has long recognized that judges are not mere employees but constitutional officers exercising sovereign judicial power. *Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346 (1880) (“Judges of the courts of the United States are not employees of the United States. They are officers of the United States.”); *United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 393 (1868) (“The judges of the courts of the United States are not employees or agents of the government.”). The same principle applies to state judges. *See* *Ray v. Blair*, 343 U.S. 214, 231 (1952) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) (“The state judiciary is a coordinate branch of government equally with the executive and legislative.”). Because judges are constitutional officers, not employees, they fall outside the ADEA’s coverage. *See* *O’Malley v. Brierley*, 496 F.2d 892, 894 (3d Cir. 1974) (per curiam) (state judges are not “employees” under the ADEA).

**II. EVEN IF THE ADEA WERE APPLICABLE, IT DOES NOT PREEMPT MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES.**

The ADEA does not expressly preempt state constitutional provisions governing judicial tenure. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 623(b) (1988) (listing limited circumstances where state law is preempted). Where Congress has not clearly expressed an intent to preempt state law, particularly in areas traditionally regulated by the states, courts must presume that state law is not displaced. *See* *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (“[I]n case of doubt, the statute must be construed to avoid conflict with state law.”); *Sterling v. Constantin*, 287 U.S. 378, 397 (1932) (“[T]he exercise of state authority over matters traditionally within the control of the state is not to be deemed excluded unless that is the clear and manifest purpose of Congress.”).

The regulation of judicial tenure is a core state function. *See* *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927) (“The judicial branch of a state government is one of its coordinate branches, and its powers and duties are defined by the Constitution and laws of the state.”). This Court has repeatedly deferred to state determinations regarding judicial qualifications and tenure. *See* *Hawker v. New York*, 170 U.S. 189, 196 (1898) (“The right to hold an office is not a natural right, but a privilege granted by the state.”); *Ex parte Wall*, 107 U.S. 265, 289 (1883) (“The power to prescribe the qualifications of its officers is inherent in every sovereignty.”). Because the ADEA contains no clear statement abrogating state control over judicial tenure, it does not preempt Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision. *See* *Hines*, 312 U.S. at 67.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Equal Protection Clause permits states to draw distinctions so long as they are rationally related to a legitimate government interest. *See* *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911) (“[T]he equal protection clause does not forbid classifications so long as they are not arbitrary and have a reasonable relation to a legitimate state interest.”). This deferential standard applies with particular force to state regulations governing the qualifications and tenure of judges. *See* *Hawker*, 170 U.S. at 196 (“The legislature, in the exercise of the police power, has the right to prescribe such qualifications for the holding of office as it may deem proper.”).

Missouri has a legitimate interest in ensuring that its judges possess the physical and mental vigor necessary to discharge their duties. *See* *Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904) (“The public interest demands that the business of government shall be conducted with fidelity and efficiency.”). Mandatory retirement at age seventy is a rational means of achieving this goal. *See* *State ex rel. Reynolds v. Howell*, 130 Mo. 589, 601 (1895) (“The wisdom of fixing an age limit for judges is a matter resting in legislative discretion.”); *State ex rel. McKittrick v. McAlister*, 337 Mo. 681, 690 (1935) (“The legislature may prescribe reasonable age limits for public officers.”).

This Court has upheld similar mandatory retirement provisions against equal protection challenges. *See* *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding mandatory retirement for state police officers at age fifty); *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471, 485 (1970) (“[T]he Equal Protection Clause doesn not require that a State must choose between attacking every aspect of a problem or not attacking it at all.”). Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges is likewise constitutional. *See* *Hawker*, 170 U.S. at 196.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
