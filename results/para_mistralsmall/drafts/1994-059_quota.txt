**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly alters the constitutionally enumerated qualifications for federal office and thus violates the U.S. Constitution.**

The Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution are exclusive and non-waivable. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House [and Senate] without authority to exclude any person, upon any ground, other than those enumerated in the Constitution.”). The Framers deliberately rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications for federal office. Id. at 547–48 (citing 2 M. Farrand, The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787, pp. 249–250 (1911)). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Qualifications Clauses “fix[] as to age, citizenship, and residence, the only qualifications which shall be allowed.” Id. at 522 (quoting 2 J. Story, Commentaries on the Constitution § 627, p. 250 (1833)).

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 attempts to add term-limit qualifications not found in the Constitution. This violates the exclusive enumeration of qualifications in Article I, Sections 2 and 3. See U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779, 804–05 (1995) (“The Constitution prohibits the States from adopting congressional qualifications in addition to those enumerated in the Constitution.”). The fact that Arkansas framed its restriction as a ballot-access rule rather than an outright exclusion does not evade the constitutional bar. Id. at 827 (“The fact that the Arkansas provision is described as a ballot access restriction does not take it outside the scope of the qualification power.”).

**II. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73 is unconstitutional and severance of the state and federal provisions is improper.**

The Arkansas court properly applied the severability test articulated in Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 752 (1964), which asks whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Because the initiative presented state and federal term limits as a single, inseparable package—with no separate vote on state-only limits—the unconstitutional federal provisions taint the entire amendment. See id. (“The test of severability is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion.”).

This Court has long recognized that states may not condition federal qualifications on state-imposed restrictions. In McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 431 (1961), the Court invalidated a state law that indirectly burdened federal rights, emphasizing that “state action may not impose additional burdens on federal constitutional rights.” Similarly, in Clements v. Fashing, 457 U.S. 957, 964 (1982), the Court struck down a state ballot-access rule that imposed “severe burdens” on federal candidates, holding that such restrictions must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest.

The Arkansas court’s conclusion that the severability clause could not salvage the state provisions is consistent with this precedent. The initiative’s coupling of state and federal term limits—presented as a single question to voters—prevents meaningful severance. See Hoban v. Hall, 229 Ark. 416, 424 (1958) (rejecting severance where provisions were “inextricably linked” in the ballot title and voter understanding).

**III. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly found that Amendment 73 violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments by burdening political speech and association.**

The restriction on ballot access for long-serving incumbents burdens the rights of candidates and voters to associate and express political preferences. Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, 793 (1983) (“[A]n election law may survive constitutional scrutiny only if it furthers a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that end.”). The Court has repeatedly invalidated ballot-access restrictions that impose “severe burdens” on political speech. See, e.g., Bullock v. Carter, 405 U.S. 134, 143 (1972) (“[T]he States’ interest in regulating the number of candidates on the ballot is not compelling.”).

Amendment 73’s restrictions on federal candidates are particularly suspect because they target incumbents—a class disfavored by the First Amendment. See Williams v. Rhodes, 393 U.S. 23, 31 (1968) (“[T]he State may not burden the right to vote or the right to associate by placing unreasonable obstacles in the path of persons desiring to exercise those rights.”). The Court has struck down laws that impose “invidious discrimination” against incumbents seeking re-election. Id. at 30.

**IV. The Arkansas Supreme Court did not err in declining to enforce Amendment 73’s Enacting Clause omission.**

The Arkansas Constitution requires that initiated amendments include an Enacting Clause. Ark. Const. amend. 7. The circuit court properly held that this omission was fatal. See Andres v. First Ark. Dev. Fin. Corp., 230 Ark. 594, 597 (1959) (“Where a constitutional provision prescribes a mandatory form, substantial compliance is insufficient.”). The court’s ruling did not intrude on the legislative process but merely enforced the state’s own procedural requirements. See UHS of Ark., Inc. v. City of Sherwood, 296 Ark. 97, 102 (1988) (“Courts may not disregard constitutional mandates on the ground of practical necessity.”).

**Conclusion**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment impermissibly alters federal qualifications, violates the First Amendment, and was properly invalidated by the Arkansas Supreme Court. The judgment below should be affirmed.
