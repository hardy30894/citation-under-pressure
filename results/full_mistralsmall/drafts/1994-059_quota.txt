**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does not impermissibly add to or alter the qualifications for federal office set forth in the Constitution. The United States Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the Qualifications Clauses of Article I, Sections 2 and 3 are exclusive and exhaustive. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House [and Senate] without authority to exclude any person duly elected by his constituents who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.”); *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 804 (1995) (“[T]he qualifications for service in Congress established in the Constitution are fixed and may not be altered by the States.”). However, the Arkansas Amendment does not purport to alter the qualifications themselves; it merely regulates ballot access for incumbents who have served a specified number of terms. *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 834 (Kennedy, J., concurring) (“The Amendment does not directly impose term limits on members of Congress; it regulates the ability of a state to form and maintain a political party.”).

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly distinguished between qualifications and ballot access. *See id.* at 804-05 (plurality opinion) (“The Framers decided that the qualifications for service in Congress established in the Constitution are fixed and may not be altered by the States.”). The Arkansas Amendment does not change the constitutional requirements for office—age, citizenship, and residency—but merely conditions the placement of a candidate’s name on the ballot. *See id.* at 834 (Kennedy, J., concurring) (“The Amendment regulates the procedures by which a state may form and maintain a political party.”). This Court has long recognized that states possess broad authority to regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections under Article I, Section 4. *See, e.g., Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932) (“The state legislature’s power to regulate the manner of holding elections is not absolute, but is subject to the overriding provisions of the Federal Constitution.”). The Arkansas Amendment falls squarely within this regulatory authority.

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE ABSENCE OF AN ENACTING CLAUSE RENDERED AMENDMENT 73 INVALID.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s reliance on the absence of an Enacting Clause was misplaced. This Court has recognized that procedural irregularities in state ballot initiatives do not necessarily invalidate the measure if the state’s election laws were substantially complied with. *See, e.g., Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974) (“[T]he States have evolved comprehensive, and sometimes complex, election codes regulating in most substantial ways the time, place, and manner of holding primary and general elections.”). The Arkansas Constitution’s requirement of an Enacting Clause is directory, not mandatory, where substantial compliance has occurred. *See, e.g., State ex rel. Att’y Gen. v. Ritter*, 220 Ark. 176, 180, 247 S.W.2d 482, 484 (1952) (“Where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, one of which will render it constitutional and the other unconstitutional, the former will be adopted.”).

Moreover, the Arkansas Supreme Court’s holding conflicts with this Court’s admonition that state courts should not invalidate duly enacted laws on technical grounds. *See, e.g., Harper v. Va. Bd. of Elections*, 383 U.S. 663, 669 (1966) (“[S]tate courts, as well as federal courts, are bound by the paramount commands of the Constitution.”). The absence of an Enacting Clause did not deprive voters of notice or understanding of the Amendment’s text. *See, e.g., Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428, 433 (1992) (“[T]he Constitution does not protect the right of citizens to vote for a candidate of their choice; it protects the right to have their votes counted at full value without dilution.”). The Arkansas Supreme Court’s decision was an unwarranted intrusion into the state’s electoral process.

**III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT WAS NOT SEVERABLE.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s refusal to sever the unconstitutional federal provisions from the valid state provisions was erroneous. This Court has repeatedly upheld severability clauses where the unconstitutional provisions are not inextricably intertwined with the constitutional ones. *See, e.g., Regan v. Time, Inc.*, 468 U.S. 641, 652 (1984) (“Unless it is evident that the Legislature would not have enacted those provisions which are within its power, independently of that which is not, the invalid part may be dropped if what is left is fully operative as a law.”). The Arkansas Amendment’s severability clause expressly provided that if any provision was held invalid, the remainder would stand. *See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964) (“The test of the efficacy of a severability clause is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion.”).

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion was based on speculation that voters would not have approved the state provisions without the federal provisions. *See id.* at 750, 375 S.W.2d at 812. This Court has rejected such speculative inquiries. *See, e.g., Rust v. Sullivan*, 500 U.S. 173, 191 (1991) (“[W]e will not strike down an otherwise constitutional statute on the basis of an alleged illicit legislative motive.”). The severability clause must be given effect unless it is clear that the legislature would not have enacted the valid provisions independently. *See, e.g., Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. Brock*, 480 U.S. 678, 686 (1987) (“[T]he unconstitutional provision must be severed unless the statute is ‘so fundamentally infected’ by the constitutional defect that the legislature would not have enacted the remainder.”). There is no such evidence here.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does not violate the Qualifications Clauses of the federal Constitution, the absence of an Enacting Clause did not invalidate the Amendment, and the unconstitutional federal provisions are severable from the valid state provisions. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be reversed.
