The Ninth Circuit erred in exempting documents relating to the effects of explosions from disclosure under the FOIA because they are "predominantly" for internal use and present a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court's adoption of the "predominantly internal" standard is an unwarranted expansion of Exemption 2, which is meant to be narrowly construed. Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976); U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989).

Exemption 2 exempts from disclosure matters that are "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this exemption to protect information that is not of "genuine and significant public interest" and may risk circumvention of agency regulation. Rose, 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The Ninth Circuit's prior caselaw, as seen in Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), limited Exemption 2 to "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation." Id. at 656.

The D.C. Circuit's "predominantly internal" standard, adopted by the Ninth Circuit in this case, is not supported by the statutory language or Supreme Court precedent. Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981), which introduced this standard, is not binding on this Court, and its reasoning is not persuasive. The Ninth Circuit's existing caselaw does not support the adoption of this broader standard, and Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997), actually suggests that the exemption should be applied more narrowly.

Furthermore, the Navy has not demonstrated that the withheld documents present a risk of circumvention of agency regulation. The ESQD arc maps are not "law enforcement" materials, and their disclosure would not risk circumvention of agency regulation. The Navy's own actions, such as voluntarily providing similar documents for the Bangor base, suggest that the withheld documents do not pose a significant risk.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to exempt the documents under Exemption 2 distorts Congress's careful balance between public access to information and the need to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure. John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the documents relating to the effects of explosions are not exempt from disclosure under Exemption 2.
