The Ninth Circuit's holding that a plaintiff's "actual knowledge" under ERISA's three-year statute of limitations requires awareness of both the underlying transaction and its alleged imprudence is contrary to this Court's precedent and the weight of circuit authority. ERISA's text and legislative history support the conclusion that "actual knowledge" means knowledge of the underlying facts, not knowledge of their legal significance.

ERISA's statute of limitations, 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), bars an action "more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." The Ninth Circuit's interpretation of "actual knowledge" in this case is inconsistent with the plain meaning of the statute and the holdings of other circuits. See, e.g., Gluck v. Unisys Corp., 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992) ("[T]he relevant knowledge for triggering the statute of limitations is knowledge of the facts or transaction that constituted the alleged violation."); Fink v. National Savings & Trust Co., 772 F.2d 951, 957 (D.C. Cir. 1985) ("What [the plaintiff's] knowledge entails is the facts or transaction that constituted the alleged violation.").

This Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "actual knowledge" under ERISA, but its decision in Tibble v. Edison Int'l, 135 S.Ct. 1823 (2015), suggests that the limitations period begins to run when the plaintiff has knowledge of the underlying transaction. The Ninth Circuit's requirement that the plaintiff also know that the transaction was imprudent effectively reads the "actual knowledge" standard out of the statute, as it would allow plaintiffs to delay filing suit until they have obtained legal advice or discovered the alleged breach.

The facts of this case illustrate the practical problems with the Ninth Circuit's holding. Sulyma had access to numerous documents disclosing the investment decisions and strategies employed by Intel, but he claims not to have read or recalled them. Requiring Intel to defend against Sulyma's claims, despite his having had access to the relevant information for years, undermines the purpose of the statute of limitations, which is to prevent stale claims and ensure that defendants are not unfairly prejudiced by delayed litigation.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with a correct interpretation of "actual knowledge" under ERISA. Specifically, the Court should hold that "actual knowledge" means knowledge of the underlying facts or transactions that constitute the alleged breach or violation, not knowledge of their legal significance or imprudence.
