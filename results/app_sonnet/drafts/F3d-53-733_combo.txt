# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Admitting American Steamship's Expert Opinion on the Cause of the Line's Parting.

Expert testimony is admissible only when it assists the jury with matters beyond the ken of ordinary experience. In *Salem v. United States Lines Co.*, 370 U.S. 31 (1962), the Supreme Court reversed a judgment where an expert had been permitted to testify to conclusions that "laymen are quite as competent as [experts] to weigh . . . and draw a conclusion." *Id.* at 35. The same infirmity taints the testimony offered by American Steamship's witness. He did not explain metallurgical or fiber-stress principles unknown to laymen; he simply looked at photographs of a frayed manila rope and told the jury how, in his opinion, it broke. That is not expertise—it is advocacy dressed as science. Where, as here, "the jury is as capable of formulating [the] inference as is the witness," *Salem*, 370 U.S. at 35, the opinion should have been excluded, and its admission was not harmless. The jury's 50% allocation of fault to Cook cannot be disentangled from an opinion that told the jury, in essence, that Cook's own conduct—not a defective or improperly maintained line—caused the parting.

## II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on Unseaworthiness.

The shipowner's duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel is one of the oldest and most exacting obligations known to maritime law. *The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158 (1903), established that the vessel and her owner are "liable to an indemnity for injuries received by seamen in consequence of the unseaworthiness of the ship, or a failure to supply and keep in order the proper appliances appurtenant to the ship." *Id.* at 175. That duty was reaffirmed and sharpened in *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96 (1944), which held that the shipowner's obligation is not measured by the standard of ordinary negligence but is absolute and nondelegable, and in *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), which extended that absolute duty to every seaman performing the ship's work. The standard is well settled: the vessel and her appurtenances—including her rigging and lines—must be "reasonably fit for their intended use." *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 431 (1939). Liability for breach of that duty does not depend on notice, fault, or the exercise of due care by the shipowner; it is, as the Supreme Court later confirmed, a species of liability without fault. *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539 (1960).

The undisputed evidence at trial establishes unseaworthiness as a matter of law. The line supporting Cook's bos'n's chair parted. The bearings on the boom were frozen with unchanged summer grease, forcing the crew to thaw them with open flame under emergency time pressure. The rigging line itself had been left exposed and in continuous service without proper winterization. No reasonable juror could conclude that a boom and line in that condition were "reasonably fit for their intended use," *Socony-Vacuum*, 305 U.S. at 431, and the district court therefore erred in submitting the question to the jury rather than directing a verdict for Cook on unseaworthiness.

## III. The District Court Did Not Err in Submitting Comparative Negligence to the Jury.

While the vessel's unseaworthiness was established as a matter of law, the degree to which Cook's own conduct contributed to his injury remained a jury question. Contributory negligence under the Jones Act, like unseaworthiness, does not bar recovery but only reduces damages in proportion to fault. *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406, 408–09 (1953). The Jones Act incorporates the FELA standard of causation, under which "the test of a jury case is simply whether the proofs justify with reason the conclusion that employer negligence played any part, even the slightest, in producing the injury." *Rogers v. Missouri Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 506 (1957). That featherweight standard governs the defendant's burden, not the plaintiff's; where, as here, there was conflicting evidence regarding Cook's decision to continue using an open flame near the rigging line while it was being laid out, reasonable jurors could differ on the extent of his own fault. The Act's broadly remedial purpose, *Boyd v. Grand Trunk Western R. Co.*, 338 U.S. 263 (1949), does not require that every close factual dispute be resolved in the seaman's favor as a matter of law; it requires only that the jury be permitted to weigh the evidence, which it was.

## IV. The District Court Erred in Admitting Evidence of Cook's Alcohol-Related Ailments.

Even relevant evidence must be excluded where its probative value is outweighed by unfair prejudice. *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469 (1948), recognizes that courts must guard against evidence whose principal effect is to blacken a party's character rather than illuminate the issues before the jury. Cook's alcohol-related medical history had no bearing on how the line parted or on the reasonableness of his conduct on the icy deck; its only function was to invite the jury to view him as an unreliable, blameworthy witness. That is precisely the kind of prejudice *Michelson* condemns, and its admission requires a new trial untainted by this evidence.
