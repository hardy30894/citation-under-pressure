**Argument**

Respondents respectfully submit that the decision of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The district court certified the class only after the rigorous analysis that Rule 23 requires, and it did so based on ample admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis.

**I. The District Court Conducted the Rigorous Analysis Required by Rule 23**

Rule 23(b)(3) permits certification only when “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate.” The district court’s obligation is not to accept the pleadings at face value but to perform a “rigorous analysis” of the evidence. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011). That analysis “will entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” *Id.* at 2551–52. Here, the district court did exactly that.

After originally certifying the class, the court vacated its predominance ruling after the Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), which requires a district court to resolve factual disputes and find by a preponderance of the evidence that Rule 23’s requirements are satisfied. Consistent with *Hydrogen Peroxide*, the district court held a four-day evidentiary hearing, received 32 expert reports, heard live witness testimony, and examined deposition excerpts and documents. It then narrowed the plaintiffs’ theory of antitrust impact to a single theory: that Comcast’s clustering deterred entry by overbuilders. Only after this exhaustive inquiry did the court conclude that the plaintiffs had established, by a preponderance of the evidence, that antitrust impact and damages were susceptible to common proof. That is precisely the process this Court endorsed in *Wal-Mart*.

**II. The District Court Had Adequate Admissible Evidence Supporting a Common Damages Methodology**

Petitioners contend that the district court certified the class without adequate evidence that damages could be measured class-wide. The record demonstrates the opposite. The plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James McClave, proffered a damages model that estimated the overcharge attributable to Comcast’s alleged deterrence of overbuilders. The district court considered that model and found that it provided a common methodology applicable to all class members. See *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 161–65 (E.D. Pa. 2010).

The question presented asks whether certification may proceed without *adequate* admissible evidence. The answer is that the district court had more than adequate evidence—it had a fully developed expert model, tested in adversarial hearings, and the court found that the model could measure damages on a class-wide basis. This Court has long recognized that antitrust damages need not be proven with individualized precision. “[T]he fact of damage may be established by just and reasonable inference, although the result may be only an approximation.” *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 265 (1946). Where, as here, the plaintiffs’ theory of liability and injury is common to the whole class, a common methodology for calculating damages satisfies Rule 23(b)(3).

Neither *Wal-Mart* nor *Hydrogen Peroxide* demands more. *Wal-Mart* addressed a case in which the plaintiffs had “no significant proof” of a common policy of discrimination. 131 S. Ct. at 2556 (emphasis omitted). The Court did not hold that a district court must resolve every merits dispute over damages before certification. Rather, the district court here resolved the specific dispute that mattered: whether plaintiffs’ damages could be proven through a class-wide methodology. It found they could.

**III. The Court of Appeals Correctly Applied the Deferential Standard of Review**

Comcast asks this Court to second-guess the district court’s fact-laden certification decision. But factual findings made during a Rule 23 inquiry are reviewed for clear error. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312. A finding is clearly erroneous only if the appellate court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). If the district court’s account of the evidence “is plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety,” the court of appeals may not reverse it. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573–74 (1985).

Here, the district court’s findings were amply supported by the expert testimony and reports. The court did not merely accept the plaintiffs’ assertions; it held an adversarial hearing, asked the parties follow-up questions, and issued a detailed 81-page opinion. Its conclusion—that a common methodology existed to quantify damages—was plausible and anchored in the record. The Court of Appeals correctly declined to substitute its own view of the evidence.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s certification order satisfies Rule 23. It followed the rigorous analysis mandated by *Wal-Mart* and *Hydrogen Peroxide*, and it rested on admissible expert evidence establishing a class-wide damages methodology. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
