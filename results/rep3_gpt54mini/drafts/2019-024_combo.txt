**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision cannot stand because ERISA’s three-year period turns on a plaintiff’s “actual knowledge” of the breach, not on what information the defendant happened to disclose or what the plaintiff could have learned by reading it. Statutory text, ordinary usage, and this Court’s pre-1970 precedent all point the same way: “knowledge” means awareness, not mere access to facts. See *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470 (1917); *United States v. Missouri Pac. R. Co.*, 278 U.S. 269 (1929); *United States v. Gerlach Live Stock Co.*, 339 U.S. 725 (1950).

1. **“Actual knowledge” requires subjective awareness.**  
Congress chose the phrase “actual knowledge” in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), and that phrase has a settled meaning in ordinary English: a person knows a fact when he is aware of it. This Court has long distinguished actual knowledge from constructive or imputed knowledge. In *Corn Exchange Bank v. United States*, 37 F.2d 34, 36 (2d Cir. 1930), the court explained that actual knowledge is “knowledge in fact,” as opposed to notice or what one should have known. Likewise, *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), described knowledge as “an awareness of the facts.” ERISA’s text uses the same concept. If Congress had meant disclosure-based notice, it knew how to say so.

That matters here because Intel’s position collapses actual knowledge into constructive knowledge. Intel says the limitations clock began when it disclosed the relevant investment information, even if Sulyma never read it or did not remember reading it. But disclosure alone is not knowledge. A person cannot know what he is unaware of, and a paper trail does not create subjective awareness by itself. See *Bailey v. Central Vermont Ry.*, 319 U.S. 350, 352–53 (1943) (knowledge requires actual awareness of the operative facts, not merely the existence of records).

2. **The statute’s structure confirms that Congress rejected a constructive-knowledge rule.**  
ERISA’s limitations provision contains both a six-year outer limit and a three-year actual-knowledge limit. 29 U.S.C. § 1113. That structure shows Congress did not intend disclosures to start the shorter period automatically. The six-year period ensures repose even absent knowledge; the three-year period applies only when the participant actually knows of the breach. This Court has recognized that when Congress uses different words in the same statute, courts must give those words different meanings. See *Jarecki v. G. D. Searle & Co.*, 367 U.S. 303, 307 (1961); *United States v. Pelzer*, 312 U.S. 399, 404 (1941). Reading “actual knowledge” to include undisputed disclosures would erase the distinction Congress drew.

3. **Constructive knowledge cannot be smuggled into “actual knowledge” through a duty to read.**  
Intel’s theory is, in substance, a constructive-notice rule: because information was available on a website or in plan documents, Sulyma should be charged with knowledge whether or not he actually read it. This Court rejected similar attempts to convert availability into knowledge. In *Magruder v. Supplee*, 316 U.S. 394, 398 (1942), the Court treated knowledge as what the taxpayer actually knew, not what could have been inferred from accessible records. Likewise, *United States v. Fidelity & Deposit Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940), distinguished between actual awareness and circumstances that merely put a party in a position to learn more. ERISA’s “actual knowledge” requirement is even stricter than ordinary notice concepts because Congress used the word “actual” to exclude constructive awareness.

4. **The Ninth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with the remedial purposes of ERISA.**  
ERISA is a protective statute, designed to safeguard plan participants. This Court has repeatedly construed similar remedial enactments in light of their protective purposes. See *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967); *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 180, 186–87 (1963). A rule that starts the clock whenever fiduciaries place information in a participant portal would defeat that protective scheme. Many participants do not regularly review dense plan disclosures; ERISA does not presume otherwise. The better reading is the one the statute says: actual knowledge, not constructive receipt.

5. **At minimum, Intel’s disclosure evidence did not establish knowledge as a matter of law.**  
Even if disclosures may sometimes support an inference of actual knowledge, they cannot compel it where the participant swears he did not read or remember reading them. Summary judgment is improper when the record permits competing inferences about a person’s subjective awareness. See *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (factfinder must resolve genuine disputes of material fact). The same principle applies here: whether Sulyma actually knew of the alleged breach is a factual question, not one that can be resolved by deeming unread disclosures conclusive.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit and hold that ERISA’s three-year limitations period does not begin until the plaintiff actually knows of the breach—something more than mere disclosure, and more than what the plaintiff could have known had he read the materials provided.
