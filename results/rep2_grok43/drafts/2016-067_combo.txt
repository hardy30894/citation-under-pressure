The plain language of a statute controls its meaning. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917). Words must be given their ordinary import, and courts may not enlarge or restrict them. Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889). When Congress employs distinct terms, those terms are presumed to carry distinct meanings. Bate Refrigerating Co. v. Sulzberger, 157 U.S. 1, 36 (1895).

A person who collects debts owed to itself acts as a creditor, not an agent for another. Mandeville v. Welch, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 277, 286 (1820). Collection activity undertaken for one's own account falls outside any statutory category limited to debts "owed or due another." United States v. Hartwell, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 385, 396 (1868). The status of the debt at acquisition is irrelevant once legal title has passed; the collector's relation to the obligation is fixed by ownership. The Emily, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 381, 388 (1824).

Statutory exclusions are to be read in light of the affirmative definitions they modify, not as independent grants of coverage. Hawaii v. Mankichi, 190 U.S. 197, 214 (1903). An exclusion for debts not in default at acquisition presupposes that the actor already satisfies the primary definition of collecting for another; it does not create coverage where none otherwise exists. Schooner Paulina's Cargo v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 52, 60 (1812). Ejusdem generis confines general language to the class illustrated by specific preceding terms. United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805).

A company that purchases receivables and thereafter collects them in its own name therefore stands in the position of the original creditor. Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 602 (1900). Its practices are not subject to a statute whose operative provisions address only those who regularly collect debts owed another. Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949). The complaint alleges precisely such ownership and collection; it therefore fails to state a claim.
