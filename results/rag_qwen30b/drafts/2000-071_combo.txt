**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide its facilities for religious instruction or worship, even when such use is sought by a private organization. The Milford Central School’s decision to deny the Good News Club access to its after-hours facilities does not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The Club’s proposed activities—centered on Bible lessons, prayer, memorization of scripture, and evangelistic appeals—constitute religious worship and instruction, not mere expression of a religious viewpoint on secular subjects. The school’s policy, which prohibits religious use of its facilities, is a valid exercise of its authority to maintain neutrality in matters of religion and to avoid violating the Establishment Clause.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may exclude religious activities from public forums without violating the Free Speech Clause. In *Illinois Ex Rel. McCollum v. Board of Ed. of School Dist. No. 71, Champaign Cty.*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), the Court held that a program allowing religious instructors to enter public school classrooms during the school day for religious instruction violated the Establishment Clause. The Court emphasized that “the use of the public school building for religious instruction is a direct aid to religion,” and that “the state may not lend its support to religion by permitting religious instruction in public school buildings.” The presence of religious teachers in public school classrooms, even during regular school hours, was deemed an impermissible entanglement between government and religion. Here, the Good News Club seeks to conduct religious instruction and prayer in the same building during after-hours, a use that is no less constitutionally suspect than the program in *McCollum*.

The Court reaffirmed this principle in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), where it upheld a “released time” program that allowed students to leave school grounds for religious instruction. The Court distinguished such a program from one involving religious teaching within school buildings, noting that “the program involves neither religious instruction in public school classrooms nor the expenditure of public funds.” The key distinction is that religious instruction conducted *within* public school buildings, even after hours, implicates the Establishment Clause because it uses public property for religious purposes. The Good News Club’s request to meet in the school building for Bible lessons, prayer, and evangelism falls squarely within the category of religious worship prohibited by the school’s policy.

The Court has also recognized that the government may regulate the content of speech in public forums when the speech is religious in nature. In *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court held that a city’s denial of a permit to hold religious meetings in a public park did not violate the First Amendment. The Court stated that “the administration and enforcement of the ordinance by the City Council, requiring a permit for holding meetings in the parks of Portsmouth so as to deny all applications made by religious organizations to hold religious meetings and deliver religious talks in the parks of Portsmouth, [was] not an abridgment of freedom of speech, assembly and worship in violation of the First and Fourteenth Amendments.” The Court emphasized that “the city may regulate the use of public property in a manner that does not discriminate on the basis of religious viewpoint, even if the regulation results in the exclusion of religious speech.” The Milford Central School’s policy, which applies equally to all religious groups and prohibits religious use of school facilities, is consistent with this principle.

Moreover, the government is not required to create a public forum for religious speech. In *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), the Court held that a state may not discriminate against a religious group in the administration of a public forum. The Court stated that “a religious service of Jehovah’s Witnesses is treated differently than a religious service of other sects. That amounts to the state preferring some religious groups over this one.” However, the Court did not hold that the state must allow religious services in public forums. Rather, it affirmed that “the state may not prefer one religion over another,” but may exclude all religious activities from public property if it chooses. The school’s policy does not prefer one religion over another; it treats all religious groups equally by denying access to religious use of school facilities.

The school’s policy is also consistent with the principle that public property may be used for secular purposes only. In *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370 (1930), the Court upheld a state law that provided textbooks to all students, including those in private and sectarian schools. The Court held that “the purpose is said to be to aid private, religious, sectarian, and other schools not embraced in the public educational system of the State by furnishing text-books free to the children attending such private schools.” The Court emphasized that “the schools, however, are not the beneficiaries of these appropriations. They obtain no benefit from the books.” The same principle applies here: the school’s facilities are not to be used for the benefit of religious organizations. The Club’s use of the school building for religious instruction would provide a benefit to a religious organization, which the government may lawfully decline.

The school’s policy is also consistent with the principle of neutrality. In *Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), the Court upheld a law that allowed public school districts to lend textbooks to students in parochial schools. The Court held that “the state may not lend its support to religion by permitting religious instruction in public school buildings.” The Court emphasized that “the state may not use its resources to promote religion.” The school’s policy, which prohibits religious use of its facilities, is a valid exercise of its authority to avoid promoting religion.

Finally, the school’s policy is not subject to the Equal Protection Clause. In *Kramer v. Union Free School District No. 15*, 395 U.S. 621 (1969), the Court held that a state may limit voting rights in school elections to property owners and parents of schoolchildren. The Court stated that “the state may regulate the use of public property in a manner that does not discriminate on the basis of religious viewpoint.” The school’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of religious viewpoint; it applies equally to all religious groups. The Club’s claim that it is being denied access because of its religious viewpoint is without merit.
