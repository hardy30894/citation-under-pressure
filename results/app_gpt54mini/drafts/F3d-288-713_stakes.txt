**ARGUMENT**

I. **The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Riddle was entitled to habeas relief on his due process claim arising from the prosecutor’s dual role as advocate and witness.**

A criminal trial violates due process when the prosecutor’s conduct renders the proceedings fundamentally unfair. See *Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 181 (1986); *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974). The Supreme Court has also emphasized that a prosecutor’s participation as a witness can create an intolerable risk of unfairness and prejudice. *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (“the prosecutor’s interest in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done”); *United States v. Johnston*, 690 F.2d 638, 643-44 (7th Cir. 1982) (recognizing the prejudice inherent when a prosecutor testifies and continues to act as advocate). Here, the chief prosecutor, Danny Hill, took the stand, offered rebuttal testimony to attack Riddle’s credibility, and then continued to prosecute the case, including through closing argument. That conduct created precisely the type of “appearance of impropriety” and structural unfairness that due process forbids. *Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton et Fils S.A.*, 481 U.S. 787, 809-14 (1987) (holding that a prosecutor’s divided role may undermine the integrity of the proceeding).

The state’s use of Hill as both witness and advocate was especially prejudicial because Hill’s testimony was deployed to rebut Riddle’s explanation for his escape attempts and to bolster the prosecution’s theory that Riddle was lying. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “[t]he influential conduct of a prosecutor” may “affect[] the substantial rights of the accused.” *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88. When the prosecutor becomes a witness on a disputed factual issue and then argues the case to the jury, the prosecutor effectively places the prestige of the office behind the evidence and invites the jury to credit the State’s version because it comes from the prosecutor himself. See *United States v. Johnston*, 690 F.2d at 643-44. That is incompatible with the “fundamental fairness” required by the Due Process Clause. *Darden*, 477 U.S. at 183; *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941).

II. **The district court should not have denied an evidentiary hearing because Riddle alleged specific facts that, if proven, would entitle him to relief.**

A habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing when he alleges facts that, if true, would establish a constitutional violation and when the existing record does not conclusively resolve the claim. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963); *Schriro v. Landrigan*, 550 U.S. 465, 474 (2007) (a hearing is warranted where the record leaves factual issues unresolved and the petitioner’s allegations, if proven, would entitle him to relief). Riddle made that showing. He alleged that the chief prosecutor testified against him, then remained in charge of the prosecution, and that the State used that testimony in closing argument to brand him a liar. Those allegations go to the fairness of the trial itself and cannot be rejected as a matter of law on summary judgment absent a record conclusively showing harmlessness. See *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 312-13.

The district court’s reliance on summary judgment was especially inappropriate because the prejudice from Hill’s dual role turns on how the testimony was presented, how the jury perceived the prosecutor’s credibility, and whether the remaining evidence rendered the error harmless. See *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637-38 (1993) (habeas relief is appropriate when the error had a “substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the verdict). In a capital case, where “the qualitative difference of death from all other punishments requires a correspondingly greater degree of scrutiny,” *Caldwell v. Mississippi*, 472 U.S. 320, 329 (1985), the court should not have foreclosed factual development of a claim that the State’s chief prosecutor functioned as both witness and advocate.

III. **At a minimum, the error was not harmless under the governing federal habeas standard.**

Even if this Court concludes that the prosecutor’s conduct is reviewed for harmlessness, the error warrants relief because it had a substantial and injurious effect on the verdict. *Brecht*, 507 U.S. at 637-38. The State’s case on the contested issues was not overwhelming. Riddle’s account differed materially from the State’s theory on entry, intent, and the circumstances of the killing. Hill’s testimony and his continued advocacy directly bore on Riddle’s credibility, which was central to the jury’s assessment of the defense. In a case turning on whether the jury believed Riddle’s explanation or the State’s narrative, the chief prosecutor’s sworn rebuttal testimony and subsequent summation could only have reinforced the State’s case in a uniquely prejudicial way. See *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 n.25 (1984) (recognizing that certain circumstances create a presumption of prejudice because the adversary process itself is compromised).

Because the proceedings below were infected by a due process violation, because Riddle alleged facts warranting a hearing, and because the error was not harmless, the judgment denying habeas relief should be reversed and the case remanded for an evidentiary hearing and further proceedings.
