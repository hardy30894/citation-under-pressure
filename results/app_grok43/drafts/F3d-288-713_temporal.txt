I. The District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Relief, as the Prosecutor’s Testimony and Continued Advocacy Denied Due Process.

The Due Process Clause requires that a criminal trial be fundamentally fair. See Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935). When a prosecutor assumes the dual role of witness and advocate, that fairness is destroyed. See Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (“He may prosecute with earnestness and vigor—indeed, he should do so. But, while he may strike hard blows, he is not at liberty to strike foul ones.”).

Here the district attorney testified to rebut Riddle’s account of threats made at arraignment, then resumed prosecution and permitted his assistant to argue that the testimony proved Riddle a liar. Such conduct converts the state’s representative into an interested witness whose credibility the jury is invited to accept on the basis of official position rather than evidence alone. See Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927) (due process demands “a fair trial in a fair tribunal”).

The resulting prejudice is not cured by the presence of co-counsel. See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 57 (1932) (the right to a fair trial “cannot be sacrificed to the demands of expedition or convenience”). Because the state court record demonstrates this structural defect without need for further factual development, the district court’s denial of the writ and refusal of an evidentiary hearing cannot stand.

II. The District Court’s Summary Disposition Likewise Violated Riddle’s Right to Be Heard.

A habeas petitioner is entitled to an opportunity to establish facts showing that his conviction rests on a denial of due process. See Frank v. Mangum, 237 U.S. 309, 326 (1915). The district court’s grant of summary judgment on an incomplete record therefore compounded the constitutional error. See Ex parte Royall, 117 U.S. 241, 249 (1886) (federal courts must examine whether restraint is “in violation of the Constitution”). Reversal and remand for an evidentiary hearing are required.
