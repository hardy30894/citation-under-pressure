# ARGUMENT

## I. Punitive Damages Under Title VII Require Proof That The Employer's Conduct Was Itself Egregious, Not Merely That The Employer Possessed A Culpable State Of Mind

Since the earliest development of exemplary damages in American law, courts have insisted that such awards rest on the *character of the wrong*, not merely on the defendant's mental state. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1852), this Court explained that a jury may award punitive damages only "having in view the enormity of his offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." *Id.* at 371. The inquiry into "enormity" is a separate, conduct-focused inquiry from the question whether the defendant acted knowingly or recklessly.

That distinction was reaffirmed in *Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore R.R. Co. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202 (1859), and sharpened in *Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), where the Court held that punitive damages are unavailable unless the wrongful act is "characterized by some such circumstances as amount to a wanton or willful injury, or that entire want of care which would raise the presumption of a conscious indifference to consequences." *Id.* at 493. Notice that *Arms* required not just recklessness in the abstract, but circumstances "amounting to" wantonness—an evaluation of the conduct's severity, layered atop any mental-state showing.

This Court's decision in *Missouri Pacific Ry. Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), and *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), confirm the same two-part structure: exemplary damages are reserved for torts inflicted "maliciously or wantonly, and with circumstances of contumely or indignity." *Barry*, 116 U.S. at 562. Likewise, in *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), the Court cautioned that such damages punish "something more than the mere breach of duty which constitutes the ground of the ordinary action for damages"—that is, "something of an aggravated character." *Id.* at 106–08. Under this unbroken line of authority, a showing of "malice" or "reckless indifference" describes only the actor's *mind*; it does not, standing alone, establish that the underlying conduct was sufficiently aggravated to warrant punishment beyond compensation.

## II. Congress Legislated Against This Background And Should Be Presumed To Have Incorporated It

Section 1981a(b)(1) uses the common-law vocabulary of "malice" and "reckless indifference" without further elaboration. This Court has long held that when "Congress borrows terms of art in which are accumulated the legal tradition and meaning of centuries of practice, it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). Because the common-law "cluster of ideas" attached to punitive damages always included an independent requirement of aggravated, outrageous conduct—see *Day*, *Arms*, *Barry*, and *Prentice*, *supra*—Congress must be understood to have imported that same limitation into § 1981a(b)(1), rather than silently jettisoning a century of settled doctrine.

## III. Vicarious Punitive Liability Against An Employer Additionally Requires Egregious Conduct Attributable To The Employer Itself

Even where an individual agent's state of mind might satisfy a bare "reckless indifference" standard, this Court has never permitted an innocent principal to bear exemplary damages for the isolated misconduct of a subordinate. In *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546 (1818), Justice Story held that punitive damages could not be visited upon principals for the acts of their agents absent the principals' own participation in the wrong. And in *Washington Gas Light Co. v. Lansden*, 172 U.S. 534 (1899), this Court held that a corporation cannot be subjected to exemplary damages for the act of a subordinate employee unless that act was authorized, participated in, or ratified by the corporation's superior officers. Ordinary employment decisions—like the promotion decision at issue here—simply do not rise to the level of corporate wrongdoing that *Lansden* and *The Amiable Nancy* require before exemplary liability may attach to the employer itself.

## IV. Applying This Standard, Kolstad Failed To Establish The Independent Egregiousness Necessary To Sustain Punitive Damages

At most, the record shows a contested promotion decision and isolated remarks by a mid-level supervisor—conduct wholly unlike the "fraud, malice, gross negligence or oppression" contemplated in *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371, the "wanton or willful injury" required by *Arms*, 91 U.S. at 493, or the "aggravated character" of wrongdoing demanded by *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 108. Nor did Kolstad show that ADA's senior management authorized or ratified any such conduct, as *Lansden* and *The Amiable Nancy* require. Because Title VII's punitive-damages provision incorporates the traditional requirement of egregious conduct independent of state of mind, and because no such showing was made here, the judgment below should be affirmed.
