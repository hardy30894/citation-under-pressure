**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), and the Ninth Circuit’s decision to the contrary is irreconcilable with Supreme Court precedent. The Court should reverse because the use of prior tribal court convictions—valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA)—to establish an essential element of a federal felony offense does not violate the Sixth Amendment, particularly where the government bears the burden of proving each element beyond a reasonable doubt and the prior convictions are not used to enhance punishment but to establish guilt.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Thus No Sixth Amendment Violation Occurs When Tribal Court Convictions Are Used as Predicate Offenses**

The foundational principle governing this case is that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to Indian tribal courts. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24, 98 S.Ct. 1079, 55 L.Ed.2d 303 (1978), reaffirmed that tribal courts exercise “the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty,” and that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709, 171 L.Ed.2d 457 (2008). Because tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment, a conviction obtained in such a court—even without appointed counsel—does not violate the Constitution. *Id.* at 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709. The fact that a tribal court may not provide the full panoply of rights guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment does not render its judgments invalid for purposes of federal law, so long as they comport with the minimum standards of due process under the ICRA.

The ICRA, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a), provides that “no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law” in tribal court proceedings. This standard is not coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, but it is sufficient to render a tribal court conviction constitutionally valid for federal purposes. *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir.1989) (citing *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. at 323–24). The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that tribal court convictions are “constitutionally infirm” simply because they lack a Sixth Amendment right to counsel is a misapplication of constitutional law. The Constitution does not require tribal courts to provide counsel in misdemeanor cases, and the absence of such a right does not invalidate the conviction. *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 372, 99 S.Ct. 1158, 59 L.Ed.2d 383 (1979) (holding that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not attach in non-punitive misdemeanor proceedings).

**II. The Use of Prior Convictions to Establish an Essential Element of a Federal Offense Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment When the Convictions Are Valid Under ICRA**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 114 S.Ct. 1921, 128 L.Ed.2d 745 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, so long as the conviction does not violate the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 746–47, 114 S.Ct. 1921. But *Nichols* was a sentencing case, where the government need only prove a sentencing enhancement by a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 748, 114 S.Ct. 1921. Here, the government seeks to prove an essential element of a federal felony offense—two prior domestic violence convictions—beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068, 25 L.Ed.2d 368 (1970). The higher burden of proof in the guilt phase reflects the Constitution’s core concern with reliability and accuracy. *Middleton v. McNeil*, 541 U.S. 433, 437, 124 S.Ct. 1830, 158 L.Ed.2d 701 (2004). The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to extend *Nichols* to the guilt phase—where the stakes are far higher—ignores this critical distinction.

Moreover, *Nichols* did not purport to address the use of prior convictions in the guilt phase. The Court explicitly limited its holding to sentencing enhancements. *Id.* at 747, 114 S.Ct. 1921. The Court recognized that the sentencing phase allows for greater flexibility and defendant opportunity to challenge the reliability of prior convictions. *Id.* at 747–48, 114 S.Ct. 1921. But in the guilt phase, the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068. The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively creates a constitutional bar to using any prior conviction—no matter how valid—when it is used to establish an essential element of a federal crime, a result the Supreme Court has never endorsed.
