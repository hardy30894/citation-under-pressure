# ARGUMENT

## THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN AFFIRMING CLASS CERTIFICATION WITHOUT REQUIRING ADMISSIBLE EVIDENCE THAT DAMAGES COULD BE MEASURED ON A CLASS-WIDE BASIS

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). The Third Circuit itself recognized that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence before certifying a class, In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig., 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008), and that this analysis requires "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations." *Id.* at 317. Yet the court below abandoned this rigor precisely where it mattered most—in scrutinizing whether Respondents' damages model was capable of measuring harm attributable to the *single* theory of antitrust impact the district court permitted to proceed to trial.

### A. The District Court Certified a Class Without Any Admissible Method to Measure Class-Wide Damages Attributable to the Certified Theory of Liability

The district court narrowed Respondents' theories of antitrust impact to a single theory: that Comcast's "clustering conduct" had "the effect... to deter the entry of overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA." Pet. App. 00032. Having rejected three of Respondents' four theories of anticompetitive conduct, the district court was required to ensure that Dr. McClave's damages model actually measured damages flowing from the *surviving* theory—overbuilder deterrence—rather than an amalgam of all four rejected and accepted theories combined. It did not do so. Dr. McClave's regression model calculated the difference between actual cable prices in the Philadelphia DMA and a hypothetical benchmark price, but that model made no attempt to isolate damages attributable solely to overbuilder deterrence as opposed to the three other theories (including alleged harms from Comcast's conduct toward RCN, penalty provisions, and long-term contracts) that the district court had explicitly excluded from the case.

This Court's traditional deference to fact-finding, articulated in Anderson v. Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564 (1985), does not extend to permitting a court to accept a methodology that is, on its face, "completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support displaying some hue of credibility," Krasnov v. Dinan, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972)—the very standard the Third Circuit purported to apply. A model that fails to distinguish between four analytically distinct theories of harm, three of which were rejected, cannot supply "minimum evidentiary support" for the proposition that damages are measurable on a class-wide basis under the one theory that remains.

### B. Admissibility of Expert Evidence Cannot Be Relaxed at the Class Certification Stage

The court below erred in treating the reliability of Respondents' expert methodology as a merits question inappropriate for resolution at certification. While Hydrogen Peroxide correctly held that "[a]n overlap between a class certification requirement and the merits of a claim is no reason to decline to resolve relevant disputes when necessary to determine whether a class certification requirement is met," 552 F.3d at 317, the Third Circuit failed to apply that principle here. Instead, it treated the admissibility and reliability of Dr. McClave's model as beyond its reviewing function, notwithstanding that predominance under Rule 23(b)(3) cannot be established without admissible evidence that common proof will, in fact, resolve the damages question for the class as a whole.

This Court has recognized that rigorous scrutiny of expert evidence is not merely a merits inquiry to be deferred until trial; it is a threshold requirement for certification. Just as this Court demanded that Congress's own factual predicates for cable regulation rest on evidentiary support—rather than speculation—when reviewing legislative findings under intermediate scrutiny, see Turner Broad. Sys., Inc. v. FCC, 520 U.S. 180, 195–96 (1997) (canvassing specific evidence supporting Congress's findings regarding cable operators' "considerable and growing market power"), so too must a district court demand specific evidentiary support—not mere assertion—that a proposed damages methodology fits the theory of liability actually certified for trial.

### C. The Predominance Inquiry Requires That Common Evidence Resolve the Specific Theory of Liability Certified, Not Some Broader or Different Theory

Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement is not satisfied merely because *some* method exists to calculate aggregate damages; the method must correspond to the theory of antitrust impact that will actually be tried. The Third Circuit's Krasnov standard asks whether the district court's finding "bears... rational relationship to the supportive evidentiary data." 465 F.2d at 1302. Here, no rational relationship exists between a damages model built to capture the effects of four theories of anticompetitive conduct and a certified class action proceeding on only one such theory. The mismatch between theory and methodology is not a "merits" question to be deferred; it is the very essence of whether common questions predominate under Rule 23(b)(3).

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
