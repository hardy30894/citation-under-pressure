**ARGUMENT**

**THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE ATTORNEY GENERAL TO DETAIN A REMOVABLE ALIEN INDEFINITELY BEYOND THE 90-DAY REMOVAL PERIOD.**

**I. The Plain Language of § 1231(a)(6) Does Not Clearly Authorize Indefinite Detention, and Any Ambiguity Must Be Resolved in Favor of a Reasonable Time Limitation.**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien ordered removable “may be detained beyond the removal period.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). This language is ambiguous as to the permissible duration of such detention. It does not state that detention may continue indefinitely, nor does it provide any temporal limit. When a statute is ambiguous concerning the duration of detention, this Court must construe it to avoid serious constitutional questions. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932).

Reading § 1231(a)(6) to authorize indefinite detention would raise grave constitutional concerns because it would permit the government to imprison a resident alien permanently without the procedural protections required by the Due Process Clause. *See Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (holding that indefinite detention without adequate procedural safeguards violates due process). Accordingly, this Court should adopt the interpretation that limits detention to a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *See Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 689 (2001) (construing § 1231(a)(6) to contain an implicit “reasonable time” limitation to avoid constitutional doubts).

**II. Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Ordered Removed Violates Substantive Due Process.**

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects resident aliens within the United States. *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953). Once a final removal order is entered, the government’s interest in detention is limited to ensuring the alien’s presence for removal and preventing flight or danger to the community. *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 538 (1952). Where removal is not reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves these purposes and becomes punitive in nature.

This Court has long recognized that the government may not indefinitely detain an individual solely on the basis of a final removal order. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987) (holding that preventive detention must be narrowly tailored to serve a legitimate governmental interest). Here, petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas has been in INS custody for years with no realistic prospect of removal. Germany has refused to accept him as a citizen. *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 284 (5th Cir. 1999). Lithuania has likewise refused. *Id.* As the district court found, petitioner “will never be deported because there is no place to send him.” *Zadvydas v. Caplinger*, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1027 (E.D. La. 1997). Under these circumstances, continued detention violates substantive due process.

**III. The Government’s “Good Faith” Standard Does Not Satisfy Constitutional Requirements.**

The court of appeals held that detention is permissible as long as “good faith efforts to effectuate the alien’s deportation continue.” *Zadvydas*, 185 F.3d at 297. This standard is insufficient because it permits indefinite detention even when removal is impossible. The Constitution requires a meaningful likelihood of removal within the reasonably foreseeable future. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. at 701. Here, the government has failed to demonstrate any such likelihood. Petitioner’s continued detention, therefore, is not authorized by statute and violates the Due Process Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and petitioner should be ordered released.
