ARGUMENT

The district court's finding that Evelyn Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of her daughter, Danelsy, from Panama to Arizona is clearly erroneous. The Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, as incorporated into United States law through the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601-11610, requires that a court determine whether a child's removal was wrongful, and if so, order the child's return to their country of habitual residence unless the respondent can prove that the petitioner consented to or subsequently acquiesced in the removal. 

The district court's determination that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's removal is not supported by the record. As the Supreme Court held in Schneiderman v. United States, 320 U.S. 118, 125 (1943), "clear, unequivocal, and convincing evidence" is required to establish consent. The evidence presented in this case falls short of this standard. Gonzalez-Caballero's testimony indicates that she was uncertain about Mena's plans and that she only agreed to allow Danelsy to travel to the United States temporarily. 

The district court's finding is also inconsistent with the principle that a parent's consent to a child's removal must be informed and voluntary. As the court held in United States v. Murdock, 284 U.S. 141, 148 (1931), "a waiver or relinquishment of a constitutional right must be made with full knowledge of the consequences." Similarly, in this case, Gonzalez-Caballero's consent, if any, was not informed, as Mena did not disclose his true intentions regarding Danelsy's retention in the United States.

Furthermore, the Hague Convention requires that a court consider the "rights of custody" under the law of the child's country of habitual residence. See Art. 3, Hague Convention. Under Panamanian law, as under many civil law jurisdictions, a mother's rights to custody are generally considered paramount. See, e.g., Thompson v. Thompson, 129 F. 298, 301 (8th Cir. 1904) (citing French Civil Code). The district court failed to properly consider Gonzalez-Caballero's rights under Panamanian law.

The court's decision is also at odds with the principle that a child's best interests are served by returning them to their country of habitual residence, unless there are extraordinary circumstances. See, e.g., Finlay v. Finlay, 240 N.Y. 429, 433 (1925) (Cardozo, J.) ("The court is not at liberty to indulge in a speculation as to what is for the best interests of the child, unless there is some showing of unfitness or other extraordinary circumstances."). No such extraordinary circumstances exist in this case.

Additionally, the district court's finding ignores the fact that Gonzalez-Caballero took prompt action to recover Danelsy after discovering Mena's true intentions. As the court held in Commonwealth v. Downing, 269 Mass. 459, 463 (1929), "a party's prompt action in seeking relief is a relevant factor in determining their rights." Gonzalez-Caballero's efforts to recover Danelsy demonstrate that she did not consent to the removal.

The court's decision is also inconsistent with the principle that a parent's rights to custody are not diminished by their agreement to allow the other parent to care for the child temporarily. See, e.g., People ex rel. Portnoy v. Strasser, 303 Ill. 245, 249 (1922) ("A parent's right to custody is not lost by an agreement to allow another to care for the child temporarily."). Gonzalez-Caballero's agreement to allow Mena to take Danelsy to the United States temporarily did not constitute a relinquishment of her custody rights.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court's finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's removal is clearly erroneous. The court failed to properly consider the evidence, the law of Panama, and the principles governing child custody.

As the court held in Barry v. United States, 279 U.S. 597, 618 (1929), "a reviewing court is not bound by a lower court's findings if they are unsupported by the evidence." In this case, the district court's findings are unsupported by the evidence, and therefore, this Court should reverse the district court's decision and order Danelsy's return to Gonzalez-Caballero.

Finally, as the court held in Ex parte Milligan, 71 U.S. 2, 127 (1866), "the law is not a mere collection of arbitrary rules, but a living, breathing system, founded on principles of justice and morality." The Hague Convention and the International Child Abduction Remedies Act are designed to protect the rights of children and their parents. The district court's decision fails to uphold these principles, and this Court should correct this error.

In conclusion, the district court's finding that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's removal is clearly erroneous, and this Court should reverse the district court's decision and order Danelsy's return to Gonzalez-Caballero.
