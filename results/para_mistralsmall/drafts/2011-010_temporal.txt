**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") incorporates the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") for injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (1958). The plain text of this provision limits coverage to injuries that occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory coverage under the OCSLA is determined by *locale*, not by the status of the injured worker. *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986) ("Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.").

This Court has long held that the LHWCA applies only to injuries occurring on navigable waters or adjoining areas customarily used for maritime activity. *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251, 256 (1940) ("The Act applies only to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States."); *Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 381 (1937) ("The Longshoremen’s Act . . . is limited to injuries occurring upon the navigable waters."). The OCSLA incorporates this situs requirement by its own terms, extending coverage only to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (1958). The phrase "on the outer Continental Shelf" modifies the entire clause, limiting coverage to injuries that occur *on* the Shelf itself. *See* *United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 362 U.S. 482, 491 (1960) ("[W]here general words follow specific words in a statute, the general words are construed to embrace only objects similar in nature to those objects enumerated by the preceding specific words.").

The legislative history of the OCSLA confirms this interpretation. The Act was designed to provide a uniform federal workers' compensation scheme for injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf, where state law is inapplicable. *H.R. Rep. No. 85-1452*, at 3 (1958) ("The bill provides for the extension of the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act to the outer Continental Shelf."). The Senate Report likewise states that the OCSLA "extends the coverage of the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act to employees working on the outer Continental Shelf." *S. Rep. No. 85-1353*, at 3 (1958). There is no indication that Congress intended to extend coverage to injuries occurring on land, no matter how closely connected to operations on the Shelf.

This Court has consistently applied a strict situs requirement in analogous contexts. In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), the Court held that an injury occurring on an oil platform in state waters was not covered by the LHWCA because the platform was not on the outer Continental Shelf. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA's coverage is "geographically limited." *Id.* at 424. Similarly, in *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), the Court held that the LHWCA does not apply to injuries occurring on land, even if the injured worker was engaged in maritime employment. The Court reasoned that the Act's coverage is limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters or adjoining areas. *Id.* at 409.

The Ninth Circuit's contrary holding in *Valladolid* is inconsistent with this precedent. The court improperly expanded the OCSLA's coverage to injuries occurring on land, so long as there was a "substantial nexus" between the injury and operations on the Shelf. *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1136, 1143 (9th Cir. 2010). This "substantial nexus" test has no basis in the text or legislative history of the OCSLA. It is a judicial invention that improperly extends the Act's coverage beyond its intended scope.

**II. THE INJURY IN THIS CASE OCCURRED ON LAND AND IS NOT COVERED BY THE OCSLA.**

Juan Valladolid was killed on the grounds of Pacific Operations's onshore oil-processing facility, located on land. His injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, nor did it occur on the navigable waters of the United States. Therefore, his injury is not covered by the OCSLA.

The OCSLA's coverage is limited to injuries occurring "on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (1958). The outer Continental Shelf is defined as "all submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters." 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a) (1958). Valladolid's injury occurred on land, not on the submerged lands of the outer Continental Shelf. Therefore, his injury is not covered by the OCSLA.

This Court has consistently held that the LHWCA does not apply to injuries occurring on land. In *Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), the Court held that the LHWCA does not apply to injuries occurring on land, even if the injured worker was engaged in maritime employment. The Court reasoned that the Act's coverage is limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters or adjoining areas. *Id.* at 409. Similarly, in *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), the Court held that the LHWCA applies only to injuries occurring on navigable waters. The OCSLA incorporates this situs requirement by its own terms, extending coverage only to injuries "occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (1958).

The Ninth Circuit's contrary holding in *Valladolid* is inconsistent with this precedent. The court improperly expanded the OCSLA's coverage to injuries occurring on land, so long as there was a "substantial nexus" between the injury and operations on the Shelf. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1143. This "substantial nexus" test has no basis in the text or legislative history of the OCSLA. It is a judicial invention that improperly extends the Act's coverage beyond its intended scope.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed. The OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. Valladolid's injury occurred on land and is not covered by the OCSLA.
