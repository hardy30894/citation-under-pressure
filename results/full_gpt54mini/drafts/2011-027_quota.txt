Respondent’s position is correct because the Constitution’s navigability-for-title inquiry turns on whether the river segment was navigable in fact at the moment of statehood, not at some later time. Under the equal-footing doctrine, a State takes title to the beds of waters that were “navigable waters” when it entered the Union. Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75-76 (1931). The question is historical and segment-specific: the relevant inquiry focuses on the particular stretch of river and asks whether it was used, or was susceptible of being used, as a highway of commerce at statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 76-77.  

This Court has repeatedly held that title navigability is determined “as of the time of statehood.” PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana, 565 U.S. 576, 592-93 (2012); United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935). That rule follows from the equal-footing doctrine itself: the State’s title is fixed when it is admitted to the Union and cannot be enlarged or diminished by later changes in use or condition. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 57-58 (1894); Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 551-52 (1981). Thus, later improvements, dams, or changes in commercial activity do not determine title. PPL Montana, 565 U.S. at 592-93; United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 408-09 (1940).  

Petitioner’s contrary approach would improperly conflate title navigability with the broader federal power to regulate commerce. That distinction is longstanding. For title purposes, the river must have been navigable at statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563; United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 76. By contrast, Congress’s regulatory power can extend to waters that are navigable in fact at a later time or that may be made navigable by reasonable improvements. United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co., 311 U.S. at 407-10. But this Court has never used that broader regulatory concept to alter the State’s title to riverbeds. PPL Montana, 565 U.S. at 592-94.  

The trial court here correctly examined historical evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork segments as they existed at Montana’s admission in 1889. That is precisely what the doctrine requires. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 83-84 (courts must consider “physical characteristics” and “natural and ordinary condition” of the waters at statehood); PPL Montana, 565 U.S. at 592-94. The court need not identify a continuous voyage by the exact same craft or an uninterrupted commercial use. The test is susceptibility to use as a highway of commerce in the ordinary condition of the river segment at statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563; PPL Montana, 565 U.S. at 592-94.  

Petitioner’s suggestion that navigability should be assessed based on present-day conditions would invert settled law. If title turned on current use, the State’s title would vary with later developments, contrary to the equal-footing doctrine’s core premise that state sovereignty is fixed upon admission. Shively, 152 U.S. at 57-58; Oregon ex rel. State Land Bd. v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co., 429 U.S. 363, 374-76 (1977). This Court in Corvallis held that state-law and post-statehood events cannot defeat the federal rule fixing title at statehood. Id. at 376-77.  

Nor does Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544 (1981), help petitioner. Montana recognized that the United States held title to riverbeds until statehood and then conveyed title to the State if the waters were navigable at that time. Id. at 551-52. That decision confirms, rather than undermines, respondent’s position.  

In short, the constitutional test asks a single question: was the relevant river segment navigable in fact at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union? The answer must be yes for title to pass to the State, and no if later conditions alone create navigability. The district court applied the correct historical inquiry, and this Court should affirm. PPL Montana, 565 U.S. at 592-94; United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 75-77; The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. at 563; Shively, 152 U.S. at 57-58; Corvallis, 429 U.S. at 374-77.
