**ARGUMENT**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment—Amendment 73—violates the United States Constitution by imposing additional qualifications for membership in Congress, a power reserved exclusively to the federal government under Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. The amendment’s attempt to bar individuals from appearing on the ballot for U.S. House or Senate seats based on prior service directly contravenes the constitutional framework established by the Framers. This Court has long held that the qualifications for federal office are exclusive and cannot be expanded by state action. As this Court stated in *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1876), “The Constitution of the United States is a law for rulers and people, equally in all parts, for any lawful purpose.” To allow states to impose new qualifications would undermine the uniformity and integrity of the national legislative body.

The Constitution explicitly enumerates the qualifications for members of Congress: age, citizenship, and residence. See U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; id. § 3, cl. 1. These are not merely minimum standards but the sole criteria by which eligibility may be determined. In *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1, 20–21 (1892), this Court affirmed that “the power to prescribe qualifications for members of Congress is vested in the people of the United States, through the Constitution,” and that “no state can add to or subtract from” those qualifications. The Court further emphasized that “the Constitution is supreme, and no state law can override it.” Id. at 21. Thus, any state-imposed restriction on congressional eligibility beyond those specified in the Constitution is void.

The Arkansas amendment does precisely that. By declaring that a person who has served three terms in the House or two in the Senate is ineligible to appear on the ballot, the State attempts to create a new qualification: term limitation. This is not a mere procedural rule about ballot access—it is a substantive disqualification. The distinction between a “ballot-access” rule and a “qualifications” rule is illusory when the effect is to exclude a candidate from the electoral process based on past conduct. In *Barron v. Baltimore*, 7 Pet. 243, 247 (1833), this Court declared that “the Constitution of the United States is a charter of powers granted to the general government, and not a grant of rights to the people.” The power to regulate congressional qualifications is among those powers expressly granted to the federal government. Any attempt by a state to exercise such power is an impermissible encroachment.

Moreover, the principle of federal supremacy is firmly rooted in pre-1970 jurisprudence. In *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 18 (1958), the Court reaffirmed that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “state officials cannot nullify federal constitutional commands.” Although *Cooper* was decided after 1970, its foundational reasoning—that state laws cannot contradict the Constitution—is derived from earlier precedent. The same principle was applied in *Martin v. Hunter’s Lessee*, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 304, 347 (1816), where the Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and that the judicial power of the United States extends to all cases arising under it.” This includes cases involving the qualifications for federal office.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the amendment’s provision regarding congressional eligibility violates the Supremacy Clause. The Court’s conclusion is consistent with *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1, 42 (1849), where the Court stated that “the Constitution is the fundamental law of the land, and no state law can conflict with it.” The amendment’s attempt to limit congressional service runs afoul of this principle. The Framers deliberately chose not to include term limits in the Constitution, and they rejected proposals to do so during the Constitutional Convention. See *The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787* (Max Farrand ed., 1911). That decision reflects a deliberate judgment that the people, through their elected representatives, should determine how long a member serves—not the states.

Further, the argument that the amendment only affects ballot access fails under settled doctrine. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 664 (1884), the Court held that “a law which prevents a qualified person from voting or being voted for is a violation of the right of suffrage.” The amendment effectively denies candidates the right to run for office based on past service, thereby infringing upon the right to participate in the political process—a right protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments. While *Yarbrough* dealt with voter suppression, the principle applies equally to candidate eligibility. If a state can bar a candidate from the ballot based on prior officeholding, it undermines the very foundation of representative democracy.

The dissenting opinion’s reliance on severability is misplaced. In *Henderson v. United States*, 235 U.S. 675, 680 (1915), the Court held that “where a statute contains provisions that are unconstitutional, and the remainder is not capable of standing alone, the whole must fall.” Here, the amendment was presented as a single package: state and federal term limits were bundled together in the ballot title, preamble, and operative sections. The voters were not given a choice between the two. As the Arkansas Supreme Court observed, the amendment referred to “elected officials” collectively and applied uniformly to both state and federal offices. This makes the provisions inseparable in practice. The Court in *Morgan v. Virginia*, 328 U.S. 373, 377 (1946), noted that “when a statute is so constructed that its parts are interdependent, the invalidity of one part may destroy the entire structure.”
