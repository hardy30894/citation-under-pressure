# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 289 Limits Recovery to the Profit on the "Article of Manufacture" to Which the Patented Design Is Applied, Not the Entire End Product

The statute permits recovery of the infringer's "total profit," but only as to "the article of manufacture to which [the patented] design or colored design has been applied." This limiting phrase would be meaningless if "article of manufacture" always meant the entire multi-component consumer product as sold. Congress chose the word "article," not "product" or "device," and this Court has long recognized that a design patent protects only the *appearance* embodied in a discrete article, not the utilitarian machine that houses it. In *Gorham Manufacturing Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 511 (1871), the Court explained that design-patent infringement turns on whether "the appearance" of the accused article would deceive an "ordinary observer, giving such attention as a purchaser usually gives," *id.* at 528. That inquiry is necessarily tethered to the component bearing the patented ornamentation—here, for example, a rounded bezel or a grid of icons—not to the multifunction smartphone as a whole, most of which (chips, radios, batteries) has nothing to do with the patented appearance.

## II. Bedrock Apportionment Principles Confirm That Profit Must Be Tied to the Infringing Feature

Long before § 289's predecessor was enacted, this Court insisted that an infringer disgorge only the profit causally attributable to the patented contribution, not profit generated by unrelated features of a larger commercial product. In *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), the Court held that a patentee "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features," *id.* at 121, unless he could show that "the entire value of the whole machine … is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature." *Id.* The same principle governed equitable accountings generally. In *The Elizabeth*, 97 U.S. 126 (1877), the Court refused to award profits on an entire pavement structure where the patent covered only a component improvement, requiring the patentee to isolate the value added by his contribution. And in *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 620 (1871), the Court reiterated that recoverable profits must be those "actually realized" from the use of the patented improvement, not profits flowing from independently valuable, non-infringing elements of the defendant's business. These cases establish a uniform rule: profit recovery follows the infringing feature, and where that feature is embodied in a component of a larger article, the component—not the whole—is the proper measure.

## III. The Dobson Cases and the 1887 Act Confirm That "Article of Manufacture" May Be a Component, Not the Whole Commercial Product

This principle applied with full force to design patents before Congress acted. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), and *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), the Court confronted design patents covering carpet *patterns* and held that the patentee could recover only the profit attributable to the pattern itself, not the profit earned on the carpet as a marketable textile good. Congress responded to the practical evidentiary difficulty patentees faced in isolating that profit—not by redefining "article of manufacture" to mean the entire finished good—but by relieving patentees of the burden of apportionment once infringement of the design applied to a particular article was shown. Nothing in that legislative response purported to expand the "article" itself beyond the component to which the design was, in the statute's words, "applied."

Lower courts applying the 1887 Act understood this immediately. In *Untermeyer v. Freund*, 58 F. 205 (2d Cir. 1893), the Second Circuit, addressing a design patent on a watch case, confined the profit recovery to the case itself rather than the assembled watch, explaining that the relevant "article" was the component bearing the patented design. Similarly, in *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916), the court analyzed a design patent covering a piano *case* by asking whether the case was the "article of manufacture" to which the ornamental design had been applied, recognizing that a multi-component product may contain more than one "article" for these purposes.

## IV. Applying These Principles Here Requires Limiting Damages to the Relevant Component

Samsung's smartphones are complex, multicomponent devices whose profitability derives overwhelmingly from technology—processors, displays, wireless radios, software—having nothing to do with the ornamental design elements Apple patented. Under *Garretson*, *The Elizabeth*, *Mowry*, and the *Dobson* line, disgorgement must be tied to the article bearing the infringed design, not to unrelated engineering achievements bundled into the same product. Just as the carpet manufacturer in *Dobson* could not be stripped of profits earned on the fabric itself, and the watchmaker in *Untermeyer* could not be stripped of profits on the movement inside the case, Samsung cannot be forced to disgorge profits on baseband chips and wireless antennas because a jury found infringement of a design covering only a screen bezel or icon arrangement. The "article of manufacture" in § 289 must be construed consistently with a century of apportionment doctrine: as the component to which the patented design was applied, not the entire finished smartphone.
