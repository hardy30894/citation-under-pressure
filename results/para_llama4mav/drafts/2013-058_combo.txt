The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker, holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under the U.S. Bankruptcy Code, is erroneous. The court's interpretation of "retirement funds" under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is too narrow and fails to consider the plain meaning of the statute and relevant precedent.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." The plain language of the statute does not require that the retirement funds be held for the debtor's retirement. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Gilbert, 286 U.S. 6, 8 (1932), "the plain meaning of the statute is the primary source of its interpretation." 

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be construed liberally in favor of the debtor. In Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 190 (1904), the Court stated that "exemption laws are to be liberally construed in favor of the exemption." This principle is supported by the decision in White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 312-313 (1924), where the Court held that exemptions should be interpreted broadly to achieve their intended purpose.

The Seventh Circuit's decision conflicts with the Fifth Circuit's decision in In re Chilton, which held that an inherited IRA is exempt under §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The Fifth Circuit's reasoning is more persuasive, as it recognizes that the statute does not require that the retirement funds be held for the debtor's retirement. As the Court held in Helvering v. Bliss, 293 U.S. 144, 151 (1934), "the language of the statute is clear and unambiguous, and it is not for us to add to or subtract from it."

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit's reliance on the economic attributes of an inherited IRA is misplaced. The Court has long recognized that the tax benefits associated with certain accounts are relevant to their treatment under the Bankruptcy Code. In Burnet v. Harmel, 287 U.S. 103, 110 (1932), the Court held that the tax treatment of certain transactions is determined by their "ordinary and usual meaning." 

The Seventh Circuit's decision also fails to consider the legislative history and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code. As the Court held in United States v. American Trucking Associations, 310 U.S. 534, 543-544 (1940), "the legislative history of a statute can provide valuable insight into its meaning and purpose." The exemption for retirement funds is intended to protect debtors' retirement savings, and inherited IRAs are a type of retirement account that is exempt from taxation under the Internal Revenue Code.

In addition, the Court has recognized that the Bankruptcy Code is intended to provide a fresh start for debtors. In Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), the Court stated that the Bankruptcy Code is intended to "relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh." Exempting inherited IRAs from the debtor's estate is consistent with this purpose.

The decision in Clark v. Rameker should be reversed, and the district court's decision holding that the inherited IRA is exempt from the debtor's estate should be affirmed. This interpretation is consistent with the plain meaning of the statute, relevant precedent, and the legislative history and purpose of the Bankruptcy Code. As the Court held in Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), "it is elementary that the meaning of a statute must be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and when that is free from ambiguity, it is conclusive."
