**ARGUMENT**

**THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED BY EXPANDING EXEMPTION 2 BEYOND ITS STATUTORY TEXT AND THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT.**

The Freedom of Information Act (“FOIA”) mandates “full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). The nine exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and “must be narrowly construed.” *United States Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989); *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Navy bears the burden of proving that withheld documents fall within an exemption. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B); *United States Dep’t of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173 (1991).

The Ninth Circuit’s decision to exempt explosive safety quantity distance (“ESQD”) maps under Exemption 2—which covers matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency,” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2)—cannot be reconciled with the statute’s plain language or this Court’s teachings.

**A. The ESQD Maps Do Not Relate “Solely” to Internal Personnel Rules and Practices.**

Exemption 2’s text is narrow. The word “solely” limits the exemption to matters that are *exclusively* about internal personnel rules and practices. The ESQD maps are not such matters. They are technical data about the physical effects of explosions—blast radii, safe distances, and storage configurations. These maps govern the *physical layout* of an ammunition depot, not the internal rules or practices of Navy personnel.

This Court has never applied Exemption 2 to information like the ESQD maps. In *Rose*, the Court held that Exemption 2 covers only “minor employment matters” and “trivial administrative matters” such as parking and lunch rules. 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The ESQD maps are anything but trivial. They concern the safety of a community living near a naval magazine. They are not “personnel rules” of the kind Congress intended to shield.

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard—borrowed from the D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc)—impermissibly dilutes the statutory requirement that the matter relate “solely” to internal personnel rules. The word “predominantly” is not in the statute. This Court has repeatedly instructed that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed,” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361, and that courts should not “engraft[] onto the statutory language unique concepts of [their] own making,” *Weinberger v. Catholic Action of Hawaii/Peace Education Project*, 454 U.S. 139, 146 (1981). The Ninth Circuit did precisely that.

**B. The ESQD Maps Do Not Present a Risk of Circumventing Agency Regulation.**

Even if the maps could be characterized as “internal,” the Ninth Circuit’s alternative holding—that disclosure would risk circumvention of agency regulation—is unsupported. The maps are planning tools for safe ordnance storage. They do not contain enforcement guidelines, inspection procedures, or any information that would enable a person to evade agency rules. The Navy’s concern that disclosure could aid an attack is a security argument, not a circumvention argument. Congress provided specific exemptions for national security and law enforcement information—Exemptions 1 and 7—but the Navy chose not to invoke them here. See *Weinberger*, 454 U.S. at 146 (noting that classified information is exempt under Exemption 1). The Navy cannot use Exemption 2 as a catch-all for information it prefers to keep secret.

**C. The Ninth Circuit’s Expansion of Exemption 2 Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent.**

This Court has consistently rejected efforts to broaden FOIA exemptions beyond their text. In *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 290-91 (1979), the Court emphasized that FOIA exemptions are permissive, not mandatory, and that agencies cannot withhold documents unless they fall within a specific exemption. In *United States Dep’t of Defense v. FLRA*, 510 U.S. 487, 495 (1994), the Court reiterated that “the only relevant public interest in the FOIA balancing analysis” is whether disclosure would “shed light on an agency’s performance of its statutory duties.” The ESQD maps would shed light on the Navy’s safety practices—a matter of profound public interest.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 152 (1989), which held that FOIA exemptions must be “narrowly construed” and that the government bears the burden of proving their applicability. The Navy failed to carry that burden here.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed. Exemption 2 does not shield the ESQD maps. The maps are not “internal personnel rules and practices,” and their disclosure would not circumvent agency regulation. This Court should reaffirm that FOIA exemptions are to be narrowly construed and that the public’s right to know about government activities—especially those affecting community safety—must be respected.
