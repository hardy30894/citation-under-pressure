**ARGUMENT**

The District Court correctly concluded that Petitioner is not entitled to habeas relief because the failure to advise him of his right to appeal was harmless. Rule 32(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure requires the district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal. *See* Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2) (1992). However, this Court has long held that a procedural error does not warrant reversal unless it prejudiced the defendant. *See Hopt v. People of Territory of Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884) ("[A]n error committed in the course of a trial, which does not affect the substantial rights of the accused, is not ground for reversal."); *see also United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 253-54 (1940) ("[I]f the error did not prejudice the defendant, it is not ground for reversal.").

Here, the District Court found that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review, concluding that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was satisfied because Petitioner knew of his right to appeal. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) ("If, upon the whole record, the reviewing court can say with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error, the error is harmless."); *see also Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The judgment must be treated in the same way in the appellate court as the verdict of the jury and the finding of the trial judge when the question is whether it can be held that the error is harmless.").

This principle is well-established in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Lewis v. United States*, 279 U.S. 637, 640 (1929), the Court held that "[a]n error which does not affect substantial rights is not ground for reversal." Similarly, in *Salinas v. Texas*, 233 U.S. 37, 45 (1914), the Court stated that "[a]n error which does not prejudice the rights of the accused is not ground for reversal." These cases confirm that harmless-error review applies to procedural violations, including those under Rule 32(a)(2).

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly emphasized that the harmless-error standard is not limited to constitutional errors but applies to all procedural violations. *See Bruno v. United States*, 308 U.S. 287, 290 (1939) ("The error, if any, was harmless."); *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The judgment must be treated in the same way in the appellate court as the verdict of the jury and the finding of the trial judge when the question is whether it can be held that the error is harmless.").

The District Court’s finding that Petitioner was aware of his right to appeal is dispositive. This Court has long recognized that a defendant’s knowledge of his rights can render a procedural error harmless. *See Morgan v. United States*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954) ("The purpose of a procedural rule is to promote the proper administration of justice, and if that purpose is otherwise served, the rule may be deemed satisfied."); *see also United States Gypsum Co. v. United States*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) ("A finding is 'clearly erroneous' when although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.").

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
