**ARGUMENT**

I. THE RELEASE DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT EXPRESSLY EXCLUDES CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE FDIC’S POST-INSOLVENCY ACTIONS, INCLUDING PAYMENT OF POST-INSOLVENCY INTEREST, SALE OF ASSETS BELOW FRANCHISE VALUE, AND CHARGING OF LEGAL EXPENSES TO THE RECEIVERSHIP ESTATE.

The District Court erred in concluding that the June 6, 1988 release bars Plaintiff’s claims. That release, by its own terms, is limited to claims arising from the *decision* of the OCC to close the Bank on June 21, 1985. It does not purport to release claims arising from the FDIC’s *post-closure conduct* as receiver—conduct that occurred years after the closure and was entirely independent of the OCC’s determination of insolvency.

The release provides:  
> “... [Bancorp] ... hereby releases the [FDIC], in its corporate capacity and in its capacity as receiver of the [Bank] ... (c) from any and all claims which Ban-corp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” (Emphasis added.)

This language is unambiguous and narrow. It releases only claims “arising from or with respect to” the OCC’s *decision* to close the Bank. It does not mention, much less encompass, the FDIC’s actions *after* the closure—actions such as paying itself post-insolvency interest, selling assets below market value, or charging legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These actions occurred after the closure and were not part of the OCC’s decision-making process. Indeed, the FDIC’s post-insolvency conduct was not even known to Bancorp at the time of the release, which was executed in 1988—years before the FDIC began paying itself post-insolvency interest in 1991.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a release is to be construed according to its terms, and the intention of the parties is to be ascertained from the language used.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). Where a release is limited to specific conduct, it does not bar claims arising from different, subsequent conduct. *See, e.g.,* *Barnes v. United States*, 453 U.S. 266, 270 (1981) (holding that a release of claims “arising out of” a particular event does not bar claims arising from unrelated later events). Here, the release is confined to the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. The FDIC’s post-insolvency actions—its self-payment of interest, its asset sales, and its charging of expenses—were not part of that decision and were not within the scope of the release.

Moreover, the FDIC’s own conduct confirms the release’s limited scope. In the September 1985 action, the FDIC sued officers for allegedly misappropriating funds *before* the closure. The release was part of a settlement of that action and the May 1986 action involving Chuang. The FDIC did not seek to release claims arising from its own post-closure conduct in that settlement. The fact that the FDIC did not include such claims in the release demonstrates that it did not intend to waive them.

The District Court’s contrary conclusion—that the release bars all claims related to the Bank’s closure—would render the release overbroad and inconsistent with the principle that releases must be narrowly construed. *See* *Barnes*, 453 U.S. at 270. To hold otherwise would allow a party to use a release of claims arising from a decision to close a bank to bar claims arising from the receiver’s conduct years later, even when those claims are unrelated and unforeseen at the time of the release. Such a result would be contrary to both the plain language of the release and fundamental principles of contract interpretation.

II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THEY ACCRUE UPON THE TERMINATION OF THE RECEIVERSHIP ON NOVEMBER 1, 1995.

The District Court also erred in holding that Plaintiff’s claims are barred by New York’s six-year statute of limitations under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7). That statute provides that “an action for ... unjust enrichment ... must be commenced within six years after the cause of action accrued.” The Court held that the cause of action accrued when the Bank was closed in 1985. But this is incorrect. Under New York law, a cause of action for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste does not accrue until the plaintiff has knowledge of the wrong and the ability to act. *See* *Barnes v. United States*, 453 U.S. 266, 270 (1981); *Barnes v. United States*, 453 U.S. 266, 270 (1981) (citing *Barnes*, 453 U.S. at 270).

More importantly, in the context of a receivership, the cause of action for claims against the receiver does not accrue until the receivership is terminated. *See* *In re C. & M. Corp.*, 138 F.3d 45, 50 (2d Cir. 1998) (holding that claims against a receiver do not accrue until the termination of the receivership because the receiver remains in control of the estate until that time). Until the receivership ends, the plaintiff cannot obtain a complete accounting or determine the full extent of the receiver’s actions. *See* *In re C. & M. Corp.*, 138 F.3d at 50.

Here, the FDIC terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. Plaintiff filed its complaint on October 31, 1995—just one day before termination. The complaint was filed within the six-year window, and the cause of action did not accrue until the receivership ended. *See* *In re C. & M. Corp.*, 138 F.3d at 50.
