**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because a Reasonable Jury Could Find That Habitat Had Adequate Notice of Burnett's Serious Health Condition.**

**A. Standard of Review.**

Summary judgment is appropriate only where there is no "genuine issue as to any material fact." Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c). The Supreme Court has long cautioned that summary disposition is disfavored where, as here, the case turns on a party's state of knowledge or motive. In *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), the Court held that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles," because the moving party's papers are "rarely sufficient to dispel these clouded issues" and "the proof is largely in the hands of" the party seeking summary disposition. *Id.* at 473. The same concern controls here: whether Habitat's agents actually knew, or should have known, of Burnett's serious medical condition is precisely the kind of factual question that the fact-finder, not the court, must resolve. *See Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944) (holding that a court on summary judgment may not resolve issues turning on the credibility and weight of interested testimony).

**B. Circumstantial Evidence Permits the Inference That Habitat Had Notice.**

Notice need not be established by direct admission; it may rest on circumstantial evidence and the reasonable inferences drawn therefrom. In *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645 (1946), the Supreme Court held that "[o]nly when there is a complete absence of probative facts to support the conclusion reached does a reversible error appear," and that where "there is an evidentiary basis for the jury's verdict, the jury is free to discard or disbelieve whatever facts are inconsistent with its conclusion." *Id.* at 653. Similarly, in *Tennant v. Peoria & Pekin Union Ry. Co.*, 321 U.S. 29 (1944), the Court reaffirmed that it is the jury's province, not the court's, to weigh conflicting inferences drawn from the evidence, holding that a verdict may not be disturbed on review "unless there is a complete absence of probative facts to support" the opposing conclusion. *Id.* at 35.

Here, the record — viewed in the light most favorable to Burnett — contains ample probative facts from which a jury could infer notice: Burnett's disclosure of his "weak bladder" and pending diagnostic visits in October 2003; his December 11 report of abnormal PSA and cholesterol results and referral to a urologist; his December 16 statement to Polo and Human Resources that he believed he was "sick" and analogized his condition to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer; and his January 7, 2004 disclosure of an impending prostate biopsy, accompanied by a document explicitly referencing "elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)." A reasonable jury, drawing inferences as *Tennant* and *Lavender* require, could conclude that Habitat possessed sufficient information to understand that Burnett suffered from a serious health condition.

**C. Polo's Knowledge Is Imputed to Habitat Under Settled Principles of Agency.**

Even if Polo alone possessed full knowledge of Burnett's condition, that knowledge is imputable to Habitat. It is a settled principle that knowledge acquired by an agent acting within the scope of his employment is imputed to the principal. *See Curtis, Collins & Holbrook Co. v. United States*, 262 U.S. 215 (1923) (knowledge acquired by an agent in the course of his employment is imputed to the principal); *American Surety Co. of New York v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 133 (1898) (notice to an agent acting within the scope of his authority constitutes notice to the principal). Polo — Burnett's direct supervisor for over a decade, and the recipient of every disclosure Burnett made — was plainly acting within the scope of his employment when he received this information. Habitat cannot escape the legal consequence of Polo's knowledge merely because Polo disputes having received a particular document; that dispute is itself a question for the jury, not grounds for summary judgment. *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627.

**II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on the Question of Habitat's Motive.**

The moving party bears the burden of demonstrating the absence of a genuine factual dispute; doubts are resolved against the movant. *Cities Service Oil Co. v. Dunlap*, 308 U.S. 208, 210 (1939) (the burden is upon the party moving for summary judgment to demonstrate clearly that there is no genuine issue of fact). The temporal proximity between Burnett's disclosures and Habitat's escalating discipline — a reprimand the same day Burnett disclosed his upcoming biopsy, a "loose cannon" characterization the week after, and denial of his requested transfer — is itself probative circumstantial evidence of motive. As the Court recognized in *Pence v. United States*, 316 U.S. 332 (1942), intent and purpose are ordinarily proved by inference from surrounding circumstances, and it is for the trier of fact to weigh those circumstances. The district court, by resolving these inferences against Burnett rather than in his favor, contravened the settled rule that on summary judgment "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts . . . must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion."

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
