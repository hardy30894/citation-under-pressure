**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects “liberty” interests that are not merely created by state law, but are “fundamental” to the individual’s freedom and dignity. *Washington v. Glucksberg*, 521 U.S. 702, 720 (1997). While the Supreme Court has long recognized that “the power to grant clemency is not itself a constitutional right,” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464, 101 S.Ct. at 2464, it has also held that when a state creates a procedure that significantly affects a person’s life or liberty, due process may attach to that procedure—even if the underlying right is not constitutionally guaranteed. *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557, 94 S.Ct. 2963, 2975 (1974). The critical inquiry is not whether the state *must* provide clemency, but whether the state’s clemency process, by its very nature, “imposes a significant restraint on liberty” and thus triggers due process protections.

Here, Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere formality. It is a structured, state-administered mechanism that directly determines whether a death row inmate will live or die. The Ohio Supreme Court has affirmed that clemency is “a vital component of the criminal justice system,” *State v. Woodard*, 68 Ohio St.3d 70, 76, 623 N.E.2d 75, 80 (1993), and the state’s own regulations mandate that the Ohio Adult Parole Authority (APA) initiate a clemency review 45 days before an execution date. *Ohio Admin. Code § 5120-1-07*. This procedural obligation, coupled with the governor’s power to grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons under *Ohio Const. Art. III, § 11*, transforms clemency from a discretionary act into a critical stage of the capital punishment process.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that when a state creates a procedure that “has a significant impact on the individual’s liberty,” due process applies. *Wolff*, 418 U.S. at 557, 94 S.Ct. at 2975. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 99 S.Ct. 2100 (1979), the Court recognized that a prisoner’s liberty interest in parole was protected by due process because parole decisions “affect the duration of confinement.” Id. at 17, 99 S.Ct. at 2109. Similarly, in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 115 S.Ct. 2293 (1995), the Court held that even non-constitutional liberty interests may be protected when a state’s procedures “impose atypical and significant hardship on the inmate in relation to the ordinary incidents of prison life.” Id. at 484, 115 S.Ct. at 2301. The clemency process here imposes precisely such a hardship: the difference between life and death.

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that no liberty interest exists is inconsistent with precedent. The Court in *Dumschat* did not hold that no liberty interest could ever arise from clemency proceedings; rather, it held that the Connecticut Board of Pardons’ failure to provide reasons for denying clemency did not violate due process because no protected interest had been created. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465, 101 S.Ct. at 2465. But here, Ohio has created a structured, mandatory process that directly determines life or death. The APA’s own regulation explicitly states that the clemency review “shall be conducted in accordance with the procedures set forth in this rule,” indicating that the process is not arbitrary. The state’s investment in this procedure—its timing, its formality, its mandatory nature—demonstrates that it is not a mere “courtesy” but a critical stage of the capital punishment system.

Thus, the clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment. The state’s failure to afford due process at this stage violates the fundamental right to be free from arbitrary deprivation of life.

II. The APA’s clemency interview procedure imposes an unconstitutional condition by coercing a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be witnesses against themselves. *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 460, 86 S.Ct. 1602, 1619 (1966). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a person may not be compelled to incriminate himself,” and that “the privilege against self-incrimination is a personal right.” *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609, 614, 85 S.Ct. 1229, 1232 (1965). When the state conditions the receipt of a benefit on the waiver of a constitutional right, it creates an “unconstitutional condition” that violates due process. *Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446, 105 S.Ct. 3249, 3257 (1985).
