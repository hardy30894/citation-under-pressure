# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Ties Coverage to the Causal Source of the Injury, Not to the Geographic Point of Impact

Section 1333(b) extends compensation for "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The operative phrase—"resulting from"—asks a causal question, not a locational one. Congress located the required situs in the statute where it wanted one: the "operations" must occur "on the outer Continental Shelf." It imposed no parallel requirement that the "injury" itself occur there. Where Congress uses different language in proximate parts of the same statute, that choice is deliberate, and this Court should not import a situs limitation that the text itself omits. See United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542–44 (1940) (courts must give effect to the language Congress actually chose and the purpose that language serves, rather than supplying limitations Congress did not enact).

This causal reading is confirmed by analogous compensation and tort statutes using "resulting from" or "arising out of" language, which this Court has consistently construed as causal inquiries untethered to the claimant's precise location at the moment of injury. In Rogers v. Missouri Pacific Railroad Co., 352 U.S. 500 (1957), the Court held that a statute conditioning liability on injury "resulting in whole or in part" from certain causes requires only that the covered cause "played any part, even the slightest, in producing the injury," id. at 506—a test of causal contribution, not geography. The same logic controls here: an injury "resulting from" Shelf operations is compensable if Shelf operations contributed to it, wherever the worker happened to be standing when the harm occurred.

## II. The Liberal-Construction Canon Governing Workers' Compensation Statutes Forbids Grafting an Unwritten Situs-of-Injury Requirement onto Section 1333(b)

This Court has long held that the Longshore Act—which Section 1333(b) borrows wholesale—"must be liberally construed in conformance with its purpose, and in a way which avoids harsh and incongruous results." Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953). The Act was enacted to remedy the inadequacy of state compensation remedies for a distinct class of workers, Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 414 (1932), and doubts in its application have consistently been resolved in favor of coverage rather than exclusion. Cardillo v. Liberty Mutual Insurance Co., 330 U.S. 469, 478 (1947); Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244, 249 (1941).

That same solicitude governs the Defense Base Act, which—like Section 1333(b)—extends Longshore Act coverage by reference to the causal relationship between employment and injury rather than by strict situs. In O'Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc., 340 U.S. 504 (1951), this Court held that "the test of recovery" under such an extension "is not a causal relation between the nature of employment of the injured person and the accident," but "whether the obligations or conditions of employment create the zone of special danger out of which the injury arose." Id. at 507. A roustabout's duties in receiving, processing, and disposing of platform-generated materials at the shore facility that serviced Pacific Operations's Shelf platforms falls squarely within the "zone of special danger" created by Shelf operations, even though the fatal accident occurred on land. A situs-of-injury rule would defeat this remedial design by denying compensation to workers whose injuries are unquestionably caused by Shelf operations merely because the accident's last link happened onshore—precisely the "harsh and incongruous result" Voris forbids.

## III. The Footnote in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire Is Unconsidered Dictum That Does Not Bind This Court

Petitioner leans on a single footnote in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986), remarking that Section 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." But the Tallentire Court itself disclaimed any holding on the point, stating that Section 1333(b) "has no bearing on this case." Id. The issue was neither briefed nor argued; it was not necessary to the disposition of the choice-of-law question actually presented.

This Court has never treated such passing remarks as controlling. "General expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and "their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399–400 (1821); accord Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). An observation offered without argument, analysis, or necessity to the judgment is not entitled to the deference due a considered holding. The Tallentire footnote, addressing a provision the Court expressly found irrelevant to the case before it, is dictum of the least authoritative kind and cannot supply the situs-of-injury requirement that the statutory text withholds.

## Conclusion

The statute's causal language, the settled canon of liberal construction for workers' compensation extensions, and the absence of any binding precedent to the contrary confirm that Section 1333(b) covers Valladolid's death, which resulted from operations conducted on the Shelf, without regard to the situs of the fatal accident itself.
