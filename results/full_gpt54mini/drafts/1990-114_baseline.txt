**Argument**

I. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the ADEA.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof,” and also excludes “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In **Gregory v. Ashcroft**, 501 U.S. 452 (1991), the Supreme Court held that Missouri state judges are excluded from the ADEA as appointees on the policymaking level. Id. at 467-69. The Court emphasized that judges do not merely apply fixed rules; they “necessarily will resolve issues previously unsettled and thus will create law.” Id. at 462. That lawmaking function, together with judges’ significant supervisory responsibilities in the Missouri court system, placed them within the statutory exclusion. Id. at 462-66.

The Court also rejected the contention that the policymaking exception is limited to executive-branch appointees. It explained that such a reading would leave appointed judges outside every exemption in § 630(f), a result it found highly implausible. **Gregory**, 501 U.S. at 465-66. And because the ADEA regulates an area of state sovereignty of special importance—the composition of state courts—the Court required a clear congressional statement before reading the statute to displace Missouri’s constitutional retirement rule. Id. at 460-61. Finding no such clear statement, the Court declined to interpret the ADEA to cover appointed state judges. Id. at 467-69.

That conclusion is reinforced by the Court’s earlier federalism decisions. In **Gregory**, the Court reiterated that “Congress should make its intention ‘clear and manifest’” before intruding on “functions essential to separate and independent existence.” 501 U.S. at 460-61 (quoting **Will v. Michigan Department of State Police**, 491 U.S. 58, 65 (1989), and **Pennhurst State School & Hospital v. Halderman**, 465 U.S. 89, 99 (1984)). Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule for judges is precisely the sort of state structural choice that demands that clear statement. **Gregory**, 501 U.S. at 461-62.

II. Missouri’s retirement age for judges does not violate equal protection.

The equal protection claim is governed by rational-basis review because age is not a suspect classification. **Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia**, 427 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1976) (per curiam). Under that standard, a classification will be upheld if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. **Nordlinger v. Hahn**, 505 U.S. 1, 11 (1992). In **Gregory**, the Court held that Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule for judges readily satisfied that deferential standard. 501 U.S. at 473-76.

The Court recognized several legitimate state interests supporting the rule: ensuring a “competent judiciary,” avoiding the burdens of individualized fitness proceedings, promoting public confidence in the courts, and facilitating regular turnover on the bench. **Gregory**, 501 U.S. at 473-75. Citing decisions upholding similar provisions, the Court explained that states may reasonably conclude that “faculties [that] age tends to erode” justify mandatory retirement in the judiciary. Id. at 474 (quoting **Trafelet v. Thompson**, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979)); see also **Malmed v. Thornburgh**, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); **Vance v. Bradley**, 440 U.S. 93, 111-12 (1979). The Court made clear that even if reasonable minds might disagree with those policy judgments, that disagreement does not render the law irrational. **Gregory**, 501 U.S. at 475-76.

The Equal Protection Clause does not require Missouri to adopt the least restrictive or most individualized means of achieving its objectives. **FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.**, 508 U.S. 307, 313-15 (1993). Missouri was entitled to conclude that a fixed retirement age is a permissible line-drawing mechanism for the judiciary. **Gregory**, 501 U.S. at 474-76. Because the provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests, it does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment.

III. The judgment below should be affirmed.

The Supreme Court has already resolved both questions in Missouri’s favor. **Gregory v. Ashcroft**, 501 U.S. 452 (1991). The ADEA does not cover Missouri’s appointed state judges, and Missouri’s age-70 retirement requirement for judges satisfies rational-basis review under the Equal Protection Clause. Id. at 467-69, 473-76. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
