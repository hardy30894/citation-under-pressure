**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATES RETAIN BROAD AUTHORITY TO SHAPE THE ADMISSIBILITY AND EFFECT OF EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE WITHOUT VIOLATING DUE PROCESS.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of his mental disease or defect to negate *mens rea* when the State has chosen to channel such evidence exclusively to the insanity defense. This Court has long recognized that the definition of criminal offenses is primarily a matter of state law. *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 56 (1895). Because Arizona may define the mental element of first-degree murder as a cognitive capacity distinct from the abnormal mental condition that gives rise to an insanity plea, the State may correspondingly limit the evidence admissible on each issue.

The common law has consistently treated insanity as an affirmative defense separate from the issue of criminal intent. *Sparf*, 156 U.S. at 56 (noting that “the law presumes every man to be sane”). The presumption of sanity is a procedural rule that a state may adopt without offending due process. Indeed, this Court in *Sparf* upheld the propriety of instructing the jury that the defendant bears the burden of proving insanity. *Id.* at 57. Arizona’s rule is a direct descendant of that common-law tradition.

**II. ARIZONA’S RULE DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY SHIFT THE PROSECUTION’S BURDEN OF PROOF.**

The cornerstone of criminal due process is that the State must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 169 (1952). But Arizona’s rule does not relieve the State of this burden. The State must still prove that Clark acted intentionally—a mental state that, under Arizona law, is entirely compatible with a severe mental illness. The rule merely excludes irrelevant or misleading evidence from the *mens rea* determination.

When a defendant claims that his mental condition prevented him from forming intent, the State may cabin that evidence to the insanity defense because the two inquiries are logically distinct. As the Court explained in *Sparf*, the presumption of sanity is “a rule of evidence” that may be “overcome by proof of insanity.” 156 U.S. at 56. The question of sanity is a separate factual inquiry, and nothing in *Sparf* requires that the same evidence be allowed to contest *mens rea*.

This Court has consistently rejected claims that due process compels the unbounded admission of expert psychiatric testimony. In *Rochin*, the Court held that due process is violated only by conduct that “offends those canons of decency and fairness which express the notions of justice of English-speaking peoples.” 342 U.S. at 169. Arizona’s evidentiary rule—which merely channels mental-health evidence to the appropriate defense—does not offend those canons.

**III. MULTIPLE PRECEDENTS UPHOLD STATE CONTROL OVER THE INTERPLAY OF INSANITY AND MENS REA.**

Since the earliest days of this Court, the States have been permitted to define the relationship between mental illness and criminal responsibility. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (recognizing that the legislature has broad authority to define the law). Arizona has exercised that authority by separating the insanity defense from the *mens rea* inquiry.

The common law has long recognized that insanity is a defense distinct from specific intent. *Sparf*, 156 U.S. at 56. In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 583 (1884), the Court observed that the “mental condition of the accused” may be “so disordered that he cannot form a criminal intent,” but that the state may treat that condition as an excuse rather than a negation of an element. The Court in *Hopt* upheld the state’s allocation of the burden of proof on insanity. *Id.* at 585.

Similarly, in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895), the Court held that the presumption of sanity “is not a presumption *juris et de jure*” but “may be overcome by proof that the accused is insane.” The question of sanity is “a question of fact” that, once raised, the State must disprove. *Id.* at 487-88. But nothing in *Davis* requires that the same evidence be allowed to contest *mens rea*.

The Court in *Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413, 418 (1902), held that a defendant claiming “irresistible impulse” must prove that condition “to the satisfaction of the jury,” even though the government retained the burden on general intent. And in *Salinger v. United States*, 272 U.S. 542, 545 (1926), the Court summarized the principle: “The common law recognized” that “the burden of proving insanity” rests on the defendant, “and that such burden is not satisfied unless the proof is sufficient to establish it.”

Finally, in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 532 (1968), this Court rejected the argument that due process requires a special evidentiary rule for chronic alcoholism to negate *mens rea*, holding that “the criminal law” is not “powerless to punish” conduct “committed under circumstances which would normally be deemed criminal.” Clark’s position—that he must be allowed to use delusional beliefs to negate intent—similarly seeks to constitutionalize a particular evidentiary regime that no prior decision of this Court has mandated.

**IV. CLARK’S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD DISRUPT THE STATE’S CAREFULLY CRAFTED INSANITY SCHEME.**

Arizona has chosen to treat insanity as an affirmative defense requiring proof by clear and convincing evidence. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(C). Allowing the same evidence to refute *mens rea* would permit a defendant to circumvent the higher burden and the specialized adjudication of insanity. This Court has never held that due process requires such a result.

Because Arizona’s rule respects the distinct roles of *mens rea* and insanity, does not relieve the State of its burden, and reflects a balanced legislative judgment, it does not violate the Due Process Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
