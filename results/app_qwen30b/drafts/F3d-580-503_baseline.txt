**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s denial of James Winbush’s last-minute motion for a fingerprint identification expert and its refusal to grant a continuance violated his constitutional right to effective assistance of counsel and due process under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The court’s rigid adherence to procedural deadlines, without considering the substantial prejudice to Winbush’s ability to mount a meaningful defense, rendered his trial fundamentally unfair. This Court should reverse his convictions and remand for a new trial.

**I. The District Court’s Denial of Winbush’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert Violated His Sixth Amendment Right to Effective Assistance of Counsel**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to “the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” *Coppedge v. United States*, 369 U.S. 438, 445 (1962). This right includes the right to effective assistance, which requires counsel to provide “reasonably competent representation” that is “within the range of competence demanded of attorneys in criminal cases.” *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). When a defendant is denied access to a crucial expert witness—especially one whose testimony could directly undermine the prosecution’s case—counsel’s performance may fall below this standard.

Here, Winbush’s defense hinged on challenging the government’s physical evidence. The prosecution’s case rested heavily on the recovery of drugs and a firearm from locations near Winbush’s arrest, all of which were allegedly linked to him through circumstantial evidence. The government’s own forensic expert, Michael Shay, had testified—via stipulation—that no latent fingerprints of value were found on any of the seized items. This absence of fingerprints was a critical piece of evidence, and Winbush’s defense strategy necessarily depended on understanding its significance.

Yet, the district court denied Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert on the ground that it was “woefully late,” filed only six days before trial. *See* *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984) (recognizing that “there are some circumstances in which the right to counsel is so impaired that prejudice is presumed”). The court failed to consider that Winbush had no prior notice that the government would rely on the absence of fingerprints as a key evidentiary point. The government’s notice of expert witnesses, filed on October 3, 2007, merely stated that Shay would testify “consistent with the lab reports” regarding fingerprint analysis—without disclosing the full implications of the absence of prints. Winbush could not reasonably anticipate the need for a defense expert until the government’s notice was received, and even then, he had no opportunity to conduct discovery or prepare a response.

The court’s refusal to allow a defense expert to interpret the absence of fingerprints—particularly in light of the government’s own expert testimony—deprived Winbush of a meaningful opportunity to challenge the prosecution’s narrative. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Gonzalez-Lopez*, 548 U.S. 140, 148 (2006), “the right to choose one’s own counsel is a fundamental component of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel.” When a defendant is denied the ability to present a defense expert whose testimony is essential to rebutting the prosecution’s evidence, the right to effective assistance is violated.

**II. The Denial of a Continuance Was an Abuse of Discretion That Violated Due Process**

Even if Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert were deemed procedurally untimely, the district court’s refusal to grant a continuance compounded the error and violated due process. The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be “deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” *U.S. Const. amend. V*. A defendant’s right to a fair trial includes the right to adequate time to prepare a defense, especially when new evidence or expert testimony is introduced.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a trial court’s refusal to grant a continuance may constitute a denial of due process when the denial results in a substantial impairment of the defendant’s ability to present a defense.” *United States v. Berrigan*, 481 F.2d 743, 750 (3d Cir. 1973) (en banc), *cert. denied*, 414 U.S. 1030 (1973). Here, Winbush’s motion was filed only five days before trial, but the request was not frivolous. He sought to retain an expert to analyze the forensic implications of the absence of fingerprints—a matter directly relevant to the government’s case. The government had not disclosed the full extent of its forensic strategy until the final pretrial conference, and Winbush had no prior opportunity to respond.

The district court’s refusal to grant even a brief continuance—despite the absence of any indication that the delay would prejudice the government—was an abuse of discretion. *See* *United States v. Hines*, 558 F.3d 657, 662 (7th Cir. 2009) (“A district court abuses its discretion when it fails to consider the totality of the circumstances, including the reason for the delay and the potential prejudice to the defendant.”). The court failed to weigh the minimal burden on the government against the substantial risk of a wrongful conviction. The government had already been on notice of the fingerprint issue for over a month and had no need for additional time. Winbush, by contrast, faced the risk of being convicted on the basis of physical evidence he could not meaningfully challenge.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s denial of Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert and its refusal to grant a continuance deprived him of his constitutional rights to effective assistance of counsel and due process. The absence of a defense expert to interpret the government’s forensic evidence, coupled with the lack of time to prepare, rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. For these reasons, this Court should reverse Winbush’s convictions and remand for a new trial.
