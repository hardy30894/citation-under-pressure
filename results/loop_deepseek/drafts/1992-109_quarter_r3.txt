**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The same constitutional standard that governs competency to stand trial—whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and has “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him,” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam)—applies equally to competency to waive constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a more demanding standard unsupported by this Court’s precedent.

**I. The Dusky Standard Governs All Competency Determinations**

This Court has consistently applied the *Dusky* standard to competency to stand trial. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 386 (1966). That standard requires only that the defendant understand the proceedings and be able to assist counsel. Nothing in *Dusky* or its progeny suggests a different, higher standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. To the contrary, this Court has treated the competency inquiry as unitary. In *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962), the Court held that a waiver of counsel cannot be presumed from a silent record, requiring the trial court to make a specific inquiry into the waiver. The *Carnley* Court did not articulate a separate competency standard for waiver—only that the waiver must be “knowing and intelligent.” *Id.* That inquiry is distinct from the competency determination.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard conflates competency with the voluntariness of a waiver. A defendant may be competent under *Dusky* yet still make an unwise or irrational choice. But the Constitution does not guarantee that a defendant will make a “reasoned” decision; it guarantees only that the defendant has the capacity to understand the choices and the consequences. The *Dusky* standard already ensures that the defendant possesses the rational understanding necessary to make a knowing and intelligent waiver. No heightened standard is required.

**II. The Trial Court Properly Found Moran Competent**

The trial court had before it two psychiatric evaluations concluding that Moran was competent to stand trial. Those evaluations, conducted under the *Dusky* standard, established that Moran understood the charges and could assist counsel. The court also questioned Moran directly. Nothing in the record raised a good-faith doubt requiring a further hearing. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 385 (due process requires hearing only when evidence raises a bona fide doubt). Moran’s monosyllabic responses and medication did not alone create such doubt. This Court has never held that medication or depression automatically triggers a competency hearing. *See Bishop v. United States*, 350 U.S. 961, 961 (1956) (per curiam) (remanding for competency hearing where evidence of mental illness was substantial, but not establishing a per se rule).

**III. The Post-Conviction Hearing Cured Any Error**

Even if the trial court should have held a competency hearing, the state post-conviction hearing provided a full and fair opportunity to litigate Moran’s competency. The state court found Moran competent after considering the same psychiatric evidence. That finding is entitled to a presumption of correctness. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 318 (1963) (state factual findings presumed correct absent statutory exceptions). The Ninth Circuit erred by rejecting that presumption based on its view that the state court applied the wrong standard. The state court’s order, read as a whole, applied the correct *Dusky* standard. The court referenced the psychiatrists’ conclusions that Moran was “competent,” which necessarily encompassed the *Dusky* standard. No heightened standard existed at the time.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent**

This Court has never required a “reasoned choice” standard. In *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954), the Court held that the question whether a defendant is mentally competent to waive counsel is one that must be determined by the trial court. That determination, however, is governed by the same *Dusky* standard. In *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 314 (1966) (per curiam), the Court applied the same standard to competency to waive appeals. The Court did not demand a higher level of functioning. The Ninth Circuit’s innovation finds no support in *Dusky*, *Pate*, *Carnley*, or *Rees*. It improperly elevates the competency threshold beyond what the Constitution requires.

**V. The Waiver Inquiry Is Separate from Competency**

The Ninth Circuit confused competency with the knowing and intelligent nature of a waiver. Competency is a threshold question: can the defendant understand the proceedings? If yes, the court then examines whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent. The trial court here conducted that second inquiry. Moran stated he understood his rights and wished to waive them. His decision to prevent mitigating evidence, however ill-advised, does not demonstrate incompetence. *See Hawk v. Olson*, 326 U.S. 271, 278 (1945) (waiver of counsel must be “competently and intelligently made,” but competency is measured by understanding, not wisdom). The Constitution does not require a defendant to make a “reasoned” choice; it requires only that the choice be made with a rational understanding of the options. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. at 516.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. The *Dusky* standard is the sole constitutional standard for competency to stand trial, plead guilty, or waive counsel. The trial court and state post-conviction court properly found Moran competent under that standard. The Ninth Circuit’s heightened “reasoned choice” standard has no basis in this Court’s pre-1970 precedent and should be rejected.
