**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—requires this Court to confront a fundamental constitutional principle: the government may not imprison an individual without a legitimate, time-bound purpose. In *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001), the Court held that *indefinite* detention under § 1231(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) raises “serious constitutional concerns” and that the statute must be construed to contain an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. Id. at 2495. The Court further clarified that once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by statute. Id. at 2503. The Fifth Circuit’s prior opinion, which upheld indefinite detention so long as the government made “good faith efforts” to deport, misapplied this standard and failed to recognize that the burden shifts to the government to demonstrate a *realistic prospect* of removal after a reasonable period has passed.

The Court’s holding in *Zadvydas* was not merely interpretive—it was constitutional. The Court emphasized that the statute “does not apply narrowly to ‘a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals’” but instead “broadly to aliens ordered removed for many and various reasons, including tourist visa violations.” Id. at 2499. This expansive reach, combined with the absence of any meaningful time limit, creates a risk of indefinite confinement for individuals who pose no threat to public safety and whose removal is effectively impossible. Such a regime, the Court warned, “would raise serious constitutional concerns” under the Due Process Clause. Id. at 2495. The Court did not leave the resolution of this concern to the political branches alone. Instead, it mandated judicial review to determine whether detention exceeds a “period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” Id. at 2504.

The Fifth Circuit’s prior decision, which held that detention was lawful so long as the government made “good faith efforts” to deport, failed to heed this directive. That standard demands more than mere effort—it demands a showing of *reasonably foreseeable removal*. As the Supreme Court made clear, “if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” Id. at 2504. The Court further established a practical framework: after six months beyond the removal period, an alien may assert that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, and the government must then rebut that showing with evidence. Id. at 2505. The Court explicitly rejected a standard that would require an alien to prove *absolute* impossibility of removal, noting that such a burden “demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” Id.

Here, Zadvydas has been in custody for over three years beyond the removal period. He has provided “good reason to believe” that removal is not reasonably foreseeable—Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him, and his reapplication for Lithuanian citizenship remains pending. The INS has not presented any evidence of a concrete, near-term prospect of removal. Indeed, the government has not even claimed that Zadvydas poses a threat to the community or is likely to flee. The district court’s finding that Zadvydas “will never be deported because there is no place to send him” necessarily includes the finding that removal is not reasonably foreseeable. That finding, confirmed by the Supreme Court’s own observation that “there is no meaningful possibility of doing so has not been clearly established,” id. at 291, compels the conclusion that continued detention is no longer authorized.

The Fifth Circuit’s subsequent reversal of its prior opinion, while consistent with *Zadvydas*, was incomplete. It affirmed the district court’s order of release but failed to recognize that the government may not re-detain Zadvydas without a *new* showing of a substantial likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future—now a shrinking window. The Court’s instruction that “what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink” as the period of confinement grows is not a mere suggestion—it is a constitutional mandate. Id. at 2505. The government cannot rely on the same speculative efforts that failed for years. If removal is not reasonably foreseeable today, it cannot be deemed so tomorrow without new, compelling evidence.

The Court has long held that “the Constitution forbids the government from imprisoning a person without a legitimate, time-bound purpose.” *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 746, 107 S. Ct. 2095, 95 L. Ed. 2d 697 (1987). Detention for the sole purpose of removal, when removal is no longer reasonably possible, is not a legitimate purpose. The government may supervise, condition release, and re-detain upon violation of conditions—but it may not detain indefinitely in the absence of a realistic prospect of removal. To hold otherwise would render the Due Process Clause a nullity.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit must be affirmed. The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite confinement. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention exceeds the statute’s limits and violates the Constitution. The Court should affirm the district court’s order of release, with the understanding that the government may seek re-detention only upon a new, credible showing of a substantial likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future.
