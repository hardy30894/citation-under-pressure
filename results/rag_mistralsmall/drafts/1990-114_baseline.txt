**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s appointed state judges are not “employees” under the ADEA because they are “appointees on the policymaking level” as defined in 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603 (8th Cir. 1990). This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the ADEA, as well as the Supreme Court’s guidance on interpreting the statute’s exceptions.

The ADEA’s definition of “employee” expressly excludes “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Supreme Court has emphasized that exceptions to the ADEA must be construed narrowly, *Western Air Lines, Inc. v. Criswell*, 472 U.S. 400, 412 (1985), but this principle does not alter the plain meaning of the statute. The legislative history confirms that Congress intended the policymaking-level exception to apply to high-level advisors and department heads in state and local governments—positions that share essential characteristics with state judges. *See* Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180.

The Eighth Circuit properly recognized that judges exercise policymaking authority by interpreting and applying the law, filling gaps in statutes and constitutions, and shaping legal standards that guide future conduct. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604 (citing *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988)). This is not merely mechanical application of settled rules; it is the exercise of judgment and discretion that is “indisputably descriptive” of policymaking. *Id.* (quoting *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). The Supreme Court has acknowledged that judges perform functions analogous to policymakers in other branches, particularly when they establish legal rules that govern public behavior and institutional operations. *See Johnson v. Mayor and City Council of Baltimore*, 472 U.S. 353, 368 (1985) (noting that the ADEA tests state discretion against a “reasonable federal standard”).

Moreover, many Missouri judges exercise significant administrative and policymaking responsibilities, such as supervising court operations, adopting rules of professional conduct, and resolving disputes within the judicial hierarchy. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 605 (citing Mo. Const. art. V, § 4). These duties further demonstrate that judges are not mere functionaries but key policymakers within the state government.

Petitioners’ argument that judges are not policymakers because they decide cases between litigants is unavailing. *See* Brief for Petitioners at 10. As the Eighth Circuit observed, judging often involves resolving unsettled legal questions, interpreting ambiguous statutes, and creating rules that have precedential effect. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604. This is quintessential policymaking, even if it occurs in the context of adjudication.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly deferred to state classifications that draw distinctions among officials based on their policymaking roles. *See Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (upholding mandatory retirement for state police officers because the state had a rational basis for its policy). The same deference applies here. Missouri’s decision to require retirement at age 70 for its judges is a rational means of ensuring that the judiciary remains capable of performing its demanding functions.

**II. THE ADEA DOES NOT EXPLICITLY AND UNEQUIVOCALLY PRE-EMPT MISSOURI’S CONSTITUTIONAL MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION.**

Even if appointed judges were deemed “employees” under the ADEA, the statute does not clearly and manifestly pre-empt Missouri’s constitutional mandatory retirement provision. The Supreme Court has long required that federal statutes intrude on traditional state functions only where Congress has spoken with “clarity and force.” *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). This clear-statement rule is particularly stringent when the federal law touches on the structure of state government, including the judiciary. *Id.*

The ADEA contains no express reference to state judges, and the Supreme Court has cautioned against inferring pre-emption from “a federal statute’s broad language.” *Oscar Mayer & Co. v. Evans*, 441 U.S. 750, 760 (1979). The Eighth Circuit correctly applied this principle, noting that Congress would not have extended the ADEA to judges without “serious consideration” of the implications for state sovereignty. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 602. The absence of any such consideration in the legislative history weighs strongly against pre-emption.

Furthermore, the ADEA’s remedial scheme—including its deferential standard for bona fide occupational qualifications (*see* 29 U.S.C. § 623(f)(1))—suggests that Congress did not intend to displace state mandatory retirement rules for judges. *See Public Employees Retirement System of Ohio v. Betts*, 492 U.S. 158, 173 (1989) (upholding retirement plans as not being “subterfuges” to evade the ADEA). The Court has recognized that age-based distinctions in employment are not inherently suspect, and states retain broad latitude to regulate their own officials. *See Johnson*, 472 U.S. at 368.

**III. MISSOURI’S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION FOR JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Eighth Circuit correctly upheld Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision as rationally related to legitimate state interests. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 607. The Supreme Court has repeatedly deferred to state classifications that are not arbitrary or invidious, even when they draw lines based on age. *See Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313; *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 108 (1979).

Missouri has articulated several rational bases for its rule: ensuring judicial competence, reducing delays caused by aging judges, and maintaining public confidence in the judiciary. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 607 (citing *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979)). These interests are not speculative; they are grounded in the unique demands of judicial office. *See Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988) (upholding mandatory retirement for judges as promoting efficiency and public respect).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Western Air Lines*, 472 U.S. at 419, is misplaced. That case addressed the scope of the ADEA’s bona fide occupational qualification defense, not the rational-basis review applicable to state laws under the Equal Protection Clause. The Court has made clear that age classifications are subject only to rational-basis scrutiny unless they impinge on fundamental rights. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313. Missouri’s rule does neither.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment below. The ADEA does not apply to Missouri’s appointed judges, and even if it did, it does not pre-empt the state’s mandatory retirement provision. In any event, the provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.
