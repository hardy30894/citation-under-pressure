**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment prohibits the government from using an uncounseled conviction as an essential element of a subsequent criminal offense.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” This right exists because “the guiding hand of counsel” is essential to protect the defendant’s right to a fair trial. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 345 (1963). The right attaches whenever a defendant is sentenced to a term of imprisonment. *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373 (1979).

The government concedes that Bryant’s tribal court convictions would have been unconstitutional had they been obtained in federal or state court, because he was imprisoned without counsel. Yet the government asks this Court to permit those constitutionally infirm convictions to serve as the *sine qua non* of federal criminal liability under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Sixth Amendment does not permit such a result. A conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment is unreliable, and the government may not bootstrap that unreliability into a new criminal conviction. *See United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1395-96 (9th Cir. 1989).

**II. *Nichols v. United States* does not authorize the use of uncounseled convictions at the guilt phase.**

The government relies on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), which permitted the use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott*, to enhance a subsequent sentence. But *Nichols* does not control here for three reasons.

*First*, *Nichols* was a sentencing case. The Court stressed that the prior conviction was used solely to “enhanc[e]” the defendant’s sentence, not to establish an element of an offense. *Id.* at 746-48. The distinction is constitutionally dispositive. At sentencing, the government need prove facts only by a preponderance of the evidence, and the sentencing judge may disregard unreliable information. *Id.* at 748. At the guilt phase, however, the government must prove “every fact necessary” to constitute the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The reliability accommodations present in *Nichols* are entirely absent here.

*Second*, the Court in *Nichols* repeatedly emphasized that the predicate conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment because the defendant was not imprisoned. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47. Here, Bryant *was* imprisoned on each tribal conviction. Under *Scott*, those convictions would be constitutionally invalid if rendered by a federal or state court. *Scott*, 440 U.S. at 373. The Sixth Amendment’s protections cannot be evaded by the simple expedient of relocating the conviction to tribal court.

*Third*, *Nichols* expressly reaffirmed the continued authority of *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), which held that a prior conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment cannot be used to enhance punishment. *Id.* at 115. If an uncounseled conviction cannot enhance a sentence, it certainly cannot supply an element of a felony offense.

**III. The tribal court convictions are unreliable and may not serve as predicates for § 117(a).**

This Court has long recognized that prior convictions are constitutionally usable in subsequent proceedings only if they are reliable. *See United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 447-49 (1972); *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115. A conviction obtained without counsel, in violation of *Gideon*, is presumptively unreliable because “[t]he presence of counsel” is “the best test” of a reliable adjudication. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 345.

The government’s contrary argument proves too much. If an uncounseled tribal court conviction could supply a § 117(a) predicate, then the government could convict a defendant of a federal felony based solely on proceedings that would be void in any federal or state court. That result “would erode the integrity of the federal courts and the Sixth Amendment rights they enforce.” *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1395.

Moreover, *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), confirms that the infirmity lies in the imprisonment itself. There, the Court held that a suspended prison sentence imposed without counsel violates the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 662. *Shelton* thus underscores the fundamental principle: a term of imprisonment imposed without counsel is unconstitutional. Bryant was sentenced to imprisonment. His convictions are therefore constitutionally infirm, and the government cannot give them new life as felony predicates.

**IV. The government’s comity and policy arguments do not overcome the Sixth Amendment.**

The government invokes tribal sovereignty and the importance of § 117(a) to combat domestic violence. These concerns are legitimate, but they cannot override the Constitution. Congress may exercise broad authority over Indian tribes, *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004), but it may not authorize the federal government to convict a person based on proceedings that violate the Sixth Amendment.

Critically, the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, while not coextensive with the Sixth Amendment, reflects Congress’s understanding that tribal courts must provide meaningful procedural protections. Congress expanded tribal court authority under the Tribal Law and Order Act only with a commensurate right to counsel. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c). Nothing in ICRA suggests Congress intended tribal convictions to carry greater weight in federal court than they would carry at home.

Respect for tribal courts does not require federal courts to validate convictions that violate fundamental constitutional norms. To the contrary, treating tribal judgments with comity respects tribal sovereignty; importing unreliable convictions into federal felony prosecutions does not. *See Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997).

Finally, § 117(a) remains fully operational for the vast majority of tribal court convictions—those obtained with counsel or without a sentence of imprisonment. Only those convictions that would violate the Sixth Amendment in federal or state court are excluded. The statute’s purposes are thus served while the Constitution is honored.

**CONCLUSION**

Bryant’s prior tribal court convictions were obtained without counsel and resulted in imprisonment. They would violate the Sixth Amendment if rendered in federal or state court. They cannot supply the predicate offenses required for a felony conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
