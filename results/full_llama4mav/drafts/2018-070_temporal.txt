The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law. Instead, the OCSLA allows state law to apply only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the outer Continental Shelf "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with ... Federal laws." The Supreme Court has not directly addressed the issue presented here, but the Fifth Circuit has interpreted the OCSLA's choice of law provision in a manner that supports Petitioner's argument.

In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the Fifth Circuit held that state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf only when there is a "significant void or gap" in federal law. Id. at 1036. This interpretation is consistent with the language and purpose of the OCSLA, which was enacted to provide a comprehensive framework for the governance of the outer Continental Shelf.

The legislative history of the OCSLA also supports this interpretation. As noted in Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the OCSLA was enacted to address the jurisdictional dispute between the federal government and adjacent states over the outer Continental Shelf. The Supreme Court observed that Congress rejected the idea of extending state boundaries to the outer Continental Shelf and instead chose to adopt a framework that would provide a uniform body of law. Id. at 357-58.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), provides guidance on the assimilation of state law into federal law. Although Sharpnack involved the Assimilative Crimes Act, 18 U.S.C. § 13, the Court's reasoning is relevant here. The Court held that the assimilation of state law into federal law is permissible when there is a gap in federal law that needs to be filled. Id. at 293-94.

In this case, the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's provisions are not "deficient in the provisions necessary to furnish suitable remedies" for workers on the outer Continental Shelf, see 42 U.S.C. § 1988(a) (analogous statute discussed in Sharpnack). Therefore, there is no gap in federal law that needs to be filled by state law.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to the contrary is inconsistent with the language and purpose of the OCSLA. By allowing state law to apply whenever it pertains to the subject matter of the lawsuit and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law, the Ninth Circuit has effectively created a regime of dual regulation on the outer Continental Shelf. This outcome is not supported by the OCSLA or relevant case law.

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the OCSLA permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law.
