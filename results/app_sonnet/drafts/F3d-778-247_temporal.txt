# ARGUMENT

## I. The Warrant Affidavit Failed to Establish a Nexus Between the Alleged Crimes and Joubert's Parents' Home

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant rest on facts, not conclusions. In *Nathanson v. United States*, 290 U.S. 41 (1933), the Supreme Court held that a "mere affirmance of belief or suspicion" is insufficient to support a warrant; the affidavit must disclose the underlying circumstances from which probable cause may be judged. *Id.* at 47. Probable cause itself has long been defined as requiring "reasonable grounds" tied to the specific place to be searched, not merely to the suspect's general conduct. *Locke v. United States*, 7 Cranch (11 U.S.) 339, 348 (1813).

The affidavit here offered no facts connecting evidence of Joubert's alleged abuse to his parents' Manchester residence beyond the bare assertion that Joubert had recently moved there and that "persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of ... minors often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims." That generalized profile of offender behavior is precisely the sort of conclusory boilerplate condemned in *Nathanson*. The affiant supplied no averment that any specific item of evidence—photographs, videotapes, or computer files—had ever been present in that house, as opposed to Joubert's prior residences.

Probable cause must be assessed from the facts actually presented to the magistrate, not from inferences the police might personally have drawn. *Johnson v. United States*, 333 U.S. 10, 14 (1948) (probable cause determinations must be made "by a neutral and detached magistrate instead of by the officer engaged in the often competitive enterprise of ferreting out crime"). And where an affidavit rests on hearsay or secondhand information, the magistrate must be given the underlying circumstances supporting both the informant's reliability and the basis of his knowledge. *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 114 (1964); *Giordenello v. United States*, 357 U.S. 480, 486 (1958). SJ's account—that Joubert destroyed a computer tower and stacked components "inside his parents' home"—supplied, at best, evidence that discarded hardware might be found there. It supplied nothing establishing that photographs or videotapes of victims, the object of the search, would be located at that address. The warrant therefore authorized a search unsupported by the particularized nexus the Fourth Amendment demands.

## II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting SJ's Testimony of Uncharged Molestation from the 1980s

The common law has long distrusted evidence offered merely to show that a defendant has a criminal disposition. As the Supreme Court explained in *Boyd v. United States*, 142 U.S. 450, 458 (1892), proof of collateral offenses risks convicting a defendant "not of the offence charged ... but of being a bad man." That concern persists even where evidence is nominally offered for a permissible purpose. In *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 476 (1948), the Court cautioned that character and propensity evidence carries a "tendency to prejudice" that can lead a jury toward "over-persuasion," and that courts must guard against its indiscriminate admission.

SJ's testimony concerned conduct alleged to have occurred in the mid-1980s—more than two decades before the charged conduct involving KC. Even assuming an exception exists in sexual-offense cases for evidence bearing on a defendant's disposition toward a class of victims, *see Lovely v. United States*, 169 F.2d 386, 390 (4th Cir. 1948), that exception cannot be stretched to embrace episodes so remote in time that their probative value is marginal at best. The remoteness here diminishes any legitimate inferential value while retaining the full prejudicial force *Michelson* warned against. The district court's failure to weigh that imbalance was an abuse of discretion.

## III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert's Purely Local Conduct

Congress's commerce power, though broad, is not unlimited. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937), sustained regulation only of activity having a "close and substantial relation to interstate commerce." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 118 (1941), likewise upheld regulation of production for interstate shipment—not purely local conduct with no connection to any market. Even *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111, 125 (1942), which permitted aggregation of trivial local effects, did so only because the regulated activity was economic in character and demonstrably affected the interstate wheat market.

Joubert's production and possession of a single videotape, made and kept entirely within New Hampshire and never transported, sold, or transmitted across state lines, bears no resemblance to the economic activities regulated in *Darby* or *Wickard*, nor to the commercial enterprises at issue in *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241, 258 (1964). The government's reliance on the interstate manufacture of the videotape or camera cannot substitute for a substantial relation between Joubert's own conduct and interstate commerce.

## IV. The 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

Sentencing discretion, while broad, is not boundless. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949), recognized wide latitude in sentencing but did not immunize sentences from review for excessiveness. Punishment must remain proportionate to the offense, *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910) (condemning sentences "cruel and unusual" in their disproportion), and must be tested against evolving norms of decency, *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). A forty-year sentence for a first-time federal offender, imposed substantially above what comparable defendants receive, exceeds what proportionality principles permit and should be vacated.
