**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), provided the conviction is valid under the applicable constitutional standards governing tribal courts. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit, which held that such a conviction violates the Sixth Amendment when used to establish an essential element of a federal felony offense, is contrary to settled precedent and misapplies the principles of due process and comity that govern federal recognition of tribal court judgments.

The foundation of the petitioner’s position rests on the well-established principle that the Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, does not apply to Indian tribes. *United States v. Wheeler*, 389 U.S. 288, 295 (1967), held that “the Constitution does not apply to Indian tribes in the same manner as it applies to the States.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978), where the Court emphasized that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” Thus, a tribal court proceeding, even one resulting in a sentence of imprisonment, is not subject to the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel. The absence of a right to appointed counsel in tribal court does not render the conviction “unreliable” or “infirm” under federal constitutional law, because the constitutional standard governing tribal courts is not the Sixth Amendment, but the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302, which provides a right to counsel only in certain circumstances and does not require it in all cases.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. *Ant* was decided before the Supreme Court’s definitive pronouncement in *Wheeler* and predates the full development of the doctrine of tribal sovereignty. More critically, *Ant* was based on a flawed understanding of the constitutional status of tribal courts. It treated the Sixth Amendment as a universal standard applicable to all criminal proceedings, regardless of jurisdiction. This approach is inconsistent with *Wheeler*, which recognized that tribal courts are “sovereign” entities with the power to “punish tribal offenders” without being bound by the full panoply of constitutional protections applicable in federal or state courts. 389 U.S. at 323–24. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to impose a Sixth Amendment-like standard on tribal court proceedings—requiring that a tribal court provide a right to counsel “coextensive” with that guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment—amounts to judicial paternalism and directly contravenes the principle of tribal self-governance.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts must respect the judgments of tribal courts, even when those judgments are rendered under procedures that differ from those in federal or state courts. In *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997), the court observed that “comity does not require that a tribe utilize judicial procedures identical to those used in the United States Courts.” This principle was rooted in *Hilton v. Guyot*, 159 U.S. 113, 205 (1895), where the Court held that foreign judgments are entitled to full faith and credit unless they violate fundamental principles of justice. The same principle applies to tribal court judgments. As the Court stated in *Iowa Mutual Insurance Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987), “the federal courts must respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems.” To invalidate a tribal court conviction simply because it did not afford counsel under the Sixth Amendment is to disregard this principle of comity and to impose a standard that is not constitutionally required.

Moreover, the use of a prior conviction as a predicate offense under a recidivist statute does not implicate the same due process concerns as a conviction for the offense itself. The Supreme Court has consistently held that recidivist statutes “penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant.” *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (citing *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 747 (1994)). The punishment under § 117(a) is for the current domestic assault, not for the prior offenses. The prior convictions serve only to establish a pattern of conduct, not to prove guilt of the current offense. The burden of proof for the predicate offenses is not beyond a reasonable doubt, but rather a preponderance of the evidence, which is the standard applied in sentencing and other non-guilt phases. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), established that the beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard applies only to the elements of the offense charged. Since the prior tribal court convictions are not elements of the offense under § 117(a), but rather predicate facts, the lower standard of proof is constitutionally sufficient.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split and undermines the effectiveness of federal efforts to combat domestic violence on tribal lands. Congress enacted § 117(a) to address the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country, where tribal courts often lack the resources to prosecute repeat offenders. The statute’s purpose is to empower federal authorities to intervene when tribal courts have already found a defendant guilty of prior domestic violence offenses. To invalidate such convictions on Sixth Amendment grounds—when the tribal courts themselves are not bound by that amendment—is to frustrate Congress’s intent and to leave victims without protection.

In sum, the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The conviction is valid under the ICRA and the principles of tribal sovereignty. Federal courts must respect tribal court judgments, even when they differ from federal procedures. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
