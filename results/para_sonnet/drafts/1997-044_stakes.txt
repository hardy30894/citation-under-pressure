# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 523(a)(2)(A)'S TEXT EXCEPTS FROM DISCHARGE ALL LIABILITY ARISING FROM FRAUD, NOT MERELY THE VALUE FRAUDULENTLY OBTAINED

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge "any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). Petitioner asks this Court to read "to the extent obtained by" as limiting nondischargeability to the specific dollar amount the debtor fraudulently procured, thereby discharging treble damages, attorney's fees, and costs. That reading cannot be squared with the statutory term Congress chose to except: "debt."

The Bankruptcy Code defines "debt" broadly as "liability on a claim," 11 U.S.C. § 101(12), and "claim" is defined broader still as any "right to payment, whether or not such right is reduced to judgment, liquidated, unliquidated, fixed, contingent, matured, unmatured, disputed, undisputed, legal, equitable, secured, or unsecured." 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). This Court has repeatedly recognized that Congress intended the "broadest available definition" of claim. *Johnson v. Home State Bank*, 501 U.S. 78, 83 (1991) (quoting H.R. Rep. No. 95-595, p. 309 (1977)). Once a debt is found to be "for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by" fraud, the entire debt—that is, the entire liability arising from the fraudulent conduct—falls outside discharge. The phrase "to the extent obtained by" modifies the type of debt that is excepted (fraud-based debts, as opposed to other debts), not the *quantum* of damages recoverable within that debt.

This construction accords with the traditional rule, applicable when a statute uses a common-law term without further elaboration, that Congress intends to incorporate the established meaning of that term. *See Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 288 (1991) (interpreting § 523(a)(2)'s "actual fraud" language against the background of "traditional" doctrines). At common law, a victim of fraud was entitled to recover consequential damages proximately caused by the fraud, not merely the value of the specific money or property fraudulently obtained. Nothing in the text of § 523(a)(2)(A) signals a departure from that background rule.

## II. THE THIRD CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT PETITIONER'S ENTIRE LIABILITY—INCLUDING TREBLE DAMAGES, FEES, AND COSTS—IS NONDISCHARGEABLE

The Third Circuit properly rejected the reasoning of *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), which held that only the compensatory portion of a fraud judgment is nondischargeable because punitive damages are not themselves "obtained by" fraud. Pet. App. at [cite]. Instead, the court below adopted the more persuasive analysis of the Eleventh Circuit in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which "focused on 'debt' and whether that term was sufficiently broad to include a judgement requiring the payment of both compensatory and punitive damages," and "concluded that it is." Pet. App. at [cite] (quoting *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 679).

This conclusion follows directly from *Grogan*, where this Court held that § 523(a)(2)(A)'s fraud exception should be construed against the backdrop of the "fresh start" policy's limits. As *Grogan* explained, the fresh start is reserved for the "honest but unfortunate debtor," not one who has defrauded his creditors. 498 U.S. at 286–287. A debtor who commits fraud has, by definition, forfeited the benefit of discharge as to the debt his fraud created—including all damages that liability comprises under applicable law.

The Third Circuit's decision below is also consistent with the practical reality that fraud liability frequently includes elements beyond the bare amount taken. Here, the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages, attorney's fees, and costs for the precise fraudulent conduct at issue. N.J. Stat. Ann. § 56:8-19. As the court below observed, this is not a case involving a discretionary award of punitive damages layered atop a fraud judgment; rather, "the non-compensatory aspect of the award in this case was codified and therefore entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations to the plaintiffs." Pet. App. at [cite]. The treble damages thus were part and parcel of "the debt he incurred through his conduct, rather than . . . punishment." *Id.*

## III. PETITIONER'S FRAUD SATISFIES EVERY ELEMENT OF § 523(a)(2)(A), AND THE ENTIRE RESULTING DEBT IS THEREFORE NONDISCHARGEABLE

The bankruptcy court found, and the Third Circuit affirmed, that Cohen committed actual fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A). Cohen's silence coupled with his act of charging unlawful rents constituted an implied misrepresentation that the rents complied with the Hoboken ordinance. Pet. App. at [cite]. Applying this Court's preponderance-of-the-evidence standard from *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 291 (1991), the bankruptcy court further found that Cohen acted with reckless indifference to the truth of that representation—a standard the Third Circuit has recognized satisfies both the knowledge and intent-to-deceive elements of fraud. Pet. App. at [cite] (citing *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118–1119 (3d Cir. 1995) ("the intent to deceive can be inferred from the totality of the circumstances, including the debtor's reckless disregard for the truth")).

Once fraud is established, the resulting "debt"—the entire liability flowing from that fraud under New Jersey law, including trebled damages, fees, and costs—is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). To hold otherwise would permit fraudfeasors to use the bankruptcy laws as a shield against the very remedial and deterrent measures state legislatures have enacted to redress fraud. Nothing in the text, structure, or purpose of § 523(a)(2)(A) supports so anomalous a result.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
