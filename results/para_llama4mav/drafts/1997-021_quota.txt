The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA grants the NLRB the primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy, and its decisions are entitled to deference. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). However, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees is too strict and renders employer polls useless. Three courts of appeals have rejected this standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice for an employer to conduct a poll. See Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1297 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 866-67 (6th Cir. 1982); A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1144, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981).

The NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer should be allowed to poll employees if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is not sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. Mingtree Restaurant, 736 F.2d at 1299; Thomas Indus., 687 F.2d at 867; A.W. Thompson, 651 F.2d at 1145.

In this case, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) and the NLRB ignored or discounted significant evidence of employee dissatisfaction with the union, including statements from multiple employees. See Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988); Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). The NLRB's decision was arbitrary and capricious, as it failed to consider relevant evidence and relied on an overly strict standard.

The Court should defer to the reasoning of the Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits, which have adopted a more nuanced approach to employer polling. Under this approach, an employer may poll employees if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support and complies with procedural safeguards. Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967). The NLRB's decision should be reversed, as it is not supported by substantial evidence and is inconsistent with the NLRA.

Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978), establishes that the NLRB's decisions are entitled to deference, but this deference is not unlimited. The Court should review the NLRB's decision to ensure that it is rational and consistent with the NLRA. In this case, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence, and the Court should reverse it. Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (5th Cir. 1978), and Westbrook Bowl, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000, demonstrate that the NLRB has inconsistently applied its standard, further supporting reversal. 

By adopting a more reasonable standard for employer polling, the Court can ensure that employers are able to test employee sentiment in a fair and reliable manner, consistent with the NLRA's goals.
