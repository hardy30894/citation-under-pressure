# ARGUMENT

## THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN WHERE REMOVAL IS NOT REASONABLY FORESEEABLE

### A. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires a Limiting Construction of § 1231(a)(6)

Section 1231(a)(6) authorizes the Attorney General to detain an alien "beyond the removal period," but nowhere states that such detention may continue indefinitely, potentially for life, without any prospect of removal. Where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, this Court has long held that the construction avoiding serious constitutional doubt must be adopted. That interpretive principle applies with full force here.

This Court's decision in *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), is directly on point. There the Government argued that a companion provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(d), authorized the Attorney General to demand of a detained alien "any information" he deemed relevant, without limitation. This Court rejected that reading, construing the statute instead to authorize inquiry only into matters "reasonably calculated to keep the Attorney General advised regarding the continued availability for departure of aliens whose deportation is overdue." *Witkovich*, 353 U.S. at 202. The Court reasoned that a broader construction "would raise substantial constitutional questions," and it accordingly narrowed the statute's reach rather than confront those questions directly. The same approach controls the interpretation of § 1231(a)(6): where the Government's construction of indefinite detention authority raises serious constitutional concerns, this Court should construe the statute to avoid them by reading in an implicit limitation tied to the statute's purpose—assuring the alien's presence at removal.

That purpose itself confirms the limitation. Detention under § 1231(a)(6) is not an end in itself; it exists only to make removal possible. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention no longer serves the statutory purpose and is not authorized by the text.

### B. Indefinite Civil Detention of a Removable Alien Raises Serious Due Process Concerns That the Statute Should Be Read to Avoid

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause applies to "all 'persons' within the United States," and this protection extends to aliens even where their continued presence is unlawful. This Court's precedents distinguish sharply between the detention of aliens who have never effected entry into the United States and aliens, like petitioner, who were lawfully admitted and later ordered removed.

*Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), and its companion case, *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193 (1958), illustrate this distinction. Both cases concerned aliens paroled into the country pending exclusion proceedings; this Court held that such aliens' parole "did not alter her status as an excluded alien or otherwise bring her 'within the United States'" for certain statutory purposes. *Leng May Ma*, 357 U.S. at 187. The excludable alien at the border occupies a fundamentally different constitutional position from the alien who has been admitted and resides within the United States. Petitioner here was lawfully admitted as a resident alien decades ago; his position is not that of an alien seeking initial entry.

Civil detention untethered to any legitimate, non-punitive purpose is constitutionally suspect. In *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), this Court sustained detention without bail of alien Communist Party members pending deportation, but did so only upon a record showing individualized risk—the Court there examined whether "there had been no abuse of administrative discretion in refusing bail," 342 U.S. at 526, and whether the alien's own conduct and affiliations justified continued custody pending a deportation that could actually be effectuated. *Carlson* thus confirms that detention pending removal is tolerated only as ancillary to an achievable removal, not as an open-ended incapacitation.

Indeed, this Court has recognized that deportation itself, though a civil rather than criminal sanction, is a "drastic measure" that must be administered with attention to its severe consequences for the individual. *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) (deportation statutes should not be given "the added script of severity" beyond what Congress plainly intended). If courts must construe deportation statutes narrowly against harsh, unintended consequences for the alien, they must apply the same caution to detention statutes whose unconstrained reading would produce potentially permanent civil confinement—a result far more severe than deportation itself.

Even the classic remedy against unlawful executive detention—habeas corpus—confirms the seriousness of the liberty interest at stake. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), this Court held that Congress could not subject aliens to imprisonment amounting to punishment without the protections of the criminal process, explaining that to detain in a manner amounting to punishment "could not be inflicted except by judicial sentence." 163 U.S. at 237. Although *Wong Wing* addressed a different detention scheme, its core principle—that the political branches' plenary power over immigration does not extend to open-ended, punitive-like confinement without judicial check—supports a limiting construction here.

### C. Where Removal Is Not Reasonably Foreseeable, Detention No Longer Serves Any Statutory Purpose

The removal-period framework of § 1231, as this Court has recognized in construing its predecessor provisions, contemplates detention as incidental to an active, ongoing removal process. *See Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685 (1957) (construing predecessor deportation provisions strictly according to their terms). Where, as with petitioner, the alien's country of citizenship has disclaimed him and no other nation will accept him, continued detention no longer serves to "assure the alien's presence at the moment of removal"—it becomes pure preventive detention unmoored from any deportation that will actually occur.

This Court should therefore hold that § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, and should affirm the judgment below.
