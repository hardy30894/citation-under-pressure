The petitioners argue that their constitutional challenge to the school policy is not moot because they seek nominal damages. However, this Court has long held that a case becomes moot when the challenged conduct ceases and there is no reasonable expectation that it will recur. See United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629, 632-33 (1953). The school's revision of its "Freedom of Expression Policy" and removal of the challenged portion of its "Student Code of Conduct" render the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot.

The petitioners' request for nominal damages does not save their otherwise moot claims. As this Court held in Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), a case becomes moot when the plaintiff's personal stake in the outcome is eliminated, even if the plaintiff seeks nominal damages. The Court has consistently applied this principle in subsequent cases. See, e.g., Barker Painting Co. v. Local No. 734, 281 U.S. 462, 464 (1930); Atherton Mills v. Johnston, 259 U.S. 13, 15-16 (1922).

The petitioners' reliance on cases involving ongoing harm or continuing violations is misplaced. See, e.g., Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911). In this case, the school's revised policies have eliminated the challenged conduct, and there is no reasonable expectation that it will recur.

The petitioners also argue that the district court erred in denying them leave to amend their complaint. However, the district court's decision was proper under the circumstances. See, e.g., United States v. Newbury Mfg. Co., 123 F.2d 453, 454 (1st Cir. 1941); Fed. R. Civ. P. 15(a). The petitioners failed to properly seek leave to amend, and the district court was not obligated to grant them leave to amend sua sponte. See, e.g., Peck v. Foote, 71 F.2d 596, 597 (5th Cir. 1934).

Furthermore, the petitioners' proposed amendment would not have cured the mootness issue. As this Court held in St. Pierre v. United States, 319 U.S. 41, 42-43 (1943), a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. The petitioners' request for compensatory damages would not have changed the fact that their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot.

In addition, the Court has held that a plaintiff's failure to allege actual injury precludes recovery of compensatory damages. See, e.g., Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137, 163-64 (1803); see also Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. v. Wallace, 288 U.S. 249, 262-63 (1933). The petitioners' complaint failed to allege actual injury, and their request for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims.

The district court's dismissal of the petitioners' complaint as moot was proper. The school's revision of its policies rendered the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, and their request for nominal damages is insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.

In Berry v. Davis, 242 U.S. 468 (1917), this Court held that a case is moot when the plaintiff has failed to demonstrate a personal stake in the outcome. Similarly, in California v. San Pablo & Tulare R. Co., 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the Court held that a case becomes moot when the challenged conduct ceases and there is no reasonable expectation that it will recur. These precedents support the conclusion that the petitioners' claims are moot.

The Court should affirm the judgment of the court of appeals, as the district court properly applied the relevant legal principles to the facts of this case.
