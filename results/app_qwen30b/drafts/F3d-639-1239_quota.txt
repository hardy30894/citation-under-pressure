**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mr. Lucero’s class-action complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction on the ground of mootness, despite the defendant’s Rule 68 offer of judgment. The court’s conclusion—that a named plaintiff’s individual claim is automatically moot upon satisfaction of his demand, regardless of pending class certification efforts—contradicts settled precedent recognizing that a class action is not merely a vehicle for individual relief but a mechanism for resolving issues of common concern on behalf of a broader group. The mere offer of judgment to a named plaintiff, without a corresponding class certification, does not extinguish the justiciable controversy when the plaintiff continues to seek class-wide relief and the class certification motion is timely filed. This case presents a clear instance where the federal courts must preserve the integrity of the class action device by refusing to allow defendants to circumvent the class certification process through tactical offers of judgment.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a suit brought as a class action is not merely a collection of individual claims, but a single action in which the claims of the named plaintiffs are representative of those of the class.” *Sosna v. Iowa*, 419 U.S. 393, 402, 95 S.Ct. 553, 42 L.Ed.2d 532 (1975). In *Sosna*, the Court rejected the notion that a named plaintiff’s individual claim could be deemed moot simply because the defendant had satisfied the plaintiff’s individual demand, where the plaintiff continued to seek class certification and the class action remained viable. The Court emphasized that “the class action device is designed to permit a single lawsuit to resolve issues affecting a large number of persons,” and that “the individual claim of the named plaintiff is not the sole focus of the litigation.” Id. at 402–403, 95 S.Ct. 553. Here, Lucero’s individual claim was not the end goal; rather, it was a means to achieve class-wide relief under the FDCPA and New Mexico law. The district court’s failure to recognize this distinction renders its dismissal of the complaint legally unsound.

This principle was reaffirmed in *U.S. Parole Commission v. Geraghty*, 445 U.S. 388, 401, 100 S.Ct. 1202, 63 L.Ed.2d 479 (1980), where the Court held that “a case is not moot merely because the named plaintiff’s individual claim has been satisfied, if the plaintiff continues to have a personal stake in the outcome of the litigation and the class action remains viable.” The Court further explained that “the existence of a live controversy is not destroyed by the fact that the named plaintiff’s individual claim has been satisfied, so long as the plaintiff continues to seek relief on behalf of a class.” Id. at 401, 100 S.Ct. 1202. Lucero’s continued pursuit of class certification—filed on February 22, 2010, well within the discovery schedule set by the court—demonstrates his ongoing personal stake in the litigation. The offer of judgment did not extinguish that stake, nor did it eliminate the need for judicial resolution of the class-wide issues.

The Tenth Circuit has previously acknowledged this principle. In *Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins.*, 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir.2009), the court stated that “a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified.” However, *Clark* did not address the situation where a class certification motion is timely filed and the class action remains pending. The court’s general statement must be read in context: it applies only when no class certification motion has been filed or when the motion is untimely. Here, Lucero filed his motion for class certification within the court-ordered discovery schedule, and the court had already set a hearing date. The mere existence of a Rule 68 offer does not negate the viability of the class action when the plaintiff is actively pursuing certification.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has cautioned against allowing defendants to “buy off” class actions through early offers of judgment. In *Sosna*, the Court observed that “the availability of a judgment in the amount of the plaintiff’s individual claim does not necessarily moot the case when the plaintiff seeks to represent a class.” 419 U.S. at 403, 95 S.Ct. 553. The Court emphasized that “the class action device is not a mere procedural convenience but a substantive right to collective redress.” Id. at 403, 95 S.Ct. 553. To allow a defendant to terminate a class action by offering full individual relief before certification would undermine the very purpose of the class action mechanism.

This Court has also recognized that a class certification motion relates back to the filing of the complaint for purposes of jurisdiction. In *Weiss v. Regal Collections*, 385 F.3d 337, 343 (3d Cir.2004), the court held that “a timely motion for class certification, filed within the discovery period, relates back to the filing of the complaint and preserves subject-matter jurisdiction.” The Third Circuit further noted that “the filing of a class certification motion is sufficient to maintain jurisdiction even after the named plaintiff’s individual claim has been satisfied.” Id. at 344. The Tenth Circuit has not rejected this principle, and there is no basis to do so here.

Finally, Rule 23 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure contemplates that class certification may be sought at any time before final judgment. The rule does not require certification before a defendant may make an offer of judgment. Indeed, the offer of judgment under Rule 68 is not a substitute for class certification. As the Supreme Court has stated, “the purpose of the class action is to allow a single lawsuit to resolve issues of common concern.” *Sosna*, 419 U.S. at 402, 95 S.Ct. 553. To allow a defendant to defeat that purpose by offering full individual relief before certification would be to permit procedural manipulation that undermines the federal judiciary’s ability to administer justice efficiently and fairly.
