**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MEANING OF “SHIELDING” BECAUSE THE DEFINITION PROVIDED WAS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE AND CONTRARY TO THE STATUTORY TEXT, THEREBY VIOLATING YE’S DUE PROCESS RIGHTS UNDER THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be “deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law.” *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922). A criminal defendant’s right to due process includes the right to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation, which requires that criminal statutes be sufficiently clear to provide fair notice of what conduct is prohibited. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). When a court supplies a jury instruction on a statutory term, that instruction must not render the statute unconstitutionally vague or expand its scope beyond the plain meaning of the text. *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 292 (2008).

Here, the district court instructed the jury that “shielding” means “to protect or conceal from detection, to prevent the government from discovering or apprehending.” App. 124. This definition, while seemingly commonsensical, is fatally flawed because it imports a non-statutory, expansive standard that conflates “shielding” with mere concealment or failure to report—conduct that Congress explicitly criminalized under different subsections of § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). The statute itself defines the offense as “concealing, harboring, or shielding from detection” persons known to be illegal aliens. 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). The use of the disjunctive “or” indicates that each term must be understood as distinct and not interchangeable. *United States v. Brown*, 557 F.3d 705, 710 (7th Cir. 2009) (“When Congress uses the word ‘or’ in a statute, it generally intends to create separate and distinct offenses.”).

The Seventh Circuit has previously recognized that “shielding” requires more than passive concealment—it implies an affirmative act designed to obstruct or impede governmental detection. *United States v. Mendoza*, 487 F.3d 1055, 1060 (7th Cir. 2007). In *Mendoza*, we held that shielding involves “an active effort to prevent the government from discovering the presence of an illegal alien,” such as by providing false documents, creating a false paper trail, or otherwise interfering with official investigations. Id. The district court’s instruction, however, collapsed the distinction between “shielding” and “concealing,” effectively equating the two. This is not only inconsistent with our precedent but also renders the term “shielding” meaningless in practice, as it becomes indistinguishable from the broader offense of concealment.

Moreover, the instruction as given is unconstitutionally vague. By defining “shielding” as “to protect or conceal from detection,” the court invited the jury to convict based on conduct that may have been merely negligent or even lawful—such as failing to file I-9 forms or not maintaining payroll records. Such conduct is not “shielding” under the statute, nor is it the kind of affirmative, obstructive act Congress intended to criminalize. *See United States v. Soto*, 527 F.3d 657, 662 (7th Cir. 2008) (holding that “shielding” requires more than mere failure to report or document). The instruction thus failed to provide fair notice of the conduct proscribed and allowed for arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement, violating due process.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Mendoza* and *United States v. Soto* is misplaced. In those cases, we upheld convictions where defendants had actively provided false documents, arranged for fake identification, or instructed employees to lie to investigators—conduct that clearly constitutes an affirmative effort to shield. Here, the district court’s instruction, by contrast, allowed the jury to convict based on passive inaction or failure to comply with administrative requirements, which are not shielded under the statute. The instruction, therefore, was not merely erroneous—it was constitutionally defective.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct that it prohibits.” *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. at 292. The instruction given here failed that test. It did not reflect the statutory text, ignored our precedent, and risked convicting Ye for conduct that is not “shielding” under the law. The error was not harmless. It directly influenced the jury’s understanding of the charged offense and likely contributed to the conviction on Count One.

For these reasons, the district court’s supplemental instruction on “shielding” was legally erroneous and constitutionally infirm. The conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed.
