The Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches every employment performed in a workplace in a State. Its text contains no exception for vessels, inspected or otherwise. Preemption under section 4(b)(1) arises only when another federal agency has actually exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting the precise working conditions at issue. Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp., 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (“the exercise of federal supremacy is not lightly to be presumed”). Where the asserted displacing authority has not been exercised, concurrent federal regulation remains permissible. Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941).

The Coast Guard’s statutory mandate, 14 U.S.C. § 2, authorizes regulations promoting safety of life and property on navigable waters. That grant, however, has never been construed to occupy the entire field of occupational safety for every person aboard every vessel. Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605, 610-611 (1926) (federal safety statute displaces state law only to the extent it “cover[s] the subject”). For uninspected vessels the Coast Guard’s regulations have historically addressed discrete equipment requirements, not comprehensive workplace standards. Pennsylvania R. Co. v. Public Service Comm’n, 250 U.S. 566, 569 (1919) (limited federal rules do not exclude supplementary regulation of related but unregulated matters).

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over the working conditions that OSHA cited, section 4(b)(1) does not bar OSHA jurisdiction. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion rests on an unwarranted extension of preemptive effect beyond the actual exercise of regulatory power recognized in the cases above. OSHA therefore possessed authority to issue the citations.
