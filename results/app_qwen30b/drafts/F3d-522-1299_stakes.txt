**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the ’658 and ’400 patents are invalid as anticipated under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) because it improperly denied PowerOasis the benefit of the filing date of its Original Application—February 6, 1997—on the ground that the Original Application failed to provide a written description of the claimed invention. That conclusion rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the requirements of 35 U.S.C. § 112 and the doctrine of priority under § 120. The district court’s construction of the “customer interface” term, which it based solely on new matter added in the 2000 CIP Application, further distorted the scope of the claims and led to an erroneous anticipation analysis. This Court should reverse.

**I. The Original Application Provided a Written Description of the Invention Claimed in the Asserted Patents**

Under 35 U.S.C. § 112, a patent applicant must provide a written description of the invention sufficient to enable one skilled in the art to make and use the claimed invention. *See* In re Wertheim, 541 F.2d 257, 262 (C.C.P.A. 1976) (“The written description requirement is satisfied when the disclosure of the application shows that the inventor had possession of the claimed subject matter at the time of filing.”). The requirement is not satisfied by mere speculation or a general idea; rather, the specification must demonstrate that the inventor possessed the claimed invention at the time of filing. *See* Vas-Cath, Inc. v. Mahurkar, 935 F.2d 1555, 1563 (Fed. Cir. 1991) (“The written description requirement ensures that the inventor had possession of the claimed invention at the time of filing.”).

Here, the Original Application, filed on February 6, 1997, described a system for vending telecommunications access via a networked device that enables a user to connect a portable computing device to a telecommunications channel. The specification explicitly disclosed a “vending machine” that includes a control unit, a payment mechanism, a telecommunications access circuit, and a connector for linking to a customer’s device. *See* ’643 patent, col. 3, ll. 15–25; col. 4, ll. 10–20. Crucially, the specification described the system as enabling a user to “plug in a portable computer” and gain access to a network, with the system automatically terminating access upon completion of the transaction. *Id.*, col. 4, ll. 30–35. This disclosure clearly encompasses the concept of a “customer interface” located on the customer’s laptop, as the system is designed to interact with the user’s device to initiate and terminate the transaction.

The district court erred in concluding that the Original Application failed to describe the invention because it did not use the exact phrase “customer interface” or explicitly state that the interface could be on the customer’s laptop. *See* PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc., No. 05-ev-42-PB, 2007 WL 962937, at *2 (D.N.H. Mar. 30, 2007). The law does not require that every claim term be used in the original application. *See* In re Wertheim, 541 F.2d at 262. Rather, the inquiry is whether the specification, as a whole, demonstrates that the inventor possessed the claimed invention. *See* Ariad Pharms., Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co., 598 F.3d 1336, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (“The written description requirement is not satisfied by a mere statement of a result or a general idea.”). The Original Application’s disclosure of a system that enables a user to connect a portable computer to a network and automatically control access—without requiring the user to manually intervene—demonstrates possession of the claimed invention, including the concept of a customer interface on the user’s device.

**II. The District Court’s Claim Construction Was Based on New Matter and Misapplied the Doctrine of Priority**

The district court’s claim construction of “customer interface” as encompassing an interface on the customer’s laptop was based entirely on language added in the 2000 CIP Application, which the court described as “substantial new matter.” *See* 2007 WL 962937, at *2. This was error. Under 35 U.S.C. § 120, a continuation-in-part application may claim the benefit of the filing date of an earlier application only for subject matter disclosed in the earlier application. *See* In re Berg, 140 F.3d 1428, 1431 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (“A continuation-in-part may claim the benefit of the filing date of the parent application only for the subject matter disclosed in the parent.”). However, the doctrine of priority does not depend on the claim construction adopted in the later application. *See* In re NTP, Inc., 654 F.3d 1268, 1277 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (“The scope of the claims in a continuation-in-part is not determinative of the scope of the earlier application’s disclosure.”).

The district court’s construction of “customer interface” as including a laptop interface—based on new matter in the 2000 CIP Application—cannot be used to limit the scope of the Original Application’s disclosure. *See* In re Oetiker, 977 F.2d 1443, 1445 (Fed. Cir. 1992) (“The written description requirement is assessed at the time of filing, not at the time of claim construction.”). The Original Application disclosed a system that enabled a user to connect a portable computer to a network and automatically control access. That disclosure, read in context, necessarily includes the concept of a customer interface on the user’s device.
