**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Population Reduction Order Is Lawful Under the PLRA Because It Is the Least Restrictive Means Necessary to Remedy Ongoing Violations of the Eighth Amendment.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) governs prospective relief in prison litigation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). It permits such relief only if it is “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” *Id.* § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court correctly applied this standard and found that reducing California’s prison population to 137.5% of design capacity was the least restrictive means necessary to remedy ongoing Eighth Amendment violations. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 523 (2011) (“Plata”).

The PLRA’s “least intrusive means” requirement is not a novel or heightened standard. Rather, it codifies the traditional equitable principle that “[t]he scope of the remedy is determined by the nature and extent of the constitutional violation.” *Swarthout v. Cooke*, 562 U.S. 216, 219 (2011) (quoting *Pell v. Procunier*, 417 U.S. 817, 828 (1974)). The Supreme Court has long recognized that overcrowding can itself constitute cruel and unusual punishment when it leads to systemic deficiencies in medical and mental health care. *Id.* at 220 (citing *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 349 (1981)). Here, the three-judge court found that chronic overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions, including inadequate medical and mental health care. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 510.

The PLRA does not bar population reduction as a remedy. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has held that population caps are a permissible form of injunctive relief under the PLRA when they are the least restrictive means necessary to remedy constitutional violations. *Id.* at 523. The Court rejected California’s argument that the PLRA’s “narrowly drawn” and “least intrusive means” provisions precluded population reduction, explaining that the statute “does not categorically prohibit” such relief. *Id.* at 522-23.

The three-judge court’s order satisfies the PLRA’s requirements. It is narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found by the court—systemic deficiencies in medical and mental health care caused by overcrowding. *Id.* at 510. The court considered less restrictive alternatives, including increased funding and staffing, but found that these measures were insufficient to remedy the violations. *Id.* at 523. The Supreme Court affirmed this conclusion, holding that the order was “the least intrusive means necessary to remedy the violation of the federal right.” *Id.*

**II. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Prospective Relief Provisions.**

The PLRA imposes additional limitations on prospective relief in prison litigation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A)-(C). California argues that the three-judge court’s order violates these provisions, but its arguments are unavailing.

First, California contends that the order violates the PLRA’s “termination” provision, which requires that prospective relief terminate after ten years unless the court makes certain findings. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(2). The Supreme Court rejected this argument in *Plata*, holding that the three-judge court’s order was not subject to the PLRA’s ten-year termination provision because it was issued under a different statute, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A). *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 524-25. The Court explained that the PLRA’s termination provision applies only to orders issued under § 3626(a)(1)(A), not to those issued under § 3626(a)(3)(A). *Id.* at 525.

Second, California argues that the order violates the PLRA’s “exhaustion” requirement, which mandates that prisoners exhaust administrative remedies before seeking relief in federal court. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The Supreme Court rejected this argument as well, holding that the exhaustion requirement does not apply to class actions challenging systemic conditions of confinement. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 525-26 (citing *Porter v. Nussle*, 534 U.S. 516, 524 (2002)).

Finally, California contends that the order violates the PLRA’s “cap” on attorneys’ fees, which limits fees to 150% of the judgment in certain cases. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(2)(C). The Supreme Court rejected this argument, holding that the fee cap does not apply to orders issued under § 3626(a)(3)(A). *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 526.

**III. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Is Consistent With Traditional Equitable Principles.**

California argues that the three-judge court’s order violates traditional equitable principles, but its arguments are unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have broad equitable powers to remedy constitutional violations in prison systems. *Swarthout*, 562 U.S. at 219 (citing *Pell*, 417 U.S. at 828). The three-judge court’s order is consistent with these principles.

First, California argues that the order violates the principle that injunctive relief must be “no broader than necessary” to remedy the violation. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 523 (quoting *Swarthout*, 562 U.S. at 219). The Supreme Court rejected this argument, holding that the order was narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found by the court. *Id.* at 523.

Second, California contends that the order violates the principle that injunctive relief must not “impose significant burdens on nonparties.” *Id.* at 523 (quoting *Swarthout*, 562 U.S. at 219). The Supreme Court rejected this argument as well, holding that the order did not impose significant burdens on nonparties. *Id.* at 523-24.

Finally, California argues that the order violates the principle that injunctive relief must not “displace state officials’ authority.” *Id.* at 524 (quoting *Swarthout*, 562 U.S. at 219). The Supreme Court rejected this argument, holding that the order did not displace state officials’ authority but instead required them to comply with constitutional mandates. *Id.* at 524.

**Conclusion**

The three-judge court’s population reduction order is lawful under the PLRA and consistent with traditional equitable principles. It is the least restrictive means necessary to remedy ongoing violations of the Eighth Amendment, and it does not violate the PLRA’s prospective relief provisions. The Supreme Court has affirmed these conclusions, and this Court should do the same.
