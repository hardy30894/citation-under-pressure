**ARGUMENT**

I. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT RIGHT TO COUNSEL IS A FUNDAMENTAL RIGHT ESSENTIAL TO A FAIR TRIAL.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that "[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence." This right has long been recognized as "fundamental" and "essential to a fair trial." *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342-44 (1963). The Supreme Court has held that "the Sixth Amendment withholds from federal courts, in all criminal proceedings, the power and authority to deprive an accused of his life or liberty unless he has or waives the assistance of counsel." *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938). This right ensures that a defendant receives the "guiding hand of counsel at every step in the proceedings against him." *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 69 (1932). Without counsel, the adversarial process cannot function reliably, and the accused stands "merely as a spectacle to be gazed upon" in a system designed to secure convictions. *Id.* at 68-69.

II. AN UNCOUNSELED CONVICTION IS INHERENTLY UNRELIABLE AND CANNOT SERVE AS THE BASIS FOR ENHANCED PUNISHMENT.

This Court has consistently held that an uncounseled conviction is tainted by unreliability and cannot be used to enhance the severity of a later offense. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), the Court considered whether a prior conviction obtained in violation of the defendant's right to counsel could be used to elevate a subsequent offense to a felony. The Court held that such a conviction was "void" and that its use to enhance punishment violated the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 114-15. The Court reasoned that "to use a conviction obtained in violation of the Sixth Amendment to enhance punishment for a subsequent offense" would be to "erode the principle" that a defendant is entitled to counsel at every critical stage. *Id.* at 115. The Court's holding in *Burgett* was premised on the understanding that a conviction obtained without counsel is so fundamentally unreliable that it cannot bear the weight of a subsequent prosecution.

In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), this Court made clear that the right to counsel is a bedrock principle of our criminal justice system. The Court observed that "reason and reflection require us to recognize that in our adversary system of criminal justice, any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him." *Id.* at 344. The Sixth Amendment's guarantee of counsel is not a mere formality; it is the "very premise" of a fair proceeding. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. at 68-69.

III. THE USE OF AN UNCOUNSELED TRIBAL COURT CONVICTION AS AN ESSENTIAL ELEMENT OF A FELONY OFFENSE UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) VIOLATES THE SIXTH AMENDMENT.

18 U.S.C. § 117(a) criminalizes domestic assault by any person who "has a final conviction on at least 2 separate prior occasions in Federal, State, or Indian tribal court proceedings" for offenses that would qualify as domestic violence offenses. The statute does not merely enhance a sentence; it creates a new felony offense whose very existence depends on the validity of the prior convictions. The Government's entire case against Bryant rests on his tribal court convictions, which were obtained without the assistance of counsel.

Under *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 114-15, an uncounseled conviction is "void" and cannot be used to support a subsequent criminal prosecution. This principle applies with equal force here. The Government argues that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings, and therefore the tribal convictions are valid. But this argument misses the point. The question is not whether the tribal court violated the Constitution when it convicted Bryant; the question is whether the federal government can rely on those convictions to secure a federal felony conviction. The Sixth Amendment governs the federal prosecution, and it does not permit the Government to bootstrap an unreliable, uncounseled conviction into an element of a federal crime.

The reliability concerns that animated *Burgett* and *Gideon* are squarely presented here. An uncounseled conviction is unreliable because the defendant lacked the assistance of counsel to test the prosecution's evidence, cross-examine witnesses, and present a defense. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344. That unreliability does not disappear because the conviction was obtained in a tribal court. As this Court has long recognized, the right to counsel is fundamental precisely because it protects against the risk of wrongful conviction. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 462-63; *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. at 68-69.

IV. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT'S USE OF TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS MUST SATISFY THE SIXTH AMENDMENT.

The Government's contrary position rests on the notion that tribal court proceedings are governed not by the Sixth Amendment but by the Indian Civil Rights Act, which does not require appointed counsel. But this Court has made clear that the Bill of Rights applies to the federal government's actions, including its criminal prosecutions. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342-44. When the federal government chooses to rely on a tribal court conviction to prove an element of a federal offense, it must ensure that the conviction is sufficiently reliable to satisfy the Sixth Amendment's demands. See *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 114-15.

The Government cannot avoid this obligation by pointing to the sovereignty of tribal courts. While tribal courts are "unique" and exercise "inherent" sovereign powers, *United States v. Mazurie*, 419 U.S. 544, 557 (1975), the federal government's decision to use tribal convictions in a federal prosecution is a federal action that must comply with federal constitutional requirements. The Sixth Amendment does not command respect for tribal court proceedings; it commands that no person be deprived of life or liberty without the assistance of counsel. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 343-45.

V. THE GOVERNMENT'S POSITION WOULD UNDERMINE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT.

If the Government's position were accepted, the Sixth Amendment would be reduced to a mere technicality. The Government could use uncounseled tribal court convictions to secure a federal felony conviction, even though the same convictions could not be used if they had been obtained in state or federal court. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 114-15. This result is untenable. The Sixth Amendment's guarantee of counsel is not a geographic accident; it is a fundamental right that protects all persons accused of crimes. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. at 68-69. The federal government cannot circumvent this guarantee by relying on convictions obtained under a system that does not provide it.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. An uncounseled conviction—whether obtained in tribal court or any other court—cannot be used to prove an essential element of a federal felony offense without violating the Sixth Amendment.
