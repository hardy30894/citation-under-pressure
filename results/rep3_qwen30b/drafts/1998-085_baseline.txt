**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” *independent of its state of mind* to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which amended Title VII to authorize punitive damages for intentional discrimination. The answer is no. Congress did not require conduct to be “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the employer’s mental state. Rather, punitive damages are available under § 1981a(b)(1) when a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference* to the plaintiff’s federally protected rights. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals, which correctly held that the jury may consider punitive damages based on the employer’s state of mind, without requiring additional proof of conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.”

**I. Congress Rejected the “Egregious Conduct” Standard in Favor of a Mental-State Standard**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 explicitly authorizes punitive damages under Title VII when a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language is unambiguous and reflects Congress’s deliberate choice to align the standard for punitive damages under Title VII with the common law principles governing punitive damages in tort. As this Court has long recognized, “the touchstone of the punitive damages award is the defendant’s state of mind.” *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 575 (1996). The inquiry is not whether the conduct was “egregious” in the abstract, but whether the defendant acted with the requisite *mental state*—malice or reckless indifference.

The legislative history confirms that Congress intended to adopt a mental-state standard, not a conduct-based one. The House Report, which the Court of Appeals correctly rejected as “unadopted,” stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “should be available only in cases where the employer’s conduct was so egregious that it warranted punishment.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991). But this language was not adopted in the final version of the Act. The Senate version, which became law, replaced the “egregious” standard with the clear mental-state standard of *malice or reckless indifference*. See S. Rep. No. 102-166, at 10 (1991). The final statute reflects the Senate’s intent, not the House’s unadopted proposal. As this Court has held, “the final enacted statute is the law.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). The Court should not resurrect a rejected standard.

Moreover, the Senate Judiciary Committee explicitly rejected the idea that punitive damages require “extraordinarily egregious” conduct. Senator Dole, who authored the interpretive memo cited by the dissent, stated that punitive damages should be available “in cases where the employer’s conduct was so egregious that it warranted punishment.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). But this statement was not a legislative directive—it was a *suggestion* from a single senator. The final statute, which the Senate passed, did not incorporate that standard. The Court of Appeals correctly concluded that “the legislative history does not compel a higher standard than malice or reckless indifference.” *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 110 F.3d 1433, 1441 (D.C. Cir. 1997). The Court should follow that reasoning.

**II. The “Egregious Conduct” Standard Would Undermine Congress’s Purpose**

Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1991 to strengthen Title VII’s deterrent effect. The Act’s purpose was to “provide additional remedies under Federal law to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a (note). A “egregious conduct” standard would severely undermine this purpose. It would allow employers to engage in intentional discrimination—such as preselecting a male candidate over a qualified female employee, as occurred here—while escaping punitive damages so long as the conduct did not meet some vague threshold of “outrageousness.” That would render the punitive damages provision toothless.

The Court has long recognized that punitive damages serve a deterrent function. *BMW of North America, Inc.*, 517 U.S. at 574. When Congress authorizes punitive damages, it “intends to punish and deter particularly reprehensible conduct.” *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 419 (2003). But the “reprehensibility” of conduct is not measured by its objective severity, but by the defendant’s *subjective state of mind*. *Id.* at 425. An employer who acts with *reckless indifference* to a plaintiff’s rights—knowing or consciously disregarding the likelihood that its actions will violate federal law—deserves punishment, even if the conduct would not be deemed “egregious” under a purely objective standard.

Here, the jury could reasonably find that ADA acted with reckless indifference. The evidence showed that ADA preselected Spangler before the position was posted, copied his job description verbatim into the new position’s questionnaire, and failed to interview Kolstad despite her qualifications. The jury also heard testimony that the supervisor, Wheat, made sexually offensive remarks and referred to professional women as “bitches.” This evidence supports a finding that ADA’s conduct was not merely negligent, but *recklessly indifferent* to Kolstad’s rights under Title VII. The Court should not require a plaintiff to prove conduct that is “egregious” in addition to showing reckless indifference. That would create a double standard inconsistent with the statute.
