# ARGUMENT

**An Uncounseled Tribal Court Conviction Used to Establish an Element of Guilt Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) Violates the Sixth Amendment**

## I. Nichols Permits Uncounseled Convictions Only at Sentencing, Not as Proof of Guilt

The government's reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), for a blanket rule that any uncounseled conviction may be used so long as it did not itself violate the Sixth Amendment misreads that decision. *Nichols* held only that a valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be considered in *calculating a sentencing enhancement* under the Guidelines. *Id.* at 746–49. The Court's rationale was expressly tied to features unique to sentencing: a defendant may "convince the sentencing court of the unreliability of any prior valid but uncounseled convictions," and the government's burden at sentencing is only a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 747–48.

Those safeguards are absent here. Section 117(a) does not use the prior tribal convictions to adjust a sentencing range; it uses them as an *element of the offense itself*—without proof of which Bryant could not have been convicted at all. This Court has long distinguished between facts that increase punishment and facts that constitute the crime, holding that the latter must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (the Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction "except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged"); *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 483–84 (2000). Nothing in *Nichols* purports to authorize the use of an uncounseled conviction to satisfy that heightened burden of proof at the guilt phase.

## II. Using Uncounseled Convictions to Establish Guilt Undermines the Reliability Interests the Sixth Amendment Protects

This Court has repeatedly recognized that a conviction obtained without the assistance of counsel carries an inherent risk of unreliability that disqualifies it from use to a defendant's detriment in later proceedings. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), the Court held that a conviction obtained in violation of the right to counsel established in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), could not be used "either to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense," because doing so would "erode the principle" that counsel is necessary to a fair adjudication of guilt. *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115. That principle applies with full force here: the prior tribal convictions are not incidental sentencing facts but the very predicate that transforms a misdemeanor assault into a federal felony carrying up to ten years' imprisonment. 18 U.S.C. § 117(a).

*Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), confirms that the Sixth Amendment's concern is squarely implicated whenever an uncounseled conviction is later used to expose a defendant to incarceration. *Shelton* held that a suspended sentence which "may end up in the actual deprivation of a person's liberty" cannot rest on an uncounseled conviction. *Id.* at 662. The same logic controls here: Bryant's uncounseled tribal convictions form the sole basis for a term of imprisonment under § 117(a), a consequence far graver than the "actual deprivation of liberty" that concerned the *Shelton* Court.

## III. Respecting Tribal Sovereignty and the Indian Civil Rights Act Reinforces, Rather Than Undermines, This Rule

The government errs in suggesting that requiring counsel-equivalent protections before tribal convictions may be used federally disrespects tribal sovereignty. Tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment because they exercise inherent sovereign authority, not delegated federal power. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 322–23 (1978); *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 56 (1978). The Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301–1303, reflects Congress's deliberate choice to provide tribal defendants with due-process protections tailored to tribal traditions rather than a mirror of the Bill of Rights. That choice is entitled to respect precisely because it is *tribal* law, not federal constitutional law.

But it does not follow that Congress intended those tribal-court judgments, calibrated to a different due-process regime, to serve as the linchpin of a federal felony prosecution. Indeed, Congress's subsequent amendments to ICRA—requiring tribal courts to furnish counsel equivalent to the Sixth Amendment before exercising expanded sentencing authority, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c)—demonstrate that Congress itself recognizes a right to counsel is a necessary safeguard whenever tribal proceedings are used to justify serious deprivations of liberty. Extending federal comity to a valid tribal judgment on its own terms, see *Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987), is entirely different from conscripting that judgment to satisfy an element of a federal crime for which the Constitution demands counsel.

## IV. Conclusion

The Sixth Amendment's core guarantee—that no person be deprived of liberty for a conviction obtained without the assistance of counsel—cannot be circumvented by relabeling an uncounseled conviction as an "element" rather than a "sentence." Because § 117(a) uses Bryant's uncounseled tribal convictions to establish guilt of a federal felony, rather than merely to enhance a sentence within the *Nichols* framework, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Ninth Circuit.
