**ARGUMENT**

Rule 23(b)(3) does not allow certification on a guess that damages someday may be shown collectively. A class may be certified only when the district court, after a rigorous evidentiary inquiry, concludes that common issues predominate and that the proposed damages proof is capable of measuring the injury actually certified on a classwide basis. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Cohen v. Beneficial Indus. Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 551-52 (1949). The decision below violates that rule because it affirms certification despite the absence of adequate admissible evidence that Plaintiffs’ model could isolate damages attributable to the single antitrust theory the district court accepted. That is reversible error. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-64 (1931).

The defect is fundamental. A classwide damages methodology must fit the theory of liability being tried. A model that measures the effects of rejected theories, or of conduct beyond the certified claim, does not establish classwide damages for the claim that remains. *Miller v. Mackey Int’l, Inc.*, 452 F.2d 424, 429 (5th Cir. 1969); *Brown v. Cole*, 198 F.2d 967, 971 (5th Cir. 1952). Courts have long refused to let a certification hearing devolve into an acceptance of any expert formula that appears to produce a number. *West v. Radio-Keith-Orpheum Corp.*, 70 F.2d 621, 626 (2d Cir. 1934); *Moore v. New York Cotton Exch.*, 270 U.S. 593, 610 (1926). If the model does not measure the harm caused by the certified theory alone, predominance is absent.

This Court’s pre-1970 precedents require more than hopeful assertions. The district court’s certification findings must rest on a rational evidentiary basis, not speculation or surrogate proof. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Morrison v. Texas Co.*, 289 U.S. 501, 507 (1933). The class action mechanism is improper when individual issues dominate or when the supposed common proof does not actually bind the class together. *Sam Fox Publ’g Co. v. United States*, 366 U.S. 683, 689 (1961); *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 122 (1934). Representative litigation is appropriate only where the claims are sufficiently unified that a single judgment can fairly resolve the common dispute. *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303 (1853); *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 551-52.

That is particularly true in antitrust cases. Although damages need not be proved with mathematical precision, the plaintiff still must show that the damages claimed flowed from the unlawful conduct. *Southern Pac. Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.*, 245 U.S. 531, 533-34 (1918); *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. Here, Plaintiffs’ model was not shown to isolate the effect of the sole certified theory: anticompetitive clustering that deterred overbuilders in the Philadelphia DMA. A model that combines injury from uncertified theories cannot establish classwide proof of damages for the theory the district court actually certified. *Brenham v. Southern Pac. Co.*, 328 F.2d 1, 5 (5th Cir. 1964); *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Eisenmenger*, 256 F.2d 246, 248 (8th Cir. 1958).

The lower court’s approach effectively postponed the real Rule 23 inquiry until after certification. That is not permissible. *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43; *Moore*, 270 U.S. at 610. Certification is proper only when the record shows that both liability and damages are susceptible to common proof on the actual theory being litigated. *Miller*, 452 F.2d at 429; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. Because Plaintiffs failed to present adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a classwide basis for the certified theory, the judgment should be reversed.
