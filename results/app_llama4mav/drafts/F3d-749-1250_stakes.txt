## Argument

Stanley Hill's conviction should be reversed because the district court erred in admitting the expert testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones, which impermissibly invaded the province of the jury and prejudiced Stanley's right to a fair trial.

The admission of Agent Jones's testimony regarding Stanley's credibility and guilt was plain error. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, expert testimony is admissible if it is based on sufficient facts or data and is the product of reliable principles and methods. However, expert testimony that expresses an opinion on a witness's credibility or a defendant's guilt is generally inadmissible. See United States v. Charley, 189 F.3d 1251, 1260 (10th Cir. 1999) (holding that expert testimony that vouches for a witness's credibility is inadmissible); United States v. Toledo, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993) (stating that expert testimony that "tells the jury what result to reach" is inadmissible).

Agent Jones's testimony crossed this line by stating that Stanley's answers "did not make sense" and were "not worthy of credence," and that Stanley displayed behaviors "common among the criminal element." Jones also testified that he had "never in his career" seen an innocent person make a statement like Stanley's, and that people who invoke their faith to validate their statements are "deceptive." This testimony was not limited to providing background or context; rather, it directly assessed Stanley's credibility and guilt. See United States v. Benally, 541 F.3d 990, 995 (10th Cir. 2008) (holding that expert testimony that "directly comments on a defendant's guilt or innocence" is inadmissible).

The admission of this testimony was plain error because it affected Stanley's substantial rights. See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993) (stating that plain error is error that is "plain" and "affects substantial rights"). The jury's verdict was likely influenced by Agent Jones's testimony, which effectively told the jury that Stanley was guilty. See United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 16 (1985) (stating that the plain error doctrine is designed to prevent "miscarriages of justice").

Because Stanley did not object to the admission of Agent Jones's testimony at trial, we review for plain error. United States v. Gonzalez-Huerta, 403 F.3d 727, 732 (10th Cir. 2005). Under this standard, we may reverse if the error was plain, affected substantial rights, and seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Id. Here, the error was plain, and it affected Stanley's substantial rights by prejudicing his right to a fair trial. Therefore, we should reverse Stanley's conviction and remand for a new trial.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, Stanley Hill's conviction should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for a new trial.
