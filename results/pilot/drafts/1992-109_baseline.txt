**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment should be affirmed.

The right to self-representation and the right to plead guilty are not mere procedural conveniences—they are fundamental constitutional rights that implicate a defendant’s autonomy, dignity, and liberty. *See Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 819 (1975) (right to self-representation is “an essential component of due process”); *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) (guilty plea must be “voluntary, knowing, and intelligent”). Because these rights are so profound, the Constitution demands more than a mere inquiry into whether a defendant understands the charges or the potential penalties. The law requires that a defendant possess the capacity for *reasoned choice*—the ability to understand the alternatives available, to appreciate the consequences of each, and to make a deliberate decision in light of those consequences. *See Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986) (en banc); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981).

This standard is not merely a judicial gloss—it is rooted in the Constitution’s guarantee of due process. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Supreme Court held that a defendant must have “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him” to be competent to stand trial. But the Court made clear that this standard is *lower* than the one required for waiver of counsel or entry of a guilty plea. *Id.* at 172, 95 S.Ct. at 904. The Court explained that “the right to counsel is not a mere procedural convenience,” and that “the waiver of that right must be made with an understanding of the nature of the charges, the statutory penalties, and the range of possible defenses.” *Id.* at 172–73, 95 S.Ct. at 904. This “understanding” must be more than passive awareness—it must reflect a *deliberate and informed decision-making capacity*. *See also Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966) (competency to waive counsel requires “a rational and factual understanding of the nature of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel”).

The record here demonstrates that the trial court failed to apply this higher standard. At the time of the plea, Moran was still recovering from a suicide attempt, had been taking four psychotropic medications—including Inderal, Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Vistaril—each of which can impair cognitive function, judgment, and decision-making. The court was aware that Moran was on medication but made no inquiry into its effects. His responses to the court’s questions were monosyllabic, and he expressed a desire to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented—suggesting a lack of interest in a fair trial, not a reasoned strategy. *See United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981) (a good faith doubt about competency arises when there is “substantial evidence” of mental impairment).

The state court’s post-conviction finding of competency was based on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were designed solely to assess competency to stand trial—not the higher “reasoned choice” standard. *See Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. The reports of Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky focused on whether Moran could understand the charges and assist counsel—precisely the lower standard. They said nothing about his ability to weigh the consequences of waiving counsel or pleading guilty. *See also Evans I*, 705 F.2d at 1480 (competency to waive counsel requires “a higher level of mental functioning” than competency to stand trial). The state court’s reliance on these reports to support a finding of competency to waive constitutional rights was legally erroneous. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963) (federal courts must assume that a state court applied the correct legal standard unless there is reason to believe otherwise).

The Due Process Clause does not permit a trial court to ignore clear signs of mental impairment when a defendant seeks to waive fundamental rights. *See Seiling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973) (due process requires a court to initiate a competency hearing “whenever it has or should have a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency”). Here, the court had such a doubt. The record contained substantial evidence—suicidal ideation, medication use, monosyllabic responses, and a desire to foreclose mitigation—that should have triggered a hearing under the “reasoned choice” standard. The failure to do so violated Moran’s constitutional rights.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The Due Process Clause demands more than a mechanical inquiry into a defendant’s awareness of the charges. It demands that a defendant be capable of making a *reasoned choice* among the alternatives before the court. The trial court failed to ensure that Moran met this standard. The state court’s post-conviction determination, based on the wrong legal standard, cannot cure that constitutional defect.

**For these reasons, the petition should be granted.**
