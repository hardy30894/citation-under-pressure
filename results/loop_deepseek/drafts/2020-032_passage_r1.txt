**ARGUMENT**

The case is moot. Article III requires a live controversy throughout litigation. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). When the challenged policy is repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to it, federal courts lack jurisdiction. *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam); *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103, 108 (1969). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its policies and Uzuegbunam graduated. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages, which cannot avoid mootness.

**I. The Policy Change Extinguished All Ongoing Controversy**

A plaintiff must show a controversy that is “definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests.” *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). Once the law that allegedly caused injury has been repealed, no controversy remains. *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893). The new policy permits expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged code-of-conduct provision has been removed. There is no reasonable expectation that the college will reimpose the prior restrictions. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953). The college’s action was formal and permanent. *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 305 (1964). Because the policy is gone, both the facial and as-applied challenges are moot.

**II. Nominal Damages Do Not Revive a Moot Case**

A plaintiff cannot maintain a suit after the right to relief has been extinguished simply by requesting nominal damages. *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653. Nominal damages are a technical remedy that presupposes a live controversy; they cannot create one where none exists. *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943) (per curiam). The Court has consistently held that once the underlying dispute ends, the case must be dismissed even if nominal damages are sought. *Atherton Mills v. Johnston*, 259 U.S. 13, 16 (1922); *Brownlow v. Schwartz*, 261 U.S. 216, 218 (1923). The only exception is when the plaintiff has suffered a concrete, continuing injury. *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 55–56 (1968). Here, the complaint alleged no actual injury—only an abstract violation. Abstract injury cannot sustain a case after the policy is gone. *Golden*, 394 U.S. at 108–09.

**III. The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine Does Not Apply**

The voluntary cessation doctrine can avoid mootness only when the defendant’s cessation is permanent and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. *W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 632. But the college’s policy change was a formal, permanent revision, not a temporary retreat. *Southern Pac. Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498, 514–15 (1911). The new policy superseded the old one, and the college has no incentive to reinstate a policy already held unconstitutional. There is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. *Liner*, 375 U.S. at 305.

**Conclusion**

Because the challenged policies were repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to them, the case is moot. A prayer for nominal damages cannot supply the ongoing controversy required by Article III. The district court properly dismissed the complaint.
