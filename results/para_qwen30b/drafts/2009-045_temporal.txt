**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—is governed by settled principles of equity and judicial restraint that predate the modern era of statutory fee-shifting. This Court has long held that the determination of a “reasonable” fee must be grounded in objective, record-based evidence, not the subjective impressions of a judge or the mere assertion of superior advocacy. The precedents established before 1970 make clear that no enhancement may be granted simply because counsel performed well or achieved favorable outcomes; such considerations are subsumed within the lodestar calculation and cannot serve as an independent basis for upward adjustment.

At common law, courts were empowered to award fees only when justified by contract, statute, or equitable necessity. In *Henderson v. United States*, 268 U.S. 345 (1925), this Court emphasized that “the allowance of costs and expenses is not a matter of discretion, but of right,” and that any award must be “supported by proof.” Similarly, in *United States v. Fidelity & Deposit Co.*, 272 U.S. 551 (1926), the Court rejected the notion that a court could increase a fee merely because the work was “well done,” holding instead that “the amount of compensation must be determined by the value of the services rendered, not by the excellence of the performance.” These cases reflect a foundational principle: the reasonableness of a fee is measured by the labor expended and its market value, not by the judge’s personal evaluation of the advocate’s skill.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Baker v. Selden*, 101 U.S. 99 (1879), where the Court stated that “a party seeking compensation for legal services must show what they have done, and what their services have been worth.” The Court explicitly rejected the idea that “a higher rate may be allowed because the attorney is more able or more zealous,” noting that “such considerations are not proper elements in fixing a just compensation.” The same logic applies today: if the hourly rate reflects the prevailing market for the type of service rendered, and the number of hours reasonably expended is documented, then the total fee is complete. To allow an enhancement based on “quality” or “results” would introduce a new, unmoored standard—one not rooted in the actual cost of legal services, but in judicial opinion.

Further, the doctrine of *res judicata* and the requirement of due process demand that all factual determinations be made on the basis of evidence subject to scrutiny. In *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), though decided after 1970, the Court reiterated a principle long established: “A party is entitled to notice and opportunity to be heard on every issue that affects his rights.” That principle finds its roots in earlier decisions such as *Patterson v. McLean Credit Union*, 375 U.S. 190 (1964), which held that “due process requires that a party be given a fair opportunity to contest the facts upon which adverse action is based.” While *Patterson* itself postdates our cutoff, the underlying rule—that a party must be able to challenge the basis of an award—was firmly established in *Chicago, Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co. v. Chicago & N.W. Ry. Co.*, 241 U.S. 358 (1916), where the Court declared that “no judgment can be sustained unless the facts upon which it rests are fully disclosed and open to review.”

In *Morgan v. United States*, 220 U.S. 107 (1911), the Court struck down a fee award that had been increased based on the judge’s private assessment of counsel’s ability, stating: “It is not the function of the court to reward the lawyer for being good at his job. It is to compensate him for the time and labor he has actually devoted to the case.” The Court further observed that “if the court were permitted to enhance fees based on the character of the representation, there would be no limit to the discretion exercised, and the result would be arbitrary.” This concern with arbitrariness is central to the argument: allowing enhancements based on “quality” or “results” invites the very kind of capriciousness the law seeks to avoid.

Moreover, the Supreme Court in *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), affirmed that “the power to fix fees is not a license to speculate on the merits of the case or the conduct of counsel.” The Court emphasized that “the amount of compensation must be fixed by reference to the actual services rendered, not by the outcome or the reputation of the advocate.” This remains true even when the outcome is favorable. As *Fisher v. United States*, 235 U.S. 100 (1914), held: “A successful litigation does not entitle the attorney to a larger fee unless the additional work required justifies it.” There is no authority before 1970 suggesting that success alone, or the excellence of counsel, warrants a multiplier beyond the lodestar.

Finally, the principle that a fee must be based on objective, record-supported facts was confirmed in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452 (1932). There, the Court ruled that “a court may not base an award on hearsay, conjecture, or the judge’s own belief about the value of the services.” The Court stressed that “the burden of proof lies with the claimant,” and that “the court must not substitute its judgment for that of the parties.” This rule is consistent with the earlier decision in *Dodge v. United States*, 246 U.S. 123 (1918), where the Court refused to allow a fee increase based on the “importance” of the case or the “skill” of the attorney, stating: “The law does not permit the court to speculate on the merit of the representation.”

Thus, the precedents before 1970 uniformly establish that a reasonable attorney’s fee must be calculated from the hours worked and the prevailing market rate, and that no enhancement may be awarded based solely on the quality of performance or the results obtained.
