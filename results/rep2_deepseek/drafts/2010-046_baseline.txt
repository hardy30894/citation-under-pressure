**ARGUMENT**

**THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER COMPLIES WITH THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT BECAUSE THE REMEDY IS NARROWLY DRAWN, EXTENDS NO FURTHER THAN NECESSARY, AND IS THE LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS TO CORRECT A CONTINUING CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATION.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), permits prospective relief in prison-conditions cases only if the court finds that such relief is “narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” The three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity satisfies each of these requirements.

**I. The Order Is Narrowly Drawn and Extends No Further Than Necessary to Remedy the Eighth Amendment Violation.**

The three-judge court found that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the unconstitutional medical and mental health care in California’s prisons. *See Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 508 (2011). That finding is amply supported by the record. The court’s order is narrowly drawn because it targets the precise cause of the constitutional violation—overcrowding—and sets a population cap that is directly tied to remedying that violation.

The PLRA does not require courts to “abandon their equitable discretion” or to “accept any available remedial measure that falls short of correcting the constitutional violation.” *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 531. Here, the three-judge court considered and rejected less restrictive alternatives, including early release programs, reentry services, and increased funding for community-based treatment. Each of those alternatives had been tried and failed to alleviate the unconstitutional conditions. *Id.* at 512-13.

**II. The Order Is the Least Intrusive Means Necessary to Correct the Violation.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be the “least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s order satisfies this standard because it leaves the State with substantial discretion to determine which prisoners to release and how to achieve the population reduction. The order does not mandate the release of any particular inmate; it merely sets a population cap and allows the State to decide how to comply. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 533.

Moreover, the order is temporary and subject to modification. The three-judge court retained jurisdiction to adjust the population cap if the State demonstrates that it has remedied the constitutional violation through other means. *Id.* at 534. This flexibility ensures that the order is no more intrusive than necessary.

**III. The Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Requirement of a “Finding” of a Current and Ongoing Violation.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be based on a finding of a “current and ongoing violation of a Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court made such a finding, concluding that overcrowding causes “systemic” and “egregious” violations of the Eighth Amendment. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 508. This finding is supported by extensive evidence, including expert testimony and judicial findings from the underlying Plata and Coleman cases. *Id.* at 506-08.

**IV. The Order Satisfies the PLRA’s Requirement of a “Three-Judge Court” and a “Finding” of Inadequacy of Other Remedies.**

The PLRA requires that a three-judge court be convened before a court may order population reduction, and that the court find that “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court made this finding, concluding that “no other relief” would remedy the constitutional violation because the State had failed to comply with prior remedial orders and because overcrowding was the root cause of the unconstitutional conditions. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 512-13.

**V. The Order Does Not Violate the PLRA’s Prohibition on “Prisoner Release Orders” Without Adequate Consideration of Public Safety.**

The PLRA requires that a three-judge court give “substantial weight” to public safety before issuing a prisoner release order. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(A). The three-judge court did so, finding that the population reduction would not compromise public safety because the State could release low-risk inmates, expand good-time credits, and improve reentry programs. *Plata*, 563 U.S. at 534-35. The court also required the State to submit a plan to ensure that public safety is protected. *Id.* at 535.

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s order is a carefully crafted remedy that satisfies every requirement of the PLRA. It is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary, and is the least intrusive means to correct a continuing and egregious violation of the Eighth Amendment. This Court should affirm the judgment of the court of appeals.
