**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Protects Groups, Not Individuals**

The Equal Protection Clause prohibits discrimination against identifiable groups, not isolated individuals. This Court has consistently held that a plaintiff must allege membership in a protected class or that the state action was motivated by discriminatory animus toward a group. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976) (requiring proof of “discriminatory purpose” to establish an equal protection violation). In *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 272 (1979), the Court defined discriminatory purpose as a decisionmaker’s selection of a course of action “because of,” not merely “in spite of,” its adverse effects on an “identifiable group.” The Clause thus targets invidious classification, not arbitrary treatment of a single person.

The Seventh Circuit’s “class of one” theory—allowing a claim based solely on a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—finds no support in this Court’s precedent. No decision of this Court has ever recognized an equal protection claim by a plaintiff who does not allege membership in a class or group. The few cases cited by the court below, such as *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441 (1923), and *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336 (1989), involved tax assessments that systematically discriminated against a class of taxpayers. Those cases do not establish a freestanding right for a single property owner to challenge a discretionary government decision.

**II. The “Spiteful Effort” Standard Conflicts with Rational Basis Review**

Under rational basis review, a government action is presumed valid if any conceivable legitimate purpose supports it. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961); *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993). Courts do not inquire into the actual motivations of decisionmakers. *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 383-84 (1968) (warning against “inquiries into legislative motives”). The Seventh Circuit’s standard—requiring courts to determine whether the government acted out of “spite”—impermissibly invites such inquiries. It would transform every arbitrary or even negligent government act into a constitutional claim, overwhelming federal courts with routine disputes over easements, permits, and zoning decisions. *See New Orleans v. Dukes*, 427 U.S. 297, 303 (1976) (states may draw lines that “appear arbitrary” without violating equal protection).

**III. Olech Failed to State a Claim Even Under a “Class of One” Theory**

Even if this Court were to recognize a “class of one” theory, Olech’s complaint is insufficient. To prevail, a plaintiff must allege that she was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that the difference in treatment lacked any rational basis. *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432, 448 (1985) (comparing treatment of similar facilities). Olech did not allege that the Village’s demand for a 33-foot easement was irrational or that other property owners were identically situated. The Village may have required a larger easement from Olech due to site-specific conditions—such as drainage, topography, or utility needs—that are not alleged in the complaint. The mere fact that the Village required a different easement from Olech than from others does not, without more, establish an equal protection violation. *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) (rational basis satisfied if “any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify” the distinction).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
