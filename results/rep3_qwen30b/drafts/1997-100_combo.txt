**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing enhancements—contravenes settled constitutional principles established by the Supreme Court of the United States prior to 1970. The Clause, which protects against “being twice put in jeopardy of life or limb,” is not confined to the trial on guilt or innocence; it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense.” *United States v. Jenkins*, 325 U.S. 108, 110 (1945). The sentencing phase, when it involves the determination of a prior conviction that triggers a harsher penalty, is precisely such a proceeding.

The foundational principle that double jeopardy applies to sentencing determinations was affirmed in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896). There, the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a crime could not be retried on the same charge, even if the acquittal was based on a technical error. The Court emphasized that “the double jeopardy clause is not to be evaded by a new trial on a different count of the same indictment.” Id. at 671. This principle applies with equal force when the issue is not the guilt or innocence of the current offense, but the truth of a prior conviction that affects the sentence. The prior conviction is not a mere “fact” to be considered in sentencing; it is an element of a separate offense—the offense of being a recidivist—whose determination may result in a significantly more severe punishment. As the Court stated in *United States v. Pizzuto*, 275 U.S. 257, 260 (1927), “The double jeopardy clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense, and the offense is the same when the same act or transaction is the basis of the prosecution.”

The Court has long recognized that a prior conviction may be the subject of a separate prosecution. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), the Court held that a defendant could be prosecuted for a federal offense even after having been convicted of a state offense arising from the same transaction. The Court reasoned that “the same act may be an offense against two sovereignties,” and that “the double jeopardy clause does not prevent a second prosecution by a different sovereign.” Id. at 382. This principle is equally applicable when the “second prosecution” is not by a different sovereign, but by the same sovereign in a distinct phase of the same criminal proceeding—namely, the sentencing phase. The determination of a prior conviction is not a mere administrative step; it is a judicial determination that may result in a life sentence or a doubling of a prison term. Such a consequence is no less “life or limb” than a conviction on the original charge.

The Court has also held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a defendant is subjected to multiple trials on the same factual basis. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 158 (1833), the Court stated that “the same offense” may be prosecuted in different forms, and that “the double jeopardy clause is violated when a man is twice put in jeopardy for the same offense.” The Court further held in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671, that “the same offense” is not limited to the same indictment or the same count, but includes any prosecution based on the same transaction or act. The prior conviction allegation in this case is not a mere procedural formality; it is a factual determination that, if true, subjects the defendant to a sentence twice as long. To allow retrial after an acquittal on that issue is to subject the defendant to a second prosecution for the same offense—namely, the offense of being a recidivist.

The Court has consistently rejected the notion that sentencing proceedings are outside the scope of double jeopardy. In *United States v. Pizzuto*, 275 U.S. at 260, the Court held that “the double jeopardy clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense,” and that “the offense is the same when the same act or transaction is the basis of the prosecution.” The prior conviction allegation here is based on the same act—the 1992 assault—that is the basis of the enhancement. To allow retrial after a finding of insufficient evidence is to subject the defendant to a second prosecution on that same act.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies even when the second prosecution is not for the same crime, but for a different penalty. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. at 382, the Court held that “the same act may be an offense against two sovereignties,” and that “the double jeopardy clause does not prevent a second prosecution by a different sovereign.” The same logic applies when the second prosecution is by the same sovereign in a different phase of the same proceeding. The sentencing phase is not a mere administrative function; it is a judicial determination that may result in a life sentence. The Court has long recognized that “the double jeopardy clause is not to be evaded by a new trial on a different count of the same indictment.” *United States v. Jenkins*, 325 U.S. at 110.

Finally, the Court has held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense.” *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. The determination of a prior conviction is not a mere fact-finding exercise; it is a judicial determination that may result in a significantly more severe punishment. The Court has consistently held that such determinations are subject to the protections of the Double Jeopardy Clause. To hold otherwise would allow the state to retry a defendant on the same factual basis after an acquittal, in violation of the Fifth Amendment.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding is erroneous and must be reversed.
