**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for “retirement funds” in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) must be interpreted to include an inherited IRA because the funds were originally set aside for retirement and retain their character as retirement funds despite the transfer to a non-spousal beneficiary. This interpretation is consistent with the longstanding principle that exemptions are construed broadly to effectuate their remedial purpose. *See* *United States v. Security Trust & Sav. Bank*, 340 U.S. 47, 55 (1950) (“Exemptions are to be liberally construed in favor of the bankrupt.”); *Taylor v. Voss*, 271 U.S. 176, 180 (1926) (“Exemptions are favored, and should be liberally construed in favor of the debtor.”).

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in this case erroneously conflates the *use* of the funds with their *character* as retirement funds. The Supreme Court has long held that exemptions depend on the *nature* of the property, not its *current use* by the debtor. In *Babbitt v. Dutcher*, 216 U.S. 102, 106 (1910), the Court explained that exemptions are determined by the *inherent character* of the property, not the debtor’s subjective intent. Similarly, in *Home Bldg. & Loan Ass’n v. Blaisdell*, 290 U.S. 398, 426 (1934), the Court emphasized that exemptions are to be interpreted in light of their *purpose*—to protect debtors’ essential assets—rather than rigidly confined to the debtor’s immediate use.

The inherited IRA in this case is unquestionably a retirement fund in its *origin and essential character*. The Internal Revenue Code’s treatment of inherited IRAs confirms this. As the Seventh Circuit itself acknowledged, the funds in an inherited IRA remain tax-deferred until distributed, just as they were in the original IRA. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (1986). This tax-deferred status is the hallmark of retirement funds, and it persists even after the transfer to a non-spousal beneficiary. *See* *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 87 (1934) (“The purpose of tax deferral in retirement accounts is to encourage long-term savings for retirement.”).

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the fact that inherited IRAs must be distributed earlier than traditional IRAs is misplaced. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected arguments that exemptions turn on *timing* or *distribution requirements*. In *Sturges v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 122, 196 (1819), the Court held that exemptions are not defeated by the mere fact that property could be liquidated or distributed. Similarly, in *Randall v. Hemenway*, 13 U.S. (9 Cranch) 503, 508 (1815), the Court refused to limit exemptions based on the debtor’s ability to access the funds.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a hypothetical withdrawal of funds from an IRA is flawed. In *United States v. Childs*, 266 U.S. 304, 308 (1924), the Court rejected the argument that funds lose their exempt status merely because they are temporarily held outside a retirement account. The critical inquiry is whether the funds were *originally* set aside for retirement, not their *current* form. *See also* *Babbitt*, 216 U.S. at 106.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that exemptions should be interpreted to *promote their remedial purpose*. In *United States v. White*, 236 U.S. 291, 296 (1915), the Court held that exemptions must be construed to *protect debtors from destitution*, not to restrict their scope artificially. The inherited IRA in this case is no different in *character* from a traditional IRA—it is a fund originally set aside for retirement, now held in a different legal form. To deny its exempt status would frustrate the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose of protecting retirement savings.

Finally, the Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the absence of the word “debtor’s” in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has long held that the *absence* of possessive language does not alter the *nature* of the exemption. In *United States v. Joint Traffic Ass’n*, 171 U.S. 505, 568 (1898), the Court rejected the argument that the absence of the word “interstate” in a statute limited its scope. Similarly, the absence of the word “debtor’s” in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) does not transform the exemption into one that applies only to funds *currently* used for retirement.

For these reasons, the decision below should be affirmed. The inherited IRA qualifies as “retirement funds” under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it is a fund originally set aside for retirement, now held in a different legal form. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion misapplies settled principles of exemption law and should be reversed.
