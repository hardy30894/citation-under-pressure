**ARGUMENT**

I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED EQUITABLE JURISDICTION TO ISSUE THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER.

The District Court’s remedial order rests on a long line of this Court’s decisions recognizing that federal courts possess broad equitable powers to remedy constitutional violations in prisons. *Ex parte Ayers*, 103 U.S. 466, 480 (1881) (federal courts may enjoin state officials to prevent ongoing constitutional violations); *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978) (approving injunctive relief to remedy Eighth Amendment violations); *Coffin v. Reichard*, 143 F.2d 443, 445 (6th Cir. 1944) (federal courts may fashion remedies to protect constitutional rights of prisoners). The PLRA does not strip courts of this traditional equitable authority. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (preserving court’s authority to “grant prospective relief” to remedy constitutional violations).

The District Court’s order is consistent with this Court’s instruction that remedial decrees must be narrowly tailored to address the constitutional injury. *Graham v. Richardson*, 403 U.S. 365, 369 (1971) (remedial orders must be “tailored to remedy the [constitutional] violations”). Here, the court found that severe overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions, *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 607 F.3d 953, 960 (9th Cir. 2010), and the population cap was the least intrusive means to remedy the harm. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (requiring relief to be “narrowly drawn” and “the least intrusive means necessary”).

II. THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PLRA’S “NECES­SARY MEANS” STANDARD.

The PLRA’s “necessary means” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” The District Court’s order satisfies this standard.

This Court has long held that remedial orders must be tailored to the scope of the constitutional violation. *Graham v. Richardson*, 403 U.S. at 369; *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 16 (1958) (remedies must be “necessary to eliminate the [constitutional] wrong”). The District Court’s order is the least intrusive means to remedy the Eighth Amendment violations caused by overcrowding.

III. THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PLRA’S “TIME LIMITS” PROVISION.

The PLRA’s “time limits” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(e)(1), requires that prospective relief “shall not extend beyond the earliest date on which the underlying claim can be resolved.” The District Court’s order does not violate this provision because it is not a “term” of relief but a remedial measure to address ongoing constitutional violations. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(g)(9) (excluding “prospective relief” from the definition of “term”).

This Court has recognized that remedial orders may remain in effect until the constitutional violation is remedied. *Flast v. Cohen*, 392 U.S. 83, 100 (1968) (remedial orders may continue until the constitutional violation is corrected). The District Court’s order is consistent with this principle.

IV. THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PLRA’S “IMMUNITY” PROVISION.

The PLRA’s “immunity” provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(B), does not bar the District Court’s order because it does not impose liability on the State for past violations. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(g)(4) (defining “liability” as “a judgment for money damages”).

This Court has long held that injunctive relief does not constitute an unconstitutional taking or violate state sovereign immunity. *Ex parte Ayers*, 103 U.S. at 480; *Edelman v. Jordan*, 415 U.S. 651, 664 (1974) (injunctive relief does not violate the Eleventh Amendment). The District Court’s order is consistent with this precedent.

V. THE POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER IS CONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENT ON PRISON OVERCROWDING.

This Court has repeatedly upheld remedial orders addressing prison overcrowding. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 687 n.9; *Tate v. Short*, 401 U.S. 395, 399 (1971) (recognizing that overcrowding may violate the Eighth Amendment). The District Court’s order is consistent with this precedent.

**CONCLUSION**

The District Court’s population reduction order is a lawful exercise of equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations. It is narrowly tailored, the least intrusive means necessary, and consistent with this Court’s precedent. The PLRA does not bar the order, and it does not violate the Eleventh Amendment. The judgment below should be affirmed.
