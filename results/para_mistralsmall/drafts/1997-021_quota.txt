**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status is inconsistent with the Act and unsupported by reasoned decisionmaking. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987) (deferring to the Board’s interpretation of the Act only when it is “rational and consistent with the Act”). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling serves a legitimate purpose: enabling employers to obtain reliable information about employee sentiment before making irrevocable decisions to withdraw recognition or refuse to bargain. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). Yet the Board insists that polling is permissible only when the employer already has sufficient evidence to withdraw recognition—rendering the poll superfluous. This circular logic defies the Act’s purpose of encouraging stable collective-bargaining relationships by allowing employers to verify union support through minimally intrusive means. *See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 614 (1969) (recognizing the Board’s broad discretion to fashion remedies that effectuate the Act’s policies).

The Board’s standard also conflicts with the statutory framework governing representation disputes. Section 9 of the Act expressly authorizes the Board to conduct elections to test union support, but it does not prohibit employers from conducting their own polls when they have a good-faith doubt. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 159(c). The Board’s insistence on a “reasonable doubt” standard for polling—while permitting withdrawal of recognition on the same showing—ignores the fact that polling is less disruptive than withdrawal and provides more certainty than subjective doubt. *See Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc. v. NLRB*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (recognizing the Board’s authority to set standards for withdrawal of recognition). The Board’s approach is therefore irrational and warrants deference only to the extent it is consistent with the Act. *See Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984) (requiring deference to agency interpretations that are permissible constructions of the statute).

**II. The Board’s Standard for Polling Is Inconsistent with Decisions of the Courts of Appeals and Should Be Rejected.**

Three circuits have rejected the Board’s “reasonable doubt” standard, holding instead that polling is permissible when the employer has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. *See A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). These courts recognized that polling serves a vital function in resolving employer uncertainty without the need for a full-scale Board election, which is more cumbersome and time-consuming. *See Thompson*, 651 F.2d at 1145 (noting that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with “sincere doubts” about union support).

The Board’s attempt to distinguish these decisions is unpersuasive. The Board argues that polling is inherently disruptive and that elections are the preferred method for testing union support. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061. But the Board offers no empirical support for this claim, and its preference for elections does not justify foreclosing employers from using less intrusive means to verify union support. *See Gissel*, 395 U.S. at 614 (acknowledging the Board’s broad discretion but emphasizing that its remedies must be tailored to effectuate the Act’s policies). The Board’s standard is therefore arbitrary and capricious. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (holding that agency action must be based on reasoned decisionmaking).

**III. The Board’s Factual Determination That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board found that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt because only seven of the 32 employees in the bargaining unit made statements repudiating the union. *See Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc.*, 325 N.L.R.B. 1020, 1023 (1998). This conclusion is unsupported by the record. The Board improperly discounted the statements of employees who expressed dissatisfaction with the union during job interviews, holding that such statements were not probative of their true sentiments. *See Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978) (holding that anti-union statements made during job interviews are unreliable). But the Board’s own precedent recognizes that employees may express dissatisfaction with union representation without opposing the union itself. *See Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51, 52 (1988) (holding that dissatisfaction with union services does not equate to opposition to union representation).

Moreover, the Board ignored the unrefuted evidence that the union’s support had eroded. The employees’ vote against the union—19 to 13—demonstrated that the union no longer enjoyed majority support. *See NLRB v. Arkansas Grain Corp.*, 490 F.2d 105, 110 (8th Cir. 1974) (holding that a union’s loss of majority support may be established by employee conduct, including a vote against the union). The Board’s refusal to credit this evidence is arbitrary and capricious. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951) (requiring that the Board’s factual findings be supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The courts of appeals have rejected this standard, and the Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should grant the petition for review and vacate the Board’s order.
