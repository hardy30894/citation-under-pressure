**Argument**

Peguero is not entitled to habeas relief because the district court’s omission, though a violation of Rule 32(a)(2), did not deprive him of any cognizable federal right once the court found as a matter of fact that he already knew he could appeal and chose not to do so. Habeas corpus lies for custody that violates the Constitution or laws of the United States, not for every procedural misstep divorced from prejudice. See *Smith v. O’Grady*, 312 U.S. 329, 334 (1941); *Woods v. Nierstheimer*, 328 U.S. 211, 217 (1946). Here, the rule’s purpose was served: Peguero had actual knowledge of his appellate right, so the omission caused no harm.

This Court has long refused to turn sentencing or post-trial errors into automatic habeas relief absent a showing of prejudice or fundamental unfairness. In *Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736, 740–41 (1948), relief was warranted because sentence rested on “assumptions concerning his criminal record which were materially untrue,” and counsel’s absence prevented correction. In contrast, the Court emphasized that counsel may not have changed the sentence, but could have prevented sentencing from proceeding on misinformation. *Id.* at 741. Likewise, in *Mempa v. Rhay*, 389 U.S. 128, 134 (1967), the Court recognized that the right to counsel at sentencing serves to protect against unfairness at a critical stage. But those cases turn on prejudice, not on per se vacatur for any procedural omission. *See also* *McConnell v. Rhay*, 393 U.S. 2, 3–4 (1968); *Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 584–85 (1959).

The same principle controls here. Rule 32(a)(2) required the court to advise Peguero of his right to appeal, but federal courts do not grant collateral relief for a technical violation that had no effect on the defendant’s ability to pursue review. In *Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82, 84–86 (1946), the Court declined collateral relief where the petitioner’s complaint about sentencing could not establish the invalidity of his custody absent a showing that the defect mattered to the judgment. And in *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 731–32 (1948), the Court rejected a habeas attack on a sentence where the asserted error did not justify disturbing the custody actually authorized by law. So too here: Peguero knew of his right and decided against appealing. The district court’s failure to repeat what he already knew did not cause forfeiture of review, nor did it render his custody unlawful.

Peguero’s contrary position would create automatic collateral relief for every Rule 32 omission, regardless of whether the defendant was prejudiced. That is inconsistent with the Court’s treatment of procedural irregularities in other contexts. In *Hamilton v. Alabama*, 368 U.S. 52, 53–55 (1961), the Court found denial of counsel at arraignment constitutionally significant because Alabama law required certain defenses to be raised then or lost. In *Reece v. Georgia*, 350 U.S. 85, 87–90 (1955), the Court similarly focused on the practical loss of a right caused by the procedural defect. Here, by contrast, no right was lost: Peguero was aware of his appellate right and elected not to invoke it. The omission therefore did not work the kind of forfeiture that justifies habeas relief.

The Third Circuit correctly recognized that harmless-error principles govern this claim. That approach accords with the Court’s longstanding refusal to upset criminal judgments where the asserted defect did not affect the outcome or the defendant’s substantive position. *See* *Hunter v. Martin*, 334 U.S. 302, 303–04 (1948) (federal habeas relief denied where custody remained independently supported); *Sigler, Warden v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969) (mem.) (recognizing that defects irrelevant to the validity of the conviction or sentence do not supply a basis for relief). Rule 32 is designed to ensure that a defendant knows of the chance to seek appellate review; once that knowledge exists, the rule’s purpose is fulfilled.

Finally, there is no unfairness in refusing relief when the defendant knowingly declined to appeal. The due process cases on which habeas petitioners rely involve deception, ignorance, or the loss of a meaningful opportunity to challenge the judgment. *Smith v. O’Grady*, 312 U.S. 329, 334–35 (1941), involved a plea induced by deception and ignorance of the true charge. *Canizio*, *Woods*, and *Gryger* all confirm that collateral relief does not follow from a bare procedural error absent a showing that the defect affected the legality of the custody. Because Peguero knew of his appeal right at sentencing and chose not to appeal, the omission of the formal advisement did not prejudice him and does not warrant habeas relief. The judgment should be affirmed.
