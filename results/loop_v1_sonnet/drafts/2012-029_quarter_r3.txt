**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal "arising under" jurisdiction over patent issues is narrow and does not reach a state malpractice claim merely because it requires resolution of a hypothetical patent question.**

Congress gave federal district courts jurisdiction over cases "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), but this Court has long cautioned against reading "arising under" so broadly that any state-created claim touching a federal issue is swept into an exclusive federal forum. This Court has explained that a suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action, rather than under any law that a case may incidentally require the court to apply. *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Minton's suit against his former lawyers is a Texas tort—legal malpractice—created entirely by Texas law. That the case-within-a-case requires the state court to ask what would have happened had counsel raised the experimental-use exception does not transform the claim into one "arising under" federal patent law.

This Court's well-pleaded-complaint doctrine confirms the point. In *Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908), this Court held that jurisdiction depends on the plaintiff's own statement of his cause of action, not on any anticipated federal defense, and that a suit does not arise under federal law simply because a party expects a federal issue to surface in the litigation. Minton's petition alleges negligence and the elements of Texas malpractice law; it states no patent-law cause of action. Even an embedded federal issue must be one on which the plaintiff's right to relief genuinely and substantially depends, not one that is merely incidental to the controversy. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). The experimental-use question here is a fact-bound, backward-looking inquiry confined to two former litigants; it does not implicate the patent statute in any way that affects parties beyond this case.

This Court has likewise cautioned against opening the arising-under door indiscriminately, favoring instead "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). A hypothetical defense never adjudicated by the Patent Office or any patent tribunal is not "substantial" in the sense *Gully* demands; it is one strand in the web of an ordinary malpractice case.

**II. Precedent confirms that suits requiring only incidental application of patent principles remain within the States' traditional jurisdiction.**

Long before the modern "arising under" test was refined, this Court held that suits requiring courts to apply patent concepts to resolve contract or property disputes do not thereby become federal patent cases. In *Wilson v. Sandford*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 99 (1850), the Court held that a suit over rights under a patent license was not a case "arising under" the patent laws, because the plaintiff's claim rested on the contract, not the patent grant itself. *Albright v. Teas*, 106 U.S. 613 (1883), likewise held that a suit founded on a contract relating to a patent right does not arise under the patent laws merely because the contract concerns a patent, since the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the agreement of the parties rather than on the patent statute. And in *New Marshall Engine Co. v. Marshall Engine Co.*, 223 U.S. 473 (1912), the Court again confirmed that state courts may adjudicate disputes over rights derived from patents where the plaintiff's right to relief flows from contract rather than from the patent statute itself.

The same logic controls here. Minton's right to relief flows entirely from Texas's law of professional negligence; the patent question is, at most, background necessary to prove or disprove causation—just as license and contract terms were background to the claims in *Wilson*, *Albright*, and *New Marshall Engine Co.*

**III. Exercising federal jurisdiction here would disturb, rather than preserve, the balance between federal and state judicial responsibilities.**

Embedded-issue jurisdiction is appropriate only where a federal forum may entertain the question without disturbing the balance Congress has struck between state and federal courts. That balance tips toward federal jurisdiction only in the rare case—like *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180 (1921)—where the federal question carries broad, prospective importance, there the constitutionality of a federal bond program affecting countless future transactions. No comparable stakes exist here. The Texas court's resolution of Minton's hypothetical defense binds no one but the parties, will not affect the Patent and Trademark Office or other patentees, and will not disturb the uniform administration of the patent laws. Texas courts are fully capable of applying settled patent doctrine to decide this backward-looking malpractice claim, consistent with the States' traditional authority over the practice of law within their borders.

For these reasons, the Texas courts possessed—and should have exercised—jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim.
