ARGUMENT

The district court’s omission of the Rule 32(a)(2) advice did not entitle petitioner to relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. In Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), this Court held that “the failure to follow the formal requirements of Rule 32(a) is not of itself an error which can be raised by collateral attack.” Id. at 426. The Court explained that collateral relief is available only when the omission produces “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice.” Id. at 428.

The district court’s unchallenged finding that petitioner already knew of his right to appeal at sentencing precludes any such defect. Where a defendant possesses actual knowledge, the rule’s sole purpose—to apprise him of the right—has been fulfilled. See Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 275 (1942) (recognizing that a defendant who “understands” his rights may not later complain of omitted formalities). Because petitioner elected not to appeal with full awareness of the opportunity, the omission caused no prejudice.

Harmless-error principles confirm the same result. In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), this Court instructed that a procedural default warrants reversal only if it “had substantial influence” on the outcome or leaves “grave doubt” about that influence. Id. at 765. The district court’s finding eliminates any such doubt. Petitioner’s knowledge rendered the omitted statement immaterial.

Accordingly, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
