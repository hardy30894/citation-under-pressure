ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Section 5(b) of the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act imposes on a vessel owner the duty to “exercise the same care that a reasonably prudent person would” under the circumstances. Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156, 167, 101 S.Ct. 1614, 1622, 68 L.Ed.2d 1 (1981). This duty encompasses an “active operations” obligation whenever the vessel “actively involves itself in the cargo operations” or “retains control” over equipment whose misuse creates a hazard. Id. at 167, 101 S.Ct. at 1622.

The record establishes that the snatch blocks remained under the exclusive control of Bora’s crew. The blocks were fixed to the vessel, operated by the crew through the block-and-pulley system, and required the crew to reposition them after each hatch cover was opened. Serbin testified without contradiction that longshoremen were not responsible for moving the blocks and that he had never been instructed to do so. A vessel that retains such operational control “must exercise reasonable care to avoid exposing longshoremen to harm from hazards” it has created. Id. at 167, 101 S.Ct. at 1622.

Bora’s crew left one block in the down position atop cargo in the No. 3 hatch. That placement both prevented removal of the underlying fruit without damage and created an acknowledged tripping and falling hazard in the precise area where the hookup man was required to stand. Because the hazard arose from equipment the vessel alone was authorized to move, a jury could find that Bora failed to exercise reasonable care in its active operations. See id. at 172, 101 S.Ct. at 1624 (vessel may be liable when it “knows or should know” its equipment creates danger to which stevedore workers may be exposed).

The district court’s conclusion that Serbin assumed the risk by attempting to reposition the block misapplies Scindia. The Court there expressly rejected the notion that a longshoreman’s decision to confront an obvious hazard necessarily bars recovery; instead, the question is whether the vessel’s breach of duty was a legal cause of the injury. Id. at 176-77, 101 S.Ct. at 1626-27. Here, the crew’s failure to return the block to the vertical position after opening the hatch cover directly precipitated Serbin’s decision and the resulting fall. Whether that failure constituted negligence remains a genuine issue for trial.

Reversal is required.
