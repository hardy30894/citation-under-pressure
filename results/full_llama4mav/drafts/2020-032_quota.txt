The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation. The mootness doctrine is rooted in Article III's requirement that federal courts only decide live cases or controversies. See DeFunis v. Odegaard, 416 U.S. 312, 316 (1974). When a challenged policy is revised or repealed, the controversy is typically rendered moot, unless the plaintiff can demonstrate that the defendant will likely revert to the original policy. See City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc., 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982).

In Flanigan's Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), this Court held that a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law. The Court reasoned that nominal damages are not sufficient to establish a live controversy when the challenged policy is no longer in effect. Id. at 1265-66. This holding is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Arizonans for Official English v. Arizona, 520 U.S. 43 (1997), where the Court held that a challenge to a state constitutional provision was moot when the plaintiff resigned from state employment, rendering the provision inapplicable to her. Id. at 72.

The appellants' attempt to distinguish their case from Flanigan's on the grounds that they brought an as-applied challenge is unavailing. The Eleventh Circuit has held that the distinction between facial and as-applied challenges is not dispositive in determining mootness. See Harrell v. The Florida Bar, 608 F.3d 1241, 1266 (11th Cir. 2010). Moreover, the appellants' complaint did not allege sufficient facts to support a claim for compensatory damages, which is required to establish a live controversy. See Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247, 264 (1978).

The district court properly applied Flanigan's when it dismissed the appellants' complaint as moot. The court correctly concluded that the appellants' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were moot due to the revision of the challenged policies and Uzuegbunam's graduation. See Adler v. Duval County School Board, 112 F.3d 1475, 1477 (11th Cir. 1997). The court also correctly determined that the appellants' request for nominal damages was insufficient to save their otherwise moot claims.

The appellants' argument that they should have been allowed to amend their complaint to add a request for compensatory damages is also without merit. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the appellants' request to amend, as they failed to properly seek leave to amend and did not specify the substance of their proposed amendment. See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018).

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the appellants' constitutional challenge was rendered moot by the revision of the challenged policies. The district court's dismissal of the complaint was proper, and the appellants' attempts to distinguish their case from Flanigan's and to amend their complaint are unavailing. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
