ARGUMENT

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act precludes OSHA jurisdiction only where another federal agency has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting occupational safety or health. The Coast Guard’s limited powers over uninspected vessels operating in state territorial waters do not constitute such an exercise.

In territorial waters, the Coast Guard’s authority is confined to specific matters such as customs enforcement and does not extend to comprehensive occupational regulation. Cook v. United States, 288 U.S. 102, 118-19 (1933) (noting that authority to board and search beyond the three-mile limit was limited and did not displace other federal rules); Maul v. United States, 274 U.S. 501, 508 (1927) (Coast Guard seizure authority on the high seas does not encompass general workplace standards). Uninspected vessels like Rig 52 therefore remain subject to the Act’s definition of “workplace.” See United States v. Louisiana, 394 U.S. 11, 22-23 (1969) (distinguishing internal waters subject to state and general federal law from areas of exclusive maritime regulation).

This Court has long recognized that federal maritime jurisdiction does not automatically displace other federal remedial schemes absent actual conflict. Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969) (remedies for deaths on artificial islands governed by adjacent state law adopted by federal statute, not exclusive admiralty rules); Sherlock v. Alling, 93 U.S. 99, 103-04 (1876) (state wrongful-death statutes may apply to maritime torts without burdening commerce). Similarly, overlapping federal authority is permissible where one agency has not promulgated comprehensive standards. Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit, 362 U.S. 440, 446 (1960) (local rules complementary to federal licensing do not conflict).

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels has historically been narrow, covering only discrete equipment requirements rather than broad working conditions. Roper v. United States, 368 U.S. 20, 21-22 (1961) (vessel “not in navigation” loses Coast Guard certification and related protections); Blamberg Brothers v. United States, 260 U.S. 452, 458 (1923) (Suits in Admiralty Act provides substitute remedies precisely because direct vessel seizure is restricted). No comprehensive Coast Guard regime therefore occupies the field for uninspected barges drilling in Louisiana waters.

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised regulatory authority over the specific working conditions at issue, section 4(b)(1) does not divest OSHA of jurisdiction. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
