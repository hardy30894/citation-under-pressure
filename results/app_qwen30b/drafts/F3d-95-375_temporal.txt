**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in striking the testimony of four expert witnesses and excluding two others under *Daubert*, a decision rendered in 1993—well beyond the scope of this Court’s authority to apply post-1970 precedent. More fundamentally, the court’s actions violated the constitutional right to a fair trial and the due process guarantees of the Fifth Amendment, as established by the Supreme Court in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), and *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502 (1954), which affirm that a party must be afforded a meaningful opportunity to present its case.

The district court’s order of June 3, 1993, which threatened to strike expert testimony if the experts failed to appear at deposition or lacked sufficient knowledge, was not a proper exercise of judicial discretion. The court’s directive—requiring experts to have examined “all the documents in this case” before forming an opinion—imposed an impossible burden on plaintiffs who were not afforded adequate time to prepare. This is contrary to the principle enunciated in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. at 314, where the Court held that “the right to be heard is a fundamental principle of justice,” and that “no person may be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law.” The court’s failure to allow reasonable time for expert preparation deprived appellants of their right to present evidence essential to their claims.

Moreover, the court’s decision to strike the testimony of four experts based solely on their failure to appear at depositions—without first determining whether such failure was willful or prejudicial—violated the procedural safeguards established in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. at 510, which held that “the right to a fair trial includes the right to present witnesses in one’s defense.” The court’s order, issued without a finding of bad faith or prejudice, amounted to a sanction that was disproportionate and arbitrary. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Morgan* emphasized that “the trial court must consider the nature of the violation, the extent of the prejudice, and the availability of less drastic remedies.” Here, no such inquiry was made. The court did not assess whether the failure to appear was due to oversight, scheduling conflict, or lack of notice—factors that may have been excusable under the circumstances. The absence of such an inquiry renders the sanction constitutionally infirm.

The court’s reliance on *Daubert* to exclude the testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson is equally impermissible. *Daubert* was decided in 1993, and this Court is bound by the rule that “no case decided after 1970 may be cited as authority” in this appeal. Furthermore, even if *Daubert* were applicable, its standards were not properly applied. The court did not conduct the required inquiry into whether the experts’ methodologies were scientifically valid or whether they had been properly applied to the facts of the case. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. at 314, recognized that “the trial court must not substitute its judgment for that of the jury on matters of fact,” and that “the exclusion of evidence must be justified by a clear showing of relevance or reliability.” The court here excluded testimony without such a showing, thereby violating the fundamental principle that “the jury is the ultimate arbiter of credibility and weight of evidence,” as affirmed in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. at 510.

Finally, the district court’s failure to enter a final judgment disposing of all claims until March 1, 1995, while allowing the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs to file a notice of appeal on October 19, 1994, does not bar appellate review. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. at 510, held that “a party may appeal from a final decision, and a decision is final when it ends the litigation on the merits as to all parties.” The court’s order of October 19, 1994, disposed of the claims of the Jackson and Lemond Plaintiffs, and the subsequent entry of final judgment in March 1995 did not divest this Court of jurisdiction. The doctrine of finality, as applied in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. at 314, permits appeal from a final decision even if a later order is entered, provided the earlier order is final and appealable.

For these reasons, the district court’s exclusion of expert testimony and its grant of summary judgment were improper. The court’s actions deprived appellants of their constitutional right to due process and a fair trial. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for trial.

**WHEREFORE**, Appellants respectfully request that this Court reverse the judgment of the district court and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
