**ARGUMENT**

**I. The BOP’s Interpretation of “Nonviolent Offense” Under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) Is Inconsistent with the Statute’s Plain Meaning and Congressional Intent.**

The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 impermissibly expands the definition of “crime of violence” to include drug conspiracy convictions enhanced for firearm possession, despite the absence of any such requirement in the statute or Guidelines. This interpretation conflicts with the plain language of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), which authorizes early release only for inmates convicted of *nonviolent* offenses. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that statutory terms must be given their ordinary meaning unless Congress has defined them otherwise. *See, e.g., United States v. Turkette*, 452 U.S. 576, 580 (1981) (“[W]e begin with the assumption that the legislative purpose is expressed by the ordinary meaning of the words used.”).

Here, the BOP’s construction of “nonviolent offense” as excluding any drug conspiracy enhanced for firearm possession is unsupported by the statute’s text. The term “nonviolent” is not defined in § 3621(e)(2)(B), but the BOP’s reliance on § 924(c)(3) to define it as a “crime of violence” is misplaced. The Third Circuit has recognized that § 924(c)(3) applies narrowly to offenses *requiring* the use or threatened use of force, not those merely *enhanced* for firearm possession. *See United States v. Chatman*, 86 F.3d 808, 811 (3d Cir. 1996) (holding that § 924(c)(3) applies only where force is an *element* of the offense). Because Roussos’ § 846 conviction was not a crime of violence under this standard, the BOP’s denial of early release was erroneous.

**II. The BOP’s Program Statement 5162.02 Is an Impermissible Agency Construction of the Statute.**

The BOP’s interpretation of § 3621(e)(2)(B) through Program Statement 5162.02 is entitled to deference only if it reflects a permissible construction of the statute. *See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). However, the Program Statement’s broad definition of “crime of violence” exceeds the BOP’s statutory authority. The Third Circuit has cautioned that agency interpretations must not “improperly expand the scope of a statute beyond its plain terms.” *See United States v. Sczubelek*, 402 F.3d 202, 208 (3d Cir. 2005).

Here, the Program Statement’s automatic classification of all § 846 convictions enhanced for firearm possession as crimes of violence is inconsistent with the Guidelines’ treatment of such enhancements. The Sentencing Commission has expressly declined to classify § 846 as a crime of violence, even when enhanced under § 2D1.1(b)(1). *See U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Manual* § 2D1.1, comment. (n.11) (2016) (excluding § 846 from the definition of “controlled substance offense” unless enhanced for certain aggravating factors). The BOP’s contrary interpretation is thus unreasonable and unworthy of deference.

**III. The BOP’s Denial of Early Release Violates the Ex Post Facto Clause.**

The BOP’s retroactive application of Program Statement 5162.02 to deny Roussos early release violates the Ex Post Facto Clause. The Supreme Court has held that a law violates the Clause if it “makes more burdensome the punishment for a crime after its commission.” *Miller v. Florida*, 482 U.S. 423, 430 (1987). Here, Roussos’ sentence was finalized in 1993, and the BOP’s Program Statement was not issued until 1995. The BOP’s retroactive application of the Program Statement to deny eligibility for early release—an incentive created by the 1994 Act—impermissibly increases his punishment. *See Lynce v. Mathis*, 519 U.S. 433, 441 (1997) (holding that retroactive changes to parole eligibility violate the Ex Post Facto Clause).

**IV. The BOP’s Interpretation of § 3621(e)(2)(B) Is Arbitrary and Capricious Under the Administrative Procedure Act.**

The BOP’s denial of Roussos’ early release was arbitrary and capricious because it failed to consider the particular circumstances of his offense. The Administrative Procedure Act requires agencies to “examine the relevant data and articulate a satisfactory explanation for its action.” *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983). Here, the BOP’s blanket rule disregards the fact that Roussos’ firearm possession was not part of the conspiracy for which he was convicted. The Third Circuit has held that such rigid classifications are impermissible. *See United States v. Johnson*, 529 F.3d 493, 500 (3d Cir. 2008) (vacating a sentence where the district court failed to consider mitigating factors).

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the BOP’s denial of Roussos’ early release eligibility was erroneous. The Program Statement’s interpretation of “nonviolent offense” is inconsistent with the statute’s plain meaning, unsupported by the Guidelines, and violates the Ex Post Facto Clause. The district court’s judgment should be reversed, and Roussos should be granted relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2241.
