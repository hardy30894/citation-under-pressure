**ARGUMENT**

I. A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES PRECLUDES MOOTNESS.

A case becomes moot only when it is impossible for a court to grant “any effectual relief whatever” to the prevailing party. *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013). This Court has long recognized that a request for nominal damages can supply that relief when a plaintiff alleges the violation of a completed past constitutional injury. *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266–67 (1978); *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 n.11 (1986). Nominal damages “are not a consolation prize,” but a judgment that vindicates a legal right. *Farrar v. Hobby*, 506 U.S. 103, 112 (1992).

That rule follows from the nature of nominal damages themselves. Where a plaintiff proves the violation of a constitutional right but cannot prove actual injury, nominal damages remain available. *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266–67; *Stachura*, 477 U.S. at 308 n.11. A live controversy therefore persists because the court can still award meaningful, albeit symbolic, relief for the completed wrong. *Farrar*, 506 U.S. at 112–13.

This Court’s mootness cases are consistent with that principle. In *Los Angeles County v. Davis*, 440 U.S. 625, 631 (1979), the Court explained that a case is not moot unless interim events have “completely and irrevocably eradicated the effects of the alleged violation.” Here, the alleged violation occurred when Respondent enforced an unconstitutional speech restriction against Petitioners. The later policy revision did not erase that completed injury; it merely ended the challenged practice going forward. Under *Carey*, *Stachura*, and *Farrar*, the availability of nominal damages preserves Article III jurisdiction.

II. VOLUNTARY CESSATION DOES NOT MOOT THIS CASE.

When a defendant voluntarily ceases challenged conduct, the case is not moot unless the defendant meets the “heavy burden” of showing that the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000); *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982). A strategic policy revision during litigation is the classic case for applying that rule. *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013).

Respondent’s revision of its policy did not moot Petitioners’ case. Petitioners alleged that Respondent’s prior policy violated the First Amendment as applied to their speech. The claim concerns a past constitutional injury, and Respondent remained free, absent a judicial ruling, to reinstitute the same restriction. *City of Mesquite*, 455 U.S. at 289. The existence of a live damages claim makes the mootness problem even clearer: a defendant cannot moot a completed constitutional violation by changing the policy that caused it. *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266–67; *Farrar*, 506 U.S. at 112–13.

III. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT NOMINAL DAMAGES CANNOT SAVE AN OTHERWISE MOOT CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE.

The Eleventh Circuit’s rule conflicts with this Court’s settled damages doctrine. A plaintiff who proves a completed constitutional injury may recover nominal damages even without actual damages. *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266–67; *Stachura*, 477 U.S. at 308 n.11. If nominal damages are available for that injury, then a controversy over that injury remains live. *Farrar*, 506 U.S. at 112–13.

The court below relied on the fact that Petitioners also sought declaratory and injunctive relief. But the loss of forward-looking relief does not extinguish a damages claim for a past violation. *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 141 S. Ct. 792, 802 (2021) (recognizing that a request for nominal damages by itself can redress a completed constitutional violation). The correct mootness inquiry is whether any judicial relief remains available. *Chafin*, 568 U.S. at 172. Here, nominal damages plainly do.

IV. CONCLUSION

Because Petitioners alleged a completed constitutional violation and sought nominal damages, their case was not moot when Respondent revised its policy. The judgment should be reversed.
