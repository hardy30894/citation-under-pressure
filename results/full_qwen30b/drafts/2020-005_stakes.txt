**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s purposeful contacts with the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle ensures that a defendant is not subjected to the jurisdiction of a court where it has not reasonably anticipated being haled into court. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision in *Gullett v. Ford Motor Co.*, 2020 MT 123, 467 Mont. 1, 267 P.3d 1001, violates this foundational requirement by asserting jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company based on the mere sale of vehicle parts within Montana—contacts that had no causal connection to the plaintiff’s injury.

Ford did not design, manufacture, or sell the vehicle in Montana. The Ford Explorer involved in the accident was assembled in Kentucky and first sold to a dealership in Washington State. The vehicle was then transferred to an Oregon resident, who later sold it to a purchaser who brought it to Montana. *Gullett v. Ford Motor Co.*, 2020 MT 123, ¶ 7. Ford’s only connection to Montana was the sale of replacement parts through a distributorship. The Montana Supreme Court concluded that this activity—advertising and selling parts—constituted “availing” itself of the privilege of doing business in the state, thereby subjecting Ford to specific jurisdiction. *Id.*, ¶ 12. This reasoning is legally unsound and directly contradicts *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985), which held that “a defendant’s conduct must create a substantial connection with the forum State” for jurisdiction to be proper. The sale of parts, even if occurring in Montana, does not create a substantial connection to a product liability claim arising from a tire failure in a vehicle that never entered Montana through Ford’s distribution channels.

The Court’s decision also ignores the critical distinction between general and specific jurisdiction. *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014), reaffirmed that specific jurisdiction requires a “direct relationship” between the defendant’s forum-related conduct and the plaintiff’s claim. Here, the tire failure occurred on a Montana highway, but the tire was not manufactured or sold by Ford in Montana. The vehicle was not sold in Montana, nor was it ever serviced by Ford in Montana. The causal chain between Ford’s part sales and the plaintiff’s injury is too attenuated to satisfy due process. *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987), emphasized that “the relationship between the defendant, the forum, and the litigation must be such that the defendant should reasonably anticipate being haled into court.” Ford had no reason to anticipate that a tire failure in a vehicle it never sold in Montana would give rise to litigation in that state.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Conway v. Benefis Health System*, 2013 MT 73, 369 Mont. 309, 297 P.3d 1200, and *Newbury v. State Farm*, 2008 MT 156, 343 Mont. 279, 184 P.3d 1021, is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance coverage and the collateral source rule, not personal jurisdiction. *Conway* held that a plaintiff could not recover the difference between what an insurer paid and what a provider billed, but it did not address whether a defendant could be subject to jurisdiction based on unrelated commercial activity. *Newbury* similarly dealt with the interpretation of insurance policy language, not the constitutional limits on jurisdiction. The Montana Supreme Court’s attempt to analogize part sales to the sale of medical services is legally incoherent. The sale of a tire part in Montana is not analogous to the provision of medical care, which is inherently tied to the patient’s location and treatment.

Moreover, the Court’s decision creates a dangerous precedent. If a manufacturer can be haled into court in any state where it sells replacement parts—even if those parts are unrelated to the product at issue—then any nonresident defendant with a nationwide distribution network risks being subject to jurisdiction in every state where it sells components. This would undermine the predictability and fairness that due process demands. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 924 (2011), held that “a defendant’s mere ‘random, fortuitous, or attenuated’ contacts with a forum are insufficient to justify the exercise of jurisdiction.” Ford’s part sales in Montana were precisely such contacts—random and fortuitous, with no causal link to the injury.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed” itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana by selling parts is legally untenable. *Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 789 (1984), recognized that “a defendant’s conduct may be so directed at the forum state that it is reasonable to expect the defendant to be haled into court there.” But Ford’s part sales were not directed at Montana; they were part of a national distribution system. The plaintiff’s claim does not arise from those sales. The Due Process Clause does not permit jurisdiction based on “a mere possibility of harm” or “a speculative connection.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court’s exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company violates the Due Process Clause. The judgment should be reversed.
