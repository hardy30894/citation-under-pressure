I. Erdman Accumulated the Requisite Hours of Service.

The District Court erred in holding that Erdman failed to qualify because she lacked 1,250 hours. All time necessarily spent in an employer's service constitutes hours worked. Tennessee Coal, Iron & R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944) ("workweek" embraces "all time spent in physical or mental exertion, whether burdensome or not"). Extra hours Erdman performed outside the office, regularly authorized by her supervisor as compensable or usable as comp time, therefore count toward eligibility. Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co., 328 U.S. 680, 690-91 (1946) ("the statutory workweek includes all time during which an employee is necessarily required to be on the employer's premises, on duty or at a prescribed workplace").

Nationwide's later prohibition on comp time cannot retroactively exclude those hours already performed. Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 137 (1944) ("Whether in a concrete case such time falls within or without the Act is a question of fact to be resolved by appropriate findings of the trial court"). Erdman's consistent performance of extra duties, approved for years, created hours of service that Nationwide cannot now disavow.

II. The Record Precludes Summary Disposition on Eligibility.

Genuine issues remain whether Erdman's total hours met the threshold once all compensable time is included. Walling v. Helmerich & Payne, Inc., 323 U.S. 37, 40 (1944) (hours worked turn on "the actual circumstances of employment"). Viewing the facts favorably to Erdman, her part-time schedule plus authorized extra hours satisfied the statutory requirement. The District Court's contrary conclusion rests on an incomplete reckoning of time spent in Nationwide's service.

III. Firing for Asserting Statutory Rights Violates Established Protections.

Discharge for requesting protected leave constitutes unlawful interference. See NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 45-46 (1937) (employees may not be penalized for asserting rights secured by federal labor statutes). Erdman's announcement that she would seek leave if vacation were denied, followed promptly by termination, raises the inference that the request itself prompted the adverse action. The employer's stated reasons, disputed on the record, do not insulate the discharge from scrutiny under these principles.
