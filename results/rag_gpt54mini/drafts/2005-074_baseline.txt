**Argument**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 reentry.**

Congress did not clearly provide that the reinstatement statute reaches aliens who illegally reentered before the statute’s effective date. The Court has repeatedly required clear statutory language before construing immigration legislation to attach new legal consequences to past conduct. In **Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson, 353 U.S. 685 (1957)**, the Court upheld application of the 1952 Act to pre-enactment facts only because Congress expressly said the provision applied “notwithstanding” prior entry and prior facts. **Id.** at 690-91. Likewise, in **Mulcahey v. Catalanotte, 353 U.S. 692 (1957)**, the Court enforced deportation based on a 1925 conviction only because the Act by its terms made deportable any alien “who at any time has been convicted” of the specified offense. **Id.** at 694-95. The key feature in those cases was express statutory reach. See also **Mrvica v. Esperdy, 376 U.S. 560 (1964)** (denying relief where prior departure made the alien ineligible under the continuous-residence requirement).

Section 241(a)(5) lacks the kind of explicit retroactive command present in **Lehmann** and **Mulcahey**. It does not state that it applies to aliens who reentered before 1997. It instead authorizes reinstatement when an alien “has reentered” illegally after prior removal. That phrasing naturally reaches post-effective-date reentries and ongoing unlawful presence, not completed reentries years before Congress acted. When Congress wants immigration rules to apply to earlier conduct, it knows how to say so. **Lehmann**, 353 U.S. at 690-91; **Mulcahey**, 353 U.S. at 694-95.

Nor does **Fernandez-Vargas’s** long residence after reentry transform his case into one Congress clearly swept up. The government’s reading would make the statute reach back to legal consequences fixed at the moment of reentry, even though the statute became law fifteen years later. That is precisely the sort of backward-looking construction the Court has rejected absent unmistakable text. See **Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 265-73 (1994)**.

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive.**

Even if the statute can be read to cover pre-1997 reentries, it may not constitutionally or statutorily be applied retroactively here. A statute has retroactive effect when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” **Landgraf**, 511 U.S. at 270. Fernandez-Vargas illegally reentered in 1982, but he did so at a time when he remained eligible, at least in principle, to seek discretionary relief after later changes in his circumstances. The reinstatement statute eliminated that avenue and foreclosed adjustment of status once he married a U.S. citizen and sought relief in 2001.

This Court’s retroactivity cases in immigration underscore the point. In **INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289 (2001)**, the Court held that repeal of discretionary relief could not be applied to aliens who had pleaded guilty before the repeal because they likely relied on the availability of relief in deciding whether to accept conviction. **Id.** at 321-24. The Court emphasized that retroactive elimination of relief is especially troubling where a noncitizen has structured conduct in reliance on existing law. **Id.** at 323-26. Similarly here, Fernandez-Vargas’s ability to remain and later seek adjustment was a legal consequence attached to his pre-1997 status, and the 1997 statute cut off that possibility after the fact.

The Tenth Circuit’s “no protectable expectation” rationale is too narrow. Retroactivity does not turn only on settled expectations; it also turns on whether the new statute changes the legal consequences of past acts. **Landgraf**, 511 U.S. at 269-70. Fernandez-Vargas’s past unlawful reentry became the basis for a new, mandatory bar to relief only after Congress acted. That is retroactive application. **St. Cyr**, 533 U.S. at 321-24.

The government will rely on **Mrvica** and **Lehmann**, but those cases cut the other way. In both, Congress had expressly addressed pre-enactment facts. **Mrvica**, 376 U.S. at 562-63; **Lehmann**, 353 U.S. at 690-91. Here, Congress did not. And unlike **Mulcahey**, where the statute itself made prior convictions deportable “at any time,” the reinstatement statute contains no comparably clear retroactive instruction. **Mulcahey**, 353 U.S. at 694-95. Absent that clarity, the Court should not infer that Congress intended to strip relief from an alien whose critical conduct was completed years before the statute took effect.

**Conclusion**

The Court should hold that 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. At minimum, the Court should hold that applying it to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive. Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed.
