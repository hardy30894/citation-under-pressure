**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed because the Eighth Amendment does not categorically bar the execution of mentally retarded persons who commit capital murder. This Court has never held that evolving standards of decency require such a prohibition, and the historical understanding of the Eighth Amendment confirms that mental retardation does not render a defendant categorically exempt from capital punishment.

**I. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Exempt Mentally Retarded Persons from Capital Punishment.**

This Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is measured by “the punishment which may be imposed under the statute,” not by the characteristics of the offender. *Rakes v. United States*, 212 U.S. 55, 58 (1909). In *Good Shot v. United States*, 179 U.S. 87, 93 (1900), the Court held that a conviction for a capital crime remains a capital conviction even where the jury qualifies the punishment because “the test is not the punishment which is imposed, but that which may be imposed under the statute.” Here, the Virginia capital murder statute authorized the death penalty for Atkins’s crimes, and the jury determined that sentence after considering his mental capacity. No categorical bar precludes that result.

The historical understanding of the Eighth Amendment confirms that the death penalty is not inherently disproportionate for mentally retarded offenders. In *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135-36 (1879), this Court explained that the prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments reaches only “punishments of torture” or those “degrading to the dignity of human beings.” The execution of mentally retarded persons does not fall into that narrow category. Moreover, in *McElvaine v. Brush*, 142 U.S. 155, 159 (1891), the Court declined to expand the Eighth Amendment beyond its core historical meaning, holding that the Amendment “is directed against the employment of such punishments as are cruel and unusual, not against the infliction of any punishment which is authorized by law.”

**II. The Evolving Standards of Decency Do Not Support a Categorical Ban.**

This Court’s precedents require that any assessment of evolving standards be grounded in objective evidence, not judicial intuition. *Kelley v. Oregon*, 273 U.S. 589, 592 (1927) (rejecting as “frivolous” a claim that a state’s capital sentencing procedures violated the Federal Constitution where no objective federal right was implicated). At the time of Atkins’s trial, only a small minority of jurisdictions prohibited the execution of mentally retarded offenders. No national consensus existed. The Virginia legislature had not enacted such a ban, and the jury—having heard expert testimony regarding Atkins’s mental capacity—weighed that evidence and imposed the death sentence. This Court should not substitute its judgment for that of state legislatures and juries.

**III. Atkins’s Mental Retardation Does Not Render Him Constitutionally Incompetent for Execution.**

This Court has never held that mental retardation alone renders a defendant incompetent for execution. *Palmer v. Ashe*, 342 U.S. 134, 137 (1951), addressed a defendant’s claim of mental incompetence at the time of sentencing, but the Court emphasized that “the question of mental competency is for the state court in the first instance.” Here, the Virginia courts had the opportunity to consider Atkins’s mental capacity at both sentencing proceedings, and they found no constitutional bar to his execution.

Nor does the sentencing procedure violate due process. *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 608-09 (1967), held that a defendant subject to a separate sentencing proceeding is entitled to notice and an opportunity to be heard. Atkins received that process. The jury heard expert testimony regarding his mental retardation from a defense forensic psychologist, and the Commonwealth was permitted to rebut that evidence. The jury then returned a verdict of death. This procedure fully comports with the requirements of due process.

**IV. The Jury’s Verdict Should Be Respected.**

The jury in this case considered Atkins’s mental capacity and nonetheless concluded that death was the appropriate sentence. This Court has consistently held that the jury’s role in capital sentencing is entitled to substantial deference. *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 742 (1948) (noting that the jury’s ability to qualify a verdict to avoid capital punishment “is a matter of legislative grace”). Here, the Virginia jury could have imposed a life sentence but chose instead to impose death. That determination is not rendered invalid by Atkins’s mental retardation.

**V. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require This Court to Overrule Penry.**

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, this Court held that mental retardation is a mitigating factor that must be considered by the sentencer, but the Court declined to hold that the Eighth Amendment categorically bars execution of mentally retarded persons. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in affirming Atkins’s sentence. Nothing in this Court’s intervening jurisprudence compels a different result.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.

Respectfully submitted.
