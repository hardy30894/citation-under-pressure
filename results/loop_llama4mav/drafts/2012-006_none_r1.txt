The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. As established by the Supreme Court, when a defendant withdraws from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial, as the government met its burden.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the government must prove a defendant's participation in a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt. In Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), the Court emphasized that "the burden is on the Government to prove that the conspiracy... continued into the period covered by the statute of limitations." (Id. at 216). This principle was reaffirmed in Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391 (1957), where the Court held that the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, and that the defendant's participation in it was not limited to a period outside the limitations period. (Id. at 396-397).

Furthermore, the Court has stressed the importance of proving a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant period. In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court stated that "the proof must show that [the defendant] was a member of the conspiracy... during the period covered by the indictment." (Id. at 752-753). This requirement is consistent with the Court's earlier holding in United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205 (1940), that mere knowledge of a conspiracy is not enough to prove membership.

The government's burden is not alleviated by a defendant's withdrawal from a conspiracy. As the Court noted in Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), a defendant's withdrawal from a conspiracy does not necessarily mean that they are not liable for the conspiracy, but the government must still prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Id. at 369-370). Similarly, in Blumenthal v. United States, 332 U.S. 539 (1947), the Court held that a defendant's participation in a conspiracy must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt, and that the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Id. at 556-557).

In Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), the Court established that a defendant's liability for a conspiracy is not limited to the period during which they were actively participating, but the government must still prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. (Id. at 646-647). The Court's decision in Lutwak v. United States, 344 U.S. 604 (1953), citing with approval United States v. Cohen, 197 F.2d 26 (1952), further supports the government's burden to prove that the conspiracy continued and that the defendant was a member of it during the period covered by the indictment.

In this case, the government presented sufficient evidence to prove that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period, and that the defendants were members of it during that time. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial. The Supreme Court's precedents, including Fiswick, Grunewald, Kotteakos, Falcone, Blumenthal, Hyde, and Pinkerton, establish that the government bears the burden of proving a defendant's membership in a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period, and that this burden is not alleviated by a defendant's withdrawal from the conspiracy.
