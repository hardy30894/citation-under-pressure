The conferral of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A plain reading of the relevant statutes reveals that "admission" and "lawful status" are distinct concepts.

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). The Third Circuit has interpreted "admission" in accordance with this statutory definition, holding that it involves physical entrance into the country. Hanif v. Att'y Gen., 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); Taveras v. Att'y Gen., 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). In contrast, "lawful status" describes the type of permission to be present in the United States that an individual has. Gomez v. Lynch, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016).

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien with TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for adjustment-of-status purposes under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). However, this provision does not equate to an "admission" under § 1255(a), which requires that an alien be "inspected and admitted or paroled" to be eligible for adjustment of status. The statutory text, context, and structure support this distinction.

Congress has created exceptions to the admission requirement for certain aliens, such as "special immigrants" described by § 1101(a)(27)(J) and aliens eligible for a visa. See 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i). The absence of a similar exception for TPS recipients suggests that Congress intentionally excluded them from the exception. I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987).

Furthermore, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a)'s admission requirement would render part of § 1254a superfluous. Section 1254a(h) enables Congress to pass special legislation adjusting the status of aliens receiving TPS only by a supermajority of the Senate. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h)(2). If § 1254a(f)(4) were read to facilitate permanent residence for TPS recipients, it would undermine § 1254a(h)(2)'s supermajority requirement. Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

The Sixth and Ninth Circuits have reached a contrary conclusion, holding that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission. Flores v. Barr, 930 F.3d 773 (6th Cir. 2019); Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017). However, their interpretations are unpersuasive, as they fail to recognize the distinction between "status" and "admission." Moreover, their analyses ignore the fact that TPS recipients who entered the country legally are still eligible for adjustment of status.

In conclusion, the text, context, structure, and purpose of §§ 1254a and 1255 demonstrate that a grant of TPS does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under § 1255. The Third Circuit's interpretation is consistent with the statutory language and the distinction between "admission" and "status." Therefore, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed is not the correct outcome; instead, the argument should be that the Third Circuit's decision should be upheld because it correctly interpreted the relevant statutes.
