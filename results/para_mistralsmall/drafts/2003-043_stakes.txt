**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS UNDER § 6502(a)**

The plain text of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) authorizes collection only where “the assessment of any tax ... has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto.” The statute does not permit the IRS to extend the limitations period for one taxpayer by assessing a related but distinct taxpayer. *See United States v. Galletti*, 298 F.3d 1107, 1115 (9th Cir. 2002) (holding that “the assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership”). This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction that tax statutes “must be strictly construed in favor of taxpayers.” *See United States v. Merriam*, 263 U.S. 179, 187 (1923).

The IRS’s contrary position—that a single assessment against a partnership suffices to extend the limitations period for all partners—finds no support in the statutory scheme. Section 6203 requires that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer,” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1, and the term “taxpayer” is defined to include “an individual” as well as a “partnership,” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14). Thus, an assessment must identify the specific taxpayer against whom it is made. *See Coson v. United States*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien is invalid where the underlying tax was not assessed against the individual taxpayer).

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners, thereby extending the limitations period for all. *Id.* at 562. Here, by contrast, no assessment was ever made against the individual partners. The Ninth Circuit has squarely rejected the IRS’s attempt to extend the limitations period for partners without individualized assessments. *See Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1115.

**II. CALIFORNIA PARTNERSHIP LAW DOES NOT ALLOW THE IRS TO COLLECT FROM PARTNERS WITHOUT A JUDGMENT**

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners. *See Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* (“A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.”). To reach a partner’s assets, the IRS must first obtain a judgment against the partner. *See id.*; *Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V* (“[A] partner must be individually named and served ... in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”).

The IRS has not obtained such a judgment here. The statute of limitations for bringing a collection action expired three years after the partners filed their returns. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Because the IRS failed to timely assess or sue the partners, its claim is time-barred. *See Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1117.

**III. THE IRS’S CLAIMS ARE UNENFORCEABLE IN BANKRUPTCY**

Under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), a bankruptcy court must disallow any claim that is “unenforceable against the debtor ... under applicable law.” Here, the IRS’s claims are unenforceable because the statute of limitations has expired and no individualized assessment or judgment was obtained against the partners. *See Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1117.

The IRS’s attempt to circumvent these requirements by relying on the partnership assessment fails. The assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for the partners, and California law requires a separate judgment to collect from partners. *See id.* at 1115-17.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s claims are time-barred and unenforceable. The judgment below should be affirmed.
