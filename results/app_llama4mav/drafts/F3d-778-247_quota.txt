ARGUMENT

I. The Search Warrant Lacked a Sufficient Nexus to the Place Being Searched

The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant be supported by probable cause and particularly describe the place to be searched and the items to be seized. United States v. Vigeant, 176 F.3d 565, 569 (1st Cir. 1999). To establish probable cause, there must be a "nexus ... between the items to be seized and the place to be searched." United States v. Feliz, 182 F.3d 82, 86 (1st Cir. 1999) (quoting United States v. Procopio, 88 F.3d 21, 29 (1st Cir. 1996)). The affidavit supporting the warrant must provide a "substantial basis" for concluding that evidence of a crime will be found at the place to be searched. Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983).

Here, the affidavit failed to establish a sufficient nexus between the items to be seized and Joubert's parents' home. The affidavit relied heavily on SJ's statements that Joubert had become "anxious" and "very concerned" about destroying his computer's hard drive and had torn apart the computer tower. However, SJ did not see Joubert discard the computer components, and there was no evidence that Joubert had stored any incriminating evidence at his parents' home. The officer-affiant's conclusory statement that "persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of ... minors often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims" is insufficient to establish a nexus. See United States v. Weber, 923 F.2d 1338, 1345 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that an affidavit's "boilerplate" language about the habits of child molesters was insufficient to establish probable cause).

The First Circuit has held that a warrant is invalid if the affidavit fails to provide a sufficient nexus between the items to be seized and the place to be searched. See United States v. Khounsavanh, 113 F.3d 279, 283-84 (1st Cir. 1997). In this case, the affidavit's failure to establish a nexus between the items to be seized and Joubert's parents' home renders the warrant invalid.

II. The District Court Abused its Discretion in Admitting Testimony of Uncharged Child Molestation

Federal Rule of Evidence 414 allows the admission of evidence of uncharged child molestation in a criminal case where the defendant is accused of child molestation. However, the rule is subject to the constraints of Rule 403, which requires that the probative value of the evidence not be substantially outweighed by its prejudicial effect. United States v. LeMay, 260 F.3d 1018, 1026 (9th Cir. 2001).

The district court erred in admitting testimony from multiple witnesses about Joubert's uncharged child molestation. The testimony was cumulative and highly prejudicial, and its probative value was limited. See United States v. Enjady, 134 F.3d 1427, 1433 (10th Cir. 1998) (holding that the admission of multiple witnesses' testimony about uncharged child molestation was an abuse of discretion). The district court's failure to carefully consider the potential prejudicial effect of the testimony was an abuse of discretion. See United States v. Guardia, 135 F.3d 1326, 1331 (10th Cir. 1998).

III. The Commerce Clause Supports Application of the Federal Criminal Statutes

Joubert's argument that the Commerce Clause cannot support application of the federal criminal statutes under which he was indicted is without merit. The First Circuit has held that Congress has the authority under the Commerce Clause to regulate the production of child pornography, even if the production is intrastate. United States v. Morales-de Jesus, 372 F.3d 6, 12-13 (1st Cir. 2004) (citing United States v. Morales, 344 F.3d 64, 68-69 (1st Cir. 2003)). The Supreme Court has also upheld the constitutionality of the federal child pornography statutes under the Commerce Clause. See United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 561-62 (1995) (distinguishing the federal child pornography statutes from the statute at issue in Lopez); see also Gonzales v. Raich, 545 U.S. 1, 22 (2005) (holding that Congress has the authority to regulate intrastate activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce).

IV. The 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Reasonable

The district court's imposition of a 480-month sentence was substantively unreasonable. The sentence was significantly above the Guidelines range, and the district court failed to provide a sufficient justification for the upward variance. See Gall v. United States, 552 U.S. 38, 50 (2007) (holding that a sentencing court must provide a sufficient justification for a sentence outside the Guidelines range). The First Circuit has held that a sentence is substantively unreasonable if it is "based on an unreasonable application of the factors listed in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)." United States v. Martin, 520 F.3d 87, 92 (1st Cir. 2008). In this case, the district court failed to provide a sufficient justification for the sentence, and the sentence was therefore substantively unreasonable.

For the foregoing reasons, Joubert's conviction and sentence should be reversed.
