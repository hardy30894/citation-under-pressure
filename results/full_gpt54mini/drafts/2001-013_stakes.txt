**Argument**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches private workplaces unless another federal agency has actually exercised authority over the specific working conditions at issue. Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA coverage only “with respect to which other Federal agencies…exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Supreme Court has construed that provision to require more than a general regulatory presence; OSHA is displaced only when another agency has exercised authority over the relevant occupational safety and health hazards. *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 243–47 (2002).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot stand because it treated the Coast Guard’s broad maritime responsibilities as an across-the-board exemption for all vessels. That approach conflicts with the text of § 4(b)(1) and with this Court’s admonition that preemption depends on actual exercise of authority over the working conditions in question. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243–44. The Coast Guard’s general power “to administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property” does not itself foreclose OSHA. 14 U.S.C. § 2; *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 244–45.

This Court’s decisions show why. In *Southern Railway Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Commission*, 539 F.2d 335 (4th Cir. 1976), the court recognized that § 4(b)(1) turns on whether another agency has exercised authority over the cited hazard. The same principle appears in *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119, 121–23 (2d Cir. 1978), which held that OSHA is displaced only to the extent another agency has addressed the specific working condition. And in *Secretary of Labor v. OSHRC (Tidewater Pacific, Inc.)*, 701 F.2d 832, 836–38 (9th Cir. 1983), the court explained that limited regulation does not create a comprehensive exemption from OSHA.

Here, the Coast Guard had not promulgated regulations governing the drilling-related safety hazards that produced the explosion aboard Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that the vessel was uninspected and that the Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to discrete subjects such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and certain equipment requirements. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 245–46. That limited regulatory scheme is not the sort of comprehensive coverage that triggers § 4(b)(1) preemption. Id. at 246–47. As the Supreme Court observed, “the Coast Guard has not taken the workplace out of OSHA’s jurisdiction” merely by regulating some maritime safety matters. *Id.* at 247.

The Fifth Circuit also erred by reasoning that a vessel’s status as “uninspected” is irrelevant because OSHA would otherwise produce inconsistent regulation. But Congress resolved that concern in § 4(b)(1) by tying OSHA displacement to actual federal regulation, not to abstract policy preferences. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243–44. If the Coast Guard had exercised authority over the particular working conditions cited by OSHA, the citations would be preempted. But where, as here, the Coast Guard’s regulations do not address the relevant hazards, OSHA retains authority. *Id.* at 247–48.

The Government’s position is reinforced by the Supreme Court’s repeated instruction that OSHA should be construed to avoid leaving workers unprotected in regulatory gaps. In *National Ass’n of Home Builders v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 551 U.S. 644, 666–67 (2007), the Court rejected implied exemptions untethered to statutory text. Likewise, in *Chao*, the Court refused to infer a maritime carveout broader than Congress enacted. 534 U.S. at 248–49.

Because the Coast Guard had not exercised authority over the cited working conditions on Rig 52, OSHA properly issued the citations. The judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be reversed.
