**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Plaintiffs’ Expert Witnesses Without Affording Them an Adequate Opportunity to Comply with Discovery Orders.**

A trial court’s discretion to impose discovery sanctions is not unlimited. In *Societe Internationale v. Rogers*, 357 U.S. 197, 209 (1958), the Supreme Court held that dismissal or default is improper unless the failure to comply is due to “willfulness, bad faith, or any fault” on the part of the party. Here, Plaintiffs’ failure to produce experts for deposition by July 30, 1993, stemmed not from contumacy but from their experts’ inability to finalize opinions within the truncated timeline—a problem Plaintiffs candidly raised in their May 4, 1993 motion for a protective order. The district court acknowledged this concern yet imposed the strict deadline and later struck four experts without finding willfulness. *See Hammond Packing Co. v. Arkansas*, 212 U.S. 322, 350-51 (1909) (sanctions must be “proportionate to the default”). Because the court did not find—and the record does not support—any deliberate obstruction, striking the experts was an abuse of discretion. *Cf. Hill v. Chicago & E.R. Co.*, 129 U.S. 170, 174-75 (1889) (courts may not penalize parties for delays they did not cause).

**II. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under *Daubert* Because That Standard, Announced in 1993, Was Improperly Applied Retroactively to Undermine Expert Opinions Formed Before Its Issuance.**

The district court applied *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993), to exclude Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson. However, as the Supreme Court has long held, procedural rules should not be applied retroactively to the substantial detriment of litigants who relied on prior standards. *See Union Pac. R.R. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913) (declining to apply a new rule where it would “disturb vested rights”). Before *Daubert*, the admissibility of expert testimony in this circuit was governed by the “general acceptance” standard of *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), and the liberal approach of *United States v. Hiss*, 88 F. Supp. 559 (S.D.N.Y. 1950) (allowing expert opinion if it “may aid the jury”). Plaintiffs’ experts formulated their opinions in reliance on *Frye* and the pre-*Daubert* regime. Applying *Daubert* retroactively to exclude their testimony deprived Plaintiffs of the fair notice required by due process. *See Chicago & E.R. Co. v. Minnesota*, 134 U.S. 418, 460 (1890) (retroactive application of new legal standards violates due process). Absent evidence that *Daubert* announced a routine procedural change, rather than a substantive shift in admissibility standards, retroactive application was error. *Compare Ex Parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 71 (1949) (procedural changes generally apply to pending cases).

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment When Genuine Issues of Material Fact Remained Regarding Causation and Injury.**

Summary judgment is proper only when “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact.” *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Even if the district court properly excluded the four experts whose testimony was struck, and Drs. Schroeder and Watson, Plaintiffs retained the ability to prove causation through other evidence. The record includes affidavits and deposition testimony from Plaintiffs themselves describing their exposure to chemicals and subsequent injuries. This evidence, combined with the remaining expert reports, created a genuine dispute. *See Tiller v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R.*, 318 U.S. 54, 68 (1943) (credibility determinations are for the jury). By resolving these factual disputes in Defendants’ favor, the district court invaded the province of the jury. *See Brady v. Southern Ry. Co.*, 320 U.S. 476, 479-80 (1943) (summary judgment inappropriate where “the evidence is such that reasonable men might draw differing conclusions”).

**IV. The District Court’s Sanction of Striking Four Experts—Effectively a Dismissal—Was Excessive and an Abuse of Discretion.**

Striking expert testimony operates as a severe sanction, akin to dismissal or default. *See Hammond Packing Co.*, 212 U.S. at 350-51. In *Hovey v. Elliott*, 167 U.S. 409, 414 (1897), the Supreme Court reversed a default judgment entered as a discovery sanction because the defaulting party was not guilty of willful contempt. Similarly here, Plaintiffs’ failure to meet the July 30 deadline was not willful; they had sought a protective order before the deadline expired. The district court had less drastic alternatives—such as extending the deadline or holding a hearing on the experts’ readiness. *Cf. Societe Internationale*, 357 U.S. at 209. Because the sanction effectively terminated Plaintiffs’ case on the merits, it was impermissible absent a finding of bad faith.

**V. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s orders striking Plaintiffs’ experts and granting summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
