**Argument**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly declined to confine its plain-error prejudice inquiry to the four corners of the jury trial record. Plain-error review is designed to determine whether an error actually affected the defendant’s substantial rights and whether correction is necessary to protect the fairness and integrity of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936); *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 107 (1945). That inquiry is not an abstract evidentiary sufficiency test; it asks whether the appellate court can say, on the whole record before it, that the error likely mattered. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734–35 (1993), is later authority, but the underlying principle long predates it: appellate courts have always exercised discretion to notice and correct serious errors where justice so requires. *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658–59 (1896); *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936).

This Court has repeatedly held that appellate review is not limited to the evidence actually submitted to the factfinder when the issue on appeal concerns prejudice, harmlessness, or whether an error infected the proceeding as a whole. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946), the Court explained that an appellate court must determine whether the error had “substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the outcome by considering the record as a whole. Although *Kotteakos* addressed harmless error, its methodology is the same one plain-error review employs when asking whether an admitted error affected substantial rights. See *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160; *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. at 107.

The same is true in contexts where the Court has evaluated whether an error was prejudicial in light of matters outside the narrow trial proof. In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), the Court held that constitutional error may be harmless only if the reviewing court can declare it harmless beyond a reasonable doubt on the basis of the full record. In *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), the Court rejected a formalistic approach and asked whether there was “a reasonable possibility that the evidence complained of might have contributed to the conviction.” Those decisions would make little sense if appellate courts were barred from consulting the entire record to assess whether an error was actually consequential.

Greer’s contrary rule would force courts to ignore information that plainly bears on whether a defendant knew his status, even though the question on plain-error review is not whether the government proved the omitted element to the jury, but whether the omission affected the verdict and whether reversal is necessary to avoid a miscarriage of justice. *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160; *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658–59. A court deciding whether an error undermined the integrity of the proceedings has always been entitled to consider the record as a whole, including uncontroverted facts and the defendant’s own admissions. See *United States v. Johnson*, 320 U.S. 42, 46–47 (1943) (appellate court may consider the entire record in assessing whether error was prejudicial); *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80–82 (1942) (reviewing record as a whole for prejudice); *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (reversal warranted where misconduct “so infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process”).

Nothing in the historic plain-error doctrine suggests an artificial limitation to the evidence presented at trial. To the contrary, the point of appellate discretion is to prevent reversal where the error is technical and nonprejudicial, while ensuring correction where the error threatens the judicial process’s legitimacy. *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658–59; *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. That is especially true where the omitted element concerns a fact the defendant almost certainly knew. A reviewing court may look to the entire record to determine whether there is any real prospect that the defendant would have insisted on trial or obtained a different result absent the error. See *Johnson*, 320 U.S. at 46–47; *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 765.

Greer’s proposed restriction also conflicts with longstanding appellate practice in criminal cases. Courts of review have not treated the jury trial record as a sealed box; they have long examined the record as a whole to determine whether justice has miscarried. *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88; *Glasser*, 315 U.S. at 80–82; *Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 24. The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted consistently with settled principles in consulting Greer’s prior convictions and the complete record in assessing prejudice and the fairness of allowing his conviction to stand.

Because plain-error review asks whether the error affected substantial rights and the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings, and because that inquiry has always been conducted on the full record, the judgment should be affirmed. *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160; *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658–59; *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 765; *Chapman*, 386 U.S. at 24.
