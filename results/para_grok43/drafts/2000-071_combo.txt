The school’s exclusion of the Good News Club’s proposed meetings did not abridge free speech. A state may reserve its property for the purposes for which it was created and need not open its facilities to every form of expression. See Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). Milford’s community-use policy permits only social, civic, and recreational uses; it does not create an open forum for religious worship or instruction. See Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 554 (1965) (government may enforce reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions that leave open ample alternative channels).

The Club’s activities—opening prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories followed by invitations to accept Christ as personal Savior, and distribution of missionary materials—constitute religious instruction and worship, not the discussion of secular morals from a religious viewpoint. See Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 223 (1963) (“the State may not establish a ‘religion of secularism’ by opposing or showing hostility to religion”). Permitting such use would require the school to furnish its premises and implicit endorsement for sectarian teaching. See McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948) (released-time religious classes on school property violate the Establishment Clause).

The Establishment Clause forbids any state practice that advances or inhibits religion. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947) (“Neither a state nor the Federal Government can … pass laws which aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.”). It likewise prohibits official participation in religious exercises. See Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 430 (1962). Allowing the Club’s meetings would cross that line.

Even if the denial implicated speech interests, the school’s interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies a compelling justification. See Epperson v. Arkansas, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968) (state may not use its public-school system to advance religious doctrine). The policy applies neutrally to all religious instruction, whether Christian or otherwise. See Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940) (states may regulate conduct that threatens public order or the rights of others).

The Club remains free to conduct its meetings in private facilities or after hours at churches, as it did before seeking school access. See Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 166-67 (1944) (parental religious rights do not include the right to use public property for proselytizing). The judgment below should be affirmed.
