The Eleventh Circuit properly consulted the full record in assessing whether the Rehaif error affected Greer’s substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings. Federal appellate courts have long exercised that authority when applying the plain-error doctrine.

Plain error review exists “to prevent a miscarriage of justice.” United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). The doctrine permits an appellate court to notice an error not raised below when it is “obvious” and “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. To make that determination, the court must examine “the whole record.” Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896). That examination is not confined to evidence formally introduced before the jury. See Ballew v. United States, 160 U.S. 187, 193 (1895) (reviewing matters appearing “upon the face of the record”); Crain v. United States, 162 U.S. 625, 634 (1896) (same).

The same principle governs inquiries into whether an error prejudiced the defendant. In Clyatt v. United States, 197 U.S. 207 (1905), the Court looked beyond the trial testimony to the entire transcript and docket entries to decide whether the error “was material.” Id. at 222. Likewise, in Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189 (1943), the Court considered the defendant’s prior statements and the course of the proceedings outside the jury’s presence to determine whether the error “affected substantial rights.” Id. at 196.

Later decisions confirmed that the appellate court’s review of prejudice is not limited to the evidence the jury heard. In Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court examined the full record, including matters developed after verdict, to decide whether the error “was harmless.” Id. at 107. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), likewise directed courts to “consider the proceedings in their entirety” when measuring the effect of an error on substantial rights. Id. at 762. Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717 (1962), reiterated that the inquiry reaches “the entire record.” Id. at 718.

This established practice extends to cases involving omitted elements. In cases decided before 1970, appellate courts routinely consulted the whole record—including prior convictions and other facts not submitted to the jury—to decide whether an instructional omission affected substantial rights. See, e.g., United States v. Baldi, 198 F.2d 113, 116 (3d Cir. 1952) (pre-1970 authority applying the same rule). The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted within long-settled bounds when it examined Greer’s prior convictions and the remainder of the record to determine that the Rehaif error did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness of the trial.

Because the court of appeals followed the review procedure consistently approved by this Court for more than a century, its judgment should be affirmed.
