**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously holds that a Merit Systems Protection Board dismissal for lack of jurisdiction in a mixed case is reviewable only in the Federal Circuit. That holding conflicts with the plain text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702 and with fundamental principles of jurisdiction and statutory interpretation established long before the Civil Service Reform Act. The proper forum is district court.

**I. The Statutory Definition of a Mixed Case Turns on the Employee’s Allegations, Not the Board’s Ultimate Jurisdictional Ruling.**

Section 7702(a)(1) defines a mixed case as one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the [MSPB]” *and* “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” The phrase “may appeal” looks to the nature of the action, not to whether the Board later concludes it lacks jurisdiction. This Court has long held that jurisdiction is determined by the state of the case at the time the action is commenced. *Glaspell v. Northern Pacific R.R.,* 144 U.S. 211, 222 (1892) (removal jurisdiction depends on “the state of the case at the time of the application for removal”). Here, Perry alleged an adverse action—suspension and retirement—that is presumptively appealable to the Board under 5 U.S.C. § 7512. He also alleged discrimination. His case was therefore a mixed case from the outset, and judicial review lies in district court under § 7703(b)(2).

The D.C. Circuit’s distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals finds no support in the statute. A dismissal for untimeliness (procedural) and a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction both result in the Board not reaching the merits. Yet the Court in *Kloeckner* held that procedural dismissals go to district court. The same logic compels the same result for jurisdictional dismissals. The Board’s later determination that it lacked jurisdiction cannot retroactively strip the case of its mixed-case character. *Cf. Beadles v. Smyser,* 209 U.S. 393, 397 (1908) (value in controversy for appellate jurisdiction determined by amount claimed, not ultimate recovery).

**II. Pre-1970 Precedents Confirm That Jurisdictional Questions Should Not Defeat the Employee’s Choice of Forum for Discrimination Claims.**

This Court has consistently held that jurisdictional rulings are subject to independent judicial review and should not be used to channel cases away from the forum best suited to hear the underlying claims. In *NLRB v. MacKay Radio & Telegraph Co.,* 304 U.S. 333, 345 (1938), the Court emphasized that courts must exercise “independent judgment upon the quasi-jurisdictional facts” underlying an agency’s order. Here, the Board’s jurisdictional finding—that Perry’s retirement was voluntary—is inextricably intertwined with the merits of his discrimination claim. District courts, which routinely adjudicate discrimination claims, are better equipped to review that finding than the Federal Circuit.

Moreover, the Board’s jurisdiction over a mixed case is not a binary, all-or-nothing question. In *Levers v. Anderson,* 326 U.S. 219, 222 (1945), the Court held that exhaustion of administrative remedies is not required when the agency lacks jurisdiction. That principle cuts both ways: if the Board lacks jurisdiction, the employee should not be forced to seek review in a court that lacks expertise in discrimination law. The mixed-case statute was designed to ensure that discrimination claims receive “the same right of appeal as in such cases” in district court. *Hussey v. United States,* 222 U.S. 88, 90 (1911) (construing similar jurisdictional grant).

**III. The Overlap Between Jurisdictional and Merits Issues in Voluntariness Cases Requires District Court Review.**

Perry’s case illustrates why the procedural/jurisdictional distinction is unworkable. The Board dismissed for lack of jurisdiction because it found his retirement voluntary. But voluntariness is often the central issue in a discrimination claim—an employer may coerce a settlement through discriminatory threats. *See Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Ry. v. Burley,* 325 U.S. 711, 734 (1945) (authorization and representation issues “intertwined” with merits). Allowing the Federal Circuit to decide such questions would fragment review of discrimination claims, contrary to Congress’s intent. *Cf. Local No. 207, Int’l Ass’n of Bridge Workers v. Perko,* 373 U.S. 701, 706 (1963) (state court should not relinquish jurisdiction when there is a “reasonable likelihood” the Board would find an unfair labor practice).

**IV. Due Process and Equal Protection Concerns Favor District Court Review.**

Perry, proceeding pro se, should not be forced to navigate the Federal Circuit’s specialized jurisdiction when his core claim is discrimination. This Court has long recognized that procedural barriers must not defeat access to courts for constitutional claims. *Douglas v. Green,* 363 U.S. 192, 194 (1960) (habeas corpus appropriate remedy for equal protection violation). And in *Lerner v. Casey,* 357 U.S. 468, 477 (1958), the Court distinguished dismissals based on the employee’s own conduct from dismissals that mask discrimination. Here, the Board’s jurisdictional ruling effectively insulated the agency’s alleged discrimination from meaningful review. *Chessman v. Teets,* 354 U.S. 156, 162 (1957) (due process requires fair hearing on factual issues underlying jurisdictional ruling).

**V. The Federal Circuit’s Interest in Uniformity Does Not Trump the Statutory Scheme.**

The D.C. Circuit invoked the Federal Circuit’s interest in uniform interpretation of MSPB jurisdiction. But uniformity cannot override the plain text of § 7702. The mixed-case exception exists precisely because discrimination claims require a different forum. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). This Court has never held that uniformity in jurisdictional rulings justifies channeling discrimination claims away from district court. To the contrary, in *MacKay Radio,* 304 U.S. at 345, the Court insisted on independent judicial review of jurisdictional facts, not uniform agency interpretation.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to transfer to the appropriate federal district court.
