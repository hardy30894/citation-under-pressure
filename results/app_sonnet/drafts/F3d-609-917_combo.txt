**ARGUMENT**

**I. Ray’s State-Law Tort Claims Are Preempted by Federal Aviation Regulation.**

Congress has occupied the field of aviation safety and airline operations so completely that state tort remedies premised on how a carrier manages a delayed flight cannot survive alongside it. When federal regulation is comprehensive, state law that intrudes upon the regulated conduct must yield. *Pennsylvania v. Nelson*, 350 U.S. 497 (1956) (state law preempted where the federal scheme is “so pervasive” that Congress left no room for supplementation). This Court need not find an express command; it is enough that Ray’s theory—premised on American’s staffing, planning, and operational choices during the tarmac delay—“stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress” in centralizing control over air carrier operations. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941).

American recognizes that preemption analysis begins with a presumption favoring the historic police powers of the states, which are not superseded “unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress.” *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). But that presumption is overcome here because Ray’s claims do not sound in the ordinary common-law duties owed by any occupier of property or operator of a vehicle; they attack the very operational judgments—when to deplane passengers, how to allocate ground crew, how to sequence buses—that Congress placed under uniform federal oversight to avoid a patchwork of fifty different tarmac-delay standards. Permitting a jury in Arkansas to fix the standard of care for decisions made on an Austin runway is precisely the kind of conflict *Hines* forbids.

**II. The District Court Correctly Entered Summary Judgment on the False-Imprisonment Claim.**

False imprisonment requires an actual restraint of the plaintiff’s liberty, not merely the plaintiff’s subjective belief that she was confined. The seminal decision in *Whittaker v. Sandford*, 110 Me. 77, 85 A. 399 (1912), is directly on point: a passenger aboard a vessel who was not physically restrained, but who was simply not provided an immediate means of going ashore when she wished, had no claim for false imprisonment because the shipowner’s conduct did not amount to compulsion by force or threat. The same logic controls here. American offered Ray two separate opportunities—via buses summoned to the aircraft—to leave the plane. She was told plainly, “if you want to get off, get off now.” She declined, by her own testimony, for economic reasons unrelated to any act of American. A carrier cannot be held to have imprisoned a passenger who was twice given the keys to the door and chose not to use them.

**III. The District Court Correctly Entered Summary Judgment on the Negligence Claim.**

Even where a defendant’s conduct may be negligent in the abstract, liability attaches only where the harm complained of falls within the zone of foreseeable risk created by that conduct. As Judge Cardozo explained, “[t]he risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed.” *Palsgraf v. Long Island R. Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339, 344, 162 N.E. 99, 100 (1928). Ray’s claimed injury—a single day of stomach upset, for which she sought no medical treatment—cannot be traced to any specific breach by American with the particularity negligence law requires; her own speculation that she became ill because she could not wash her hands is not evidence of causation, only conjecture.

Nor did American fail in the heightened duty owed to passengers. A common carrier is bound to exercise a high degree of care for passenger safety, but it is not an insurer against every discomfort of travel. *Stokes v. Saltonstall*, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 181 (1839). Providing snacks, beverages, and repeated opportunities to deplane during a weather-driven ground stop satisfies that duty as a matter of law; it does not create a jury question merely because the passengers found the experience unpleasant.

Summary judgment was proper because Ray offered no evidence—beyond her own subjective impressions—capable of establishing breach or causation. The purpose of summary judgment “is to separate what is formal or pretended in denial or averment from what is genuine and substantial, so that only the latter may subject a suitor to the burden of a trial.” *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Ray’s claims are exactly the kind of unsubstantiated allegations that provision is designed to screen out before trial.

**IV. The District Court Properly Denied Ray’s Rule 56(f) Motion.**

A continuance under Rule 56(f) is committed to the sound discretion of the district court, and a party seeking one must show specifically what facts further discovery would likely reveal and how those facts would preclude summary judgment—generalized hope is not enough. *The Bros., Inc. v. W.E. Grace Mfg. Co.*, 320 F.2d 594 (5th Cir. 1963). Ray identified no particular discovery that would have altered the undisputed facts of her own deposition testimony describing the delay, the announcements she heard, and her voluntary choice to remain aboard. Because Ray offered only speculation that additional discovery “might” help her case, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the continuance.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be affirmed in part and reversed in part consistent with the arguments above.
