**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings.

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The Clause safeguards against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). This Court has long recognized that the Clause’s protections extend beyond the guilt phase to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to enhance punishment based on prior convictions. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957) (holding that retrial for a greater offense after reversal of a lesser conviction violated double jeopardy).

The California Supreme Court erred in concluding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause’s protections are not limited to capital cases. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88. In *Green*, the Court held that retrial for first-degree murder after reversal of a second-degree murder conviction violated double jeopardy, even though the original trial had not resulted in an acquittal on the greater charge. *Id.* at 190. The Court reasoned that the defendant had already been placed in jeopardy for the greater offense and could not be retried for it. *Id.* at 188. This principle applies with equal force to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to relitigate a prior conviction allegation. *See Murphy v. Massachusetts*, 177 U.S. 155, 158 (1900) (holding that a defendant could not be resentenced to a greater term after reversal of a prior conviction).

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that a sentencing court could consider unadjudicated conduct in imposing a sentence, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Id.* at 245-46. The Court in *Williams* explicitly distinguished between sentencing and the protections of the Double Jeopardy Clause, noting that the Clause’s protections apply to “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *Id.* at 245. The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Green* and other cases on the ground that they involved capital sentencing is unsupported by the text of the Clause or this Court’s precedents. *See United States v. Ewell*, 383 U.S. 116, 121 (1966) (holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings).

II. The prior conviction allegation in this case is an “offense” for double jeopardy purposes.

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of an “offense” after an acquittal or reversal. U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has held that the term “offense” includes sentencing enhancements based on prior convictions. *See Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88. In *Green*, the Court reasoned that the defendant had been placed in jeopardy for the greater offense and could not be retried for it. *Id.* at 188. The same principle applies here. The prior conviction allegation is an “offense” for double jeopardy purposes because it exposes the defendant to additional punishment. *See Cichos v. Indiana*, 385 U.S. 76, 80 (1966) (holding that retrial on a lesser-included offense after reversal violated double jeopardy).

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Sigler v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969), is misplaced. *Sigler* was a per curiam dismissal of a writ of certiorari and did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Id.* at 988. The Court’s dismissal of the writ does not constitute a holding on the merits. *See McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311, 313 (1901) (holding that dismissal of a writ of error does not constitute a decision on the merits). The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Sigler* is therefore unavailing.

III. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this case.

The California Court of Appeal held that the evidence was insufficient to support the prior conviction allegation and that retrial would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause. The California Supreme Court reversed the double jeopardy ruling, holding that the Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. This holding is contrary to this Court’s precedents. *See Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this case.
