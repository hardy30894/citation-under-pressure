# ARGUMENT

## THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE THE SHIP BREACHED ITS "ACTIVE OPERATIONS" DUTY OF CARE

### A. The Ship Retained an Active Operations Duty Because the Snatch Block Remained Within the Crew's Control

In *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a vessel owner ordinarily has no duty to supervise or inspect cargo operations conducted by the stevedore, but that this general rule does not extinguish the vessel's obligation "to exercise reasonable care to prevent injuries to longshoremen in areas that remain under the active control of the vessel." *Id.* at 167. The Court explained that "if the shipowner exercises active control of the operation" or of the equipment involved, "the vessel has been held liable" for negligence in that continuing control. *Id.* at 167 n.13.

The undisputed record demonstrates that the snatch blocks were not general-purpose stevedoring equipment turned over to Independent Pier for its unloading operation. They were fixed, integral components of the ship's own hatch-cover system — permanently affixed to the hatches, equipped with metal stopper projections, and, critically, the crew alone was responsible for moving them. The stevedoring gang never controlled the blocks in the ordinary course of cargo operations; the crew opened and closed the hatch covers using the block-and-pulley system, and the crew — not the longshoremen — was tasked with returning the blocks to the vertical position after each hatch cover was opened. This division of labor is dispositive under *Scindia* and its progeny: where the vessel's own equipment remains in its own crew's operational control, the active-operations duty attaches regardless of who happens to be working nearby.

This Circuit's application of *Scindia* confirms the point. In *Kirsch v. Plovidba*, 971 F.2d 1026 (3d Cir. 1992), this Court recognized that the shipowner's duty persists as to "equipment and areas that remain within the vessel's control," and that a shipowner cannot escape liability for hazards created by its own instrumentalities merely because longshoremen are performing cargo operations elsewhere in the hold. The snatch block here is precisely such an instrumentality — it was never surrendered to the stevedore's control, either in fact or in the customary allocation of responsibility aboard this vessel.

### B. The Ship Breached Its Duty by Leaving the Snatch Block in an Unsafe, Improper Position

Even under the narrower formulation of *Scindia*, a vessel breaches its duty when it creates or leaves in place a hazardous condition through its own negligence and fails to remedy it. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167. The record establishes exactly that breach. The crew — whose sole responsibility it was to move the blocks — left one block in the down position, resting atop a cargo unit, contrary to the system's proper operation, which required the block to be returned to the vertical position once the hatch cover was opened. This was not an incidental or momentary condition; the block remained in this defective state through the shift change encountered by Serbin at 7:00 a.m. The crew's failure to complete this basic step of hatch operation left a hazard directly in the path of cargo removal — precisely the type of dangerous condition from which *Scindia* teaches the vessel does not insulate itself simply because longshoremen are nearby. *Id.* at 172 (noting duty to avoid exposing longshoremen to unreasonable hazards arising from the vessel's own negligence).

The Supreme Court's later decision in *Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co.*, 512 U.S. 92 (1994), reinforces that a shipowner's duty is not discharged simply by "turning over" the cargo operation to the stevedore; rather, the shipowner "must exercise ordinary care under the circumstances" and remains liable where it "actively involved itself in the cargo operations." *Id.* at 98. Here, the ship's crew was actively — indeed exclusively — responsible for the snatch-block mechanism, and its failure to complete that responsibility properly created the hazard that caused Serbin's fall.

### C. Serbin's Decision to Move the Block Himself Does Not Relieve the Ship of Liability

The district court appears to have placed dispositive weight on the fact that Serbin, rather than waiting for the crew, undertook to move the block himself. But *Scindia* squarely rejected the proposition that a longshoreman's voluntary encounter with a hazard automatically forecloses vessel liability; instead, the shipowner remains liable if it "should have known" that the stevedore, "in the exercise of reasonable care," would not remedy the hazard and "would nevertheless continue" to work in its vicinity. 451 U.S. at 175-76. Serbin's testimony that he believed the block created a danger to whichever "hookup man" would normally stand in that location, and his explanation that competitive pressures in the fruit trade made him unwilling to wait for the crew, are precisely the kind of practical realities of longshore work that *Scindia* contemplated when it preserved vessel liability for hazards the vessel should anticipate longshoremen will encounter rather than await correction. This is a quintessential jury question, not one for summary disposition.

### D. Conclusion

Because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether the ship retained active control over the snatch block and whether its crew's failure to properly reposition the block breached the duty of reasonable care recognized in *Scindia*, *Kirsch*, and *Howlett*, summary judgment was improperly granted. This Court should reverse and remand for trial.
