# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 875(c) Must Be Construed to Require Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Statement to Be Understood as a Threat.

The starting point is the settled presumption that Congress intends criminal statutes to require a guilty mind. In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), this Court explained that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion" but is instead "as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will." *Id.* at 250. Congress's silence on a mental-element requirement does not eliminate that requirement; rather, courts must read into a criminal statute "the mental element the common law required." *Id.* at 263. Section 875(c) contains no express *mens rea* term, but under *Morissette* that silence cannot be read as legislative abandonment of a guilty-mind requirement—it must be read as an assumption that the requirement would be understood.

The Third Circuit's objective "reasonable speaker" standard converts §875(c) into precisely the kind of public-welfare offense this Court distinguished in *Morissette* from crimes carrying substantial stigma and imprisonment. Compare *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922) (mens rea presumption may yield for regulatory offenses involving no stigma and light penalties). Elonis faced years of imprisonment and the lasting stigma of a federal threat conviction. That combination—serious punishment for conduct with deep roots in the common law of assault and extortion—places §875(c) squarely among the offenses as to which *Morissette* demands a subjective guilty-mind element, not among the strict-liability regulatory offenses of *Balint*.

## II. The First Amendment's True-Threat Doctrine Independently Requires Proof That the Speaker Meant the Statement as a Threat.

Even apart from ordinary rules of statutory construction, the First Amendment itself compels a subjective standard. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), this Court held that a statute criminalizing threats against the President "must be interpreted with the commands of the First Amendment clearly in mind." *Id.* at 707. The Court reversed a conviction for a statement made at a political rally, holding that the speaker's words amounted to "a kind of very crude offensive method of stating a political opposition to the President" rather than "a true threat." *Id.* at 708. *Watts* thus draws the constitutional line not merely by reference to how a reasonable listener might receive words stripped of context, but by asking whether the speech was in substance the kind of communication the threat statute was designed to reach—an inquiry inseparable from the speaker's own purpose in making the statement.

This concern for the speaker's actual purpose reflects the broader principle, announced in *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), that categories of speech excluded from constitutional protection—there, "fighting words"—must be defined narrowly because they occupy "no essential part of the exposition of ideas." *Id.* at 572. A standard that punishes speech based solely on how some hypothetical reasonable person might construe it, without regard to the speaker's intent, sweeps within the unprotected category far more speech than the narrow class of "true threats" that *Watts* and *Chaplinsky* permit government to punish. See also *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 310 (1940) (the constitutional guarantee of free speech requires courts to be vigilant that regulation not extend beyond conduct genuinely warranting suppression).

## III. An Objective Standard Impermissibly Chills Protected Speech and Cannot Be Squared With This Court's Scienter Jurisprudence.

This Court has repeatedly held that where speech is at stake, the absence of a scienter requirement risks self-censorship of protected expression. In *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147 (1959), the Court struck down an obscenity ordinance that dispensed with a knowledge requirement, explaining that if a bookseller "is criminally liable without knowledge of the contents...he will tend to restrict the books he sells to those he has inspected," thereby "tend[ing] to restrict the public's access to forms of the printed word which the State could not constitutionally suppress directly." *Id.* at 153. The same logic controls here: if liability under §875(c) turns on how an objectively reasonable listener might interpret ambiguous, hyperbolic, or artistic language—rap lyrics, venting, or dark humor, for example—speakers will necessarily curtail candid expression rather than risk a jury's after-the-fact judgment about how a reasonable person would have understood words the speaker never intended as threatening.

This Court's decision in *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), confirms the constitutional stakes of such chilling. There the Court observed that "the man who knows that he must bear the burden of proving" his speech lawful, or who fears liability for speech he did not intend as unlawful, "may well be deterred" from speaking altogether, and that "[t]he separation of legitimate from illegitimate speech calls for more sensitive tools" than a standard risking suppression of protected expression. *Id.* at 525-26. An objective standard for §875(c) is precisely the blunt instrument *Speiser* condemned: it permits conviction for speech the defendant never intended as a threat, based solely on a jury's retrospective assessment of how some hypothetical reasonable person might have read it.

## Conclusion

Read against the backdrop of *Morissette*'s presumption of scienter, *Watts*'s true-threat doctrine, and *Smith* and *Speiser*'s admonitions against chilling protected expression, §875(c) must require proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be understood as a threat. The judgment below should be reversed.
