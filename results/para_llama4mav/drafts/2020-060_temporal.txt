The United States respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the district court's denial of its motion to dismiss Guam's CERCLA claim. The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for recovery: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. The key issue here is whether Guam's 2004 consent decree with the EPA triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery action under section 107.

The interpretation of the consent decree is crucial in determining whether Guam's liability was "resolved" within the meaning of section 113(f)(3)(B). Although the decree includes a disclaimer of liability, this language is not dispositive. As the Court has long recognized, the substance of an agreement, not its form, determines its legal effect (United States v. Bethlehem Steel Corp., 205 U.S. 105, 118 (1907)). The consent decree's substantive provisions, which required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a "dump cover system," demonstrate that Guam's liability was, in fact, resolved.

Furthermore, the Court has held that a settlement agreement can be effective even in the absence of an explicit admission of liability (United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106, 115 (1932)). The fact that Guam did not admit liability does not negate the fact that it assumed significant obligations under the consent decree, which is consistent with a finding of liability.

Guam's argument that the consent decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it concerned non-CERCLA pollutant discharges is unpersuasive. The Complaint demanded that Guam "complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," which is a remedial action under CERCLA (see United States v. Republic Steel Corp., 362 U.S. 482, 491 (1960), where the Court recognized that the term "navigable waters" in the Rivers and Harbors Act of 1899 should be interpreted broadly).

The Court should reverse the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss the complaint. The plain meaning of the statute and the substantive provisions of the consent decree demonstrate that Guam's liability was resolved, triggering its right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B) and precluding its cost-recovery action under section 107.
