ARGUMENT

The decision below erroneously forecloses Guam’s cost-recovery action under §107 of CERCLA by treating a Clean Water Act consent decree as a “settlement” that triggers §113(f)(3)(B). That holding conflicts with the plain text of CERCLA, fundamental canons of statutory construction, and settled principles of sovereign immunity and due process.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for CERCLA Response Costs**

Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” The consent decree here resolved only Clean Water Act violations—not CERCLA liability. The decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty and install a cover system to eliminate leachate discharges. It contained no finding or admission of CERCLA liability, no allocation of response costs, and no reference to hazardous substances under CERCLA. As this Court has long held, “waivers of sovereign immunity must be strictly construed” and “the terms of the consent to be sued are not to be enlarged by implication.” *United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584, 590 (1941); *see also United States v. Wittek*, 337 U.S. 346, 359 (1949). The government cannot now retroactively characterize a Clean Water Act settlement as a CERCLA liability resolution to bar Guam’s claim.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion violates the plain-meaning rule. “Where the language of an enactment is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe the statute other than according to its terms.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). CERCLA §101(24) defines “remedial action” to include “collection of leachate and runoff,” but the consent decree’s cover system was designed to *prevent* leachate, not to remediate existing contamination. The decree did not address the cleanup of hazardous substances already released. Thus, it did not “resolve” liability for a CERCLA “response action.” *Cf. Helvering v. Hallock*, 309 U.S. 106, 119 (1940) (statutory interpretation must avoid “absurd or glaringly unjust” results).

**II. Guam May Proceed Under §107 Because It Incurred Response Costs as a Non-Liable Party**

CERCLA §107(a)(4)(B) allows “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person” to be recovered from liable parties. Guam incurred over $160 million in cleanup costs. The Navy, as the operator of the dump for decades, is a potentially responsible party. Guam itself is not liable under CERCLA for contamination caused solely by the Navy’s disposal of munitions and chemicals. At common law, a party that has paid more than its share may seek contribution from joint tortfeasors. *Union Stock Yards Co. v. Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co.*, 196 U.S. 217, 224 (1905). But Guam is not a tortfeasor; it is an innocent territory that inherited a toxic legacy. The D.C. Circuit’s rule forces Guam to bear the entire cost, contrary to CERCLA’s purpose of placing responsibility on those who caused the harm.

**III. The D.C. Circuit’s Interpretation Deprives Guam of Due Process**

The decision below effectively extinguishes Guam’s right to seek full cost recovery without notice or hearing. The consent decree was not advertised as a CERCLA settlement, and Guam had no reason to believe it triggered a three-year limitations period. Due process requires “notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action.” *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). Guam received no such notice. Moreover, the government cannot use a settlement in one statutory scheme to silently eliminate rights under another. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 267 (1946) (property rights cannot be taken without just compensation). Guam’s right to recover cleanup costs is a property interest protected by the Fifth Amendment.

**IV. The United States Cannot Shield Itself from Liability Without Clear Consent**

The United States, as sovereign, is immune from suit unless it consents. *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947). But CERCLA expressly waives that immunity for response costs. *See* 42 U.S.C. §9620(a)(1). The D.C. Circuit’s decision effectively reinstates sovereign immunity by imposing a procedural bar that Congress did not intend. This Court has cautioned that “the sovereign’s immunity from suit is not to be lightly inferred.” *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 28 (1947). The consent decree did not waive Guam’s right to sue under §107; it merely resolved discrete Clean Water Act claims. *United States v. Sponenbarger*, 308 U.S. 256, 266 (1939) (federal immunity from state law does not extend to unrelated federal obligations).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and Guam should be permitted to proceed under §107.
