# ARGUMENT

## I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Reach Aliens Who Re-entered Before Its 1997 Effective Date

Congress is presumed to legislate for the future, not the past. This Court has long held that a statute will not be read to reach conduct completed before its enactment "unless the language of the statute renders such construction indispensable." United States v. Heth, 3 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399 (1806). That presumption applies with particular force to immigration statutes carrying deportation consequences. In Chew Heong v. United States, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), the Court refused to read the Chinese Exclusion Act to extinguish the right of a laborer who had left the country before the statute's passage, holding that Congress's general language would not be construed to cut off rights accrued under prior law absent unmistakably clear intent to do so. The same interpretive caution governs here.

This Court has repeatedly instructed that deportation statutes are to be construed narrowly against the harsher result. In Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), the Court explained that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." 333 U.S. at 10. Barber v. Gonzales, 347 U.S. 637 (1954), reaffirmed that ambiguous deportation provisions must be read to avoid the more severe consequence for the alien. And in INS v. Errico, 385 U.S. 214 (1966), the Court applied precisely this canon to resolve statutory ambiguity in favor of an alien facing deportation, refusing to assume that Congress meant to strip away previously available relief without saying so plainly.

Section 241(a)(5) contains no express statement that it reaches aliens who re-entered before its 1997 effective date. Under Chew Heong, Fong Haw Tan, Barber, and Errico, that silence must be resolved in Petitioner's favor. Petitioner's re-entry in 1982—the primary conduct triggering reinstatement—occurred fifteen years before the statute existed. To read the statute as reaching back to that conduct would violate the settled rule that Congress must speak clearly before courts will assume it intended to alter the legal consequences of completed, pre-enactment acts.

## II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute to Petitioner's 1982 Re-entry Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statutory text could be stretched to cover pre-enactment re-entries, applying it to Petitioner would impose new and severe legal consequences on conduct—and on a status—that had already vested under prior law. This Court has consistently protected resident aliens from the retroactive destruction of settled expectations arising from their presence in the United States.

In Bridges v. Wixon, 326 U.S. 135 (1945), the Court emphasized that "though deportation is not technically a criminal proceeding, it visits a great hardship on the individual and deprives him of the right to stay and live and work in this land of freedom." 326 U.S. at 154. That hardship is compounded when a new statute retroactively forecloses relief—such as adjustment of status based on marriage to a citizen—that was available under the law in effect when the alien's conduct occurred. Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), likewise recognized that an alien who has entered and resided in this country acquires interests warranting constitutional protection, not to be casually extinguished by subsequent legislation. And Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath, 339 U.S. 33 (1950), confirms that deportation proceedings must respect the settled procedural and substantive expectations of resident aliens, precisely because of the gravity of the consequences at stake.

Petitioner built a life in the United States for nearly two decades before Section 241(a)(5) existed. He married a citizen and sought adjustment of status under provisions of the INA that, at the time of his 1982 re-entry and for years thereafter, permitted exactly that relief. The reinstatement statute does not merely regulate his future conduct; it reaches back and declares that his earlier re-entry—an act completed under a different legal regime—now forever bars any relief whatsoever. This is the paradigm of an impermissible retroactive statute: one that "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." Cf. Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co., 231 U.S. 190 (1913) (statutes altering legal consequences of completed transactions are disfavored absent clear congressional command).

The government may point to Harisiades v. Shaughnessy, 342 U.S. 580 (1952), and Galvan v. Press, 347 U.S. 522 (1954), for the proposition that Congress may attach new deportation consequences to past conduct. But those cases involved deportation grounds tied to conduct—Communist Party membership—that the alien could not have altered regardless of the law's timing, and the Court there stressed the plenary character of Congress's power over admission and continued presence of aliens generally, see Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893). Here, by contrast, Petitioner's 1982 re-entry was a discrete, completed act; the statute does not merely regulate ongoing presence but retroactively eliminates a form of relief—marriage-based adjustment—that vested through subsequent lawful conduct within the United States. Nothing in Harisiades, Galvan, or Fong Yue Ting compels erasing those accrued interests.

For these reasons, the reinstatement statute should not be read to reach Petitioner's 1982 re-entry, and if it is so read, its application to him is impermissibly retroactive.
