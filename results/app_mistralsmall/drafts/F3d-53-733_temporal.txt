**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE EXPERT OPINION REGARDING THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING.**

The district court abused its discretion by admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, as it failed to meet the foundational requirements for expert testimony under *Fed. R. Evid. 702* and the controlling precedent of *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923). In *Frye*, the court held that expert testimony must be based on principles or methods that are "generally accepted" in the relevant scientific community. *Id.* at 1014. Here, the expert’s opinion—that the line parted due to "fatigue" or "wear and tear"—was not grounded in any recognized scientific methodology but was instead a speculative conclusion unsupported by empirical data or industry standards.

Moreover, the expert’s testimony invaded the province of the jury, which is tasked with determining the cause of the accident. As this Court recognized in *Minneapolis & St. Louis Ry. Co. v. Bombolis*, 241 U.S. 211 (1916), "[t]he jury is not bound to accept the opinions of experts as conclusive." *Id.* at 217. The admission of this testimony was prejudicial, as it improperly bolstered the defendant’s case without providing any reliable basis for the jury’s consideration.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.**

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness because the evidence conclusively established that the vessel was unseaworthy at the time of Cook’s injury. Under *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), a vessel is unseaworthy if it is "not reasonably fit for its intended use." *Id.* at 90. Here, the evidence demonstrated that the boom’s bearings were improperly greased for winter conditions, causing them to freeze and rendering the boom inoperable. The crew’s failure to address this condition—despite Cook’s efforts to deice the bearings—created an unsafe working environment.

Further, in *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539 (1960), the Supreme Court held that a vessel’s gear must be "reasonably suitable" for its intended use. *Id.* at 550. The line used to support Cook in the bos’n’s chair was a worn, manila rope that had been left rigged for months, despite the vessel’s exposure to harsh winter conditions. This evidence was sufficient to establish unseaworthiness as a matter of law, and the district court’s denial of Cook’s motion was reversible error.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ADMITTING EVIDENCE OF COOK’S ALCOHOL-RELATED AILMENTS.**

The district court properly admitted evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments under *Rule 404(b)* of the Federal Rules of Evidence, as it was relevant to the issue of comparative negligence. In *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469 (1948), the Supreme Court held that evidence of a defendant’s prior bad acts may be admitted if it is probative of a material issue in the case. Here, Cook’s alcohol-related conditions were relevant to whether he contributed to his own injury by failing to exercise reasonable care.

Additionally, in *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172 (1997)—though decided after the relevant period—earlier cases such as *Luck v. United States*, 121 U.S. 45 (1887), recognized that evidence of a party’s character may be admitted when it bears directly on the issues in dispute. The district court did not abuse its discretion in allowing this evidence, as it was probative of Cook’s comparative negligence.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below must be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial. The erroneous admission of expert testimony, the denial of Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness, and the admission of alcohol-related evidence prejudiced Cook’s right to a fair trial. The Court should reverse the district court’s rulings and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
