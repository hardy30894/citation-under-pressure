**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action practice: *class certification is not a license to conduct a mini-trial on the merits, nor is it a vehicle for speculative or hypothetical damage models that cannot be applied uniformly to all class members.* The Third Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed certification based on a damages methodology that fails to satisfy the essential requirement of class-wide quantifiability, directly contravenes this Court’s precedent and undermines the integrity of Rule 23(b)(3). We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. The District Court’s Certification Violated Rule 23(b)(3) by Approving a Damages Methodology That Cannot Be Applied on a Class-Wide Basis

Rule 23(b)(3) mandates that “questions of law or fact common to class members *predominate* over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This requirement is not satisfied when the method used to calculate damages relies on individualized inquiries that vary from plaintiff to plaintiff. As this Court held in *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011), “the predominance requirement is not met when the damages calculation depends on individualized proof of the extent of injury.” The Court emphasized that “a class-wide damages model must be capable of being applied uniformly to all class members,” id. at 351, and that “the method of measuring damages must be common to the class,” id. at 352.

Here, the District Court certified the class based on the testimony and model of Plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. James McClave, who proposed a “but-for” damages calculation based on a hypothetical competitive market. Yet, as the Court of Appeals itself acknowledged, this model required “individualized assessments of each class member’s actual subscription behavior, pricing sensitivity, and willingness to pay.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 637 F.3d 132, 142 (3d Cir. 2011). The model assumed that every class member would have subscribed to a different provider at a lower price, but it offered no mechanism to determine *which* provider, *how much* they would have paid, or *whether* they would have subscribed at all. These are quintessentially individualized questions, not common ones.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that “the models and analyses of Plaintiffs’ damages expert, Dr. James McClave, were common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis” is legally erroneous. *See id.* at 141. The Court of Appeals failed to recognize that a damages model is not “common” merely because it is applied to the entire class—it must be *capable* of being applied uniformly, without requiring individualized proof of each plaintiff’s conduct. As this Court stated in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 622 (1997), “the class must be able to prove damages on a class-wide basis through common evidence.” The absence of such evidence renders certification improper.

### II. The Third Circuit’s “Rigorous Analysis” Standard Was Misapplied

The Third Circuit claimed to apply the “rigorous analysis” standard set forth in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008), but in practice, it permitted the District Court to engage in a de facto merits determination without the safeguards required by *Dukes*. The Court of Appeals held that the District Court’s findings were “plausible in light of the record” and thus not clearly erroneous. *Behrend*, 637 F.3d at 142. But this deference is misplaced when the underlying legal standard is violated.

The “clearly erroneous” standard does not shield a district court from legal error. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). When a court applies the wrong legal standard—such as certifying a class based on a damages model that cannot be applied uniformly—it commits an *errant conclusion of law*, which is not insulated by the abuse-of-discretion standard. *See id.* at 574 (“The factfinder’s choice between two permissible views of the evidence cannot be clearly erroneous.”) But here, there is no permissible view: the damages model is not capable of class-wide application, and no evidence was presented to show otherwise.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the District Court’s “81-page memorandum opinion” and “four-day evidentiary hearing” as justification for affirmance is misplaced. *See id.* at 141. The presence of expert testimony and a lengthy hearing does not transform a legally deficient damages model into a valid one. *See Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 352 (“The fact that a court has conducted a thorough inquiry does not excuse a failure to meet the requirements of Rule 23.”)

### III. The Certification of a Class Without a Valid Damages Methodology Violates the Seventh Amendment

The Court has long recognized that class certification must not infringe upon the right to a jury trial. *See Gen. Tel. Co. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 157 (1982) (“The right to a jury trial is not a mere formality.”). When damages are not capable of class-wide measurement, the jury must make individualized determinations—precisely the kind of factfinding that Rule 23 was designed to avoid. *See id.* at 156 (“The class action device is not a substitute for the jury’s role in assessing damages.”)
