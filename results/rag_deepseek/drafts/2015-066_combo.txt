**PETITIONER’S ARGUMENT**

The decision below violates bedrock Sixth Amendment principles by permitting an uncounseled tribal-court conviction to serve as an essential element of a federal felony. The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “in all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 341 (1963). This right applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment and prohibits imposition of a prison sentence—in any court—without counsel or a valid waiver. *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 37 (1972). Petitioner Michael Bryant Jr.’s tribal-court convictions resulted in suspended sentences that became operative, yet he had no counsel. Those uncounseled convictions cannot satisfy a federal recidivist element without violating the Sixth Amendment.

First, this Court has long held that a conviction obtained without counsel is constitutionally invalid for any purpose that triggers increased punishment. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), the Court reversed a recidivist sentence because the State introduced an uncounseled prior conviction to prove the habitual-offender charge. The Court declared it “impermissible” to use “a prior void conviction” to enhance punishment—even though the uncounseled conviction had been valid when entered. *Id.* at 114-15. *Burgett* controls here: Mr. Bryant’s tribal-court convictions are equally infirm under the Sixth Amendment, and using them as elements—not just sentence enhancers—makes the constitutional violation even more pronounced.

Second, the government cannot evade *Burgett* by arguing that tribal courts are exempt from the Sixth Amendment. The Sixth Amendment applies to any *federal* prosecution that depends on a prior uncounseled conviction. *See United States v. LaFleur*, 971 F.2d 200, 204 (9th Cir. 1992) (citing *Burgett*). In *Seymour v. Superintendent*, 368 U.S. 351, 361 (1962), the Court held that when a federal statute incorporates state or tribal convictions, “the federal definition” of the predicate offense controls. If tribal proceedings lacked counsel, they lack the reliability required by federal law. *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948) (insisting that due process requires “that the accused be tried in accordance with the law of the land”).

Third, *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), did not overrule *Burgett*. *Nichols* merely allowed uncounseled misdemeanor convictions to *enhance* a sentence, provided no imprisonment was imposed for the predicate. *Id.* at 746-47. But *Nichols* explicitly reaffirmed *Burgett*’s holding that an uncounseled “conviction that is invalid for the offense of conviction cannot be used to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense.” *Id.* at 748-49 (quoting *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 115). Here, the government used Mr. Bryant’s uncounseled tribal convictions to *establish an element* of § 117(a)—a far graver use than mere sentencing enhancement. *See also Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 731 (1948) (recidivist statutes “do not punish the prior convictions; they punish the later crime more severely”).

Fourth, the facts underscore the constitutional harm. Mr. Bryant was sentenced to one year imprisonment on each tribal conviction—precisely the penalty that triggers the Sixth Amendment right. *Argersinger*, 407 U.S. at 37. These convictions, obtained without counsel, are “presumptively unreliable.” *Burgett*, 389 U.S. at 114; *see also Chandler v. Fretag*, 348 U.S. 3, 9-10 (1954) (rejecting similar reliance on uncounseled prior used to trigger recidivist penalty). No federal court—nor any state court bound by the Sixth Amendment—would honor such judgments.

Fifth, the Ninth Circuit’s reliance on “reliability” empowers courts to ignore the Sixth Amendment when they deem a conviction “reliable” on other grounds. That approach conflicts with *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 453 (1962), which demands that recidivist proceedings “safeguard the accused’s rights” at every stage. The factfinder must know that the predicate conviction is valid. *See Price v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 488, 492 (1910) (“[D]ue process requires a full and fair trial on the merits.”). An uncounseled tribal conviction is not “valid” for Sixth Amendment purposes.

Finally, Congress cannot circumvent the Constitution by authorizing use of uncounseled tribal convictions in federal prosecutions. *Carlesi v. New York*, 233 U.S. 51, 62 (1914), held that even a presidential pardon did not insulate a prior conviction from use under a recidivist statute. But *Carlesi* assumed the antecedent conviction was constitutionally sound. The Sixteenth Amendment no more empowers Congress to create a predicate offense out of an unconstitutional conviction than it permits the states to do so. *See Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 564 (1967) (recidivist statutes “must be applied in a manner not inconsistent with constitutional requirements”).

The judgment below should be affirmed. Only Supreme Court intervention can ensure that recidivist prosecutions under § 117(a) honor the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee that no person shall be imprisoned—whether for a predicate offense or a future felony—without the aid of counsel.
