I.

The plain meaning of the Immigration and Nationality Act controls. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (“the meaning of the statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed”). Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” That language equates the grant of TPS with the maintenance of nonimmigrant status, which the Act has long treated as inseparable from lawful entry. See United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537, 543 (1950) (“an alien who seeks admission to this country”).

II.

Statutes must be read as a whole. United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805). Section 1255(a) permits adjustment for an alien “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” The same Congress that enacted § 1254a(f)(4) used “admitted” and “status” interchangeably in related provisions. See Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953) (nonimmigrant status presupposes “lawful admission”). Treating the TPS grant as an admission therefore harmonizes the two sections rather than rendering one superfluous. Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 462 (1892).

III.

Where Congress creates an exception, it knows how to do so expressly. United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). Section 1255 contains explicit exceptions to the admission requirement for certain classes of aliens. It contains none for TPS recipients. The absence of such language confirms that the lawful nonimmigrant status conferred by § 1254a(f)(4) satisfies the threshold condition of § 1255(a). See Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86, 97 (1903) (statutory conditions for admission must be given effect according to their terms).

IV.

The distinction between physical entry and subsequent conferral of status is not dispositive. Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 713 (1893) (Congress may attach consequences to an alien’s presence once lawful permission is granted). A TPS recipient is placed in nonimmigrant status by executive action after inspection of the application. That action constitutes the functional equivalent of admission for adjustment purposes. McBoyle v. United States, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (words must be read in light of their practical operation).

V.

Policy considerations reinforce the textual conclusion. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 216 (1953) (immigration statutes should not produce results “wholly incongruous with the purpose” of the enactment). TPS exists to permit continued presence during temporary crises. Allowing adjustment under § 1255 effectuates that purpose without creating a new class of permanent residents outside the statutory scheme.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
