**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously held that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lacks jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected oil and gas exploration barge. The plain text of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, coupled with the limited regulatory reach of the Coast Guard over uninspected vessels, compels the conclusion that OSHA’s citation was lawful.

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

The Act applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). A drilling barge engaged in extracting oil from the seabed is not a “vessel in navigation” within the traditional maritime meaning. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 572 (1944) (the barge “was not a vessel in navigation; it was a floating workshop used for repairing machinery”). *Gahagan Construction Corp. v. Armao*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam) (same). Like those structures, Rig 52 was stationary while drilling—its sole purpose—and thus was not “used for transportation on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870). The fact that it could be moved does not transform it into a navigational vessel. *Cf. Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 191 (1952) (ferry that actively navigated was a vessel). Consequently, Rig 52 is a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Mere jurisdiction is insufficient; the agency must actually regulate the same condition. This Court has long held that preemption requires an actual conflict, not speculative overlap. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (preemption exists when state law “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 grants it power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. It has no authority to prescribe comprehensive safety standards for drilling operations. *The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17, 35 (1903) (inspection laws applied to ships “navigating the waters of the United States” and “carrying passengers or freight”). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate the working conditions that gave rise to this explosion—well control, blowout prevention, and general workplace safety—means it has not exercised statutory authority over those conditions. *United States v. American Trucking Assns.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (exemptions in remedial statutes are construed narrowly).

**III. Workers on Uninspected Drilling Barges Are Not Seamen Entitled to an Industry-Wide Exemption**

Workers on Rig 52 performed industrial oil well work, not traditional maritime duties. Their working conditions are analogous to those on a fixed platform—areas indisputably covered by OSHA. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365 (1969) (workers on fixed platforms are not seamen for LHWCA purposes). Even under general maritime law, the remedies for seamen are limited to those specified in maritime law and do not preempt state safety statutes. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 217 (1917) (state compensation laws may apply to maritime workers where they do not conflict with federal law). Overlapping regulation is not the anomaly the lower court feared; it is the norm when maritime safety touches landside operations.

In sum, Rig 52 is a workplace, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over its working conditions, and OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment below should be reversed.
