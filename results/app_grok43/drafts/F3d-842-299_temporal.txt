ARGUMENT

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, and “the party seeking to invoke federal jurisdiction bears the burden of establishing it.” McNutt v. General Motors Acceptance Corp., 298 U.S. 178, 189 (1936). The Pressls’ complaint seeks only a declaration of rights under a 1960 Virginia flowage easement. No federal statute or constitutional provision appears on the face of the complaint.

Federal-question jurisdiction exists only when “a right or immunity created by the Constitution or laws of the United States [is] an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff’s cause of action.” Gully v. First National Bank, 299 U.S. 109, 112 (1936). The well-pleaded complaint rule precludes reliance on anticipated defenses. Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). APCO’s FERC license and any obligations it may impose constitute, at most, a defense that cannot confer jurisdiction.

A declaratory-judgment action does not enlarge federal jurisdiction. “The Declaratory Judgment Act [is] procedural only” and “enlarged the range of remedies available in the federal courts but did not extend their jurisdiction.” Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 671 (1950). Because the Pressls could not have invoked federal jurisdiction in a coercive action to enforce the easement, they cannot do so by seeking declaratory relief.

Removal jurisdiction is likewise absent. A case may be removed only when the plaintiff’s statement of his own claim shows that the suit arises under federal law. Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 460 (1894). The complaint here reveals no such federal claim.

Accordingly, the judgment must be vacated and the case remanded to state court.
