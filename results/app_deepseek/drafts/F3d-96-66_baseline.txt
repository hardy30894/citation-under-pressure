**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because the shipowner breached its duty under § 905(b) by creating an unreasonably dangerous condition and failing to warn or intervene. Under *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156, 167 (1981), a shipowner owes three distinct duties to longshoremen: (1) to turn over the vessel in a safe condition; (2) to warn of hidden dangers known to the shipowner; and (3) to intervene when it has actual knowledge of a dangerous condition created by the stevedore’s operations and fails to act. The “active operations” duty, as refined by this Court, also imposes liability when the shipowner’s own crew creates a hazard during ongoing cargo operations. *See, e.g., Hugev v. Dampskibsselskabet*, 3 F.3d 701, 705 (3d Cir. 1993) (shipowner liable for injury caused by its crew’s negligent operation of ship’s gear).

Here, the ship’s crew affirmatively created the dangerous condition. The crew had exclusive responsibility for moving the snatch blocks. After opening the hatch covers, the crew was required to return the blocks to the vertical position and tie them. Instead, the crew left the block in the down position, resting on top of a cargo unit. This was not a condition arising from the stevedore’s work; it was a direct result of the crew’s own failure to perform its assigned task. The block’s position made it impossible to remove the cargo beneath it without damage and created a tripping hazard for the hookup man. The shipowner had actual knowledge of this condition because its crew had placed the block there. *See Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167 (shipowner must warn of hidden dangers “known to the vessel”).

The shipowner’s duty to intervene is triggered when it “knows of a dangerous condition and knows that the stevedore is not taking reasonable steps to protect the longshoremen.” *Id.* at 175-76. Here, the shipowner knew the block was down. It also knew that the stevedore’s longshoremen were actively unloading cargo in that hatch. The crew’s failure to secure the block created an obvious hazard. Yet the shipowner did nothing—no warning, no correction, no request that the stevedore stop work until the block was moved. The shipowner cannot escape liability by arguing that Serbin voluntarily moved the block. The shipowner’s duty is non-delegable; it cannot shift responsibility for its own negligence to the longshoreman. *See Bjaranson v. Botelho Shipping Corp.*, 873 F.2d 1204, 1207 (9th Cir. 1989) (shipowner liable for injury caused by its crew’s failure to secure hatch covers).

Moreover, the shipowner’s duty to warn extends to dangers that are “not obvious” to the longshoreman. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167. Here, the danger was not merely the block’s position; it was the block’s weight (100 pounds) and the fact that it was fixed to the hatch with metal projections that could cause it to shift unpredictably. Serbin had no way of knowing that the block would become unstable when he attempted to move it. The shipowner, through its crew, knew the block’s characteristics and should have warned Serbin of the risk. *See Turner v. Costa Line, Inc.*, 718 F.2d 276, 279 (3d Cir. 1983) (shipowner must warn of dangers “not reasonably apparent” to longshoreman).

The district court’s grant of summary judgment improperly resolved factual disputes. Serbin testified that he moved the block to avoid delay and because he had done so before without incident. But the shipowner’s duty does not depend on the longshoreman’s subjective assessment of risk. The question is whether the shipowner, having created the hazard, took reasonable steps to protect Serbin. *See Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 176-77. A reasonable jury could find that leaving a 100-pound block precariously balanced on cargo, without warning or correction, was negligence. Summary judgment was therefore inappropriate. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (all reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of nonmovant).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
