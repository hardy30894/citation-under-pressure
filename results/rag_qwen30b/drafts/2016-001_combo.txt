**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to reaffirm the long-standing principle that a patentee is entitled to recover the full profits derived from the infringing product when the patented design is the *entire value* of the article. The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits the award of entire profits even when the patented feature is only a component, contradicts settled precedent and undermines the foundational rule that damages must be apportioned when the patented invention is not the sole source of value.

The rule that damages must be apportioned when the patented feature is only a part of a larger product is not a modern innovation but a principle rooted in the earliest decisions of this Court. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885), this Court held that in a design patent case, “the entire profit from the manufacture and sale of the carpet” could not be awarded “unless it is shown that the entire value of the whole machine, as a marketable article, is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” The Court emphasized that “there was no evidence as to the value imparted to the carpet by the design,” and thus the award of full profits was improper. The same principle applies here: where a design patent covers only a component, and the component does not confer the entire value of the product, the patentee must prove that the patented design is the *source* of the product’s marketability and profit.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614 (1912), where the Court stated: “There are many cases in which the plaintiff’s patent is only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits. In such case, if plaintiff’s patent only created a part of the profits, he is only entitled to recover that part of the net gains.” The Court further held that the patentee must “give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features, and such evidence must be reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative.” This is not a mere procedural formality—it is a constitutional requirement of due process and fairness in damages awards.

The Federal Circuit’s decision to allow recovery of entire profits in this case, despite the fact that the patented design was only a component of a smartphone, violates this settled doctrine. The Court in *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 14 (1886), made clear that “the principle on which damages are to be computed in respect to a patent for a machine, or for an improvement in a machine, or for a process, is not applicable to a patent for a design, because, in a patent for a design, the result is patented, while in the other kind of patent the means are patented.” The Court recognized that in design patent cases, “the entire profits or damages on the article containing the design are to be given” *only* when the design is the “entire value” of the article. Where the design is not the sole source of value, apportionment is required.

This principle was applied in *McCreary v. Pennsylvania Canal Co.*, 141 U.S. 459, 467 (1891), where the Court held that damages for infringement of a patented improvement must be measured by the value of the improvement itself, not the entire machine. The Court stated that “the comparison must be between the patented invention and what was known and open to the public at and before the date of the patent.” This standard requires a careful analysis of the contribution of the patented feature to the overall product’s value—a task the Federal Circuit ignored.

The Federal Circuit’s reliance on *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480, 490 (1854), to justify awarding full profits is misplaced. In *Seymour*, the Court reversed a damages award because the jury had been instructed that the plaintiff could recover “as great damages for the infringement of the patent in his second count as if he had proceeded on both counts,” even though the patent covered only an improvement. The Court held that “the plaintiff had the right to recover as great damages for the infringement of the patent set forth in the second count as if he had proceeded on both counts,” but only because the improvement was “the entire value” of the machine. The Court did not hold that damages could be awarded in full without apportionment. On the contrary, it emphasized that the measure of damages must reflect the actual contribution of the patented feature.

The same principle applies in *Sperry Gyroscope Co. v. Arma Engineering Co.*, 271 U.S. 232, 237 (1926), where the Court recognized that “the real question presented is whether that allegation was enough to deprive the District Court of jurisdiction.” The Court did not hold that a patentee could recover full profits for a component patent without proof of its contribution to the product’s value. Rather, the Court emphasized that jurisdiction depends on the nature of the claim, not the form of the remedy.

Finally, in *Lehnbeuter v. Holthaus*, 105 U.S. 94, 97 (1882), the Court held that “the patent is prima facie evidence of both novelty and utility,” but that “neither of these presumptions has been rebutted by the evidence.” The Court did not hold that a patentee could recover full profits without proof of the design’s contribution to the product’s value. On the contrary, the Court required a factual determination of whether the design was the source of the product’s value.

The Federal Circuit’s decision to allow recovery of entire profits without requiring apportionment is not only contrary to these precedents but also undermines the integrity of the patent system. It allows patentees to recover windfall profits for components that contribute only marginally to the product’s success. This is not the law of this Court. The law is clear: when a design patent covers only a component, damages must be apportioned. The judgment of the Federal Circuit must be reversed.
