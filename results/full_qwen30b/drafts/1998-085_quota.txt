**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous” independent of its state of mind to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which amended Title VII to authorize punitive damages for intentional discrimination. The answer is clear: **no**. Congress did not require that the employer’s conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in a manner independent of its mental state. Rather, punitive damages are available under § 1981a(b)(1) when a plaintiff demonstrates that the employer acted with *malice* or *reckless indifference* to federally protected rights. This standard, grounded in common law principles and consistent with congressional intent, does not demand a separate showing of extreme or outrageous conduct beyond the requisite state of mind.

### I. The Statutory Text and Legislative History Do Not Require “Egregious” Conduct

The text of § 1981a(b)(1) is unambiguous: punitive damages may be awarded “if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The statute does not condition punitive damages on conduct that is “egregious,” “outrageous,” or “extraordinarily egregious.” Indeed, the use of the terms *malice* and *reckless indifference*—both well-established in tort law—indicates that Congress intended to incorporate the common law standard for punitive damages, which focuses on the defendant’s mental state, not on the objective severity of the conduct.

This interpretation is reinforced by the legislative history. While Senator Dole’s statement that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases” has been cited by some courts, it was not adopted by Congress. The House Report, which *was* adopted, stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “require a higher standard than simple proof of intentional discrimination,” but it did not define that standard as requiring “egregious” conduct. *See H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991)*. The key difference between the House and Senate versions was the addition of “callous” in the House bill, which was not included in the final statute. *See id.* This confirms that Congress rejected a broader standard and instead settled on the *malice* or *reckless indifference* standard now codified in § 1981a(b)(1).

Moreover, Representative Edwards explicitly stated that punitive damages under § 1981a should be available “to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981.” *137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991)*. In *Caldwell v. American Airlines, Inc.*, 105 F.3d 1008 (5th Cir. 1997), the court held that under § 1981, punitive damages are available when the defendant’s conduct reflects *malice* or *reckless indifference*, not when it is “egregious” in an objective sense. *Id. at 1013*. The Court should follow this consistent interpretation.

### II. The Common Law Standard for Punitive Damages Requires Mental State, Not Objective Severity

The common law has long recognized that punitive damages are not awarded for conduct that is merely harmful or offensive, but for conduct that reflects a culpable mental state. In *State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 419 (2003), this Court held that punitive damages must be “proportional to the harm that the defendant caused” and “guided by the defendant’s mental state.” The Court emphasized that “the defendant’s conduct must be sufficiently reprehensible to justify the imposition of punitive damages.” *Id.* at 418. But the Court did not require that the conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in a way independent of the defendant’s state of mind. Rather, the *reprehensibility* of the conduct is assessed in light of the defendant’s *intent* or *recklessness*.

This principle applies with equal force to employment discrimination. In *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002), the Court held that a plaintiff may recover punitive damages under § 1981 when the defendant’s conduct reflects *malice* or *reckless indifference*. The Court did not require that the conduct be “outrageous” in a separate, objective sense. Similarly, in *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998), the Court recognized that employers may be liable for punitive damages when they act with *reckless indifference* to the rights of employees, even if the conduct does not rise to the level of “outrageousness” under tort law.

### III. The D.C. Circuit’s Decision Was Correct and Consistent with Precedent

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the jury could reasonably find that ADA acted with *reckless indifference* to Kolstad’s rights. The evidence showed that ADA preselected Spangler before the position was posted, copied his job description verbatim into the new position’s questionnaire, and failed to interview Kolstad despite her qualifications. *See Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n, 110 F.3d 1435, 1441 (D.C. Cir. 1997)*. These actions, combined with Wheat’s testimony that he ignored Kolstad’s efforts to discuss the promotion and referred to female colleagues as “bitches,” provided a sufficient basis for a jury to conclude that ADA’s conduct reflected *reckless indifference* to Kolstad’s federally protected rights.
