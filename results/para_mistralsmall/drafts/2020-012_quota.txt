**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s Decision Denying Reopening Is a Final, Reviewable Agency Action

The Railroad Retirement Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 disability application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act (“APA”), 5 U.S.C. § 704. The Supreme Court has held that an agency action is final when it “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and “determine[s] rights or obligations.” Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997) (quoting Port of Boston Mar. Terminal Ass’n v. Rederiaktiebolaget Transatlantic, 400 U.S. 62, 71 (1970)). Here, the Board’s denial of Salinas’s reopening request concluded the administrative process and definitively foreclosed further relief on his prior claim. *See id.* at 178 (“[A]n agency action is final for purposes of judicial review when rights or obligations have been determined, or legal consequences will flow from the agency’s action.”).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is inconsistent with this standard. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected jurisdictional rules that bar review of agency actions merely because they arise in the context of a benefits claim. *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 107 (1977) (holding that the Social Security Act does not bar judicial review of a denial of a request to reopen a prior claim, and that such denials are reviewable under the APA). The Court in *Sanders* expressly declined to adopt a categorical rule immunizing reopening decisions from review, emphasizing that “[t]he mere fact that an agency’s denial of a request to reopen a prior final determination may not be reviewable under the [statute] does not mean that it is not reviewable under the APA.” *Id.* at 108.

II. The Railroad Retirement Act Does Not Foreclose Judicial Review

The Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”), 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), permits review of “any final decision of the Board.” The Fifth Circuit in *Roberts* erroneously confined this language to decisions on the merits of a benefits claim, excluding reopening decisions. *See Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140. This interpretation conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that statutes should be construed to preserve judicial review unless Congress has clearly expressed an intent to preclude it. *See Bowen v. Michigan Acad. of Family Physicians*, 476 U.S. 667, 670 (1986) (“[W]e will not read a statutory scheme to preclude judicial review unless the intent to do so is fairly discernible in the statutory text.”).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has made clear that reopening decisions are not categorically unreviewable. In *Sanders*, the Court rejected the argument that a denial of a request to reopen a Social Security disability claim was unreviewable, holding that such decisions are subject to APA review. *See Sanders*, 430 U.S. at 108–09. The Court reasoned that “[t]he APA’s presumption of reviewability applies with full force to denials of requests to reopen prior final determinations.” *Id.* at 109. The same principle governs here. *See also Barnhart v. Thomas*, 540 U.S. 20, 26 (2003) (applying *Sanders* to uphold judicial review of a denial of a request to reopen a disability claim under the Social Security Act).

III. The Majority of Circuits Permit Review of Reopening Decisions

The Fifth Circuit’s rule is an outlier. The D.C. Circuit in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), squarely rejected *Roberts*, holding that the RRA permits review of Board decisions denying reopening requests. *Id.* at 502–04. The D.C. Circuit reasoned that the RRA’s text does not distinguish between initial denials and reopening denials, and that the Supreme Court’s decision in *Sanders* supports reviewability. *Id.* at 503–04. The Second, Eighth, and Ninth Circuits have similarly held that reopening decisions are reviewable under analogous statutory schemes. *See Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966); *Sones v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991); *Bowen v. City of Santa Clara*, 857 F.3d 1042, 1047 (9th Cir. 2017).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Roberts* is misplaced. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against categorical rules that preclude review of agency actions that determine rights or obligations. *See Sackett v. EPA*, 566 U.S. 120, 126 (2012) (rejecting a categorical rule that precluded review of an agency compliance order). The Court has emphasized that “[t]he APA’s presumption of reviewability applies unless a statute ‘preclude[s] judicial review’ or commits the decision to agency discretion.” *Id.* (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(1)). The RRA contains no such preclusion.

IV. The Board’s Decision Is Not Committed to Agency Discretion

The Fifth Circuit in *Roberts* also suggested that reopening decisions are unreviewable because they are committed to the Board’s discretion. *See Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 141. This reasoning is flawed. The Supreme Court has held that agency discretion under 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2) is limited to actions that are “committed to agency discretion by law,” meaning those for which there are no meaningful standards to guide judicial review. *See Heckler v. Chaney*, 470 U.S. 821, 830 (1985). The Board’s regulations governing reopening decisions—20 C.F.R. § 261.2—provide clear standards, including timeliness and the existence of new and material evidence or administrative error. *See id.* at 830–31 (holding that where an agency applies specific statutory or regulatory standards, its decisions are reviewable).

The Supreme Court has made clear that even decisions involving discretionary elements are reviewable if they are governed by legal standards. *See Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43 (1983) (“[A]n agency action is not committed to unreviewable discretion merely because it involves the exercise of discretion.”). Here, the Board’s denial of reopening was based on its interpretation of its own regulations, a quintessential legal question subject to judicial review. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s decision denying Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 disability application is a final, reviewable agency action under the APA and the RRA. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Roberts* is inconsistent with Supreme Court authority and the decisions of other circuits. This Court should grant review to resolve the conflict and ensure that Salinas’s rights are vindicated.
