**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp., Ltd., because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether the vessel’s crew breached its “active operations” duty under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), by failing to secure the snatch block in the up position after opening the hatch cover. The Supreme Court in *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156, 159, 101 S. Ct. 1614, 1617 (1981), established that a vessel owes a duty to longshoremen to “exercise reasonable care in the conduct of its own operations,” and that this duty extends to “the safe operation of the vessel’s equipment and the maintenance of a safe working environment.” The Court emphasized that “the vessel’s duty is not limited to the operation of the vessel itself, but includes the safe conduct of those operations which are essential to the loading and unloading process.” Id. at 167, 101 S. Ct. at 1621.

Here, the vessel’s crew was responsible for operating the block and pulley system, including the positioning of the snatch blocks. The record confirms that the crew had opened the hatch cover to expose the cargo on the lower deck, but failed to return the snatch block to the up (vertical) position, leaving it in the down (horizontal) position resting atop a cargo unit. This failure was not merely a mechanical oversight—it created a foreseeable hazard. The block weighed approximately 100 pounds and, when improperly positioned, posed a serious risk of falling on workers in the wing area, where the “hookup man” would stand during cargo operations. As Serbin testified, “that’s where the hookup man would normally stand in the wing. If anything was to fall he had no place to run.” This testimony, if credited, establishes a clear danger to longshoremen engaged in the unloading process.

The vessel’s duty under *Scindia* is not excused simply because the longshoreman chose to act independently. The Third Circuit has held that “a vessel may not shift its responsibility to longshoremen by failing to maintain safe equipment or by allowing hazardous conditions to persist.” *Barnes v. M/V *S.S. *Cape Cod*, 988 F.2d 425, 429 (3d Cir. 1993). The vessel’s duty is “active” and ongoing, requiring it to ensure that its equipment is properly secured and that the work environment remains safe throughout the loading and unloading process. *See also* *Barnes*, 988 F.2d at 429 (“The vessel’s duty to maintain a safe working environment is not diminished by the presence of longshoremen performing their own tasks.”).

Moreover, the vessel’s failure to secure the block was not a trivial or de minimis condition. The block was fixed to the hatch with metal projections that served as stoppers, indicating that its position was not meant to be left in the down position. The crew had the exclusive authority and responsibility to operate the block and pulley system. The fact that the block remained in the down position after the hatch was opened—despite the vessel’s control over the system—constitutes a failure to perform an essential operational duty. The vessel cannot avoid liability simply because a longshoreman chose to intervene. *See* *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167, 101 S. Ct. at 1621 (“The vessel’s duty to exercise reasonable care in the conduct of its own operations is not negated by the longshoreman’s presence or actions.”).

The district court’s conclusion that Serbin assumed the risk by moving the block himself is legally flawed. The doctrine of assumption of risk does not apply where the hazard arises from the vessel’s failure to perform its own operational duties. *See* *Barnes*, 988 F.2d at 429 (“A longshoreman does not assume the risk of a vessel’s failure to maintain safe equipment.”). The risk Serbin encountered was not inherent in the work of unloading fruit, but was created by the vessel’s failure to secure the snatch block. The danger was not obvious or self-evident—Serbin had no reason to believe the block would fall unless it was improperly secured, and the vessel’s crew had the exclusive means to prevent that risk.

Finally, the record contains sufficient evidence to support a finding that the vessel’s negligence was a proximate cause of Serbin’s injury. The block was in a hazardous position, the crew had failed to secure it, and Serbin’s attempt to move it was a reasonable response to a dangerous condition created by the vessel. The injury occurred when he fell from the crates he was standing on while attempting to reposition the block. This sequence of events demonstrates a direct causal link between the vessel’s failure to perform its active operations duty and Serbin’s harm.

For these reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. A reasonable jury could conclude that the vessel breached its duty under *Scindia* by failing to secure the snatch block, that this breach created a foreseeable hazard, and that the breach was a substantial factor in causing Serbin’s injury. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
