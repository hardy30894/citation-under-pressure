**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that Arizona’s sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it permitted a judge, rather than a jury, to find the aggravating factors necessary to impose the death penalty. *Id.* at 609. The Court explained that the Sixth Amendment requires that “any fact that exposes a defendant to a greater punishment than that authorized by the jury’s verdict alone” must be found by a jury. *Id.* at 587 (quoting *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 (2000)).

Florida’s scheme is distinguishable. Unlike Arizona, Florida does not permit a judge to find aggravating factors. Instead, the jury is tasked with weighing aggravating and mitigating factors and recommending a sentence of death or life imprisonment. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2012). The trial court then independently weighs the aggravating and mitigating factors before imposing a sentence. *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640 (1989) (per curiam). This two-step process ensures that the jury’s role in finding facts is not diminished. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647-48 (1990) (upholding Arizona’s scheme because the jury found the aggravating factors).

Respondent further submits that Florida’s scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s recommendation is advisory, not binding. *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that states may assign the jury an advisory role in capital sentencing without violating the Sixth Amendment. *Id.* at 465; *see also Walton*, 497 U.S. at 647-48. Florida’s scheme is consistent with this precedent.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Respondent submits that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment. The Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are “excessive” or “disproportionate to the crime.” *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977). However, the Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld the constitutionality of the death penalty when imposed under guided discretion statutes. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 188-89 (1976) (plurality opinion).

Florida’s scheme provides sufficient safeguards to ensure that the death penalty is not imposed arbitrarily or capriciously. The statute requires the jury to consider specific aggravating and mitigating factors, and the trial court must weigh these factors before imposing a sentence. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2)-(3). This process ensures that the death penalty is reserved for the most culpable offenders. *See Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862, 878-79 (1983) (upholding Georgia’s scheme because it narrowed the class of offenders eligible for the death penalty).

Respondent further submits that Florida’s scheme does not violate the Eighth Amendment because it does not permit the imposition of the death penalty based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation. The Supreme Court has never held that the Sixth or Eighth Amendments require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *Walton*, 497 U.S. at 651-52. Moreover, the Court has recognized that states may assign the jury an advisory role in capital sentencing without violating the Eighth Amendment. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 464-65.

**III. FLORIDA’S SCHEME FOR DETERMINING MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

Respondent submits that Florida’s scheme for determining mental retardation does not violate the Eighth Amendment. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Supreme Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded offenders. However, the Court left it to the states to determine the manner in which this constitutional restriction is enforced. *Id.* at 317.

Florida’s scheme for determining mental retardation is consistent with *Atkins*. The statute sets forth a three-pronged test for mental retardation: (1) significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning; (2) concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior; and (3) manifestation of the condition before age eighteen. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(1). The burden is on the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. *Id.* § 921.137(4). This scheme ensures that the death penalty is not imposed on mentally retarded offenders while also providing a clear and administrable standard for determining mental retardation. *See Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent respectfully submits that Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments to the United States Constitution. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
