# ARGUMENT

Federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over every case that happens to require application of patent law principles. Rather, as this Court has consistently held, federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) extends only to cases in which a federal patent issue is "necessary, disputed, and substantial," and where the exercise of federal jurisdiction will not disturb the congressionally approved balance between federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim fails this test, and the Texas Supreme Court erred in dismissing it for want of jurisdiction.

## I. Section 1338(a) Jurisdiction Requires More Than the Mere Presence of a Patent Law Question

This Court has long resisted expansive readings of "arising under" jurisdiction. In *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), the Court held that § 1338(a) jurisdiction attaches only where "the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." *Id.* at 808–09. That formulation was drawn directly from the general federal-question statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1331, because both provisions employ the identical "arising under" language. *Id.*

*Grable* refined this analysis into four discrete requirements: "does a state-law claim necessarily raise a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. Each element must independently be satisfied; the mere presence of a patent question embedded in a state cause of action is insufficient. As this Court cautioned in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), federal courts must engage in "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Id.* at 117–18. Similarly, in *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law merely because federal law originated the underlying right; it must "really and substantially involve[] a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Id.* at 569.

## II. Minton's Malpractice Claim Does Not Satisfy the Grable Factors

**A. The Patent Issue Is Not "Necessary" in the Jurisdictionally Relevant Sense.**

Minton's malpractice claim requires proof that Gunn's negligent failure to raise the experimental-use exception caused Minton to lose a case he otherwise would have won. This is a hypothetical, backward-looking factual inquiry conducted entirely within a closed universe—the record of the prior federal litigation. No live patent right is being adjudicated; the '643 Patent has already been declared invalid, and that determination will not be disturbed regardless of the outcome of the malpractice suit. The patent issue is therefore not "necessary" in the sense that matters for jurisdictional purposes: resolution of the hypothetical patent question affects only the state-law damages calculation, not any actual patent right.

**B. Resolving the Question Will Not Create the Kind of Precedent Congress Sought to Centralize.**

The animating purpose behind exclusive federal patent jurisdiction is to ensure nationwide uniformity in the interpretation and application of the patent laws. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). But a state court's hypothetical assessment of how a patent case *would have* been decided, in the context of assessing an attorney's malpractice liability, creates no binding patent-law precedent whatsoever. The decision binds no one outside the parties to the malpractice suit; it does not affect the validity of any patent; it does not bind the PTO or any future litigant. As the Nebraska Supreme Court correctly recognized in an analogous context, "[w]hen patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action... federal courts do not have jurisdiction of the case pursuant to § 1338." *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 344 (Neb. 2005).

**C. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Here Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

Legal malpractice is a quintessential state-law tort, traditionally and exclusively the province of state courts. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). States possess an overwhelming interest in regulating the conduct of attorneys licensed within their borders and in providing a forum for their citizens' malpractice claims. Sweeping every malpractice case that happens to involve a patent-related underlying dispute into federal court—simply because patent law provides part of the backdrop against which attorney conduct is judged—would work exactly the kind of disruption to the state-federal balance that *Grable* forbids. 545 U.S. at 313–14. This is precisely the "jurisdictional imbalance" identified by courts declining to follow the Federal Circuit's approach. *See E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516, 524 (Cal. Ct. App. 2010) (holding that even where patent law is relevant to a professional negligence claim, "it does not present a question of patent law that is substantial"); *Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749, 753–54 (N.D. Tex. 2010) (holding that state-court resolution of embedded patent issues "will not create or destroy any patent rights such that uniformity in the way patents are issued or enforced will be threatened").

## III. The Federal Circuit's Contrary Approach Improperly Ignores Grable's Federalism Command

The Federal Circuit's decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), erroneously conclude that federal jurisdiction attaches whenever "establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of a malpractice claim." *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1273. But this reasoning collapses the *Grable* test into a single "necessity" inquiry, disregarding the independent requirements of substantiality and federal-state balance altogether. Later Federal Circuit decisions compounded this error by abandoning any *Grable* analysis whatsoever. *See Davis v. Brouse McDowell, L.P.A.*, 596 F.3d 1355 (Fed. Cir. 2010); *Warrior Sports, Inc. v. Dickinson Wright, P.L.L.C.*, 631 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2011).

This Court should reject that truncated approach and hold, consistent with *Grable* and *Christianson*, that federal jurisdiction over state-law claims embedding patent questions requires satisfaction of all four factors—necessity, actual dispute, substantiality, and preservation of the federal-state balance. Because Minton's malpractice claim fails at least three of these requirements, exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie, and the Texas courts properly possessed jurisdiction to adjudicate his claim.
