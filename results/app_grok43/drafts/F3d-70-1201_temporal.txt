ARGUMENT

The district court erred when it directed the jury to disregard properly admitted testimony of post-accident lengthening of the infeed chute. At common law, evidence of subsequent repairs was excluded only when offered to prove antecedent negligence. Columbia & Puget Sound R.R. v. Hawthorne, 144 U.S. 202, 207 (1892) (“the taking of such precautions…is not to be considered as an admission of previous neglect of duty”). The same decisions uniformly recognized that the prohibition did not apply when the evidence was offered for another legitimate purpose, including impeachment of a claim that the original design remained unaltered and safe. See, e.g., Morse v. Minneapolis & St. Louis Ry. Co., 30 Minn. 365, 367, 15 N.W. 104, 105 (1883) (evidence admissible “for the purpose of contradicting the testimony of a witness”); Terre Haute & Indianapolis R.R. v. Clem, 123 Ind. 15, 19, 23 N.E. 965, 966 (1890) (same).

Morbark’s opening statement and cross-examination of Infinger asserted that the seventeen-inch chute “just like the one that is involved in this case” continued in daily governmental use, thereby placing in issue the absence of design change. Once that assertion was made, the common-law rule permitted rebuttal by proof of the subsequent lengthening. See St. Louis & San Francisco Ry. v. Stuart, 71 Okla. 133, 136, 175 P. 512, 514 (1918) (defendant “cannot complain if the plaintiff is permitted to show the subsequent change” after suggesting the original condition was satisfactory). The testimony was therefore admissible for impeachment, and the later instruction to disregard it was error.

Because the erroneously stricken evidence directly contradicted Morbark’s central defense that the original chute length remained acceptable, the error was prejudicial and required a new trial. See, e.g., Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. v. McGinnis, 228 U.S. 173, 176 (1913) (new trial warranted where excluded proof bore directly on the issue submitted to the jury).
