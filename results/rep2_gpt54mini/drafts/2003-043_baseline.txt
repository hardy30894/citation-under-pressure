## Argument

The judgment below should be affirmed because the Internal Revenue Code extends the collection period only when the tax is timely assessed against the taxpayer from whom collection is sought. Here, the IRS assessed the partnership, but not Abel Galletti, Sarah Galletti, Francesco Briguglio, or Angela Briguglio individually. That is fatal. The partners are separate “taxpayer[s]” under the Code, and the partnership assessment extended limitations only as to the partnership itself. 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 6501(a), 6502(a); 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14).

### I. The ten-year collection period runs only from an assessment of the taxpayer whose assets the Government seeks to reach.

Section 6502(a) authorizes collection by levy or court proceeding only if begun within ten years after “the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Section 6203 likewise requires that an assessment “identify[] the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Read together, these provisions contemplate a valid assessment against the person or entity from whom collection will be made. That is how this Court has understood assessment: it is the act that fixes the Government’s administrative collection remedies against the assessed taxpayer. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. 114, 122–23 (2004) (assessment “is a recording of the taxpayer’s liability” and “extends the Government’s collection period” as to the assessed liability). But the recording must be against the relevant taxpayer. Id.

The partnership and the individual partners are distinct taxpayers under the Code. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Because the IRS assessed only Marina Cabrillo Partners, the ten-year period ran only as to that assessed taxpayer, not automatically as to the partners. The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that an assessment against one taxpayer does not, without more, become an assessment against different taxpayers. See In re Galletti, 341 F.3d 960, 967–69 (9th Cir. 2003).

### II. Partnership liability does not eliminate the Government’s obligation to take the procedural steps required to collect from individual partners.

The Government emphasizes that California law makes general partners liable for partnership debts. But state-law liability and federal tax procedure are separate questions. A partner may be substantively liable, yet the IRS still must proceed in a manner authorized by federal law before collecting from that partner. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123–24; Commissioner v. Shapiro, 424 U.S. 614, 630–31 (1976) (government must comply with statutory prerequisites governing collection actions).

This Court’s precedents do not support the contrary view. In Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), the Court did not hold that a partnership assessment alone permits direct collection from partners after limitations has run. Rather, Young upheld denial of a refund where the partner had already paid the tax and was concededly liable under state law. Id. at 910. That is a very different issue from whether the IRS may, years later, collect from unassessed partners without a timely assessment or judgment. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123–24 (distinguishing arguments based on partner liability from the question whether assessment was properly extended).

Likewise, United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), does not help the Government. Coson involved lien validity and relied on the fact that the Government’s collection device depended on an underlying valid assessment. Id. at 458, 462–63. Nothing in Coson held that an assessment naming only the partnership suffices to extend limitations against every partner individually.

### III. Bankruptcy claim allowance turns on enforceability against these Debtors, and the IRS’s claims are time-barred.

A bankruptcy claim is allowed only if it is a “right to payment,” 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A), and it must be disallowed if it “is unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor, under ... applicable law.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Here, the IRS’s claims are unenforceable because the Government did not timely assess these Debtors and did not obtain judgments against them within the limitations period. Under California law, “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). Without a judgment against the partner, the partnership’s judgment cannot be satisfied from the partner’s assets. Id. The IRS therefore lacked an enforceable claim against these individual Debtors when they filed bankruptcy.

The Government’s contrary rule would collapse the distinction between partnership-level and partner-level liability and would rewrite §§ 6203, 6501(a), and 6502(a) to permit collection from unassessed individuals long after the period Congress prescribed. The Ninth Circuit properly rejected that reading. See In re Galletti, 341 F.3d at 969–71.

### Conclusion

Because the IRS assessed only the partnership and not these individual partners, the ten-year collection period was never extended as to respondents. The claims are time-barred and unenforceable against the Debtors. The judgment below should be affirmed.
