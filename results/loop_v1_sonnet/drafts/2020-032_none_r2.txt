# ARGUMENT

## I. Article III Requires a "Definite and Concrete" Controversy, Which a Bare Request for Nominal Damages Cannot Supply Once the Challenged Policy Is Withdrawn.

The judicial power of the federal courts extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has long refused to adjudicate disputes that have lost their live, adversarial character. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911). In *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227 (1937), this Court held that "[t]he controversy must be definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests," and that it "must be a real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief through a decree of a conclusive character, as distinguished from an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts." *Id.* at 240-41.

Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged provision of the Student Code of Conduct, no live dispute remained over the validity of rules that no longer existed. Petitioners face no threat of enforcement, no continuing relationship with the defendants arising from those provisions, and no prospect of "specific relief" beyond a symbolic label attached to a policy already abandoned. A demand for a token sum cannot resurrect that extinguished controversy. This Court's decision in *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), confirms as much: there, the Court dismissed an appeal because intervening events had eliminated any actual controversy for the Court to resolve, explaining that the duty of a federal court "is to decide actual controversies by a judgment which can be carried into effect, and not to give opinions upon moot questions." *Id.* at 653. Where the policy giving rise to the suit has been repealed, and petitioners allege no actual, continuing injury flowing from it, there is no controversy left for a court to decide.

## II. This Court Has Repeatedly Held That Insubstantial or Residual Claims Cannot Preserve Jurisdiction Once the Underlying Dispute Has Been Resolved.

This Court's mootness jurisprudence has never permitted a merely technical or residual claim to keep alive a controversy that has otherwise been resolved. In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the State sued to recover a modest sum of back taxes, but while the case was pending the legislature released the underlying claim. This Court dismissed the case as moot, holding that a court will not proceed to judgment where the matter has ceased to present any genuine, substantial dispute between the parties. *Id.* at 313-14. The same logic applies here: once GGC rescinded the challenged provisions, the only thing petitioners retained was a request for nominal damages untethered to any pleaded actual injury—the kind of vestigial claim *San Pablo* held insufficient to sustain continuing federal jurisdiction.

*United States v. Alaska Steamship Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920), confirms the same principle from another angle. There this Court recognized that intervening events which destroy the practical significance of a dispute render a case moot even though some formal remnant of the original controversy technically survives. *Id.* at 115-16. GGC's repeal of the Prior Policies is precisely such an intervening event. It eliminated any occasion for equitable relief and left behind only a nominal-damages request incapable of restoring a live controversy over provisions no longer in force.

## III. Voluntary Cessation of the Challenged Policy, Combined With No Reasonable Expectation of Recurrence, Moots the Case Notwithstanding the Residual Damages Request.

Where a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct and the plaintiff shows no continuing, concrete injury, the controversy is moot absent a reasonable expectation that the dispute will recur. *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969). In *Golden*, this Court held moot a challenge to an anonymous-handbill statute where the official whose earlier campaign had prompted the suit no longer held office, so that there was no reasonable basis to expect the same dispute would arise again. *Id.* at 108-10. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held—and petitioners do not dispute—that GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and the Student Code of Conduct mooted the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief. That same voluntary and complete abandonment of the challenged provisions likewise deprives the residual nominal-damages request of any live controversy to which it might attach.

*St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), illustrates why a technical remnant of a claim cannot rescue an otherwise moot case. This Court dismissed a contempt appeal as moot once the underlying sentence had been fully served, even though certain collateral consequences arguably persisted, because no genuine, concrete stake in the outcome remained for adjudication. *Id.* at 42-43. By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court declined to find mootness because a real and substantial dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction, giving the parties a continuing, concrete economic stake in the litigation's outcome. *Id.* at 306 & n.3. Petitioners' claim for nominal damages, wholly unmoored from any pleaded actual injury, bears none of the concrete substance that preserved jurisdiction in *Liner*; it is instead the empty formalism that this Court's precedents have never allowed to substitute for a live controversy.

## Conclusion

Because the Prior Policies no longer exist, because petitioners allege no actual injury flowing from them, and because a request for a token sum cannot supply the "real and substantial controversy" that Article III demands, *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 240, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held this case moot, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
