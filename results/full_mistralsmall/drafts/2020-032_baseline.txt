**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That Petitioners’ Claims for Nominal Damages Cannot Save Their Otherwise Moot Claims.**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), controls this case. In *Flanigan’s*, the court held that a request for nominal damages cannot preserve a live controversy when the underlying constitutional challenge to a policy or law has become moot due to repeal or revision. *Id.* at 1255–56. The court reasoned that nominal damages—“a trivial amount awarded to vindicate a technical right”—do not constitute a “legally cognizable injury” sufficient to maintain a justiciable controversy. *Id.* at 1255 (quoting *Lewis v. Continental Bank Corp.*, 494 U.S. 472, 480 (1990)).

Petitioners’ reliance on *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95 (1983), is misplaced. In *Lyons*, the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff seeking injunctive relief must demonstrate a “real and immediate” threat of future harm. *Id.* at 105. The Court did not address whether nominal damages could preserve a moot constitutional challenge, nor did it suggest that nominal damages alone could create a justiciable controversy where none otherwise exists. *See id.* at 105–11. Indeed, the Court has repeatedly recognized that nominal damages are insufficient to confer Article III standing when the underlying controversy is moot. *See, e.g., Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 93 (2013) (holding that a covenant not to sue mooted a trademark dispute and that the plaintiff’s “speculative” claim for nominal damages did not revive the controversy).

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Flanigan’s* is consistent with this principle. The court distinguished cases where a plaintiff seeks compensatory damages—actual monetary harm—for a past constitutional violation, which may preserve a live controversy even if injunctive relief is no longer available. *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256. But where, as here, the plaintiff seeks only nominal damages—“a symbolic award of one dollar”—there is no cognizable injury to adjudicate. *Id.* at 1255. Petitioners’ attempt to recast their nominal damages claim as compensatory fails because they have not alleged any actual harm, such as monetary loss, physical or emotional distress, or reputational injury. *See Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230–31 (11th Cir. 2000) (holding that compensatory damages in a § 1983 action require proof of actual injury).

**II. Petitioners’ As-Applied Challenge Does Not Save Their Claims from Mootness.**

Petitioners argue that their as-applied challenge to Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) policies distinguishes this case from *Flanigan’s*. But the Eleventh Circuit has already rejected this argument. *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256–57. An as-applied challenge becomes moot when the challenged policy is repealed or revised, just as a facial challenge does. *Id.* at 1256. The fact that Petitioners were subject to the policies at some point does not preserve their claims if the policies no longer exist and there is no reasonable expectation they will be reenacted. *Id.* at 1256–57 (citing *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189–92 (2000)).

Moreover, Petitioners’ failure to allege a continuing injury—such as a continuing policy or a credible threat of future enforcement—undercuts their as-applied argument. *See City of Erie v. Pap’s A.M.*, 529 U.S. 277, 287 (2000) (holding that a plaintiff must demonstrate a “continuing, present adverse effect” to maintain an as-applied challenge). Here, GGC has revised its policies to permit expressive activities without restriction, and Petitioners have not alleged that GGC has threatened to reimpose the old policies or that they face a credible risk of future harm. *See Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 93 (holding that a voluntary cessation of challenged conduct does not revive a moot controversy unless there is a “realistic prospect” that the conduct will recur).

**III. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend.**

Petitioners argue that the district court erred in denying their request to amend their complaint to add a claim for compensatory damages. But the district court did not abuse its discretion. *See Santiago v. Wood*, 904 F.2d 673, 675 (11th Cir. 1990). Petitioners sought to amend their complaint in a procedurally improper manner—by embedding their request in a response to a motion to dismiss—rather than filing a proper motion under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a). *See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). The district court correctly noted that Petitioners failed to specify the substance of their proposed amendment or attach a copy of the proposed complaint. *See Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018).

Even if Petitioners had properly moved to amend, the district court could have denied the motion as futile. *See EEOC v. Joe’s Stone Crab, Inc.*, 220 F.3d 1263, 1274 (11th Cir. 2000). Petitioners’ belated attempt to add a claim for compensatory damages—after the policies had been revised and their claims for injunctive and declaratory relief had become moot—would not have cured the mootness problem. *See Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255–56. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed. Petitioners’ claims for nominal damages cannot save their otherwise moot constitutional challenge, and the district court did not err in dismissing their complaint.
