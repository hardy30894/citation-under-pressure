The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. The equal footing doctrine, which grants states title to the beds of navigable waters upon admission to the Union, is rooted in the principle that newly admitted states are entitled to the same rights and privileges as the original thirteen states. United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935); Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212, 228-229 (1845). To determine whether a river is navigable for title purposes, courts have long applied the test of navigability in fact at the time of statehood. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870); United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55-56 (1926).

The trial court's determination that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889 was supported by substantial evidence, including historical records and expert testimony. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court properly considered this evidence in making its determination. The use of historical records to establish navigability is well-established in case law. See, e.g., United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 82-83 (1931); Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 591 (1922).
