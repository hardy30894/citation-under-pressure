## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Enforcing the Releases Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Exist as to Whether the Waivers of WARN Act Rights Were Knowing and Voluntary.

A release of a federal statutory claim is enforceable only if it was executed knowingly and voluntarily. This Court has long applied a "totality of the circumstances" test to determine whether an employee's waiver of a federal employment right meets that standard, examining factors such as the clarity of the agreement, the time given to consider it, the employee's role in negotiating its terms, whether the employee was encouraged to consult counsel, and whether the consideration exceeded what the employee was already owed. *Pierce v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Ry. Co.*, 65 F.3d 562, 570–71 (7th Cir. 1995); *Riley v. American Family Mut. Ins. Co.*, 881 F.2d 368, 371 (7th Cir. 1989). These factors exist precisely because Congress did not intend statutory protections to be extinguished by releases signed under conditions of haste, pressure, or unequal bargaining power.

The record here raises genuine disputes on nearly every relevant factor. Drivers were given a mere two days—from December 9 to December 11, 2008—to decide simultaneously whether to accept the ten-week severance package and how to complete their job-rebid forms, with their employment and recall rights hanging in the balance. Already-terminated office workers received only until December 22, 2008. This compressed timeframe stands in stark contrast to the seven days for revocation nominally promised in the release itself—a promise that could not meaningfully operate given that the underlying election deadline had already passed by the time most workers received or understood the documents. A jury could reasonably find that Appellants had neither the time nor the information necessary to make a genuinely voluntary choice.

Nor can DHL rely on Local 705's participation in the negotiations to establish voluntariness on the individual employees' behalf. The Supreme Court has made clear that a union cannot bargain away an individual employee's independent statutory rights through the collective-bargaining process. *Alexander v. Gardner-Denver Co.*, 415 U.S. 36, 51 (1974) ("the rights conferred can form no part of the collective-bargaining process since waiver of these rights would defeat the paramount congressional purpose" behind the statute). The WARN Act, like Title VII, confers rights that belong to the individual worker, not the bargaining unit; Local 705's agreement to the severance package speaks to the adequacy of *effects bargaining* under the collective bargaining agreement, but it does not, and cannot, establish that each individual employee's waiver of his personal WARN Act claim was knowing and voluntary. That question turns on what each employee understood and experienced, not on what the union agreed to accept on the membership's behalf.

### II. The Consideration Exchanged Was Inadequate to Support a Waiver of Statutory WARN Act Damages.

A release is more likely to be involuntary where the consideration received is less than what the employee was otherwise entitled to receive by operation of law. *Pierce*, 65 F.3d at 571. The WARN Act entitles affected employees to back pay and benefits for each day of a violation, up to sixty days. 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(1). Appellants who accepted the four-week packages received barely half of what the statute would guarantee them if DHL's notice were found deficient, and even the ten-week package fell short once benefits continuation and other statutory elements are accounted for. Where, as here, the "consideration" for surrendering a federal claim is less than the value of the claim itself, and the employee had no realistic opportunity to evaluate that shortfall, the release cannot be deemed a fair, arm's-length settlement of the WARN Act claim; it is instead an artifact of the very economic coercion the statute was designed to prevent. See *Riley*, 881 F.2d at 371 (voluntariness informed by whether "the consideration given in exchange for the waiver exceeded the employee benefits to which the employee was already entitled").

### III. WARN Act Waivers Must Be Construed Consistently with the Statute's Remedial Purpose.

The WARN Act's sixty-day notice requirement exists to give workers and their families "some transition time to adjust to the prospective loss of employment," and its damages remedy is designed to make the failure to provide that time costly to employers who skip it. Congress structured the statute so that ordinary contract principles govern disputes over its administration, but those principles operate within, not against, the statute's protective purpose. *North Star Steel Co. v. Thomas*, 515 U.S. 29, 33–34 (1995) (WARN Act claims are analyzed under general contract-law rules). Permitting an employer facing an admitted mass layoff to extract a release of accrued WARN Act back-pay claims through a rushed, take-it-or-leave-it severance offer—negotiated in the same breath as job-rebid deadlines—would allow employers to use the very transition period WARN was meant to protect as leverage to extinguish the statute's remedy. That result cannot be reconciled with the Act's design.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination, on a full record, of whether each release was knowing and voluntary.
