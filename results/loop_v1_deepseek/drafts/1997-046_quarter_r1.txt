**ARGUMENT**

**I. An Inmate Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings.**

Clemency is a matter of executive grace, not a right. The pardon power is plenary and subject to no judicial control. *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87, 120 (1925). A pardon is an act of grace that cannot be demanded as a matter of right. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). The Constitution vests this power solely in the executive, and courts have no role in its exercise. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866) (describing the pardon power as a power of grace, not of right). Because clemency is a discretionary act, an inmate possesses no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process itself. *Flemming v. Nestor*, 363 U.S. 603, 611 (1960) (recognizing that interests based on legislative grace do not constitute accrued property rights). The Due Process Clause does not create a property interest in a mere hope or expectation. *Cafeteria Workers v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (due process protection depends on the nature of the interest at stake; a gratuity does not trigger constitutional safeguards).

The Sixth Circuit erred by treating clemency as an “integral part” of the adjudicatory process. Clemency is not a stage of trial or appeal; it is a separate executive function that occurs after all judicial proceedings are final. The Supreme Court has long recognized that pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts. *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). That principle is rooted in history and tradition, not in the logic of *Evitts v. Lucey*, which addressed a first appeal as of right—a mandatory judicial proceeding. Here, Ohio has not created any entitlement to clemency. The state’s decision to offer a voluntary interview does not transform a discretionary act into a protected interest.

**II. The Voluntary Interview Option Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment privilege protects only against compelled self-incrimination. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892) (privilege applies only to compulsory incrimination). Ohio’s clemency procedure imposes no compulsion. The inmate may choose whether to request an interview; if he does, he remains free to say as much or as little as he wishes. The regulation does not require any statement, nor does it condition clemency on a waiver of rights. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598 (1896) (privilege does not protect against voluntary disclosures). The mere possibility that a voluntary statement could later be used in some future proceeding does not render the procedure unconstitutional. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 438 (1956) (the privilege is not a general right to silence that immunizes all voluntary statements).

Woodard’s claim of a “Hobson’s choice” is unfounded. He is free to decline the interview entirely. The state has not placed him in a position where he must incriminate himself or forfeit a right. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 32 (1953) (no compulsion where the taxpayer could choose not to engage in the taxed activity). Any speculative risk of self-incrimination is premature. *Boyd v. United States*, 116 U.S. 616, 634 (1886) (courts do not decide abstract questions or hypothetical scenarios). The district court properly declined to adjudicate a risk that may never materialize.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Woodard has no protected interest in clemency, and Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
