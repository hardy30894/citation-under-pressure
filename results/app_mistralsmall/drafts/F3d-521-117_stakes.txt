**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Pat-Frisk of Michael Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment’s Protection Against Unreasonable Searches and Seizures.**

The Fourth Amendment guarantees that “[t]he right of the people to be secure in their persons . . . against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. A pat-frisk constitutes a “search” under the Fourth Amendment, and thus must be “reasonable” to pass constitutional muster. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 16–19 (1968). Reasonableness is determined by balancing “the nature and quality of the intrusion on the individual’s Fourth Amendment interests against the countervailing governmental interests at stake.” *Id.* at 20–21. A pat-frisk is reasonable only if the officer has “specific and articulable facts” giving rise to a reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 21; *see also* *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 226 (1985) (officers may conduct a *Terry* frisk when they have a reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and presently dangerous).

Here, the government has failed to meet its burden of demonstrating that the officers possessed the requisite reasonable suspicion to justify the pat-frisk of Soares. The officers stopped the vehicle solely for a traffic violation—failure to illuminate headlights—which, while a valid basis for the initial stop, does not, without more, justify a frisk. *See* *Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 111 (1977) (per curiam) (officers may order a driver out of a vehicle during a traffic stop); *United States v. Cortez*, 449 U.S. 411, 417–18 (1981) (officers may conduct a *Terry* frisk only when they have a reasonable suspicion that the suspect is armed and dangerous). The government’s reliance on the passengers’ “furtive movements” is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion, as such movements are “ambiguous and susceptible to a wide range of interpretations.” *United States v. Young*, 105 F.3d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 1997); *see also* *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 125 (2000) (flight from police in a high-crime area may contribute to reasonable suspicion, but is not dispositive).

Moreover, Soares’s verbal agitation and profanity, standing alone, do not give rise to a reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous. *See* *United States v. Ashby*, 471 F.3d 256, 261 (1st Cir. 2006) (officers’ subjective fear, without objective justification, is insufficient to support a *Terry* frisk). The government’s argument that Soares’s “erratic behavior” justified the frisk is unavailing, as the First Circuit has repeatedly cautioned that “[a]n officer’s mere hunch or inchoate suspicion is not enough.” *United States v. Ruidíaz*, 529 F.3d 25, 30 (1st Cir. 2008) (quoting *United States v. Lender*, 985 F.2d 151, 154 (1st Cir. 1993)). The officers’ testimony that they felt “uneasy” or “concerned” does not transform their subjective feelings into the objective reasonable suspicion required by *Terry*. *See* *Ashby*, 471 F.3d at 261.

Finally, the government’s reliance on the fact that the stop occurred in a “high-crime area” is misplaced. While a high-crime area is a relevant factor in the *Terry* analysis, it is not dispositive. *See* *Wardlow*, 528 U.S. at 124; *United States v. Burton*, 288 F.3d 91, 96 (1st Cir. 2002). Here, the officers had no specific information linking Soares to criminal activity in the area, nor did they observe any conduct by Soares that would suggest he was involved in criminal activity. *See* *Burton*, 288 F.3d at 96 (officers must have “specific, articulable facts” linking the suspect to criminal activity).

In sum, the government has failed to demonstrate that the officers possessed the requisite reasonable suspicion to justify the pat-frisk of Soares. The district court therefore erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress the handgun found during the unlawful frisk.

II. **The Evidence Seized During the Unlawful Pat-Frisk Must Be Suppressed.**

The exclusionary rule applies to evidence seized as a result of an unlawful search or seizure. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484–85 (1963). The government bears the burden of demonstrating that the evidence in question was not obtained as a result of the constitutional violation. *United States v. Crews*, 781 F.2d 1131, 1134 (11th Cir. 1986) (citing *Brown v. Illinois*, 422 U.S. 590, 603–04 (1975)). Here, the handgun was seized directly from Soares’s person during the unlawful pat-frisk. There is no evidence in the record to suggest that the handgun would have been discovered absent the unlawful frisk. Therefore, the handgun must be suppressed as “fruit of the poisonous tree.” *Wong Sun*, 371 U.S. at 487–88.

The government’s argument that the handgun would have been discovered inevitably is unpersuasive. The inevitable discovery doctrine applies only when the government demonstrates by a preponderance of the evidence that the evidence would have been discovered through lawful means independent of the constitutional violation. *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431, 444 (1984). Here, the government has not identified any lawful means by which the handgun would have been discovered. The officers did not have a warrant, and there is no evidence that they had probable cause to arrest Soares prior to the unlawful frisk. *See* *United States v. Green*, 151 F.3d 1111, 1116 (10th Cir. 1998) (inevitable discovery requires a showing that the evidence would have been discovered through lawful means that were “actively being pursued” prior to the constitutional violation).

Accordingly, the handgun must be suppressed, and Soares’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) cannot stand.
