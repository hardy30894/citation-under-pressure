# ARGUMENT

## I. Milford's Facility Is a Limited Forum, and the School Reasonably Confined It to Secular Community Uses, Excluding Religious Worship and Instruction.

A school building opened after hours for community activities is not a traditional public forum comparable to a street or park that has "immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" and used "time out of mind . . . for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. Committee for Indus. Org.*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Milford's building is dedicated to education during the school day and, afterward, to a limited category of "social, civic and recreational" uses benefiting the community. Because a government need not open its property to every use merely by opening it to some, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). This power to define a forum's boundaries is not novel. As Justice Holmes explained for a unanimous Court, a legislative body may confine the use of public property to certain purposes, and doing so is "no more an infringement of the rights of a member of the public than for the owner of a private house to forbid" an incompatible use of his own premises. *Davis v. Massachusetts*, 167 U.S. 43, 47 (1897). So too, a regulation confining a public facility to designated purposes and applied uniformly, without regard to the content of any group's message, is a valid exercise of the government's authority to regulate the use of its own property in the interest of order and convenience. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941).

Milford's Community Use Policy rests on precisely this footing. It excludes an entire category of activity—religious worship and instruction—regardless of which faith seeks to engage in it. That is a boundary on subject matter, not a preference among viewpoints on a subject otherwise open for discussion. The Club's own submissions confirm that its meetings are not the teaching of morals or character informed by a religious perspective; they are religious exercises in the fullest sense—opening and closing in prayer, memorizing scripture, and culminating in an "invitation" leading children through a recited prayer accepting Christ as personal savior. Excluding that activity from a forum dedicated to civic and recreational use, while admitting groups such as the Boy Scouts and 4-H Club that engage in no comparable religious exercise, is a reasonable definition of the forum's limits, consistent with the State's authority to preserve its property for its lawfully dedicated use. *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47–48.

## II. Milford's Exclusion Is Independently Justified by Its Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation on School Property, Directed at School Children, Immediately After the School Day.

Even if the exclusion implicated free-speech interests, Milford's judgment that hosting the Club would create serious Establishment Clause difficulties independently justifies its decision. The Establishment Clause forbids not only compulsion but government sponsorship of religious exercise, a concern of particular force in the public schools. "No tax in any amount, large or small, can be levied to support any religious activities or institutions, whatever they may be called, or whatever form they may adopt to teach or practice religion." *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947). And "it is no part of the business of government to compose official prayers for any group of the American people to recite as a part of a religious program carried on by government." *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 425 (1962). Nor may government permit religious instruction to be conducted through the apparatus of the public schools, even voluntarily, where the school's own buildings become the site of that instruction. This Court has held it unconstitutional for a public school to make its facilities available for religious teaching, condemning the use of "tax-supported public school buildings" for that purpose. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948). Where the government's own facility becomes the site of religious exercise directed at school-age children immediately after the close of the school day, the risk of perceived school endorsement is not speculative—it is the very harm these decisions were designed to prevent. *See also Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963).

To be sure, the Religion Clauses do not require government hostility toward religion. "The First Amendment, however, does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State." *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312 (1952). But the release-time program upheld in *Zorach* was permissible precisely because it occurred away from school premises, avoiding any appearance of school sponsorship. *Id.* at 308–09. Here, by contrast, the Club's religious exercises would occur in the very classrooms used for compulsory instruction, on the same campus the children attend daily, minutes after the bell—circumstances far closer to the school-sponsored exercises condemned in *Engel*, *McCollum*, and *Schempp* than to the off-site accommodation approved in *Zorach*. The Establishment Clause requires that "the First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion." *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 104 (1968); *see also Torcaso v. Watkins*, 367 U.S. 488, 495 (1961). Milford reasonably concluded that opening its facility to the Club's religious exercises would compromise that neutrality. Its exclusion is therefore independently justified.
