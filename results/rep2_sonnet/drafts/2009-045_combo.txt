# ARGUMENT

## I. The Reasonableness of a Fee Award Has Always Turned on the Quality of Counsel's Work and the Results Achieved.

Long before the "lodestar" became a term of art, courts exercising their traditional equitable authority to award counsel fees examined the quality of representation and the results secured for the client or the fund. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court recognized the equity power of a court to make a "just allowance" to counsel who created or preserved a fund for others, an inquiry that necessarily requires the court to weigh the value of what counsel accomplished, not merely the hours counsel logged. *Id.* at 532–37. The Court reaffirmed and extended that principle in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), holding that counsel who render services benefiting persons other than their own clients are entitled to compensation measured by the character of the services and the benefit conferred. *Id.* at 124–25.

This Court applied the same approach in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), examining the labor, skill, and responsibility of counsel and the results obtained for the fund in determining whether a fee award was excessive. *Id.* at 105–06. And in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court again confirmed that federal courts possess broad equitable discretion to compensate counsel according to the value of services rendered, not according to a mechanical formula. *Id.* at 166–67. Lower courts followed suit. In *Yale Transport Corp. v. Yellow Truck & Coach Manufacturing Co.*, 3 F.R.D. 440 (S.D.N.Y. 1944), the district court expressly weighed the result obtained for the class in fixing a "fair and reasonable" fee. *Id.* at 441–42.

This unbroken line of authority establishes that quality of performance and results obtained are not foreign intruders upon the reasonable-fee inquiry; they are among its oldest and most fundamental components. Nothing in the language of 42 U.S.C. § 1988 suggests that Congress meant to sever the "reasonable fee" it authorized from this settled body of equitable practice. To the contrary, a "reasonable" fee, as courts have understood that term for well over a century, is one that fairly reflects what was actually accomplished and how well it was accomplished.

## II. Enhancement for Superior Performance and Exceptional Results Serves the Statutory Purpose of Securing Competent Counsel for Civil Rights Plaintiffs.

Fee-shifting statutes like § 1988 exist to ensure that private citizens can secure counsel of a quality sufficient to vindicate rights that Congress considered of the highest importance. This Court has repeatedly recognized that a prevailing civil-rights plaintiff's attorney serves not merely a private client but functions as a "private attorney general." *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) (per curiam). The Court there emphasized that Congress wanted such claims "vigorously" pursued and structured fee-shifting to make that possible. *Id.* If the statutory purpose is to attract and reward the kind of representation that actually vindicates the public interest, a fee calculation that is blind to whether counsel performed that function well—or performed it in a manner far exceeding ordinary practice—undermines rather than serves that purpose.

This Court's decision in *J.I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426, 432 (1964), likewise recognized that private enforcement mechanisms are a necessary supplement to public enforcement and must be structured to make private litigation an effective, attractive vehicle for vindicating statutory rights. And in *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 718 (1967), the Court explained that when Congress departs from the American Rule and authorizes fee-shifting, it does so for particular statutory ends that the courts must respect in fashioning the award. Section 1988's end—procuring representation adequate to protect constitutional and civil rights—is served, not disserved, by a fee regime in which extraordinary skill and extraordinary results can be recognized through an enhancement in the rare case where the lodestar, calculated at prevailing market rates, fails to capture what was actually delivered.

## III. The District Court's Fee Determination Is Entitled to Substantial Deference.

Because the reasonable-fee inquiry has traditionally been committed to the equitable discretion of the trial court, appellate review is deferential. *Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 166–67; *Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537. The district judge here—drawing on decades of experience with the local bar and direct observation of counsel's performance throughout years of litigation—found that the "superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." That finding, grounded in the same considerations of skill, labor, and result that have informed fee awards since *Pettus* and *Newton*, was well within the district court's discretion and should not be disturbed.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
