ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Chapter 93-121 imposes unconstitutional burdens on core First Amendment activity. The disclosure regime and contingency-fee prohibition cannot survive the exacting scrutiny required for laws that compel revelation of political associations and expenditures. See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64 (1976) (disclosure “can seriously infringe on privacy of association and belief”); NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (compelled disclosure of membership lists works a “substantial restraint upon the exercise” of associational rights).

Because the League challenges the statute on its face and no member has yet been sanctioned, the action is ripe. Appellants confronted with a choice between forgoing protected conduct and risking civil penalties need not await enforcement. Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53 (1967). Although facial challenges carry a heavy burden, United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987), the statute’s overbreadth is evident from its text: it mandates disclosure of every expenditure by category without regard to whether the communication constitutes protected petitioning of government. See Eastern Railroad Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc., 365 U.S. 127, 137-38 (1961).

The disclosure obligations are not narrowly tailored. They sweep far beyond the “least restrictive means” of achieving any informational interest. Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960). Requiring lobbyists to report expenditures for “research” and “communication” chills the confidential preparation of legislative testimony and strategy, rights safeguarded by the Petition Clause. See United Mine Workers v. Pennington, 381 U.S. 657, 670 (1965).

The ban on contingency fees likewise fails constitutional review. It directly restricts the terms on which citizens may retain advocates to petition the government, a form of content-based regulation subject to strict scrutiny. Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 422-23 (1988); Riley v. National Federation of the Blind of North Carolina, Inc., 487 U.S. 781, 789 (1988). The prohibition cannot be justified by any interest in preventing corruption when less restrictive measures, such as disclosure, are available. McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Commission, 514 U.S. 334, 348-49 (1995); Citizens Against Rent Control/Coalition for Fair Housing v. City of Berkeley, 454 U.S. 290, 299 (1981).

Because the statute’s disclosure and contingency-fee provisions each violate the First Amendment, the judgment should be reversed.
