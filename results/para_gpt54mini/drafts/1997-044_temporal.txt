**Argument**

Section 17 of the Bankruptcy Act of 1898 excepted from discharge liabilities arising from fraud. Bankruptcy is a statutory privilege, not a charter to erase obligations incurred by dishonesty. See *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). The statute’s basic policy was to afford relief to the “honest but unfortunate debtor,” while leaving fraudulent debts intact. See *Cumberland Glass Mfg. Co. v. DeWitt*, 237 U.S. 447, 450 (1915).

I. **Debts founded on fraud are not discharged, and the exception reaches the full liability flowing from the fraud.**

The Supreme Court has long held that fraud defeats discharge. In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877), the Court construed the fraud exception to protect creditors against dishonest conduct. Likewise, *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177, 185 (1900), recognized that a debt procured through fraud falls outside the discharge. The operative question is not whether the debtor is relieved from some portion of the liability, but whether the obligation is one “founded upon” the fraud. *Boyd v. Glucklich*, 116 F. 131, 134 (8th Cir. 1902), explained that when fraud is the source of the debt, the discharge does not reach it.

That principle necessarily includes consequential liabilities imposed because of the fraud. A debt is not transformed into a dischargeable obligation merely because a state court measures part of the remedy by reference to treble damages, attorney’s fees, or costs. The debt remains one arising from the fraudulent wrong. The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that exemptions from discharge are to be applied in light of the wrong that created the liability. See *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 244; *Cumberland Glass*, 237 U.S. at 450.

II. **The tenants’ judgment, including statutory treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs, arose directly from Cohen’s fraud and is therefore nondischargeable.**

Cohen’s debt did not consist merely of an isolated overcharge later reduced to judgment. The bankruptcy court found that he knowingly charged illegal rents and that his conduct constituted fraud under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. Once that fraud was established, the resulting judgment fixed the measure of liability for that fraud. Under settled discharge principles, the debtor cannot sever the compensatory component from the statutory incidents of the same wrongful conduct and discharge the rest. See *Boyd*, 116 F. at 134.

The better reading of the fraud exception is the one that preserves the creditor’s full remedy. Section 17 was designed to prevent a debtor from using bankruptcy to escape the consequences of deceit. See *Neal*, 95 U.S. at 709; *Forsyth*, 177 U.S. at 185. Treble damages under a consumer-fraud statute are not an independent, unrelated obligation; they are the legislatively prescribed measure of damages for the fraud itself. A debtor who induces or inflicts injury by fraudulent means should not be permitted to use bankruptcy to nullify the consequences the law attaches to that misconduct.

III. **Policy and precedent alike forbid giving a fraudulent debtor the benefit of discharge.**

The Bankruptcy Act embodies a distinction between failure and wrongdoing. The Supreme Court has emphasized that discharge is for debtors whose distress is not tainted by fraud. See *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 244. Allowing Cohen to discharge the entire judgment would invert that principle and convert bankruptcy into a refuge for fraud-feasors. Nothing in the Act’s language or history suggests such a result. To the contrary, the fraud exception exists precisely to prevent it. See *Cumberland Glass*, 237 U.S. at 450.

Nor is there any basis for parsing the judgment into dischargeable and nondischargeable portions where each component flows from the same fraudulent course of conduct. The debt here was fixed because Cohen charged illegal rents in violation of duties imposed by law. Once that fraud was adjudicated, the entire resulting liability—including the statutory multiplier, fees, and costs—was part of the debt “founded upon fraud” and fell outside the discharge. See *Boyd*, 116 F. at 134.

**Conclusion**

For more than seventy years before the modern Code, the Supreme Court and the lower federal courts recognized that fraud bars discharge and that the creditor’s full fraud-based recovery is protected from bankruptcy relief. Under those authorities, Cohen cannot discharge the debts arising from his fraudulent conduct, including the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded to the tenants.
