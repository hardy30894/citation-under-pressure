**ARGUMENT**

The district court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction. The Pressls’ complaint seeks only a declaration of rights under a 1960 Virginia flowage easement. No federal question appears on the face of that well-pleaded complaint.

Federal-question jurisdiction exists only when a plaintiff’s right to relief “necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal law.” Franchise Tax Bd. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Tr., 463 U.S. 1, 28 (1983). The complaint here invokes no federal statute, regulation, or constitutional provision; it asks solely whether APCO’s permit requirement exceeds the rights granted by the easement. See Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908) (jurisdiction absent when federal issues arise only as defenses).

APCO’s asserted FERC license does not create jurisdiction. A federal license “does not convert a state-law claim into a federal one merely because the defendant’s conduct was authorized by federal law.” Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386, 393 (1987). The license may supply a defense, but “a case may not be removed to federal court on the basis of a federal defense.” Id. at 393.

Nor does the complaint present a “substantial” federal question under Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308 (2005). The easement’s meaning turns on Virginia property law, not on the construction of any FERC regulation. See Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh, 547 U.S. 677, 699–700 (2006) (no jurisdiction where state-law issues predominate). “The mere presence of a federal issue in a state cause of action does not automatically confer federal-question jurisdiction.” Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804, 813 (1986).

Section 825p likewise supplies no basis for jurisdiction. That provision grants district courts authority over suits “to enforce any liability or duty created by” the Federal Power Act. The Pressls seek no enforcement of any FERC-created duty; they seek only an interpretation of a private easement. See Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 258 (2013) (federal jurisdiction requires that the federal issue be “actually disputed and substantial”).

Because the district court lacked jurisdiction, its judgment must be vacated and the case remanded to state court. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 94 (1998); Bender v. Williamsport Area Sch. Dist., 475 U.S. 534, 541 (1986).
