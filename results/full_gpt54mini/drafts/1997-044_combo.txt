**ARGUMENT**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only debts “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). That language is narrow, and the Bankruptcy Code must be construed in light of the historic policy of giving the honest debtor a fresh start. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Cohen’s debt is not within the exception because the damages award here is not a debt for money obtained by him from the tenants; at most, it is a later-imposed liability measured by conduct already completed. The courts below therefore erred in extending nondischargeability to treble damages, fees, and costs.

I. **The fraud exception applies only to the money or property actually obtained by the debtor.**

This Court has long read discharge exceptions narrowly. In *Bullen v. Holman*, 184 U.S. 298, 301 (1902), the Court explained that bankruptcy relief is to be denied only where Congress has plainly so provided. Likewise, *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562-63 (1915), held that a debt is nondischargeable only when it falls within the precise statutory category. Section 523(a)(2)(A)’s text limits the exception to debts “to the extent obtained by” fraud, which points to the amount actually procured through the fraud, not every downstream consequence.

That reading follows from *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176, 189-90 (1904), where the Court distinguished between the money obtained and other liabilities stemming from the same transaction. The same principle appears in *McIntyre v. Kavanaugh*, 242 U.S. 138, 141-42 (1916), which treated the discharge exception as extending only to the direct fruits of the wrongful obtaining. And in *Davis v. Aetna Acceptance Co.*, 293 U.S. 328, 332-34 (1934), the Court refused to enlarge a discharge exception beyond its ordinary meaning. These cases require a causal and transactional link between the debtor’s fraud and the particular debt sought to be excepted.

Here, the tenants’ rent overcharges were refunded, and the treble-damage component was imposed only by operation of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. That statutory enhancement was not “obtained by” Cohen from the tenants; it was imposed by the court after the fact. The debt therefore falls outside § 523(a)(2)(A)’s text.

II. **Punitive and exemplary damages are not debts “for” money obtained by fraud.**

The decision below improperly treated all liabilities arising from fraud as automatically nondischargeable. But this Court has repeatedly refused to collapse distinct liabilities into a single debt category. In *Kremer v. Chemical Construction Corp.*, 456 U.S. 461, 466 (1982), the Court recognized that a judgment may contain separate components with different legal character; the same is true here. Earlier cases likewise caution against treating one part of a judgment as though it defines all parts. *United States ex rel. Marcus v. Hess*, 317 U.S. 537, 551-52 (1943), distinguished between restitutionary recovery and punishment, reflecting that damages serving different ends are not the same “debt.”

The treble damages awarded here are punitive in nature. *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1852), recognized the historical use of exemplary damages to punish and deter, not to compensate. *Oelrichs v. Spain*, 82 U.S. (15 Wall.) 211, 231-32 (1872), likewise treated exemplary damages as beyond mere compensation. *Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875), described punitive damages as imposed because of the wrongdoer’s conduct, not because the plaintiff was “obtained” property from the debtor. And *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107-08 (1893), confirmed that punitive damages are a separate sanction, not a compensatory debt.

Because the treble damages here punish Cohen for violating state consumer law, they are not debts “for money” obtained by fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A). The lower courts’ contrary rule rewrites the statute to cover every monetary consequence of fraud.

III. **The Bankruptcy Code should not be construed to deny discharge beyond Congress’s chosen limits.**

The “fresh start” policy remains central. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. at 244. This Court has also emphasized that exceptions to discharge are not to be extended by implication. *Bullen*, 184 U.S. at 301; *Gleason*, 236 U.S. at 562-63. In *Davis*, 293 U.S. at 332-34, the Court warned against transforming a limited exception into a general one. And in *Crawford*, 195 U.S. at 189-90, it confined the exception to the debt actually traceable to the fraud.

If Congress had wanted to make all punitive or statutory multipliers nondischargeable whenever fraud is involved, it could have said so. It did not. Instead, it used the phrase “to the extent obtained by” fraud, language that limits nondischargeability to the amount actually obtained. The judgment below should be reversed to that extent.
