The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s],” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a term the statute defines to reach persons who “regularly collect[] or attempt[] to collect . . . debts owed or due . . . another.” The Act therefore “generally does not regulate creditors when they collect debt on their own account.” Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 134 (4th Cir. 2016). Santander purchased the loans outright and thereafter sought to collect them for its own account; it therefore falls outside the statutory definition.

That conclusion follows directly from the text. The phrase “owed or due another” refers to debts that remain owed to a third party at the time of collection. Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (“debts owed [to] . . . another”). It does not encompass debts that the collector has acquired and now owns. Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (“our inquiry . . . is whether [the defendant] regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection”); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another’”).

The status of the debt at the moment of assignment is irrelevant to this inquiry. The Act distinguishes debt collectors from creditors by reference to agency, not default: a debt collector acts “on behalf of others,” while a creditor collects “its own debts.” Henson, 817 F.3d at 135; see also S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698 (“third persons who regularly collect debts for others”). Nothing in § 1692a(6) conditions that distinction on whether the debt was in default when acquired.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would convert every purchaser of defaulted paper into a debt collector simply because the paper once belonged to someone else. That construction finds no support in the statutory language and would subject ordinary creditors to the Act whenever they collect obligations they lawfully own. The complaint alleges no facts showing that Santander was collecting debts “owed or due another” when it contacted petitioners; the district court therefore correctly dismissed the action.
