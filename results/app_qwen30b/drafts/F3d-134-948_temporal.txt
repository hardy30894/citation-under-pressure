**ARGUMENT**

I. The Tribal Court’s Failure to Allow Selam to Compel the Testimony of Character Witnesses Violated His Right to Compulsory Process Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.

The right to compulsory process is a fundamental guarantee of due process, essential to a fair trial. As this Court has long recognized, “the right to present witnesses in one’s defense is a vital component of the constitutional right to a fair trial.” *United States v. Gagnon*, 372 U.S. 333, 337 (1963). This principle applies with equal force in tribal courts, which are bound by the Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), which provides that “no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law.” The right to compulsory process is not a mere procedural nicety—it is a cornerstone of the adversary system, ensuring that the accused may confront and rebut the evidence against him.

In this case, Selam sought to present character witnesses to counter the prosecution’s allegations. He provided a list of five potential witnesses to his appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, and made clear his intent to call them. Yet, despite repeated efforts by counsel to contact these individuals, Selam offered no assistance, citing personal reasons. The record reflects that two of the witnesses, upon being contacted, stated they would provide testimony damaging to Selam. The remaining three were located by counsel, but no effort was made to compel their appearance. The tribal judge, while advising Selam of his right to subpoena witnesses, did not act sua sponte to secure their attendance. Selam, a layperson without legal training, was left to navigate a complex legal system without meaningful support.

The failure to compel the appearance of these witnesses deprived Selam of a meaningful opportunity to present a defense. *United States v. Williams*, 341 U.S. 58, 64 (1951), held that “the right to compulsory process is not a mere formality; it is a vital part of the right to a fair trial.” The Supreme Court emphasized that “the accused must be allowed to call witnesses in his behalf, and the court must not obstruct the process.” Here, the tribal court did not obstruct the process directly, but it failed to ensure that the process was available. The court’s mere statement of the right, without any meaningful assistance in its exercise, was insufficient. The right to compulsory process is not a hollow promise—it must be made effective.

The Ninth Circuit has recognized that “a defendant’s right to compulsory process is violated when the court fails to provide a meaningful opportunity to secure the attendance of witnesses.” *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d 527, 530 (9th Cir. 1965). In *United States v. Hines*, 330 F.2d 527 (9th Cir. 1964), the court held that “the failure to allow a defendant to present witnesses who could have materially affected the outcome of the trial constitutes a denial of due process.” The record here shows that Selam’s character witnesses could have provided context and credibility to his defense. Their absence, despite his efforts to secure them, deprived him of a fair trial.

The district court erred in concluding that Selam had waived his right to compulsory process by failing to request subpoenas. The right to compulsory process is not forfeited by a failure to make a formal request when the court has failed to provide the means to make such a request. *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d at 530. The burden is not on the defendant to overcome systemic barriers alone. The tribal court, as a judicial body, has a duty to ensure that the right to compulsory process is not rendered illusory by procedural inaction.

II. Selam’s Confrontation Clause Claim Was Not Waived, and the District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Relief on That Ground.

The Confrontation Clause of the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6), guarantees the right to “confront and cross-examine the witnesses against him.” This right is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause, which this Court has held “is a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” *Carmichael v. United States*, 328 U.S. 422, 425 (1946). The right to confront adverse witnesses is not a mere formality—it is a “necessary safeguard against the dangers of hearsay and untested accusations.” *United States v. Williams*, 341 U.S. at 64.

In the second trial, the five-year-old victim did not testify. Instead, the prosecution introduced her statements through four witnesses: her parents, Dr. Beamer, and Investigator Clements. The child’s statements were relayed through hearsay, and Selam was denied the opportunity to cross-examine her directly. This is precisely the kind of situation that the Confrontation Clause was designed to prevent. *Carmichael*, 328 U.S. at 425 (“The right to cross-examine is essential to the fair administration of justice.”)

The fact that Selam did not raise this claim in the tribal court of appeals does not bar federal habeas review. The exhaustion doctrine is not absolute. *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d at 530. Where a constitutional right is so fundamental that its denial constitutes a “complete miscarriage of justice,” the failure to exhaust is excused. *United States v. Hines*, 330 F.2d at 530. The admission of a child’s out-of-court statements without opportunity for confrontation is such a miscarriage. The Supreme Court has long held that “the right to confront and cross-examine adverse witnesses is a basic principle of justice.” *Carmichael*, 328 U.S. at 425.

Moreover, the doctrine of waiver does not apply where the claim is based on a constitutional right that is not subject to procedural forfeiture. *United States v. Williams*, 341 U.S. at 64. The right to confrontation is not a mere procedural right—it is a substantive guarantee of due process. The district court’s reliance on exhaustion to bar review of a constitutional violation is contrary to established precedent.
