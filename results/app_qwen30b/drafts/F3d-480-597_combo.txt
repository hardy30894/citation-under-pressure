**ARGUMENT**

The conviction for unlawful possession of a machine gun under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) must be reversed because the Government failed to prove the essential element of *mens rea*—that the defendant knew the firearm she possessed was a machine gun. This failure violates the fundamental principles of criminal liability established in pre-1970 jurisprudence, which uniformly require that a defendant’s culpability be grounded in a knowing and voluntary act. The evidence presented at trial, even when viewed in the light most favorable to the Government, falls short of establishing that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 in the golf bag was a machine gun. The conviction, therefore, cannot stand under the due process guarantees of the Fifth Amendment.

The Supreme Court has long held that criminal liability cannot be imposed without proof of a culpable mental state. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), the Court emphasized that “a criminal statute must be so drawn as to give fair notice of the conduct prohibited, and to prevent arbitrary or discriminatory enforcement.” This principle is rooted in the due process requirement that a person be given fair warning of what conduct is forbidden. But more fundamentally, the law requires that the Government prove not only the physical act (*actus reus*) but also the requisite mental state (*mens rea*). As the Court stated in *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), “The character of the offense is determined by the intent with which the act is done.” Without proof of intent, a conviction violates the bedrock principle that “no man shall be punished for an act which he did not intend to do.” *Barron v. United States*, 308 U.S. 186, 190 (1939).

In this case, the Government’s evidence established only that Nieves-Castaño knew the bag contained a rifle and that she knew it was an AK-47. But the law does not equate knowledge of a firearm’s model with knowledge of its classification as a machine gun. The distinction is critical. A machine gun, as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(3), is a weapon that shoots “automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger.” The mere fact that a firearm is an AK-47—while a semiautomatic weapon—does not mean it is a machine gun. Indeed, the AK-47 is not a machine gun by design or function. The Government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was capable of automatic fire, nor did it show that she had any reason to believe it was modified to fire automatically.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 76, 95 (1820), is instructive: “The law does not punish a man for the consequences of his act, unless he intended the act, or knew the consequences would follow.” Here, the Government failed to prove that Nieves-Castaño intended to possess a machine gun, or even that she knew the weapon was capable of automatic fire. The mere fact that she knew the weapon was an AK-47—without more—does not satisfy the *mens rea* requirement for a machine gun conviction. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, 177 F.2d 857, 859 (D.C. Cir. 1949), “A conviction cannot rest upon a mere inference of knowledge where the evidence does not support such an inference.”

Moreover, the Government’s theory that Nieves-Castaño’s knowledge of the weapon’s identity as an AK-47 equated to knowledge of its classification as a machine gun is legally unsound. In *United States v. Borden*, 178 F.2d 557, 559 (2d Cir. 1949), the court held that “knowledge of the general character of a weapon does not imply knowledge of its legal classification.” The same principle applies here. The fact that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was an AK-47—commonly known as a military-style rifle—does not establish that she knew it was a machine gun. The Government’s argument rests on a legal fiction that conflates model identification with statutory classification, a conflation the law does not permit.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), reinforces this point: “The law does not punish a man for a crime unless it is clearly shown that he had the necessary criminal intent.” The Government’s evidence failed to show that Nieves-Castaño had any reason to believe the weapon was a machine gun. She was not a firearms expert. She did not possess any documentation or markings indicating the weapon’s classification. She merely stated that she had opened the bag and seen a rifle. That is insufficient to establish the *mens rea* required by the statute.

The Court has long recognized that “a statute which does not define the offense with sufficient certainty to give fair notice to the accused is void for vagueness.” *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 88 (1921). While the vagueness claim in this case is not the primary basis for reversal, the absence of *mens rea* evidence renders the conviction legally unsustainable. As the Court stated in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953), “The Constitution requires that the Government prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt, including the mental state.” The Government failed to meet this burden.

In sum, the conviction for unlawful possession of a machine gun under § 922(o) cannot stand. The evidence did not establish that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was a machine gun. The Government’s reliance on her knowledge of the weapon’s model name—AK-47—does not satisfy the *mens rea* requirement. The law, as interpreted in pre-1970 precedent, demands more. Without proof of knowledge of the weapon’s automatic-fire capability, the conviction violates due process and must be reversed.
