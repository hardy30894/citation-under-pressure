**ARGUMENT**

An inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor’s bankruptcy estate under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) because it does not constitute “retirement funds” in the hands of the debtor.

**I. The Text of Section 522 Requires That Funds Be “Retirement Funds” in the Debtor’s Hands**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. The plain meaning of “retirement funds” is funds set aside for retirement. *See Rousey v. Jacoway*, 544 U.S. 320, 326 (2005) (recognizing that IRAs are “designed to provide” retirement savings). An inherited IRA lacks the core attributes of retirement savings. The beneficiary cannot make additional contributions, cannot roll over the account, and—critically—must begin withdrawing the funds within one year of the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. §§ 408(d)(3)(C), 401(a)(9)(B). These mandatory pre-retirement distributions mean the funds are not held for retirement. As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, an inherited IRA “is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” *In re Clark*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013). An asset that must be distributed before the owner reaches retirement age simply is not “retirement funds.”

The statute’s reference to tax-exempt status does not transform an inherited IRA into retirement funds. The exemption requires both that the funds be “retirement funds” *and* that they be in a tax-exempt account. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562. Tax deferral alone is insufficient; otherwise, any tax-deferred account would qualify, regardless of purpose. Indeed, the tax code itself distinguishes an “inherited IRA” from a traditional retirement IRA, imposing different rules that reflect its non-retirement character. The statutory phrase “retirement funds” must be given independent meaning. *See Patterson v. Shumate*, 504 U.S. 753, 758 (1992) (courts must enforce plain language of Bankruptcy Code).

**II. Funds That Belonged to a Decedent Do Not Retain Retirement Character After Death**

Petitioners argue that funds that were retirement funds for the original owner remain retirement funds when inherited. That argument finds no support in the statutory text. The phrase “retirement funds” is not modified by any temporal or ownership qualifier, but the funds must be “in” the account at the time of the bankruptcy. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not say “funds that were ever retirement funds.” When Ruth Heffron died, the funds ceased to be held for retirement. The beneficiary does not use the funds for her own retirement; she must withdraw them beforehand. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562.

The analogy to a homestead exemption is instructive. A debtor cannot exempt a house inherited from her mother as her “domicile” if she does not live there. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A). The exemption depends on the debtor’s use, not the decedent’s. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. Similarly, retirement funds are exempt only if they are retirement funds for the debtor. Holdings in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (8th Cir. BAP 2010) are incorrect. The text of § 522 does not turn on whose retirement is at issue. The relevant question is whether the funds are, at the time of bankruptcy, set aside for retirement. An inherited IRA is not.

**III. Policy Concerns Confirm the Statutory Reading**

Petitioners warn that denying the exemption will discourage retirement savings and reduce bequests. But “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987). If Congress intended to exempt inherited retirement accounts, it could have said so. It did not. The text draws a line: retirement funds are exempt; funds that can be freely spent on current consumption are not. An inherited IRA “represent[s] an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings.” *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565. The bankruptcy code favors creditors over debtors with respect to assets not exempted by clear statutory language. *See Begier v. IRS*, 496 U.S. 53, 58 (1990) (exemptions construed narrowly). Allowing an exemption here would permit debtors to shield assets they must use before retirement, a result Congress could not have intended.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
