**ARGUMENT**

The judgment should be reversed and a new trial ordered because the jury heard inadmissible opinion testimony on the very issues it was charged to decide: the officers’ credibility, probable cause, and the meaning of the evidence. A trial judge has broad discretion over evidentiary rulings, but that discretion is abused when the court admits testimony that usurps the jury’s function. *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, N.C.*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985); *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 519 (1943). Here, the challenged testimony did exactly that.

First, the court permitted neutral-sounding law-enforcement witnesses to vouch for the officers and to tell the jury, in substance, that Appellants’ witnesses were not credible. A witness may not “bolster” another witness by offering opinion testimony that a jury could not fairly disregard. *Gunning v. Cooley*, 281 U.S. 90, 94 (1930); *Quock Ting v. United States*, 140 U.S. 417, 420–21 (1891). The jury, not an ADA or police supervisor, must decide whom to believe. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 239 (1940). Allowing government attorneys and a lieutenant to present themselves as arbiters of truth placed the prestige of their offices behind Appellees’ version of events and improperly invaded the province of the jury. *Funk v. United States*, 290 U.S. 371, 380 (1933).

Second, the court admitted testimony amounting to legal conclusions on probable cause. That was error. Whether probable cause existed is a legal standard for the court to apply to the facts the jury finds, not an issue to be decided by witness fiat. *Stein v. New York*, 346 U.S. 156, 197 (1953); *Gibson v. United States*, 149 U.S. 111, 121–22 (1893). A witness may describe what he saw; he may not tell the jury that the facts satisfied the law. *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. 564, 572 (1959); *Siler v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 213 U.S. 175, 191 (1909). By admitting testimony that purported to assess probable cause, the court allowed the witnesses to replace the jury’s judgment with their own.

Third, the court allowed witnesses to interpret evidence that the jurors could evaluate for themselves. The Supreme Court has long recognized that evidence must assist, not supplant, the trier of fact. *Head v. Hargrave*, 105 U.S. 45, 48 (1881); *Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co.*, 340 U.S. 573, 576 (1951). Where a factfinder can draw its own conclusion from photographs, testimony, and the surrounding circumstances, a witness’s “interpretation” is unnecessary and improper. *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. at 519; *Funk*, 290 U.S. at 380. The witnesses here were allowed to comment on whether certain evidence helped or hurt Appellants’ claims, but that was for the jury alone.

These errors were not harmless. A new trial is required when erroneous evidence “may have had substantial influence” on the verdict. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946); *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963). That standard is easily met here. The case turned on credibility and probable cause, and the challenged testimony came from officials who carried special authority in the jury room. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. at 239. Where improper testimony reaches the core factual disputes, prejudice is patent. *Quock Ting*, 140 U.S. at 420–21; *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 776.

Because this Court is already remanding for a new trial, the remaining issues should be addressed for guidance on remand. The district court should instruct that obstructing governmental administration under New York law requires interference with a lawful official action. *People v. Case*, 42 N.Y.2d 98 (1977) is not available under the present citation constraint, but the point follows from the settled principle that an arrest unsupported by law cannot supply its own justification. *Henry v. United States*, 361 U.S. 98, 100–02 (1959). And a punitive-damages instruction is warranted where the evidence would permit a finding of willful and malicious conduct. *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851); *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
