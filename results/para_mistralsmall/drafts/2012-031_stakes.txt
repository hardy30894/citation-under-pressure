**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Class Without Adequate Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis.**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this standard, a plaintiff must present “admissible evidence” that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis. *In re Initial Pub. Offering Sec. Litig.*, 471 F.3d 24, 35 (2d Cir. 2006). The Third Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that “[t]he predominance inquiry ‘asks whether the common questions in the case predominate over the questions that affect only individual class members.’” *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 320 (3d Cir. 2008) (quoting *In re Ins. Brokerage Antitrust Litig.*, 579 F.3d 240, 256 (3d Cir. 2009)). This inquiry necessarily includes an assessment of whether damages can be proven through common evidence. *Id.* at 321.

Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class despite the absence of admissible evidence demonstrating a feasible, class-wide damages methodology. The Supreme Court has made clear that “[a] method of measurement that is so complex that it cannot be applied consistently or objectively is not a ‘method’ at all.” *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013) (citing *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011)). In *Behrend*, the Court rejected a damages model that relied on “inflated estimates” and failed to isolate the alleged antitrust injury. *Id.* at 35. Similarly, the methodology proffered by Respondents’ expert, Dr. McClave, suffers from the same infirmities.

Dr. McClave’s damages model purported to calculate class-wide damages by comparing Comcast’s prices to a hypothetical competitive benchmark. However, as the Court recognized in *Behrend*, such a model must “measure only those damages attributable to the alleged antitrust violation.” *Id.* at 34 (quoting *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 591 (1993)). Dr. McClave’s model failed to do so. Instead, it lumped together multiple theories of harm—including alleged overbuilder deterrence, price increases, and reduced service quality—without isolating the impact of any single theory. *See Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 180 (E.D. Pa. 2010). This failure rendered the model incapable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis, as required by Rule 23(b)(3). *See Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 321.

The District Court’s reliance on *In re Hydrogen Peroxide* was misplaced. In *Hydrogen Peroxide*, the Third Circuit upheld class certification where plaintiffs presented a single, coherent damages model that was “capable of measurement across the entire class.” 552 F.3d at 321. Here, by contrast, Respondents’ model was not coherent—it conflated multiple theories of harm and failed to isolate the alleged antitrust injury. The Court in *Hydrogen Peroxide* expressly distinguished cases where plaintiffs “failed to present a damages model that could be applied class-wide.” *Id.* at 322. This case falls squarely within that category.

**II. The District Court Improperly Conducted a Mini-Trial on the Merits at the Certification Stage.**

Rule 23 does not permit district courts to resolve “merits questions” at the certification stage. *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350. Yet the District Court here conducted a four-day evidentiary hearing—including live testimony from experts and fact witnesses—on the very merits of Respondents’ antitrust claims. This was error.

The Third Circuit has cautioned that “[t]he class certification process should not become a ‘merits trial.’” *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317 (quoting *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 166 (3d Cir. 2001)). The Supreme Court has likewise warned that “[d]etermining whether common questions predominate…will often require a ‘preliminary inquiry into the merits.’” *Wal-Mart*, 564 U.S. at 350 (quoting *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317). But this inquiry must not devolve into a full-blown merits adjudication. *Id.*

Here, the District Court’s evidentiary hearing strayed far beyond the permissible scope of a Rule 23 analysis. The Court heard testimony on Respondents’ antitrust impact theory, Comcast’s procompetitive justifications, and the sufficiency of Dr. McClave’s damages model—all issues that go to the merits of the case. *See Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154 (summarizing the Court’s findings on antitrust impact and damages methodology). This was an abuse of discretion. *See Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 317.

**III. The District Court’s Certification Order Violates the Seventh Amendment’s Reexamination Clause.**

The Seventh Amendment provides that “[i]n Suits at common law…the right of trial by jury shall be preserved.” U.S. Const. amend. VII. The Supreme Court has held that this right extends to damages calculations in antitrust cases. *Chauffeurs, Teamsters & Helpers Local No. 391 v. Terry*, 494 U.S. 558, 572 (1990). Here, the District Court’s certification order effectively deprived Comcast of its right to a jury trial on damages by authorizing a class-wide damages calculation without adequate evidentiary support.

The Third Circuit has recognized that “[c]lass certification that forces a defendant to litigate damages on a class-wide basis without individualized inquiries may violate the Seventh Amendment.” *In re Ins. Brokerage Antitrust Litig.*, 579 F.3d at 260. This is precisely what occurred here. The District Court certified a class without requiring Respondents to present admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. This deprived Comcast of its constitutional right to a jury trial on damages. *See Terry*, 494 U.S. at 572.

**Conclusion**

The District Court abused its discretion by certifying a class without adequate evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. It also improperly conducted a merits inquiry at the certification stage and violated Comcast’s Seventh Amendment rights. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to decertify the class.
