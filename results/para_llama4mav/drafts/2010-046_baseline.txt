ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT'S ORDER REDUCING THE PRISON POPULATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) requires that any relief ordered in a prison conditions case be "narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The district court's order reducing the prison population to 137.5% of design capacity satisfies this requirement.

The Supreme Court has held that the PLRA does not prohibit courts from ordering relief that is necessary to remedy a constitutional violation. See Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511 (2011) (holding that the PLRA "does not authorize courts to order relief that is not 'narrowly drawn' or that 'extends further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right'"). In this case, the district court found that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the constitutional violations in California's prisons, and that reducing the population to 137.5% of design capacity was necessary to remedy those violations.

The Ninth Circuit has also upheld the district court's order, holding that it was "narrowly drawn" and "necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right." See Plata v. Schwarzenegger, 603 F.3d 1088, 1094 (9th Cir. 2010). The court noted that the district court had considered alternative remedies, but had determined that reducing the prison population was the "least intrusive means" necessary to correct the constitutional violations. Id.

Other circuits have also interpreted the PLRA to permit courts to order relief that is necessary to remedy constitutional violations. See, e.g., Jones v. Bock, 549 U.S. 199, 223 (2007) (holding that the PLRA "does not strip courts of their authority to order relief that is necessary to remedy a constitutional violation"); Benjamin v. Fraser, 264 F.3d 175, 201 (2d Cir. 2001) (holding that the PLRA "does not prohibit courts from ordering relief that is necessary to remedy a constitutional violation").

In this case, the district court's order is necessary to remedy the constitutional violations caused by overcrowding in California's prisons. The order is narrowly drawn and extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right. Therefore, it does not violate the PLRA.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT'S ORDER IS A PROPER EXERCISE OF ITS AUTHORITY TO REMEDY CONSTITUTIONAL VIOLATIONS

The district court's order reducing the prison population is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy constitutional violations. The court has a "broad and flexible" equitable power to remedy constitutional violations, see Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493, 511 (2011), and the PLRA does not limit this power.

The Supreme Court has held that courts have a "duty to provide relief that is necessary to remedy a constitutional violation." See Missouri v. Jenkins, 515 U.S. 70, 88 (1995). In this case, the district court's order is necessary to remedy the constitutional violations caused by overcrowding in California's prisons.

The Ninth Circuit has also held that the district court's order is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy constitutional violations. See Plata v. Schwarzenegger, 603 F.3d 1088, 1094 (9th Cir. 2010). The court noted that the district court had carefully considered the evidence and had determined that reducing the prison population was necessary to remedy the constitutional violations. Id.

In conclusion, the district court's order reducing the prison population to 137.5% of design capacity is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy constitutional violations and does not violate the PLRA. The order is narrowly drawn and necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is therefore a valid exercise of the court's equitable power.
