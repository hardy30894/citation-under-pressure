**ARGUMENT**

### I. The Bureau of Prisons may not convert a nonviolent drug conviction into a violent offense based on a sentencing enhancement.

Congress authorized a sentence reduction for any prisoner “convicted of a nonviolent offense” who successfully completes drug treatment. 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The statute turns on the offense of conviction, not on post hoc administrative relabeling. That reading is compelled by the text and by the Supreme Court’s repeated instruction that courts must apply statutory terms as written unless Congress clearly provides otherwise. *TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 31 (2001); *Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992).

Roussos was convicted of conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846. That offense is not, by its elements, a violent crime. The Government’s weapon finding was not an element of the offense of conviction; it was a sentencing factor used to increase the guideline range. Under the categorical approach, the focus is on the statutory offense, not the particular facts of the case. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600-02 (1990); *Nijhawan v. Holder*, 557 U.S. 29, 34 (2009). The BOP therefore exceeded its authority when it treated a drug conspiracy conviction as a “crime of violence” solely because the sentencing court applied a firearm enhancement.

This Court should follow the analysis of other courts of appeals that have rejected similar attempts to use conduct-based sentencing facts to deny early-release eligibility. See *Martin v. Gerlinski*, 133 F.3d 1076, 1080-82 (8th Cir. 1998); *Downey v. Crabtree*, 100 F.3d 662, 668-69 (9th Cir. 1996); *Davis v. Crabtree*, 109 F.3d 566, 568-69 (9th Cir. 1997). Those decisions correctly recognize that the BOP may not redefine the offense of conviction by reference to sentencing enhancements.

### II. Program Statement 5162.02 conflicts with the governing statute and regulation and is not entitled to deference.

An agency may issue interpretive guidance, but it may not promulgate a policy that contradicts a statute or regulation. *Mourning v. Family Publ’ns Serv., Inc.*, 411 U.S. 356, 369 (1973); *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 302-03 (1979). Here, the BOP regulation states that an inmate “may be eligible for early release” unless the inmate’s “current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. Program Statement 5162.02 goes further and automatically treats certain § 846 convictions as violent whenever a firearm enhancement was imposed. That is not a definition found in § 924(c)(3). It is an administrative gloss that adds a new disqualifier Congress did not enact.

Agency interpretations that conflict with the controlling statute receive no deference. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 n.9 (1984); *Good Samaritan Hosp. v. Shalala*, 508 U.S. 402, 409-10 (1993). Even where an agency interprets its own regulations, deference does not extend to an interpretation that is plainly inconsistent with the regulation’s text. *Auer v. Robbins*, 519 U.S. 452, 461 (1997). Program Statement 5162.02 cannot survive because it substitutes a firearm-enhancement rule for the regulation’s cross-reference to the federal definition of “crime of violence.”

### III. A sentencing enhancement for firearm possession does not transform a narcotics conspiracy into a crime of violence under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).

Section 924(c)(3) covers only offenses that have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of force, or that by their nature involve a substantial risk that force may be used in committing the offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). A drug conspiracy under § 846 does not contain a force element. Nor does the offense, in the abstract, “by its nature” involve the use of physical force. *Leocal v. Ashcroft*, 543 U.S. 1, 7-11 (2004) (looking to the “nature” of the offense, not to speculative or tangential conduct, in construing a similar violence definition). The BOP’s contrary conclusion improperly collapses a sentencing enhancement into the offense itself.

The sentencing court’s two-level enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 for possession of a firearm during a drug offense also does not change the character of the conviction. Enhancements are not elements of the offense and do not alter the categorical nature of the conviction. *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 154-57 (1997); *Almendarez-Torres v. United States*, 523 U.S. 224, 235 (1998). If the BOP’s view were accepted, any drug offender who received a firearm enhancement would be categorically barred from early release, even though Congress limited the disqualification to prisoners convicted of “nonviolent” offenses and did not authorize the Bureau to create a broader exclusion.

### IV. Roussos is entitled to habeas relief.

Because the BOP’s policy is contrary to § 3621(e)(2)(B) and 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, the denial of eligibility was unlawful. Habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 is proper where the Executive Branch misapplies a federal statute governing the duration of custody. *Preiser v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 475, 487-88 (1973); *Barden v. Keohane*, 921 F.2d 476, 478-79 (3d Cir. 1991). Roussos completed the program Congress encouraged, and nothing in the statute authorizes the BOP to deny him the benefit based on a firearm enhancement that was not an element of his conviction.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to grant the petition.
