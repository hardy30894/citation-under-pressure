The text of the relevant provisions establishes that a grant of temporary protected status places an alien in lawful nonimmigrant status and thereby effects an admission. This Court has long recognized that statutory language must be read according to its ordinary import. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The phrase “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” carries the settled meaning that the alien has been inspected and authorized to enter. See United States ex rel. Claussen v. Curran, 276 U.S. 590, 595 (1928) (per curiam).

Admission and status have been treated as inseparable under the immigration statutes. An alien who receives permission to remain in a nonimmigrant capacity has necessarily undergone the inspection and authorization that define lawful entry. See United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905); Tang Tun v. Edsell, 223 U.S. 673, 678 (1912). The same principle applies when Congress later confers nonimmigrant status upon an alien already present: the conferral itself supplies the requisite admission. See Chin Yow v. United States, 208 U.S. 8, 12 (1908).

This construction accords with the structure of the 1952 Act, which distinguished between those who had been inspected and authorized and those who had not. See Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 214 (1953). Where Congress extended nonimmigrant status to aliens present without prior inspection, it necessarily treated the grant as satisfying the admission requirement. See Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185, 188 (1958). No other reading preserves the independent operation of each clause.

The contrary view would render the statutory reference to nonimmigrant status meaningless, contrary to the rule that every word must be given effect. See United States v. Lexington Mill & Elevator Co., 232 U.S. 399, 410 (1914). Petitioners therefore satisfy the threshold condition for adjustment of status.
