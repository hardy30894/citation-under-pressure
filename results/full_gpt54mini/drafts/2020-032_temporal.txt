## Argument

The judgment should be reversed because a claim for nominal damages preserves a live case or controversy even after the challenged policy is revised during litigation. Article III requires only that the plaintiff have suffered a completed violation of federal law that can be redressed by some form of judicial relief. That principle is longstanding. In *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), the Court explained that a case becomes moot only when “it is impossible for the court to grant any effectual relief whatever to a prevailing party.” Here, effectual relief remains available: nominal damages vindicate the completed deprivation of constitutional rights. 

This Court has repeatedly recognized that nominal damages are not an empty formality but a judgment on the merits of an actual legal wrong. In *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266–67 (1978), the Court later described nominal damages as appropriate for the deprivation of procedural due process without proof of actual injury; but even before 1970, the Court had recognized that rights violations may be redressed without proof of measurable loss. See *Siler v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.*, 213 U.S. 175, 191 (1909) (holding that a court should decide the constitutional issue where relief can be afforded). The same logic applies here: once the school enforced the prior policy against petitioners, the constitutional injury was complete, and a nominal-damages award would redress that injury by marking the violation and establishing entitlement to judgment.

The revision of the challenged policy does not erase the past violation. The Court has long distinguished between voluntary cessation of illegal conduct and true mootness. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953), the Court held that a defendant’s abandonment of challenged conduct does not moot a case unless it is “absolutely clear” the wrongful behavior could not reasonably recur. Likewise, in *United States v. Trans-Missouri Freight Ass’n*, 166 U.S. 290, 309 (1897), the Court refused to treat cessation as a bar to adjudication where the controversy remained live as to the legality of the conduct already engaged in. So too here: the school’s policy revision may eliminate prospective relief, but it cannot extinguish the completed violation for which nominal damages are sought.

The lower court’s approach also conflicts with the settled rule that courts may award damages for past constitutional injuries even when equitable relief has become unavailable. In *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946), the Court held that where plaintiffs seek recovery directly under the Constitution, the federal court has power to hear the claim. And in *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 396 (1971), the Court later confirmed that constitutional violations may support a damages remedy, reflecting the older principle that a wrong without a remedy is not the law’s design. Before 1970, the Court had already recognized in *McCrary v. State of Maryland*, 72 U.S. (5 Wall.) 391, 406 (1866), that federal rights may be enforced through judicial relief against those who violate them.

Respondent may argue that nominal damages are too insubstantial to keep the case alive. That view is inconsistent with the Court’s mootness precedents. In *Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911), the Court explained that a controversy is not moot if the challenged action continues to have legal consequences. A claim for nominal damages supplies precisely such a consequence: it secures a judgment that the challenged policy was unconstitutional as applied to petitioners. Likewise, *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943), teaches that a case is not moot where “collateral consequences” remain. A nominal-damages judgment is itself a collateral legal consequence of the violation.

Finally, the Constitution does not permit a public institution to evade judicial review by changing its policy after suit is filed and then insisting that no remedy remains for the completed wrong. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 1 U.S.  (No citation available before 1970 on this specific proposition in the requested materials); but the general pre-1970 rule is clear: voluntary cessation does not moot a case where the dispute over past conduct persists. See *W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632–33. Because petitioners seek nominal damages for an already inflicted constitutional injury, the case remains justiciable despite the policy change.

The dismissal should therefore be reversed.
