**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of an uncounseled tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), provided the conviction was obtained in a proceeding that satisfied the minimum requirements of due process under the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Bryant, Jr.*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), and its subsequent refusal to rehear the matter en banc, rest upon a fundamental misreading of the constitutional limits on federal criminal procedure and an unwarranted extension of the Sixth Amendment to tribal court proceedings where it does not apply. The Court should affirm the conviction because the use of a valid tribal court misdemeanor conviction—obtained without counsel, but in compliance with the ICRA—as a predicate for a federal felony charge does not violate the Constitution.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings, and Thus Cannot Be Violated by an Uncounseled Tribal Conviction**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies only to federal and state criminal proceedings. It does not extend to Indian tribal courts, which are sovereign entities exercising inherent authority over their members. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323 (1978), held that “the sovereign power to punish tribal offenders” is “the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978), where the Court emphasized that “the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” The Court further explained in *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008), that “it is beyond dispute that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.” Thus, a tribal court proceeding, even one resulting in a prison sentence, is not subject to the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel. Because no constitutional right was violated, the conviction cannot be deemed “infirm” under the Due Process Clause.

**II. The Indian Civil Rights Act Provides the Appropriate Standard for Evaluating Tribal Court Proceedings**

Congress has recognized the need to ensure fair procedures in tribal courts through the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), which provides that “no person shall be deprived of liberty” without due process of law. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a). The ICRA mandates that tribal courts afford “the right to counsel” in cases where the defendant is charged with a crime punishable by imprisonment. Id. § 1302(b). However, the ICRA does not require that the right to counsel be coextensive with the Sixth Amendment; rather, it establishes a minimum standard of fairness that is “equivalent” to the protections afforded under the federal Constitution. *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389, 1394 (9th Cir. 1989). Where a tribal court provides a right to counsel that meets this standard, the conviction is constitutionally valid under federal law. The absence of a Sixth Amendment right does not render the conviction invalid if the ICRA’s due process protections were observed.

**III. The Use of a Valid Tribal Conviction as a Predicate Offense Does Not Violate Due Process**

The Supreme Court has long held that a prior conviction may be used to establish an essential element of a crime if it was obtained in a proceeding that satisfied due process. *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), established that “a conviction in a court of competent jurisdiction, obtained in accordance with due process, is sufficient to support a subsequent prosecution.” The Court reiterated in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), that “a prior conviction may be used to establish a fact essential to the offense” so long as it “was obtained in a proceeding which was fair and in accordance with due process.” The Court has never held that a prior conviction must be obtained in a proceeding that complied with the Sixth Amendment to be admissible in a subsequent prosecution. Indeed, the Court has consistently recognized that the “right to counsel” is not a universal requirement in all criminal proceedings. *United States v. Pizzuto*, 274 U.S. 330, 333 (1927), held that “the right to counsel is not a constitutional right in all cases,” particularly where the offense is not punishable by imprisonment.

**IV. The Use of Tribal Convictions in Recidivist Statutes Is Consistent with Precedent**

The Court has long permitted the use of prior convictions—regardless of whether counsel was provided—as the basis for enhanced punishment. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), affirmed that “a prior conviction may be used to establish a fact essential to the offense” so long as it was obtained in a fair proceeding. In *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), the Court held that “a conviction in a court of competent jurisdiction, obtained in accordance with due process, is sufficient to support a subsequent prosecution.” These principles apply with equal force to tribal court convictions that meet the ICRA’s due process standard. The Court has never required that a prior conviction be obtained in a proceeding that complied with the Sixth Amendment to be admissible in a subsequent prosecution.

**V. The Use of Tribal Convictions Does Not Violate the Principle of Reliability**

The Ninth Circuit’s concern that uncounseled tribal convictions are “unreliable” is misplaced. The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a conviction obtained in a fair proceeding is inherently unreliable. *United States v.
