# ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Rand because genuine disputes of material fact exist concerning whether the Mouas are entitled to a three-year rescission period under the Truth in Lending Act ("TILA") and the Home Ownership and Equity Protection Act ("HOEPA"). Summary judgment is proper only when no reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). This Court reviews the district court's grant of summary judgment de novo. *Meyer v. Tenvoorde Ford, Inc.*, 526 F.3d 1091, 1093 (8th Cir. 2008).

## I. The District Court Erred by Concluding That Rand Made All Material Disclosures Required by TILA and HOEPA

TILA provides that a borrower's right to rescind a transaction extends from three days to three years if the lender fails to deliver "all material disclosures" required by the Act. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(f). Material disclosures include the annual percentage rate, the finance charge, the amount financed, the total of payments, and the payment schedule. 15 U.S.C. § 1602(u); *Rodash v. AIB Mortgage Co.*, 16 F.3d 1142, 1144-45 (11th Cir. 1994). The disclosure requirements are strictly enforced; even minor inaccuracies may trigger the extended rescission period. *Semar v. Platte Valley Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass'n*, 791 F.2d 699, 704 (9th Cir. 1986).

Here, the loan documents list an initial interest rate of 13.990% that increases to 14.990% after one year, with monthly payments of $2,883.55 escalating to $3,272.04. The Mouas contend Rand failed to disclose the true finance charge and the total of payments over the life of the loan, particularly given the adjustable-rate structure. A genuine dispute exists whether the disclosures were accurate and complete. *See, e.g., Smith v. Fid. Consumer Disc. Co.*, 898 F.2d 907, 912 (3d Cir. 1990) (holding that failure to disclose the finance charge within the tolerance permitted by Regulation Z extends the rescission period).

Moreover, HOEPA imposes enhanced disclosure requirements for high-cost loans like the one at issue, including a three-day advance disclosure period. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b)(1). The statute permits waiver of this period only in a "bona fide personal financial emergency." 15 U.S.C. § 1639(b)(2); *In re First Alliance Mortgage Co.*, 471 F.3d 977, 988 (9th Cir. 2006). Rand contends the Mouas waived this three-day period because of the pending foreclosure redemption deadline. But the Mouas testified they did not understand the waiver statement they were asked to copy, and they were not informed of its significance. Manisy Moua stated she simply copied a typed statement without comprehension. A reasonable jury could find that no genuine emergency existed—the redemption period still had 41 days remaining—and that the waiver was not knowing and voluntary. *See In re Smith*, 737 F.2d 1549, 1552 (8th Cir. 1984) (TILA imposes strict liability and requires clear disclosure).

## II. The District Court Erred in Holding That Rand Provided Adequate Notice of the Right to Cancel

TILA requires lenders to deliver two copies of a "clear and conspicuous" notice of the borrower's right to rescind. 15 U.S.C. § 1635(a); 12 C.F.R. § 226.23(b)(1). The notice must explain that the right to rescind expires three business days after the transaction, the last day to exercise the right, and the method for rescinding. The Mouas received a Notice of Right to Cancel on the closing date, April 22, 2005, which stated they had until midnight on April 26, 2005, to cancel.

However, Rand also required the Mouas to sign a "Confirmation" section stating that "[m]ore than 3 business days have elapsed since the date of the new transaction" and that the transaction had not been rescinded. The Mouas signed this confirmation on the same day as the closing—April 22, 2005—which was before the three-day rescission period had even begun. This was a de facto waiver of the rescission right. The Eleventh Circuit has held that requiring a borrower to sign a pre-printed confirmation of non-rescission contemporaneously with the closing violates TILA, because it "effectively nullifies the right to rescind." *Rodash*, 16 F.3d at 1146. The court reasoned that such a practice misleads the borrower into believing the rescission right has already expired. *Id.*

Rand does not dispute that the Confirmation was signed on April 22. It merely argues that Excel Title, not Rand, directed the Mouas to sign it. But Rand is vicariously liable for the actions of its closing agent. *See In re First Alliance*, 471 F.3d at 990 (lender liable for acts of its agents in connection with TILA disclosures). The Confirmation's simultaneous execution created a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the Mouas received meaningful notice of their right to cancel.

## III. The District Court Erred by Failing to Consider the Prepayment Penalty as a HOEPA Violation

HOEPA prohibits prepayment penalties on loans with an annual percentage rate exceeding the yield on Treasury securities by more than eight percentage points. 15 U.S.C. § 1639(c)(1). The Mouas' loan carries an interest rate of 13.990% at inception, which at the time exceeded the applicable threshold. The note contains a provision requiring payment of a penalty if the loan is prepaid within the first three years. If the prepayment penalty is impermissible under HOEPA, the transaction is subject to rescission for up to three years. *See* 15 U.S.C. § 1639(j) (giving borrowers the right to rescind for violations of § 1639). The district court did not address this argument. A genuine dispute exists whether the loan's rate qualifies it as a "high-cost mortgage" under HOEPA, and whether the prepayment penalty violates the statute.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for trial. At minimum, genuine issues of material fact preclude summary judgment on the Mouas' claim that they are entitled to a three-year rescission period under TILA and HOEPA.
