**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 795-96 (1969); *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). When the State elects to seek a sentence enhancement based on a prior conviction allegation, it must prove that allegation once, and if it fails, the Constitution forbids a second attempt. The constitutional command is not confined to the label “trial”; it turns on whether the defendant has already been put to the risk of a final adverse determination on the same factual issue. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957); *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 128-29 (1904).

That rule follows from the historical core of double jeopardy protection. This Court has long held that the prohibition against being twice put in jeopardy protects against repeated efforts by the State to secure a conviction after it has once failed. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671-72 (1896); *United States v. Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. 85, 87-88 (1916). The Clause applies when the government has had one fair opportunity and lost. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 188. It prevents the prosecution from subjecting a defendant to repeated trials, with the accompanying “embarrassment, expense and ordeal.” *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 127 (1980) (describing the settled historical understanding); but the earlier cases already recognized the same danger. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173-74 (1873); *In re Nielsen*, 131 U.S. 176, 188-89 (1889).

The State’s position fails because a prior-conviction allegation is not some free-standing administrative fact. It is an adjudicative issue that determines whether the defendant’s punishment will be increased because of a particular past offense. Once the court has received evidence and made a true-false finding, the defendant has been put in jeopardy as to that allegation. The Constitution does not permit the State to relitigate the same allegation until it obtains the result it wants. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671; *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29; *Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. at 87-88.

The State cannot evade double jeopardy by calling the proceeding “sentencing.” This Court has never held that constitutional protection disappears whenever a factual issue is resolved during sentencing. To the contrary, the Court has treated as critical whether the proceeding adjudicates a factual predicate for punishment in a manner sufficiently conclusive to expose the defendant to an enhanced penalty. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 795-96. The same principle appears in the Court’s prior-jeopardy cases: the bar applies when the government has obtained, and then lost, a definitive judgment on the relevant factual issue. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72; *United States v. Sanges*, 144 U.S. 310, 312-13 (1892).

Nor is there any sound reason to carve out noncapital sentencing proceedings from the Double Jeopardy Clause. The constitutional text does not say “capital cases only.” It says no person shall be twice put in jeopardy for the same offense. U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has repeatedly refused to permit formalism to defeat constitutional protection. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 190-91. If a prior conviction allegation is sufficiently serious to double a prison term, then the State’s failure to prove it should have the same consequence that attends any other failed prosecution: no second bite at the apple.

The California courts’ contrary rule would create an anomalous result. The State may proceed to a factual adjudication, lose because its evidence is insufficient, and yet start over until it succeeds. That is precisely the evil the Double Jeopardy Clause was designed to prevent. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
