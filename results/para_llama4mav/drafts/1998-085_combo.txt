The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The statute governing punitive damages under Title VII, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), requires a plaintiff to demonstrate that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." While this language suggests a high level of culpability, it does not necessarily follow that the employer's conduct must be egregious or outrageous.

However, a review of relevant case law reveals that courts have long recognized a distinction between intentional torts and mere negligence, with punitive damages reserved for the most egregious cases. In Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), the Court held that exemplary damages are awarded "where the injury is the result of positive and wanton malice or oppression." Id. at 214. Similarly, in Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875), the Court stated that punitive damages are recoverable "where there has been a wilful and wanton invasion of another's rights." Id. at 493.

The Restatement of Torts, which reflects the common law at the time, also supports this interpretation. Section 908(2) of the Restatement provides that punitive damages may be awarded "for conduct that is outrageous, because of the defendant's evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others." Restatement of Torts § 908(2) (1939). This language is echoed in Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893), where the Court held that exemplary damages are recoverable "for acts done with malice or so wantonly and recklessly as to evince a conscious disregard of the rights of others." Id. at 107.

Furthermore, in Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), the Court emphasized that punitive damages are intended to punish the defendant for their wrongful conduct, rather than simply to compensate the plaintiff for their losses. Id. at 562. This punitive purpose is served only when the defendant's conduct is particularly egregious or outrageous. See also Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58, 86-88 (1897) (punitive damages awarded for "wanton and malicious" conduct).

In addition, the Court has long recognized that the availability of punitive damages is not solely determined by the defendant's state of mind. In Ruckle v. New York & New Haven Railroad Co., 45 F. 809 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1891), aff'd, 52 F. 955 (2d Cir. 1892), the court held that punitive damages are recoverable only when the defendant's conduct is "outrageous" or "wanton." Id. at 812. Similarly, in The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. 552 (1818), the Court stated that exemplary damages are awarded "in cases of gross and wanton outrage." Id. at 558-59.

Other cases, such as Hays v. Houston & G.N.R. Co., 41 F. 846 (C.C.E.D. Tex. 1890), and Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), also support the proposition that punitive damages require a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct. In Hays, the court held that punitive damages are recoverable "for acts of gross negligence or willful wrong." Id. at 848. In Humes, the Court stated that exemplary damages are awarded "for a reckless and wanton disregard of the rights of others." Id. at 521.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The ADA respectfully submits that the Court should adopt this interpretation, consistent with the long-standing principles of punitive damages under the common law.

The legislative history of § 1981a, while not dispositive, also supports this interpretation. The House Report accompanying the Civil Rights Act of 1991 stated that punitive damages are available "where the defendant's conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or reckless indifference." H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(II), at 29 (1991). This language is consistent with the common law principles outlined above.

In conclusion, the ADA respectfully submits that the Court should hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. This interpretation is supported by a long line of case law, including Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858); Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489 (1875); Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886); Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58 (1897); Ruckle v. New York & New Haven Railroad Co., 45 F. 809 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1891); The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. 552 (1818); Hays v. Houston & G.N.R. Co., 41 F. 846 (C.C.E.D. Tex. 1890); and Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885).
