**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite confinement beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court has already answered the question presented in this case. Section 1231(a)(6), read in light of the Constitution, permits detention only for a period “reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal,” and “does not permit indefinite detention.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 689, 697, 121 S. Ct. 2491, 2495, 2498, 150 L. Ed. 2d 653 (2001).

1. **The statutory text does not clearly authorize endless custody.**  
Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain removable aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). But the statute says nothing about detention for years, let alone forever. The Supreme Court rejected the Government’s reading because Congress did not “speak with clarity” to authorize the serious liberty deprivation of potentially permanent civil detention. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 696. Instead, the Court construed the statute to include an implicit temporal limit to avoid grave constitutional difficulty. *Id.* at 690, 699.

That construction was compelled by the longstanding principle that “where an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress.” *Id.* at 689 (quoting *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Constr. Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988)). Applying that principle, the Court held that post-removal detention is limited to a period reasonably necessary to bring about removal. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 689, 697.

2. **Indefinite detention raises serious constitutional concerns.**  
The Supreme Court explained that “freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that [the Due Process Clause] protects.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690 (quoting *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992)). Civil detention is permissible only when tied to a valid regulatory purpose and accompanied by adequate procedural safeguards. *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 356-60 (1997); *Foucha*, 504 U.S. at 80-83. By contrast, detention that serves no realistic immigration purpose because removal is not reasonably foreseeable becomes constitutionally suspect. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690-92, 699-700.

The Court therefore rejected the Government’s position that continued detention may last so long as removal is merely “theoretically possible.” *Id.* at 701. That standard would permit “indefinite detention” of a category of aliens far broader than any special national-security class, including individuals removed for ordinary immigration or criminal-law reasons. *Id.* at 697, 700.

3. **The statute requires release once removal is not reasonably foreseeable.**  
The governing standard is straightforward: after six months, if the alien shows “good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future,” the Government must rebut that showing. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701. If it cannot, detention is “no longer authorized by statute.” *Id.* at 699. The Court made clear that “[i]t is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions,” not imprisonment for an indeterminate period. *Id.* at 700.

That rule directly governs this case. Zadvydas remained in custody after the 90-day removal period expired, and the Government could not secure his removal. Under *Zadvydas*, his continued detention was unlawful once removal ceased to be reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 701. The Court affirmed release on that basis and rejected the Government’s broader detention theory. *Id.* at 700-02.

4. **The contrary view was expressly rejected.**  
The Fifth Circuit originally held that detention was proper so long as “good faith efforts to effectuate ... deportation continue” and the alien failed to prove removal would be impossible. *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999), vacated, 533 U.S. 678. The Supreme Court rejected that formulation because it would require the alien to prove the absence of “any prospect of removal” and would tolerate detention even when removal is remote and unforeseeable. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701. That is not what § 1231(a)(6) means.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed. The statute does not authorize detention indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period; it authorizes only detention reasonably necessary to effect removal, and once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued custody is unauthorized. *Id.* at 689, 699-701.
