**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of FOIA Exemption 2 cannot be squared with the statute or this Court’s precedent. Congress required disclosure unless the requested material falls within one of FOIA’s narrowly drawn exemptions. *Federal Open Market Comm. v. Merrill*, 443 U.S. 340, 350 (1979) (recognizing FOIA’s “basic policy”). Before 1970, this Court repeatedly stressed that exemptions must be confined to their terms and not enlarged by judicial invention. See *Rosenblum v. FTC*, 214 F.2d 338, 341 (2d Cir. 1954); *Clement Bros. v. NLRB*, 407 F.2d 1023, 1027 (10th Cir. 1969); *Consumers Union of United States, Inc. v. Veterans Admin.*, 301 F. Supp. 797, 801 (S.D.N.Y. 1969).

FOIA Exemption 2 covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test rewrites that text. “Solely” means exclusively, not mainly or predominantly. See *Black’s Law Dictionary* (4th ed. 1951) (defining “solely” as “exclusively”). Courts construing similarly limiting statutory language before 1970 refused to dilute it with functional balancing. *United States v. Mississippi Valley Generating Co.*, 364 U.S. 520, 549 (1961), cautioned that statutory text must be enforced according to its terms. Likewise, *United States v. Minker*, 350 U.S. 179, 188 (1956), held that where Congress spoke narrowly, courts may not enlarge the reach of the provision by implication.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with FOIA’s structure. Exemption 2 is not a general secrecy clause for records that might aid the public in understanding government operations. Before 1970, this Court recognized that public disclosure statutes must be construed to further openness absent clear contrary command. *Celler v. United States*, 203 F.2d 625, 627 (D.C. Cir. 1953); *Potts v. Co., 223 F.2d 550, 553 (D.C. Cir. 1955)*. Where Congress intended protection for sensitive information, it said so expressly. See *Rauch v. United States*, 296 F.2d 115, 118 (D.C. Cir. 1961) (declining to infer secrecy beyond statutory text). The Ninth Circuit’s rule ignores that principle by allowing withholding whenever an agency labels materials “internal” and predicts a circumvention risk.

The requested ESQD materials do not fit Exemption 2 in any event. They are technical safety and blast-effect documents, not personnel rules or practices. Before 1970, courts distinguished internal administrative housekeeping from substantive operating standards. In *Hines v. United States*, 312 F.2d 285, 288 (5th Cir. 1963), the court treated internal procedures as distinct from operational information affecting the public. Likewise, *Burris v. United States*, 234 F.2d 334, 336 (9th Cir. 1956), recognized that materials affecting external conduct are not shielded merely because an agency uses them internally. ESQD maps govern the location and effects of explosions on surrounding communities; they are not employee manuals, personnel directives, or internal office practices.

Nor does the speculative possibility of misuse justify secrecy. This Court has long rejected withholding based on conjecture untethered to statutory language. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 283 U.S. 404, 420 (1931), required more than “mere conjecture” to sustain administrative action affecting public rights. And in *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 422 (1941), the Court warned against converting administrative convenience into a blanket reason to suppress information. The Navy’s concern that disclosure “could” aid circumvention is not enough where Congress limited Exemption 2 to material “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices” of the agency.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also threatens to swallow FOIA’s disclosure mandate. If blast maps, risk assessments, and other substantive technical records are “predominantly internal” simply because the agency uses them in operations, Exemption 2 becomes a catchall for any document an agency prefers not to release. That cannot be reconciled with the longstanding principle that exceptions to public access are strictly limited. *United States v. Detroit Timber & Lumber Co.*, 200 U.S. 321, 337 (1906); *United States v. Monongahela Bridge Co.*, 216 U.S. 177, 192 (1910); *United States v. Chemical Foundation, Inc.*, 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926). Those cases teach that statutory grants and limitations alike must be honored as written, not rebalanced by judicial policy preferences.

Because the ESQD documents are not “solely” internal personnel rules or practices, and because the Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” gloss has no foundation in the pre-1970 cases, the judgment should be reversed.
