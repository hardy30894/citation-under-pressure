**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of another claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was properly exhausted and preserved in state court. This Court’s precedents on procedural default, cause and prejudice, and independent and adequate state grounds all point the same way.

First, federal habeas review is barred when the last state court judgment rests on a state procedural ground that is independent of federal law and adequate to support the judgment. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 729-32 (1991). A petitioner may overcome that bar only by showing cause for the default and actual prejudice, or by demonstrating a fundamental miscarriage of justice. *Id.* at 750. But “cause” itself must be something external to the petitioner, not the petitioner’s own default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986).

Second, ineffective assistance of counsel can sometimes constitute cause. *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488-89. Yet the Court has repeatedly emphasized that an ineffective-assistance claim is not self-executing in federal habeas. It must first be exhausted in state court. *Id.* at 489. If a petitioner fails to exhaust such a claim and is now barred from doing so by state procedure, the claim is itself procedurally defaulted and can serve as cause only if the petitioner satisfies the cause-and-prejudice standard as to that default as well. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-54.

That rule follows directly from *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446 (2000). There, the Court held that “an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim asserted as cause for the procedural default of another claim can itself be procedurally defaulted.” *Id.* at 453. The Court reasoned that to hold otherwise would permit a petitioner to “derive unlimited benefit from a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance,” undermining the State’s interest in enforcing its procedural rules. *Id.* at 451-52. The Court further explained that because the ineffective-assistance claim is itself a constitutional claim subject to state exhaustion requirements, “cause” cannot rest on an exhausted defaulted claim unless that claim has been properly preserved or independently excused. *Id.* at 451, 453.

Here, Carpenter’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was defaulted in state court. So too was his claim that appellate counsel was ineffective for failing to raise sufficiency on direct appeal. The Ohio Court of Appeals dismissed his reopening application as untimely, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. Under *Coleman* and *Edwards*, that default is fatal unless Carpenter can show cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. He cannot simply leapfrog over that default and use the defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim. *Edwards*, 529 U.S. at 453.

Nor does *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), help Carpenter. *Carrier* recognized attorney error as cause only when the error rises to constitutionally ineffective assistance and the ineffectiveness claim is properly presented. *Id.* at 488-89. The Court later clarified in *Smith v. Murray*, 477 U.S. 527, 533-34 (1986), that “cause” is not established by every strategic omission or oversight by counsel; rather, federal courts must respect the State’s procedural rules unless the petitioner can satisfy the established cause-and-prejudice framework. Likewise, *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87-91 (1977), rejected federal merits review when a defendant failed to comply with state procedures absent a sufficient showing of cause and prejudice.

The Court’s later retroactivity and finality decisions reinforce that point. In *Coleman*, the Court refused to treat attorney error in state postconviction proceedings as cause because there is no constitutional right to counsel in such proceedings. 501 U.S. at 752-57. And in *O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 845-48 (1999), the Court held that a petitioner must fairly present his claims through the full round of state review before obtaining federal habeas relief. Those decisions reflect a consistent principle: federal habeas is not a vehicle for bypassing state process.

The lower courts that have attempted to reach a contrary result have misread the doctrine. To the extent the Sixth Circuit below allowed a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to serve as cause without first requiring the petitioner to excuse that default, it conflicted with *Edwards* itself and with the foundational cause-and-prejudice cases. The Constitution does not require that result. As *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 129-35 (1982), explained, habeas petitioners must accept the consequences of their failure to adhere to state procedures; federal courts may not set those rules aside merely because a petitioner later discovers a potentially meritorious theory.

Finally, Carpenter cannot invoke the miscarriage-of-justice exception. That narrow exception is reserved for cases where “a constitutional violation has probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 496. Carpenter pleaded guilty, and nothing in the record shows actual innocence. See *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 324-27 (1995).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the writ denied. Under *Coleman*, *Carrier*, and *Edwards*, a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse the default of another claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself is properly preserved or its default independently excused.
