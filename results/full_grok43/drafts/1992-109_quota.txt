ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard to determine a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. A single standard governs all competency inquiries: whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); see also Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966).

This Court has never imposed a more demanding test for waivers of counsel or guilty pleas. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), requires only that the waiver be knowing and intelligent; it does not prescribe a distinct competency threshold. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), likewise demands that the accused “knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open,” without elevating the mental-capacity inquiry above the Dusky formulation. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969), confirms that the same constitutional minimum applies to the acceptance of guilty pleas.

Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), and Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942), reinforce that competency to waive constitutional protections is measured by the same functional capacity that permits a defendant to stand trial. No opinion of this Court has held that due process mandates a “reasoned choice” gloss on the Dusky standard.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule lacks foundation in precedent and would fragment competency determinations without textual or historical warrant under the Fourteenth Amendment. The judgment below should be reversed.
