**ARGUMENT**

The equal-footing doctrine works a transfer of title to the beds of navigable waters to each new State "as an incident of sovereignty" upon admission to the Union. Martin v. Waddell, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 367, 410 (1842) (holding that the State, like the original States, held such lands "for the common use" of its people). Because that transfer occurs at the moment of admission, the constitutional test for title requires a court to ask whether the water was navigable *then*—there is no other date the doctrine permits.

**1.** In Pollard v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845), this Court held that Alabama, upon admission, succeeded to the same title over the beds of navigable waters within its borders that the original States possessed, on "equal footing" with them. Id. at 228–29. That holding fixes statehood as the operative date. The Court reaffirmed the point in Barney v. Keokuk, 94 U.S. 324 (1876), explaining that the "shores of navigable waters, and the soils under them" belong to the States "in their character of sovereign," a sovereignty that attaches upon admission. Id. at 338.

**2.** The corollary is that navigability for title purposes must be assessed by the river's natural condition at statehood, not by its condition today or as later improved. In The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870), the Court articulated the enduring standard: waters are navigable when they are "used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water." Id. at 563. The Court has repeatedly held that susceptibility must be measured against natural, unimproved conditions. In United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court examined the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers by reference to their "natural and ordinary condition," rejecting reliance on capacity created by post-statehood improvements. Id. at 76, 82. Similarly, in Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), the Court asked whether the Des Plaines River was navigable "in its natural state," refusing to credit navigability manufactured by later dredging or damming. Id. at 122.

**3.** Applying that fixed temporal focus, this Court's own decisions confirm that a trial court adjudicating title to a riverbed must direct its findings to the date of statehood, using the best historical evidence available. In United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), the Court reviewed surveyors' reports and early accounts of Mud Lake to determine its condition "at the time" Minnesota entered the Union. Id. at 55–56. The Court undertook precisely the same historically anchored inquiry in Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77 (1922), asking whether the Arkansas River was navigable in fact at the time of Oklahoma's admission, drawing on contemporaneous accounts of the river's use. Id. at 86–87. And in United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), the Court confirmed that "the question of navigability is determined as of the date of the admission of the state," resolving conflicting evidence about the character of several Oregon lakes as they existed at statehood. Id. at 14.

**4.** This statehood-focused methodology is not one option among several; it flows from the equal-footing doctrine's function of vesting sovereign title at the moment of Union entry. As the Court explained in Scott v. Lattig, 227 U.S. 229 (1913), a State's title to the bed of a navigable river "is conferred, not by any act of Congress, but by the admission of the State into the Union," relating back to that event. Id. at 242–43. Because sovereignty attaches irrevocably at that moment, a court cannot measure navigability by a river's condition after decades of industrial use or by the very hydroelectric construction whose lawfulness the litigation places at issue. Any contrary rule would let private parties alter, through subsequent improvement or obstruction of a waterway, the sovereign land grant the Constitution vested in the State at statehood—a result inconsistent with Utah, Economy Light, and Barney alike.

**5.** The trial court's approach tracked precisely the methodology this Court has required. It examined river-specific historical evidence—explorers' journals, land-office surveys, and early commercial records—bearing on the condition of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as they existed in 1889, the year of Montana's admission. That is the same category of proof this Court credited in Holt State Bank, Brewer-Elliott, and Oregon. Cf. United States v. Cress, 243 U.S. 316, 321–22 (1917) (navigability determined by capacity for use "in the customary modes of trade and travel on water" at the relevant time). The Montana Supreme Court's affirmance of that approach applied the very constitutional test—the navigability-at-statehood test—that Martin v. Waddell, Pollard, and their progeny require, and it should be sustained here.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below, applying the statehood-based navigability test, should be affirmed.
