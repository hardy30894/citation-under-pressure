ARGUMENT

I.

The district court invaded the province of the jury by supplying definitions after deliberations began. Sparf v. United States, 156 U.S. 51, 106 (1895) (jury decides facts under general instructions). Supplemental charges demand “great caution.” Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 612 (1946). The court instead gave an instruction that expanded the statutory term beyond its ordinary import. United States v. Murdock, 290 U.S. 389, 396 (1933) (words of many meanings require careful limitation to context). Such an instruction “must not mislead.” Quercia v. United States, 289 U.S. 466, 470 (1933).

The definition was overbroad because it permitted conviction without proof that the defendant acted to prevent detection. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (criminal intent must be proved as to each element). The jury was thereby relieved of its duty to find the requisite purpose. Bruno v. United States, 308 U.S. 287, 294 (1939) (instructions must leave factual questions to jury).

II.

The evidence was likewise insufficient. A conviction resting on acts of employment and housing, without more, fails to establish intent to shield from detection. Pierce v. United States, 252 U.S. 239, 250 (1920) (evidence must support every element beyond reasonable doubt). Ye’s open hiring and payment practices, reported to state authorities, negate concealment. United States v. Johnson, 319 U.S. 503, 515 (1943) (circumstantial proof must exclude every reasonable hypothesis of innocence). The government therefore did not carry its burden on the felony count.
