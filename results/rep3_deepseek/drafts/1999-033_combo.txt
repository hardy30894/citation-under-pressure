**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action for a “class of one” where the plaintiff alleges no membership in a discrete group or class. The Seventh Circuit’s holding—that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by asserting state action motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him”—finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 precedents and fundamentally misapprehends the Clause’s purpose.

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Prohibits Discrimination Based on Classifications, Not Isolated Instances of Unequal Treatment.**

From its inception, the Equal Protection Clause has been understood to protect against “class legislation” and intentional discrimination targeting a recognizable group. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886), this Court struck down a law that, although facially neutral, was administered “with a mind so unequal and oppressive as to amount to a practical denial” of equal protection to Chinese laundry owners—a defined ethnic class. The Court did not suggest that a single plaintiff acting alone could invoke the Clause absent evidence of group-based animus.

Later decisions confirm this limitation. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944), held that the Equal Protection Clause requires “intentional or purposeful discrimination” and that a plaintiff must show “that the discrimination was directed against him as a member of a particular class.” The Court expressly rejected the notion that “a mere showing of arbitrary classification, without more, is enough.” *Id.* at 9. Similarly, *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949), explained that the Clause “does not require that things different in fact be treated in law as though they were the same” and that legislatures may draw lines “unless the lines are so arbitrary as to be invidious.” That standard presupposes a classification—a line drawn between groups.

**II. The Rational-Basis Standard Does Not Authorize Courts to Review Individualized Government Decisions for Malice.**

This Court has consistently applied deferential rational-basis review to economic and social legislation, evaluating whether the challenged classification bears a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78–79 (1911); *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920); *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152–54 (1938). Under that framework, the government need only show “any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify” its classification. *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners*, 330 U.S. 552, 564 (1947). A claim that a single plaintiff was singled out for spiteful reasons does not implicate a classification at all; it attacks the subjective motive behind an isolated decision. The rational-basis test does not authorize courts to psychoanalyze government officials.

Moreover, this Court has held that the mere fact of unequal treatment, without more, does not violate equal protection. *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955), upheld a law that exempted existing businesses from certain requirements while imposing them on new entrants, stating that “the problems of regulation are such that the legislature may proceed one step at a time.” If a legislature may constitutionally draw lines that disadvantage some businesses or individuals while not affecting others—without any requirement of group cohesion—then a local government’s decision to demand a larger easement from one property owner than from others is, at most, an arbitrary act subject to state-law remedies, not a constitutional violation.

**III. Recognizing a “Class of One” Claim Would Convert Routine Administrative Decisions into Federal Constitutional Causes of Action.**

The Seventh Circuit’s test—that a plaintiff need only allege that the state actor was motivated by “spite”—creates a federal equal protection claim out of every intentional tort, every petty official abuse, and every case of favoritism. The Due Process Clause, through state tort law, and state administrative remedies are the appropriate mechanisms to redress malicious official conduct. The Equal Protection Clause was not designed to serve as a general federal “safety net” for all arbitrary government acts. *See McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961) (equal protection does not require “that the line drawn be mathematically precise”).

This Court has never, in any pre-1970 decision, endorsed a “class of one” theory. To the contrary, *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940), observed that the Equal Protection Clause “does not prevent inequality of treatment when the inequality is not based upon a classification which is arbitrary.” The focus is on the *basis* for the discrimination—a class-based distinction—not on the mere fact that one person received less favorable treatment.

**IV. The Seventh Circuit’s Standard Would Undermine State Sovereignty and Encourages Federal Overreaching.**

Allowing equal protection claims based solely on “spite” would subject state and local officials to litigation whenever they depart from uniform treatment, even if the departure is rational. *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27, 31 (1885) (noting that the Equal Protection Clause only prevents “class legislation” and “unjust discrimination”). The Clause does not impose a constitutional duty of perfect consistency. Government officials must have flexibility to respond to individual circumstances; requiring them to justify every deviation from past practice under federal equal protection scrutiny would paralyze administration.

**Conclusion.**

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed. The Equal Protection Clause does not recognize a cause of action where the plaintiff fails to allege membership in a discrete and identifiable class. The pre-1970 precedents of this Court uniformly require a classification—a group—as a predicate for an equal protection claim. The “class of one” theory finds no foothold in those decisions and should be rejected.
