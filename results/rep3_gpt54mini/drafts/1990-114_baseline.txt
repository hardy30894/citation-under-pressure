**ARGUMENT**

### I. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the ADEA because appointed state judges are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.”

The ADEA excludes from the definition of “employee” “any person elected to public office,” “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff,” and “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). In **Gregory v. Ashcroft**, 501 U.S. 452, 468–73 (1991), this Court held that Missouri’s appointed state judges fall within that exclusion and therefore are not covered by the Act. The Court emphasized that judges “necessarily” exercise discretion and judgment that may create law, and that “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging.” Id. at 466, 467. The Court also relied on the Missouri judiciary’s administrative and supervisory authority, concluding that state judges perform “important policymaking functions.” Id. at 468.

That conclusion is consistent with the statute’s text and structure. The ADEA’s exception is not limited to executive-branch officials. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 469–71. Nor does the phrase “policymaking level” require that the officeholder formulate policy in the narrow legislative sense. Id. at 467–68. Rather, the Court recognized that state judges, even when appointed and retained through Missouri’s nonpartisan plan, are among the state officers whose authority and duties place them within the statutory exception. Id. at 473.

The Court in Gregory also applied the federalism canon requiring a clear statement before construing a federal statute to intrude on a state’s control over the qualifications and tenure of its judges. Id. at 460–64; see also **United States v. Bass**, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Because Congress did not unmistakably include appointed state judges within the ADEA’s coverage, Missouri’s constitutional retirement requirement is not displaced by federal law. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 464, 473.

### II. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause because age-based classifications are reviewed under rational-basis scrutiny and this provision is rational.

Age is not a suspect classification, and mandatory retirement rules are tested only for rationality. **Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia**, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam). Under that standard, Missouri’s retirement age of seventy easily survives review.

In Gregory, the Court held that the State had a rational basis for requiring judges to retire at age seventy. 501 U.S. at 474–77. The Court explained that Missouri could rationally conclude that age bears a relationship to diminished capacity in a demanding judicial office, and that a fixed retirement age helps ensure a competent judiciary without forcing the State to conduct individualized competency evaluations. Id. at 472–77. The Court also recognized that a mandatory retirement rule reduces the risk of prolonged vacancies, delays, and administrative burdens associated with removal proceedings. Id. at 475–76.

This Court’s precedent confirms that a legislature may act on reasonable generalizations rather than individualized proof. See **Murgia**, 427 U.S. at 314–17. A State need not demonstrate that every judge over seventy is incompetent, only that the line it drew is rational. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 477. Missouri’s judgment that judges should retire at seventy was supported by the same kinds of considerations this Court has repeatedly found sufficient under rational-basis review. See **Vance v. Bradley**, 440 U.S. 93, 97–112 (1979); **United States Railroad Retirement Board v. Fritz**, 449 U.S. 166, 174–79 (1980).

Petitioners’ disagreement with Missouri’s policy judgment does not establish an equal protection violation. As Gregory held, “[o]ne might disagree” with the State’s reasons “and yet be unable properly to conclude that a state which thinks otherwise lacks a rational basis for its mandatory retirement rule for judges.” 501 U.S. at 477. That is the end of the constitutional inquiry.

### CONCLUSION

Because appointed Missouri judges are excluded from the ADEA as “appointee[s] on the policymaking level,” and because Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule is rational under the Fourteenth Amendment, the judgment should be affirmed.
