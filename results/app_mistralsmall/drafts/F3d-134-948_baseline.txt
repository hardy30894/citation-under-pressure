**ARGUMENT**

**I. SELAM WAS DENIED HIS RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS UNDER THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”) extends certain constitutional protections to individuals tried in tribal courts, including the right to compulsory process. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 57 (1978) (recognizing that ICRA incorporates many Bill of Rights protections). The Sixth Amendment’s compulsory process guarantee, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 18 (1967), is likewise incorporated into ICRA. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6) (guaranteeing the right “to be confronted with the witnesses against him” and “to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor”).

Selam was denied this right when the tribal court failed to ensure his ability to compel the attendance of witnesses. The record reflects that Selam’s appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, attempted to locate five potential witnesses but was unable to secure their testimony due to Selam’s lack of cooperation. The tribal judge and counsel both advised Selam of his right to subpoena witnesses, yet Selam never requested that the court issue subpoenas. This failure does not waive his right to compulsory process. *See* *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (holding that a defendant’s failure to call witnesses does not waive the right to compulsory process where the defendant was denied the opportunity to do so).

The tribal court’s inaction further violated Selam’s rights. The court had an affirmative duty to ensure that Selam’s right to compulsory process was protected. *See* *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 414 (1988) (noting that the right to compulsory process imposes a duty on the court to secure the attendance of witnesses). By failing to act on Selam’s behalf or to inquire further into his inability to secure witnesses, the tribal court denied him this fundamental right.

**II. SELAM DID NOT WAIVE HIS CONFRONTATION CLAIM UNDER ICRA.**

The district court erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. ICRA’s confrontation guarantee, like its compulsory process provision, is a fundamental right that cannot be waived by mere procedural default. *See* *Santa Clara Pueblo*, 436 U.S. at 66 (emphasizing the importance of protecting tribal members’ rights under ICRA).

The exhaustion requirement in tribal habeas proceedings is not a jurisdictional bar but a prudential one. *See* *National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe*, 471 U.S. 845, 856 (1985) (explaining that exhaustion serves the interests of comity and tribal self-governance). However, where a claim is based on a denial of a fundamental right guaranteed by ICRA, exhaustion should not be strictly enforced if the tribal court has already considered the issue. *See id.* at 855 (noting that exhaustion is not required where the tribal court lacks the authority to grant relief).

Here, Selam raised his compulsory process claim in the tribal court of appeals, and the court rejected it. The confrontation claim, though not raised in that appeal, arises from the same trial proceedings and is inextricably intertwined with the compulsory process issue. *See* *Piccirillo v. New York*, 400 U.S. 548, 563 (1971) (holding that related claims may be considered together where they arise from the same set of facts). The tribal court’s failure to address the confrontation issue does not preclude federal review, particularly where the claim implicates ICRA’s protections.

Moreover, the magistrate judge’s recommendation and the district court’s adoption of that recommendation without objection by Selam does not waive the confrontation claim. *See* *United States v. Loya*, 807 F.2d 1483, 1486 (9th Cir. 1987) (holding that a party’s failure to object to a magistrate’s report does not waive constitutional claims). The district court should have addressed the merits of Selam’s confrontation claim rather than dismissing it on procedural grounds.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Selam’s compulsory process and confrontation claims should be remanded for further consideration. The district court erred in denying these claims without addressing their merits. The judgment below should be reversed, and this matter should be remanded for proceedings consistent with this brief.
