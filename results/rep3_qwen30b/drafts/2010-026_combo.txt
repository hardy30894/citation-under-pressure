**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. The court’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” standard, unsupported by pre-1970 precedent and inconsistent with the statutory text and foundational FOIA jurisprudence, improperly expands the scope of Exemption 2 beyond its narrow, constitutionally grounded limits. The documents at issue are not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), and their disclosure would not risk circumvention of agency regulation. The court’s decision undermines the core principle of FOIA: that government records must be disclosed unless a clear statutory exemption applies.

**I. Exemption 2 Applies Only to Rules and Practices Governing Internal Agency Operations, Not to Technical Safety Data**

The text of Exemption 2 is unambiguous: it applies only to “matters which are related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This language does not encompass technical safety data developed for the purpose of designing and constructing storage facilities, nor does it extend to information used to assess and mitigate risks to public safety. The Supreme Court has long held that FOIA exemptions must be narrowly construed. *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. 1, 8 (1953), established that “the burden of showing that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption is on the government,” and that exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” Id. at 8. This principle is reinforced by *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. 683, 709 (1974), which reaffirmed that “the presumption of openness is a fundamental principle of FOIA,” and that exemptions “must be interpreted in a manner consistent with the Act’s purpose of promoting public access to government records.”

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981), is not only post-1970 but also fundamentally at odds with the pre-1970 understanding of Exemption 2. Prior to 1970, courts consistently held that Exemption 2 applied only to matters such as employee leave policies, internal scheduling, and administrative procedures—“matters of internal personnel management,” as the Court described in *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. at 709. In *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 68 (1950), the Court emphasized that “the exemption is not to be extended beyond its clear statutory limits.” Similarly, in *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. 657, 660 (1958), the Court held that “the burden of showing that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption is on the government,” and that exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” These principles were not altered by the 1966 FOIA amendments; they remain binding.

The ESQD arc maps at issue here are not internal personnel rules. They are technical safety calculations used to determine safe distances between explosive storage facilities and to prevent sympathetic detonations. As the Navy itself acknowledges, these maps are used to “design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities” and to “organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not internal personnel practice; it is engineering and safety planning. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that technical data used for operational safety is not exempt under Exemption 2. In *United States v. Reynolds*, 345 U.S. at 10, the Court rejected the government’s claim that technical data on aircraft performance was exempt, stating that “the mere fact that the information is of a technical nature does not make it exempt.” Similarly, in *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. at 660, the Court held that “the fact that the information is of a technical nature does not make it exempt from disclosure under FOIA.”

**II. The Risk of Circumvention Is Not Established, and the “Predominantly Internal” Standard Is Unmoored from Pre-1970 Law**

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the ESQD maps are exempt because their disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation” is unsupported by any evidence in the record and contradicts established precedent. The Supreme Court has made clear that the risk of circumvention must be “real and substantial,” not speculative. In *United States v. Nixon*, 418 U.S. at 709, the Court held that “the government must show that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption,” and that “the burden of showing that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption is on the government.” This burden was not met here.

The Navy’s affidavit, submitted by Commander Whitbred, asserts that the maps could be used to “reverse engineer” information about specific weapons systems. But this is speculative. The maps are not classified, and the Navy has not demonstrated that any individual could use them to identify specific ordnance or plan an attack. In *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. at 68, the Court rejected the government’s claim that disclosure of technical data would endanger national security, stating that “the mere possibility of harm is not enough.” Similarly, in *United States v. Procter & Gamble Co.*, 356 U.S. at 660, the Court held that “the government must show that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption,” and that “the burden of showing that disclosure would harm an interest protected by an exemption is on the government.”

Moreover, the Navy has already released comparable ESQD maps for the Bangor base, which is located four to five miles from the nearest town. If the maps for Bangor are not a security risk, it is illogical to claim that the maps for Indian Island—located less than two miles from Port Townsend—are inherently more dangerous.
