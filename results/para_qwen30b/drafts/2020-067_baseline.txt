**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain error standard in reviewing Greer’s claim that the Government failed to prove he knew he was a felon at the time of his possession of a firearm. The Court properly limited its inquiry to the trial record and did not exceed its authority by considering matters outside the jury’s view—such as Greer’s prior convictions or post-conviction conduct—to determine whether the error affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This approach is consistent with Supreme Court precedent and reflects the proper role of appellate review under Rule 52(b) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure.

Under *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993), plain error requires: (1) an error; (2) that is plain; (3) that affects substantial rights; and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. The fourth prong is “not easily satisfied,” and courts must exercise restraint in invoking it. Id. at 736. Critically, the Supreme Court has made clear that when reviewing for plain error, appellate courts may consider only what was before the trial court—specifically, the evidence presented to the jury and the record developed during trial. See *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 65–66 (2002) (“[T]he plain-error doctrine does not permit a reviewing court to look beyond the record to discover facts that were not before the trial court.”); *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 747, 752 (2004) (“A defendant who fails to object to a sentencing error forfeits the right to appeal unless the error is ‘plain’ and affects ‘substantial rights.’”).

Here, the error—failure to instruct the jury on the knowledge element required by *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019)—was indeed plain. The Government conceded at oral argument that no instruction was given on the defendant’s knowledge of his status as a felon. But the question remains whether this error affected Greer’s substantial rights or the fairness of the proceeding. The Eleventh Circuit did not err in concluding that Greer could not demonstrate prejudice because the trial record contained overwhelming evidence that he knew he was a felon. That record included: (1) the plea agreement explicitly acknowledging Greer’s status as a felon; (2) testimony from law enforcement officers identifying him as a convicted felon during the arrest; (3) the fact that Greer had previously been sentenced in state court for aggravated burglary, which would have put him on notice of his criminal history; and (4) the absence of any indication in the record that Greer was unaware of his status.

The Court of Appeals did not engage in fact-finding beyond the record. It considered only what was already before the district court—the evidence admitted at trial and the plea agreement. The court did not rely on extrinsic materials such as police reports not introduced into evidence, affidavits, or documents not part of the trial record. Nor did it speculate about Greer’s mental state or subjective beliefs. Instead, it assessed whether, based on the evidence actually presented, a reasonable jury could have found otherwise. As the Supreme Court held in *Olano*, “the existence of a reasonable doubt as to guilt is not enough” to establish prejudice under plain error—it must be shown that the error “undermined the fundamental fairness of the trial.” 507 U.S. at 734. The Eleventh Circuit concluded that the record demonstrated Greer’s awareness of his status, and thus no such undermining occurred.

Greer contends that the court should have looked beyond the record to evaluate the impact of the error. But this would violate the foundational principle that appellate courts do not reweigh evidence or reassess credibility. See *United States v. Bland*, 552 F.3d 1257, 1262 (11th Cir. 2008) (“We do not sit as a thirteenth juror to reevaluate the evidence or assess the credibility of witnesses.”). To allow a court to examine matters outside the trial record would transform plain error review into de novo factual reconsideration—a practice the Supreme Court has consistently rejected. In *Dominguez Benitez*, the Court emphasized that “a defendant cannot obtain relief under the plain error rule simply by showing that the error might have affected the outcome.” 542 U.S. at 752. Rather, the burden is on the defendant to show that the error actually affected his substantial rights.

Moreover, the Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that the error did not affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial was well within its discretion. The record shows that Greer voluntarily entered a plea agreement that acknowledged his status as a felon and accepted responsibility for being a career offender. He received full notice of the consequences of his plea, including the potential for enhanced sentencing under the ACCA. There is no suggestion of prosecutorial misconduct, judicial bias, or systemic failure. The trial was conducted in accordance with due process, and the jury was instructed on all elements of the offense except one—yet the omitted element was so clearly established by the record that its omission did not alter the outcome.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit applied the correct legal standard and confined its review to the trial record. Its conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice under plain error is sound and consistent with binding precedent. Reversal would undermine the finality of criminal judgments and invite appellate courts to second-guess trial outcomes based on speculative or extrinsic considerations. For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
