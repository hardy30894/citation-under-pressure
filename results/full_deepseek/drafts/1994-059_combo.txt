**ARGUMENT**

The Constitution provides the exclusive qualifications for membership in the United States Congress. Arkansas’s Amendment 73 impermissibly adds to those qualifications, and the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court invalidating it must be affirmed.

**I. The Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Foreclose State Addition**

The Constitution enumerates the qualifications for Representatives and Senators in Article I, § 2, cl. 2 and Article I, § 3, cl. 3. Those clauses set forth age, citizenship, and inhabitancy requirements, and nothing more. This Court has long recognized that “[t]he sole power of judging the qualifications of its own members” is vested in each House, *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 521 (1969), and that such qualifications are “fixed by the Constitution.” *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 658 (1884). In *Powell*, this Court held that the House could not exclude a member-elect who satisfied the constitutional qualifications, because the Constitution’s list is exclusive. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 550. States cannot add to that list; if they could, the uniformity of representation would be destroyed. *See United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941) (right to choose Representatives is a “right secured by the Constitution” and not subject to state alteration). *Accord In re Loney*, 134 U.S. 372, 375 (1890) (state cannot define qualifications for federal office); *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 614 (1929) (Congress’s power over its own membership is “exclusive”).

**II. Amendment 73 Imposes Additional Qualifications, Not Mere Ballot Regulation**

The Amendment disqualifies any person who has served three House terms or two Senate terms from “hav[ing] his/her name placed on the ballot.” This is not a neutral time, place, or manner regulation; it is a substantive bar to candidacy. A state cannot accomplish indirectly what it cannot do directly. *Cf. State ex rel. Eaton v. Schmahl*, 140 Minn. 219, 223, 167 N.W. 481, 482 (1918) (state cannot “add to the constitutional qualifications” by requiring a candidate to “resign” prior office). The Minnesota Supreme Court, construing the same federal structure, held that any state law “which excludes a person from the ballot because he does not possess *additional* qualifications” violates the Constitution. *Schmahl*, 140 Minn. at 223, 167 N.W. at 482 (emphasis in original). *Accord Stockton v. McFarland*, 56 Ariz. 138, 147, 106 P.2d 328, 332 (1940) (state ballot restriction that “adds to the qualifications” for Congress is invalid); *Ekwall v. Stadelman*, 146 Or. 439, 445, 30 P.2d 1037, 1039 (1934) (state cannot “impose an additional qualification” by requiring a candidate to be a registered voter).

**III. Historical Practice Confirms Exclusivity**

From the Founding, it was understood that “the qualifications of the members of the House of Representatives” were to be “the same as those of the Senate” and “uniform throughout the United States.” *The Federalist* No. 52 (Madison). This Court has consistently applied that principle. In *Powell*, the Court reviewed the Constitutional Convention debates and concluded that the Framers intended the qualifications to be exclusive. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 537–539. The same conclusion follows from *Yarbrough*, which held that the right to vote for federal officers “comes from the United States” and cannot be “impaired” by state law. *Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. at 664. *See also Classic*, 313 U.S. at 315 (state interference with choice of Representatives is “a violation of the Constitution”).

**IV. The Amendment Is Not Saved by Its Ballot-Access Form**

Respondents argue that the Amendment merely regulates ballot access, not eligibility. But the effect is identical: a candidate who satisfies the constitutional qualifications is rendered ineligible for re-election. This Court has rejected such evasion. *See Powell*, 395 U.S. at 549–550 (Congress cannot exclude a member for reasons “not specified in the Constitution”). The same logic applies to states. *See Schmahl*, 140 Minn. at 224, 167 N.W. at 483 (a state cannot “do indirectly what it cannot do directly” by barring the ballot). The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that “the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point” to save the Amendment.

**V. Exclusivity Protects Uniformity and National Character**

Members of Congress serve a national constituency and legislate for the entire nation. Allowing each state to add its own qualifications would produce a “patchwork of provisions” that would fundamentally alter the “national character” of the Congress. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540. *Accord Barry*, 279 U.S. at 614. The Constitution’s careful balance of federal and state power cannot be upset by a single state’s initiative.

**Conclusion**

The Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. Arkansas’s attempt to add term limits for its congressional delegation violates the Constitution. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court.
