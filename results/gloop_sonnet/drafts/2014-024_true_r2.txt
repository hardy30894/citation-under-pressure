**ARGUMENT**

**I. The presumption of scienter that pervades federal criminal law applies to 18 U.S.C. § 875(c).**

This Court has long required that criminal statutes be read to demand a guilty mind unless Congress plainly says otherwise. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952). Section 875(c) is silent on the mental state required, but that silence cannot be read as license to punish a defendant for words he did not intend as threats. The concept of a threat is bound up with the speaker's own design, not merely with a listener's reaction. This is confirmed by the Court's treatment of an analogous threat-based offense in *Sexton v. California*, 189 U.S. 319 (1903), which explained that in that companion crime "[i]t was the extorting of the money by reason of fear induced by the threat that constituted the crime," *id.* at 319–20 — a formulation that ties the offense to the wrongdoer's purpose in wielding the threat, not merely to whether a listener happened to feel afraid. The common law understanding of "threat" likewise required an inquiry into the character of the menace as intended, not simply as received. *Brown v. Pierce*, 74 U.S. 205 (1869) (discussing duress per minas and the categories of menace the law recognizes as sufficient to avoid a bargain). A rule that dispenses with the speaker's intent collapses this distinction and converts § 875(c) into a strict-liability offense for anyone whose words are, in hindsight, frightening to someone else.

**II. The First Amendment confines the "true threats" exception to statements the speaker means as threats.**

The category of unprotected speech at issue here is not self-defining; it is a narrow exception carved out of a domain otherwise protected by the First Amendment. This Court has cautioned that such categories must be defined with precision lest they swallow protected expression. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940). Consistent with that caution, this Court in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), distinguished a "true threat" from protected hyperbole, recognizing that language which might sound alarming out of context is not automatically punishable simply because a listener could construe it as menacing. The distinction between a true threat and protected expression is meaningless unless it turns on what the speaker meant to communicate. An objective, reasonable-listener standard erases that line entirely: it asks only whether some audience could read the words as threatening, without ever asking whether the defendant intended them that way.

**III. An objective standard chills protected speech and offends due process by convicting defendants of an offense they did not commit.**

This Court has recognized that the mere prospect of prosecution for ambiguous expression exerts a chilling effect on protected activity that due process cannot tolerate. *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479 (1965). Where, as here, an entire category of one-to-many communications — crude, hyperbolic, or artistic in form — can be swept into a felony charge based solely on how an unintended audience might react, speakers of every stripe are forced to self-censor rather than risk a jury's after-the-fact judgment about how a reasonable person would have read their words. Individual listeners' reactions to identical language can vary enormously, a point illustrated by this Court's own records: in one case a habeas petitioner swore under oath that threats by Federal Bureau of Investigation agents to throw him out of a window and "beat me up" "didn't bother me." *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 101 (1942). If the same category of threatening words can be experienced as unremarkable by one listener and as terrifying by another, the constitutional line between protected and unprotected speech cannot be allowed to depend on the vagaries of the audience; it must depend on the defendant's own state of mind.

Due process reinforces this conclusion. This Court has held that a defendant may be convicted only of the offense actually proved against him, not a lesser or different one substituted after the fact. *Cole v. Arkansas*, 333 U.S. 196 (1948). Just as due process forbids conviction on an unproven charge, it forbids conviction for a *mens rea* Congress never enacted and the defendant never possessed. And just as this Court has insisted that the reliability of a defendant's own statements — not simply their surface content — determines whether they may be used to convict him, *Lee v. Mississippi*, 332 U.S. 742 (1948); *Rogers v. Richmond*, 365 U.S. 534 (1961), so too must the reliability of a defendant's purpose in making a statement determine whether that statement may support a threat conviction. A jury verdict resting on an objective standard tells us only what a hypothetical reasonable person would have thought; it tells us nothing about what Anthony Elonis actually intended.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be understood as a threat, and the judgment below should be reversed.
