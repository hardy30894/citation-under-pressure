**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and its reversal must be affirmed. The record here reveals substantial evidence of mental impairment—suicidal ideation, severe depression, and the use of four psychotropic medications—combined with monosyllabic responses and a desire to foreclose all mitigating evidence, all of which should have triggered a good-faith doubt about Moran’s capacity to make a reasoned choice. The state court’s failure to conduct a competency hearing under the proper standard violated due process, and the post-conviction findings, based on an erroneous legal standard, cannot cure that constitutional defect.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights—such as the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial—must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). This standard is not satisfied by mere rational understanding of the proceedings; it demands a higher level of mental functioning: the capacity for “reasoned choice.” *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988). As this Court has held, “a defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if he has the capacity for ‘reasoned choice’ among the alternatives available to him.” *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (Evans II). This standard is distinct from, and more demanding than, the standard for competency to stand trial, which requires only a “rational and factual understanding of the proceedings” and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960) (per curiam).

Here, the trial court had ample reason to entertain a good-faith doubt about Moran’s competency. He had attempted suicide just months before the plea hearing, was still recovering from a self-inflicted gunshot wound, and was taking four medications with known cognitive and emotional side effects: Inderal (a beta-blocker), Dilantin (an anticonvulsant), Phenobarbital (a sedative), and Vistaril (an anxiolytic). *See* Ninth Cir. Op. at 9–10. The record shows that Moran was on medication at the time of the plea, and he acknowledged this to the court—but the court made no inquiry into the nature or impact of the drugs. *Id.* at 9. This failure to investigate is fatal. As this Court has emphasized, “due process requires a court to initiate a competency hearing, on its own motion if necessary, whenever it has or should have a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency.” *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981). The presence of substantial evidence of mental impairment—suicidal ideation, depression, and polypharmacy—creates precisely such a doubt.

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing was inconsistent with the ability to make a reasoned choice. His responses were monosyllabic, and he communicated his decision to waive counsel and plead guilty through leading questions. He explicitly stated his intent to prevent any mitigating evidence from being introduced, a decision that reflects not a calculated strategy but a desire to avoid emotional or psychological confrontation. *Id.* at 11. This behavior is not the hallmark of a defendant exercising reasoned judgment; it is consistent with a mind overwhelmed by depression and medication. The fact that he sought to foreclose all defense options—despite the death penalty hanging over him—undermines any claim of rational decision-making.

The state court’s post-conviction ruling, which relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted to assess competency to stand trial, is legally insufficient. *Id.* at 15–18. The reports by Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky were not designed to assess the higher “reasoned choice” standard. They focused on whether Moran could understand the charges and assist counsel—precisely the lower standard that does not govern waivers of constitutional rights. *Id.* at 24–26. The absence of any analysis of the effects of medication, or of Moran’s ability to weigh alternatives, renders these reports irrelevant to the question at hand. The state court’s reliance on them to conclude that Moran was competent to waive counsel and plead guilty was a clear application of the wrong legal standard.

This Court has repeatedly held that when a state court applies an incorrect legal standard, federal habeas review must be de novo. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 106 S.Ct. 445, 88 L.Ed.2d 405 (1985); *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 857. The presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d) does not apply where the state court’s decision rests on an erroneous legal standard. *Evans II*, 800 F.2d at 887.
