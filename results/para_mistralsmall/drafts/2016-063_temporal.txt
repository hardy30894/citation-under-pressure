**ARGUMENT**

**I. The jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case must be reviewed in district court, not the Federal Circuit.**

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978 (“CSRA”) provides that a federal employee who alleges discrimination in connection with an adverse employment action over which the Merit Systems Protection Board (“Board”) has jurisdiction may seek review in district court. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2) (1978). This Court has long recognized that the CSRA’s jurisdictional provisions are to be construed broadly to effectuate its remedial purpose. *See generally United States v. Republic Supply Co.*, 326 U.S. 793, 798 (1946) (statutes in derogation of common law are to be liberally construed in favor of those seeking their benefits).

The Board’s dismissal of a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds does not alter the nature of the claim or divest the district court of jurisdiction. In *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), this Court held that jurisdiction attaches based on the nature of the claim, not the procedural posture in which it is presented. *Id.* at 173 (“The judicial power of the United States is extended to all cases arising under the constitution.”). Similarly, in *Osborn v. Bank of the United States*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738 (1824), the Court explained that federal jurisdiction is determined by the essential character of the case, not by the defenses interposed. *Id.* at 823 (“The judicial power of the United States extends to all cases arising under the constitution and laws of the United States.”).

Here, Petitioner’s claim arises under federal anti-discrimination statutes and alleges an adverse employment action over which the Board has jurisdiction. The fact that the Board dismissed the claim on jurisdictional grounds does not transform the nature of the claim or deprive the district court of jurisdiction. *See generally Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908) (federal jurisdiction is determined by the plaintiff’s statement of his own cause of action, not by the defendant’s defenses).

**II. The Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction over jurisdictional dismissals of mixed cases.**

The CSRA vests the Federal Circuit with jurisdiction over Board decisions, but only where the Board has ruled on the merits of a claim over which it has jurisdiction. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A) (1978). Where the Board dismisses a claim on jurisdictional grounds, the Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction because there is no merits decision to review. *See generally Healy v. Ratta*, 292 U.S. 263, 270 (1934) (jurisdiction depends on the existence of a case or controversy; a jurisdictional dismissal moots the controversy for purposes of appellate review).

This Court has long held that appellate jurisdiction is limited to final decisions on the merits. *See, e.g., United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P. R.R. Co.*, 294 U.S. 499, 510 (1935) (“The jurisdiction of this Court to review the judgments of the lower courts is limited by statute to final decisions.”). A jurisdictional dismissal is not a final decision on the merits and therefore does not vest the Federal Circuit with jurisdiction. *See generally Ex parte Bakelite Corp.*, 279 U.S. 438, 459 (1929) (appellate jurisdiction exists only where there is a final judgment or decree disposing of the merits of the controversy).

**III. The district court is the proper forum for reviewing jurisdictional dismissals of mixed cases.**

The CSRA’s structure contemplates that mixed cases—those alleging discrimination in connection with an adverse employment action over which the Board has jurisdiction—shall be reviewed in district court, regardless of whether the Board reaches the merits or dismisses the claim on jurisdictional grounds. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1) (1978) (defining mixed cases as those in which an employee alleges discrimination in connection with an action appealable to the Board). The district court’s equitable powers and familiarity with discrimination claims make it the appropriate forum for such review. *See generally Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 395 (1946) (equitable remedies are within the province of the district courts).

This Court has long recognized that where a statute provides for district court review of a particular class of claims, that review is not defeated by procedural or jurisdictional rulings below. *See, e.g., United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) (“The jurisdiction of the district court is not ousted by the fact that the state court has taken some action in the premises.”). The same principle applies here: the district court’s jurisdiction over mixed cases is not defeated by the Board’s dismissal on jurisdictional grounds.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that a Merit Systems Protection Board decision disposing of a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is subject to judicial review in district court.
