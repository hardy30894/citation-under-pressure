**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), which provides that an action under § 1104 may not be commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information that was readily available and disclosed, misreads the statutory text, contradicts binding precedent, and undermines the predictability and finality that ERISA’s limitations period is designed to ensure.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplied the “Actual Knowledge” Standard by Requiring Knowledge of Imprudence, Not Just the Transaction

The Ninth Circuit held that “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) requires knowledge not only of the transaction but also of its imprudence. This interpretation is contrary to the plain language of the statute and the Supreme Court’s precedent. Section 1113(2) does not require knowledge of illegality or imprudence; it requires only “actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The statute does not define “breach” or “violation,” but the Supreme Court has made clear that “a breach of fiduciary duty under ERISA is established by a showing that the fiduciary failed to act with the care, skill, prudence, and diligence of a prudent man acting in a like capacity.” *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1830 (2015) (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1)). The *existence* of the breach—i.e., the imprudent action—is what triggers the limitations period, not the plaintiff’s subjective understanding of its legal or prudential consequences.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach, which demands knowledge of imprudence, effectively transforms the statute into a constructive knowledge standard, despite Congress’s 1987 repeal of the former constructive knowledge provision. *See* Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. The Court has long recognized that “actual knowledge” means “knowledge of the facts that constitute the breach,” not knowledge of the law or the legal significance of those facts. *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985) (holding that the statute of limitations is triggered by knowledge of the transaction, not by knowledge of its illegality). The same principle applies here: if a fiduciary invests in alternative assets, and that investment is disclosed, the breach—i.e., the imprudent allocation—is complete when the investment occurs, and the limitations period begins when the participant *knows* of the investment, not when he knows it was imprudent.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding creates a dangerous precedent: a plaintiff can delay filing suit indefinitely by simply failing to read or recall disclosures, even when those disclosures are clear, comprehensive, and accessible. This is not the law of actual knowledge. As the Supreme Court has stated, “actual knowledge” is not “a matter of subjective belief or memory,” but “a factual inquiry into whether the plaintiff was aware of the relevant facts.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1831. The fact that Sulyma did not read or recall the disclosures does not mean he lacked actual knowledge of the investment—especially when the disclosures were provided in multiple formats, including annual statements, plan descriptions, and online fact sheets, all of which were accessible during his employment.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s Approach Contradicts Binding Precedent and Creates a Circuit Split

The Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with the clear holdings of *Blanton*, *Meagher*, and *Ziegler*, all of which established that the limitations period begins when the plaintiff knows of the transaction, not when he knows it was unlawful. In *Blanton*, the trustees were held to have had actual knowledge of the transaction at the time it occurred because they were parties to it. 760 F.2d at 991. In *Meagher*, the Court held that receipt of a reduced pension check triggered the limitations period, even though the recipient did not know the reduction violated ERISA. 856 F.2d at 1423. In *Ziegler*, the administrators were held to have had actual knowledge when informed of the financial impact of their choice, even if they did not know it violated ERISA. 916 F.2d at 552.

The Ninth Circuit’s new rule—requiring knowledge of imprudence—has no basis in these cases. Indeed, the court itself acknowledged that “none of these cases squarely held that knowledge of the underlying transaction was necessarily sufficient” to trigger the limitations period. But that is precisely the point: the cases *did* hold that knowledge of the transaction was sufficient. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish them on the ground that the plaintiffs were “parties” or “specifically informed” is legally irrelevant. The statute does not require a plaintiff to be a party or to be formally notified; it requires only actual knowledge of the breach. When a plan participant has access to clear, detailed disclosures about the investment strategy and asset allocation—disclosures that explicitly identify the use of hedge funds, private equity, and commodities—then the participant has actual knowledge of the transaction, and the limitations period begins.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also creates a circuit split. The Second, Third, and Seventh Circuits have uniformly held that the limitations period begins when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach, defined as the imprudent action, not the legal conclusion that it was imprudent. *See, e.g.*, *In re Northrop Grumman Corp. ERISA Litig.*, 2015 WL 10433713, at *20 (C.D. Cal. Nov. 24, 2015) (collecting cases). The Ninth Circuit’s departure from this settled understanding undermines uniformity and invites forum shopping.
