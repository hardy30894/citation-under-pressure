**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee as interpreted in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). In *Ring*, the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the existence of any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum. *Id.* at 609. The Court emphasized that this principle applies to Arizona’s scheme because the aggravating factors there were treated as "elements of the offense" for sentencing purposes. *Id.* at 589, 609.

Florida’s scheme is fundamentally different. Unlike Arizona, Florida does not treat aggravating factors as elements of a greater offense. Instead, Florida law provides that "[t]he jury shall consider the evidence presented at trial" and "determine[] the existence or nonexistence of any mitigating circumstance." Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2015). The jury’s role is advisory; it issues a non-binding recommendation to the trial court, which then makes the ultimate sentencing decision. *See id.* § 921.141(3). The trial court must independently weigh the aggravating and mitigating factors and make its own findings by a preponderance of the evidence. *See id.* § 921.141(3); *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam) (holding that the Sixth Amendment does not require jury findings of aggravating factors when the trial judge makes the ultimate sentencing decision).

This Court has repeatedly recognized that Florida’s scheme does not run afoul of *Ring*. *See, e.g., Sochor v. Florida*, 504 U.S. 527, 531 (1992) (upholding Florida’s scheme against Sixth Amendment challenge); *Bottoson v. State*, 833 So. 2d 761, 770 (Fla. 2002) ("Florida’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate *Ring* because the jury’s role is advisory."). The Supreme Court has likewise declined to extend *Ring* to Florida’s system. *See, e.g., Johnson v. Florida*, 528 U.S. 988 (1999) (denying certiorari in a case challenging Florida’s scheme post-*Ring*).

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Hurst’s Eighth Amendment challenge fails for two independent reasons. First, Florida’s scheme does not impose the death penalty without sufficient safeguards to ensure reliability. Second, Florida’s treatment of mental retardation does not violate the Eighth Amendment.

**A. Florida’s Scheme Satisfies the Eighth Amendment’s Reliability Requirements.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that the death penalty be imposed only after "heightened reliability" in the sentencing process. *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). Florida’s scheme meets this standard. The trial court, not the jury, makes the ultimate sentencing decision after independently weighing aggravating and mitigating factors. *See Hildwin*, 490 U.S. at 640-41. The judge’s findings are subject to meaningful appellate review, ensuring that the sentence is not arbitrary or capricious. *See, e.g., Tedder v. State*, 322 So. 2d 908, 910 (Fla. 1975) (en banc) ("The standard of review in death penalty cases is whether the sentence is proportionate to the crime and the defendant.").

Moreover, Florida’s scheme requires the trial court to find at least one statutory aggravating factor before imposing the death penalty. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3). This requirement ensures that the death penalty is reserved for the most culpable offenders. *See, e.g., Zant v. Stephens*, 462 U.S. 862, 878 (1983) (upholding Georgia’s scheme requiring jury findings of aggravating factors).

**B. Florida’s Treatment of Mental Retardation Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment.**

The Supreme Court held in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), that the Eighth Amendment forbids the execution of mentally retarded offenders. Florida’s scheme complies with *Atkins*. Florida law provides a three-prong test for mental retardation: (1) significantly subaverage intellectual functioning (IQ of 70 or below); (2) concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior; and (3) manifestation of the condition before age 18. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(1); *Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009). The burden is on the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4); *Nixon*, 2 So. 3d at 145.

Hurst argues that Florida’s scheme violates *Atkins* because the jury, not the judge, determines mental retardation. This argument misreads *Atkins*. The Supreme Court did not mandate a specific procedural mechanism for determining mental retardation; it left the states free to adopt their own procedures. *See Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317. Florida’s scheme satisfies *Atkins* because it provides a substantive bar to execution for mentally retarded offenders. *See Nixon*, 2 So. 3d at 142-45.

**III. FLORIDA’S FAILURE TO REQUIRE UNANIMOUS JURY RECOMMENDATIONS DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH OR EIGHTH AMENDMENTS.**

Hurst’s challenge to Florida’s non-unanimous jury recommendation scheme also fails. The Supreme Court has never held that the Sixth or Eighth Amendments require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly upheld state schemes that permit non-unanimous jury recommendations. *See, e.g., Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 648 (1990) (upholding Arizona’s scheme allowing a non-unanimous jury recommendation); *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984) (upholding Florida’s scheme).

Florida’s scheme is consistent with the Eighth Amendment’s reliability requirements. The trial court independently weighs the aggravating and mitigating factors and makes the ultimate sentencing decision. *See Hildwin*, 490 U.S. at 640-41. The jury’s non-unanimous recommendation does not undermine the reliability of the sentencing process.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
