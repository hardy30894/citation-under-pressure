# ARGUMENT

## I. Rule 407 Does Not Bar Evidence of Subsequent Remedial Measures When Offered for Impeachment, Even in a Strict Products Liability Action

Federal Rule of Evidence 407 excludes evidence of subsequent remedial measures only when offered "to prove negligence or culpable conduct." Fed. R. Evid. 407. By its own terms, the Rule "does not require the exclusion of evidence of subsequent measures when offered for another purpose." *Id.* This Court has not yet decided whether Rule 407 even applies in strict liability actions, but the courts that have addressed the question have uniformly held that, assuming the Rule applies at all, it does not bar impeachment use of such evidence. *See Grenada Steel Indus., Inc. v. Alabama Oxygen Co.*, 695 F.2d 883, 887 (5th Cir. 1983) (recognizing that Rule 407's exclusionary principle extends to strict liability claims but does not foreclose impeachment); *Werner v. Upjohn Co.*, 628 F.2d 848, 857 (4th Cir. 1980) (explaining that the rule is grounded in a policy of encouraging safety improvements, not in shielding a party from contradiction of its own testimony).

The rationale for Rule 407 is that a manufacturer should not be discouraged from making its product safer for fear that the very act of improvement will be used against it as an admission of prior fault. *Flaminio v. Honda Motor Co.*, 733 F.2d 463, 469 (7th Cir. 1984). That policy has no application where, as here, the evidence is not offered to show that the product was defective, but to correct a false impression created by the defendant itself. Every circuit to consider the question has recognized an impeachment exception to Rule 407. *See Dollar v. Long Mfg., N.C., Inc.*, 561 F.2d 613, 618 (5th Cir. 1977) (subsequent remedial measures admissible "for the purpose of impeachment" where a witness testified that no safer design existed); *Anderson v. Malloy*, 700 F.2d 1208, 1213 (8th Cir. 1983) (same); *Chase v. General Motors Corp.*, 856 F.2d 17, 22 (4th Cir. 1988) (holding no Rule 407 violation where subsequent-measures evidence was used solely to impeach a witness's prior testimony).

## II. Morbark Opened the Door to Impeachment by Falsely Implying That the Design Remained Unchanged and Was Still the Safest Available

The impeachment exception exists precisely for circumstances like these. From opening statement forward, Morbark's counsel affirmatively represented to the jury that the seventeen-inch chute design was still in use by the Army Corps of Engineers and by the City of DeFuniak Springs, thereby implying that no design flaw existed and that the design had never been changed. R4-141-22. On cross-examination of Infinger, Morbark again elicited testimony that the City "still" used "that Morbark chipper machine," reinforcing the false impression that the machine was unaltered. R4-141-44.

Courts have consistently held that a party who creates a false impression concerning the safety or feasibility of a design cannot then invoke Rule 407 to prevent the opposing party from correcting that impression. *Bauman v. Volkswagenwerk Aktiengesellschaft*, 621 F.2d 230, 232-33 (6th Cir. 1980) (subsequent design change admissible to impeach testimony suggesting no safer alternative existed); *Probus v. K-Mart, Inc.*, 794 F.2d 1207, 1211 (7th Cir. 1986) (a party "opens the door" to otherwise inadmissible evidence when its own examination creates a misleading inference). The district court itself recognized this principle when it overruled Morbark's objection and permitted Wood's counsel to elicit the fact of the chute's lengthening, stating on the record: "you opened the door when you started making suggestions to the jury that the city was still using that chipper." R4-141-56. That ruling was correct, and Morbark does not challenge it on appeal.

## III. Having Properly Admitted the Impeachment Evidence, the District Court Erred by Instructing the Jury to Disregard It, and That Error Requires a New Trial

Having correctly ruled that Infinger's testimony was admissible impeachment evidence, the district court nevertheless instructed the jury to disregard it, effectively nullifying the very ruling it had made. A trial court may not admit evidence for a legitimate purpose and then strip it of its evidentiary effect through a contradictory instruction; doing so constitutes reversible error where the evidence bore directly on a central contested issue. *See McKnight v. Johnson Controls, Inc.*, 36 F.3d 1396, 1401-02 (8th Cir. 1994) (curative instruction cannot cure prejudice where properly admitted evidence was crucial to a party's theory of the case); *Cann v. Ford Motor Co.*, 658 F.2d 54, 60 (2d Cir. 1981) (erroneous limitation on the use of otherwise admissible subsequent-measures evidence for impeachment warrants reversal where it affects the substantial rights of the parties).

Here, the chute-length defect was the centerpiece of Wood's case. Morbark's counsel had already told the jury, without contradiction, that the identical design remained the safest in use nationwide. The court's subsequent instruction to disregard the only evidence rebutting that assertion left the jury with precisely the false impression Rule 407's impeachment exception exists to prevent. This was not harmless; it went to the heart of the disputed defect and likely affected the verdict. Accordingly, the district court abused its discretion in denying Wood's motion for a new trial, and this Court should reverse and remand for a new trial free of that error.
