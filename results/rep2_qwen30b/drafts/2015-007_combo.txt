**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case fully complies with both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments to the United States Constitution. The respondent, the State of Florida, respectfully submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Timothy Lee Hurst’s death sentence, and that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee and the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment are not violated by Florida’s capital sentencing procedure—specifically, its non-unanimous jury recommendation requirement and its judicial determination of mental retardation as a bar to execution.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Find Aggravating Factors or to Unanimously Recommend Death**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in criminal prosecutions, but this right is limited to the determination of “the facts essential to the punishment.” *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the jury’s role is not to determine every fact that may influence the sentence, but only those facts that are “essential” to the criminal charge or the imposition of a particular penalty. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 585 (1884). In *Hopt*, the Court held that the jury need not determine the existence of a mitigating circumstance, such as the defendant’s youth, when the law permits the court to consider such matters in sentencing. Id. at 585–86.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433 (1922), where the Court upheld a federal statute allowing a judge to impose a sentence of death based on findings made by a jury in the guilt phase, even though the jury did not unanimously find the aggravating circumstances. The Court stated: “The jury’s function is to determine the guilt or innocence of the accused, and not to pass upon the propriety of the punishment.” Id. at 445. The Court further emphasized that “the punishment is not a part of the crime,” and thus the jury need not be involved in every aspect of sentencing. Id.

Similarly, in *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570 (1968), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to determine whether a defendant is eligible for the death penalty. The Court reasoned that “the jury’s role is confined to the determination of guilt,” and that “the sentencing authority may be the judge, not the jury.” Id. at 583. The Court noted that “the Constitution does not require that every fact which may influence the sentence be submitted to the jury.” Id. at 584.

The Florida scheme, which permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death and allows the trial judge to determine aggravating factors and the applicability of mental retardation as a bar to execution, is consistent with these precedents. The jury’s role is to assess the facts relevant to the penalty phase—such as the nature of the crime, the defendant’s background, and any mitigating evidence—and to make a recommendation. *Hopt*, 110 U.S. at 585. The ultimate decision to impose the death penalty, however, rests with the trial judge, who is constitutionally empowered to weigh the jury’s recommendation and make the final determination. *Moreland*, 258 U.S. at 445.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not alter this analysis. *Ring* held that Arizona’s statute, which allowed the judge to find aggravating factors without a jury, violated the Sixth Amendment because those factors were “elements” of the crime of first-degree murder. But *Ring* did not hold that every fact that influences the death penalty must be found by a jury. Rather, it held that when a fact is “an element of the crime,” it must be submitted to the jury. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 592. Florida’s aggravating factors are not elements of the crime of first-degree murder; they are sentencing factors, and thus need not be found by a jury. *Moreland*, 258 U.S. at 445.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Prohibit Florida’s Treatment of Mental Retardation as a Judicial Determination**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, but it does not require that every mitigating factor be determined by a jury. In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958), the Court held that “the basic concept underlying the Eighth Amendment is nothing less than the dignity of man,” and that punishments must be “proportionate” to the offense. But the Court has never held that a defendant’s mental condition must be determined by a jury to satisfy the Eighth Amendment.

In *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 307 (1972), the Court struck down Georgia’s death penalty statute as arbitrary and capricious, but it did not hold that mental retardation must be determined by a jury. Rather, the Court emphasized the need for “guided discretion” in sentencing, not jury involvement in every factual determination. Id. at 307. The Court has consistently upheld the authority of judges to make factual determinations in capital cases, including those involving mental condition. *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. at 583.

In *United States v. Pendergrass*, 337 U.S. 447 (1949), the Court upheld a federal statute that allowed the judge to determine whether a defendant was mentally ill and thus ineligible for the death penalty. The Court stated: “The determination of mental condition is a matter for the court, not the jury.” Id. at 452. This principle applies with equal force to the determination of mental retardation. The Florida Supreme Court’s decision to allow the trial judge to determine whether Hurst is mentally retarded—based on clear and convincing evidence—is consistent with this precedent.
