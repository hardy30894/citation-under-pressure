**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Woodard lacks a constitutionally protected liberty interest in clemency proceedings. This Court has long recognized that clemency is not merely a formality but a critical stage in the administration of justice where fundamental rights may be determined. In *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), the Court held that clemency proceedings are "an integral part of the criminal justice system," id. at 190, and thus subject to due process scrutiny. Similarly, in *Ex parte Grossman*, 277 U.S. 49 (1928), the Court emphasized that clemency is a "high act of executive clemency" that directly implicates the liberty and life interests of the condemned. *Id.* at 57. The Ohio clemency process, which includes a mandatory review prior to execution, see Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, is no different. As this Court stated in *United States v. Lovett*, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), "the right to seek clemency is a substantive right," id. at 317, and its denial without due process violates the Fourteenth Amendment. *See also* *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1 (1964) (incorporating Fifth Amendment protections against self-incrimination against the states).

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A "CHOICE" THAT COMPELS WAIVER OF THE RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that Woodard faces no Fifth Amendment violation because participation in the clemency interview is "voluntary" misapprehends the nature of the right. In *Slochower v. Board of Higher Education*, 350 U.S. 551 (1956), the Court held that a state may not condition public employment on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights, declaring that "the Constitution prohibits a penalty imposed for invoking the right." *Id.* at 558. Similarly, in *Garner v. Board of Public Works*, 341 U.S. 716 (1951), the Court struck down a municipal ordinance requiring employees to waive Fifth Amendment rights as a condition of employment, stating that "a state may not impose substantial penalties because a witness invokes the privilege." *Id.* at 720. Ohio’s clemency process, which offers participation only on the condition that any statements made may be used against the petitioner in future proceedings, imposes precisely such a penalty. *See also* *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422 (1956) (holding that the Fifth Amendment "prohibits the federal government from penalizing a witness for refusing to answer incriminating questions").

**III. THE "HOBSON’S CHOICE" IMPOSED BY OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

This Court has repeatedly condemned state actions that force individuals into constitutionally untenable positions. In *Wieman v. Updegraff*, 344 U.S. 183 (1952), the Court invalidated an Oklahoma statute requiring public employees to swear they had never been members of "subversive" organizations, holding that the law imposed a "choice between surrendering [a] constitutional right and [losing] [a] preferred position." *Id.* at 191. Similarly, in *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), the Court struck down a California tax exemption statute that required veterans to waive First Amendment rights as a condition of receiving benefits, stating that "the state may not deny a benefit to a person on a basis that infringes his constitutionally protected rights." *Id.* at 526. Ohio’s clemency process, which conditions the opportunity for executive mercy on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights, presents the same unconstitutional dilemma. *See also* *Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398 (1963) (holding that the government may not condition benefits on the waiver of constitutional rights).

**IV. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN MINIMIZING THE DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS REQUIRED IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS.**

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that due process protections in clemency proceedings are "minimal" or "barely perceptible" ignores this Court’s precedents. In *Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath*, 341 U.S. 123 (1951), the Court held that even in administrative proceedings, due process requires "notice and opportunity to be heard." *Id.* at 168 (Frankfurter, J., concurring). In *United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy*, 347 U.S. 260 (1954), the Court recognized that due process attaches to discretionary executive decisions that directly affect fundamental rights. *Id.* at 266. Ohio’s clemency process, which determines whether a condemned prisoner lives or dies, cannot escape meaningful due process scrutiny. *See also* *Bates v. Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516 (1960) (holding that even in discretionary administrative proceedings, due process requires procedural safeguards).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed. Ohio’s clemency process, by conditioning executive mercy on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights and denying due process protections, violates the Constitution.
