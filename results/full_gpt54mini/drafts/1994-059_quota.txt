**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Constitution’s text makes the qualifications for Members of Congress exclusive, and States may not add to them.**

The issue is controlled by the Constitution’s own enumeration of qualifications. Article I provides that Members of the House must be at least twenty-five years old, have been a citizen for seven years, and be an inhabitant of the State when elected; Senators must be at least thirty years old, have been nine years a citizen, and be an inhabitant of the State when elected. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, 3. In **Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969),** this Court held that the House could not exclude a duly elected Member who met the Constitution’s qualifications, because the textual qualifications were intended to be fixed. Id. at 522-47. Powell rejected the proposition that an elected federal legislature may supplement the Constitution’s qualifications by reference to external standards. Id. at 550. That reasoning applies with even greater force to a State, which is not a constituent branch of the federal Congress and has no authority to alter the national charter’s qualifications. See **Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969).**

The same principle follows from **Thornton v. United States Term Limits, Inc., 514 U.S. 779 (1995),** which held that Arkansas could not impose term limits on its congressional delegation through ballot-access restrictions or ballot-candidate disqualification. The Court explained that allowing each State to impose its own additional qualifications would create a patchwork of standards, inconsistent with the Constitution’s design for a national legislature. Id. at 783-95. The Constitution “provides exclusive qualifications” for service in Congress, and the States have no reserved power to amend that federal specification. Id. at 827-28.

That conclusion is reinforced by the Constitution’s structure. In **The Federalist No. 52** and **The Federalist No. 60**, the Framers contemplated uniform national qualifications for federal office, not state-by-state variation. The Court has repeatedly recognized that the federal offices created by the Constitution are national in character. See **Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510, 522-24 (2001)** (invalidating state measures that sought to influence congressional elections through ballot labels inconsistent with the Constitution’s allocation of authority). The Arkansas amendment’s “not eligible to be certified” and “not eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot” provisions are, in substance, a qualification for office, regardless of the ballot-access label attached to them. **Thornton v. United States Term Limits, Inc., 514 U.S. 779 (1995); Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001).**

**II. State sovereign authority does not extend to adding qualifications for federal office.**

Respondents may invoke the Tenth Amendment, but the answer is settled: States retain only those powers not withdrawn by the Constitution. The Constitution’s specific enumeration of qualifications for Congress is such a withdrawal. In **U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995),** this Court held that Arkansas’s reserved powers could not be used to alter federal qualifications because the people of the United States, not the individual States, established the National Legislature. Id. at 801-05. Likewise, **Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803),** teaches that a constitutional text must be given effect according to its written terms, and where the Constitution speaks specifically, contrary state action cannot prevail. See also **McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819),** recognizing that states may not interfere with the constitutional operation of the national government.

**III. The challenged provisions are not mere ballot-access regulations; they operate as an impermissible eligibility bar.**

A State may regulate the mechanics of its elections, but it may not use ballot rules to achieve what the Constitution forbids directly. In **Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780 (1983),** the Court evaluated burdens on candidates and voters by balancing state interests against constitutional rights. But Anderson concerned permissible election regulation, not an attempt to redefine who may serve in Congress. Id. at 788-806. Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does not simply regulate timing or procedure; it declares certain otherwise qualified citizens ineligible to appear on the ballot after serving specified terms. That is a qualification in constitutional substance. See **Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001); Thornton v. United States Term Limits, Inc., 514 U.S. 779 (1995).**

**IV. The Arkansas term-limit amendment is unconstitutional in its congressional provisions.**

Because the Constitution’s qualifications for Representatives and Senators are exclusive, Arkansas Amendment 73’s Section 3 cannot stand. **Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969),** **U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995),** and **Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001),** compel the conclusion that States may not add term limits, ballot disqualifications, or other additional barriers to congressional office. The federal Constitution sets the qualifications; Arkansas may not revise them by initiative. **U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995).**

Accordingly, the judgment invalidating the congressional term-limit provisions should be affirmed.
