**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test for competency is whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding, and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him. *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself.

*Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), reaffirmed that due process forbids trying an incompetent defendant and explained that "it is contradictory to argue that a defendant may be incompetent, and yet knowingly or intelligently 'waive' his right to have the court determine his capacity to stand trial." *Id.* at 384. Nothing in *Pate* suggests that a different, more demanding standard of mental capacity applies when a defendant elects to plead guilty or to proceed without counsel rather than to stand trial. The competency inquiry described in *Pate* and *Dusky* concerns the defendant's basic capacity to understand and participate in the proceedings against him—not the wisdom of the particular choices he makes once that capacity is established.

The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But competency doctrine has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and instructed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* at 464. That test asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard.

*Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, emphasizing the defendant's awareness of the risks of self-representation—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry. *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), similarly focused on whether the record affirmatively demonstrated that a plea entered without counsel was the product of an informed and voluntary choice, not on whether the defendant possessed some elevated quantum of rational capacity beyond that required to stand trial. *Id.* at 174–75.

*Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because a defendant may competently elect to conduct his own defense—an election this Court treated as a matter of intelligent waiver, not of superior mental competence. Similarly, *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), required that the record affirmatively show a guilty plea was voluntary and intelligent, explaining that "[s]everal federal constitutional rights are involved in a waiver that takes place when a plea of guilty is entered in a state criminal trial." *Id.* at 243. *Boykin* nowhere suggested that the defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. No Additional Authority Supports a Heightened Competency Standard for Waiver Decisions.**

Taken together, *Dusky*, *Pate*, and *Massey* establish the single constitutional measure of competency, while *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* establish the separate, voluntariness-based inquiry that governs waivers of counsel and guilty pleas. No decision of this Court holds that a defendant who is competent under *Dusky* must additionally satisfy some higher "reasoned choice" threshold before he may waive counsel or plead guilty. The Ninth Circuit's contrary rule collapses two distinct constitutional inquiries—capacity and voluntariness—into a single, undefined, and unprecedented standard that finds no footing in this Court's precedents.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
