# ARGUMENT

The Center Moriches Union Free School District has excluded Lamb's Chapel from public property that the District has opened to a wide range of civic and educational speakers, solely because the Chapel's message on family values is presented from a religious perspective. This selective exclusion violates the settled principle that once government opens its property to speech, it may not shut the door on a particular viewpoint because that viewpoint happens to be religious.

## I. Once a Government Forum Is Opened to Speech, Exclusion of a Speaker Because of Viewpoint Is Forbidden.

The right of citizens to use public property for the "purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions" lies at the core of the First Amendment. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). Although school property is not a street or park, the District chose to make its facilities available after hours to civic, educational, and cultural groups of every description. Having done so, the District cannot pick and choose among speakers based on the acceptability of their message. This Court has repeatedly struck down precisely that kind of discretionary, content-driven denial of access to public facilities.

In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court invalidated the refusal of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses for religious Bible talks where the park had been made available to other groups, holding that a permit could not be withheld because local officials disapproved of the applicants' religious views. The companion case, *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), likewise condemned a licensing scheme that vested officials with unchecked discretion to deny access to speakers whose religious message they found objectionable. And in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), the Court set aside a conviction arising from a Jehovah's Witness religious service in a public park, explaining that a municipality could not apply its regulations so as to discriminate against religious gatherings while permitting comparable secular ones. The teaching of these decisions is uniform: a forum opened for speech cannot be closed selectively to speech of a religious character.

That is exactly what occurred here. The District permitted a chorus performance, a lecture series on parapsychology and "the unknown," and other civic uses of its auditorium, yet turned away Lamb's Chapel's film series on parenting and family life for no reason other than its religious viewpoint. The distinction the District draws — between "secular" civic discussion and religiously-informed discussion of the identical subject, family life — is precisely the kind of viewpoint line that *Niemotko*, *Kunz*, and *Fowler* forbid.

## II. Motion Pictures Are Protected Speech, and Their Religious Content Furnishes No Ground for Exclusion.

The District's rationale singles out the religious *content* of the Dobson film series as the reason for exclusion. But "it cannot be doubted that motion pictures are a significant medium for the communication of ideas." *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). In *Burstyn*, this Court struck down a state's power to ban a film as "sacrilegious," holding that government may not condition a film's exhibition on its conformity to officially approved religious or anti-religious views. If a State cannot censor a film because it offends religious sensibilities, it follows a fortiori that a school district cannot exclude a film because it *expresses* religious sensibilities. Just as freedom of speech "is nevertheless protected against censorship or punishment, unless shown likely to produce a clear and present danger of a serious substantive evil," *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), the District has identified no such danger here — only its own discomfort with the religious viewpoint of the speaker.

## III. Excluding Speech Because It Is Religious Discriminates Against Religion, Contrary to the Constitutional Command of Neutrality.

The Free Exercise and Free Speech Clauses do not permit government to treat religious expression as a second-class category of speech unworthy of the access afforded secular counterparts. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), established that religious communication enjoys the same constitutional protection as any other. As this Court explained in *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947), government "cannot exclude individual Catholics, Lutherans, Mohammedans, Baptists, Jews, Methodists, Non-believers, Presbyterians, or the members of any other faith, because of their faith, or lack of it, from receiving the benefits of public welfare legislation." The same neutrality applies to access to public facilities: the District may not offer its auditorium to a lecture on parapsychology while denying it to a lecture on the family grounded in Christian teaching.

Nor does the Establishment Clause require this result. As *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313 (1952), recognized, "[w]e are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being," and accommodation of religious speech on equal terms with secular speech is neutrality, not endorsement. Denying Lamb's Chapel access while welcoming other community speakers does not serve the Establishment Clause; it violates the very neutrality that Clause and the Free Speech Clause together demand.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed, and Lamb's Chapel should be permitted equal access to the District's facilities on the same terms afforded other community organizations.
