**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander, having purchased defaulted debts from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collecting them on its own behalf, acted as a creditor, not a debt collector, under the plain language of the FDCPA. The statutory definition of “debt collector” is not triggered by the mere fact that a debt was in default at the time of purchase. Rather, the Act distinguishes between creditors and debt collectors based on the nature of the collection activity: whether the entity collects debts for itself or for another. When a person acquires a debt and collects it for its own account, it is a creditor, regardless of the debt’s default status.

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as “any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” This definition is not contingent on whether the debt was in default when acquired. Instead, the critical inquiry is whether the entity collects debts owed to another. As this Court held in *Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick*, 43 U.S. 202, 207 (1844), “a person may be a debtor in one relation, and a creditor in another.” The status of a party as creditor or debtor is determined by the nature of the obligation and the relationship at the time of the transaction, not by the prior history of the debt. Here, Santander became the obligee when it purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto. It was no longer collecting on behalf of another; it was collecting its own debt.

The statutory structure confirms this interpretation. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” includes only those who collect debts owed to another. The phrase “debts owed or due another” is not ambiguous. In *Selden v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 94 U.S. 419, 423 (1877), this Court emphasized that “the law looks to the substance of the transaction, not to its form.” A person who purchases a debt and then seeks to collect it is not acting as a “debt collector” under the Act; it is acting as a creditor. The Act’s exclusion of “any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another to the extent such activity ... concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained” (15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii)) is not a definition of “debt collector,” but a narrow exception. It does not expand the definition; it merely excludes certain activities from the scope of the Act. The exclusion applies only when a person collects a debt that was not in default when obtained—precisely the opposite of the situation here. The fact that the debt was in default at the time of purchase does not transform a creditor into a debt collector.

Moreover, the Act’s legislative history and structure confirm that Congress intended to regulate only those who collect debts on behalf of others. In *Ivanhoe Building & Loan Assn. v. Orr*, 295 U.S. 243, 248 (1935), this Court stated that “a creditor is one who has a right to demand payment,” and that “the right to collect a debt is an incident of ownership.” When Santander purchased the defaulted loans, it acquired the right to collect the debt. It was not acting as an agent for CitiFinancial Auto; it was the new owner. The fact that Santander may have previously served as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto does not alter its status once it became the owner. In *Nixdorff v. Smith*, 41 U.S. 132, 135 (1842), this Court held that “a party may be a creditor in one transaction, and a debtor in another,” and that “the character of the party depends upon the relation in which he stands at the time.” Santander’s status changed when it purchased the debt. It was no longer a servicer; it was a creditor.

The plaintiffs’ argument that Santander must be a “debt collector” because it acquired the debt after default is legally unsound. The FDCPA does not define “debt collector” by the default status of the debt. In *Western Tie & Timber Co. v. Brown*, 196 U.S. 502, 507 (1905), this Court held that “the character of a transaction is determined by the intention of the parties and the nature of the obligation.” Here, the intention was clear: CitiFinancial Auto sold the debt to Santander, and Santander collected it for its own account. The fact that the debt was in default at the time of purchase does not change the nature of the obligation. In *Libby v. Hopkins*, 104 U.S. 303, 307 (1881), this Court stated that “a debt is a debt, whether it be due or not,” and that “the obligation to pay remains, though the time for payment has passed.” The default status of the debt does not alter the fact that Santander was now the obligee.

The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Mason v. Gamble*, 62 U.S. 390 (1859), is misplaced. That case involved a dispute over the collection of duties by a government officer, not the classification of a debt collector under a federal statute. The Court’s holding that a collector of duties may be sued for overpayment is irrelevant to the statutory definition of “debt collector” under the FDCPA. Similarly, *East St. Louis v.
