# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Denying Sieben's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Sex-Discrimination Claim Because the Evidence Was Insufficient to Permit a Reasonable Jury to Find Discriminatory Intent.

A verdict may not rest on speculation. Where the evidence gives rise to nothing more than the possibility that an employer acted from an unlawful motive, and equally supports an inference of a lawful, nondiscriminatory reason, the case should not go to the jury on the unlawful theory. *Pennsylvania R.R. Co. v. Chamberlain*, 288 U.S. 333 (1933) (a verdict cannot rest on an inference piled upon another inference where the evidence is equally consistent with a lack of negligence). The federal courts have long recognized that a trial court retains — and an appellate court may exercise — the power to test whether the proof, viewed most favorably to the verdict winner, permits a reasonable jury to have found in her favor, or whether it does no more than raise a "mere possibility" of the ultimate fact. *Galloway v. United States*, 319 U.S. 372 (1943); *Neely v. Eby Construction Co.*, 386 U.S. 317 (1967) (recognizing appellate authority to direct entry of judgment where the evidence, even construed favorably to the verdict, is legally insufficient).

Feltmann's proof does not meet that standard. The record establishes that Rich reprimanded and monitored *all* consultants, male and female, once overall dealership sales declined after the incentive program expired — precisely the period when Feltmann's own performance fell to the bottom of the sales force in gross profit. An employer's dissatisfaction with an employee's selling technique, expressed evenhandedly across a sales staff experiencing a general downturn, is not evidence of sex-based animus; it is evidence of ordinary business supervision. As this Court's forbears recognized under the analogous inquiry into unlawful motive in the labor-relations context, a finding of discriminatory purpose must be grounded in something more than the coincidence of adverse action and membership in a protected class; the surrounding circumstances must themselves point to unlawful purpose rather than a legitimate business concern. *NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp.*, 373 U.S. 221 (1963). Here, the memoranda regarding "guest drives," "team play," and demo-car mileage were addressed to conduct — extended personal use of a company vehicle 300 miles outside the assigned territory, and a selling technique Rich believed depressed profit per car — not to Feltmann's sex. That she was the only female consultant does not itself convert routine supervisory correction into discrimination. Disparate treatment requires proof that similarly situated men were *not* subjected to comparable scrutiny for comparable conduct, not merely that no man happened to receive an identical memorandum. *Weeks v. Southern Bell Tel. & Tel. Co.*, 408 F.2d 228 (5th Cir. 1969), makes plain that Title VII polices genuine differential treatment based on sex, not an employer's ordinary, individualized personnel decisions that happen to fall on the lone woman in a workforce. Likewise, in *Bowe v. Colgate-Palmolive Co.*, 416 F.2d 711 (7th Cir. 1969), the discrimination found was rooted in a facially sex-based classification affecting job assignment — a far cry from the individualized, performance-based memoranda at issue here.

Because Missouri courts, sitting in diversity-related postures analogous to this pendent state-law claim, apply the same sufficiency standard that federal law demands when a claim is submitted to the jury, *Cities Service Oil Co. v. Dunlap*, 308 U.S. 208 (1939) (state-law burden of proof governs sufficiency inquiry in diversity cases), the MHRA claim fails for the identical reason: the evidence, even favorably construed, shows a supervisor responding to a sales slump, not a woman singled out because she is a woman.

## II. The Retaliatory-Discharge Verdict Cannot Stand Because the Evidence Fails to Establish the Requisite Causal Link Between Feltmann's Complaint and Her Termination.

Retaliation claims require proof that the protected activity was a genuine cause of the adverse action, not merely that the two events occurred in sequence. Even in the closely analogous context of protected concerted activity, the courts have insisted on a showing that the employer's true motive, not simply temporal proximity, drove the discharge. *NLRB v. Washington Aluminum Co.*, 370 U.S. 9 (1962). Here, Feltmann's complaint about Anzalone was made in November 1992; the Capps brothers promptly investigated, gave her the choice whether to have Anzalone fired, and reprimanded him. Feltmann heard no further vulgar remarks. The probation that followed in March 1993 was imposed on *both* Feltmann and a male consultant, Beutel, for the same performance metric — low January-February sales — and both men and Feltmann rebounded together. A co-worker's stray remark that Feltmann had "polarized herself" is not evidence of the employer's motive; it is, at most, evidence of office gossip. Where, as here, the employer treated the male comparator identically, the inference of retaliatory intent collapses. *Dick v. New York Life Ins. Co.*, 359 U.S. 437 (1959) (a case should not go to the jury where the only reasonable conclusion from undisputed facts favors the moving party).

## III. Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and judgment entered for Sieben, or, in the alternative, a new trial ordered.
