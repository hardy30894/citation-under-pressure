**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Population Reduction Order Is a Valid Exercise of Equitable Authority to Remedy Systemic Eighth Amendment Violations.**

The challenged order is a lawful exercise of the federal courts’ historic equitable power to remedy constitutional violations in state penal institutions. *Ex parte United States*, 242 U.S. 27, 42 (1916) (“[T]he equity jurisdiction of the federal courts is broad enough to enjoin state officers from maintaining unconstitutional conditions in state institutions.”). This Court has long recognized that federal courts may fashion remedies—including structural injunctions and population caps—when necessary to abate constitutional violations in prisons. *Cf. Holt v. Sarver*, 300 F.Supp. 825, 828–29 (E.D. Ark. 1969) (three-judge court), *aff’d*, 397 U.S. 350 (1970) (upholding injunctive relief requiring reduction in prison population to remedy Eighth Amendment violations).

The district court’s order is consistent with this precedent. It does not exceed the scope of permissible relief under the Eighth Amendment, which prohibits “punishments which are incompatible with ‘the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.’” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). The court found that severe overcrowding—driving systemic failures in medical and mental health care—constituted cruel and unusual punishment. *See* Brown v. Plata, 692 F.3d 1005, 1013 (9th Cir. 2012) (affirming factual findings of unconstitutional conditions). Such findings are entitled to deference. *See* *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 16 (1971) (“[T]he nature of the violation determines the scope of the remedy.”).

**II. The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) Does Not Bar the Population Reduction Order.**

The PLRA’s prospective relief provisions do not strip courts of equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (2006) (limiting prospective relief in prison cases). The statute’s text and structure preserve traditional equitable principles. *See* *Weinberger v. Romero-Barcelo*, 456 U.S. 305, 313 (1982) (“The scope of injunctive relief is dictated by the nature of the violation.”). Courts interpreting the PLRA have consistently held that it does not abrogate constitutional remedies. *See, e.g., United States v. Oregon*, 798 F.2d 1452, 1456 (9th Cir. 1986) (PLRA does not “eliminate[] the court’s power to remedy constitutional violations”).

The challenged order is not an impermissible “release” order under the PLRA. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(g)(4) (defining “release” as “any reduction in the inmate population of a prison”). The court did not order categorical releases; it required California to reduce its prison population to a constitutional level by implementing evidence-based reforms. *See* *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 528 (2011) (upholding order as “a legitimate response to extreme overcrowding”).

**III. The Three-Judge Court Properly Balanced Competing Interests Under the PLRA’s “Narrowly Tailored” Standard.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The district court’s order satisfies this standard. It is the least intrusive means of remedying the systemic constitutional violations caused by overcrowding. *See* *Brown v. Plata*, 692 F.3d at 1020 (finding no less restrictive alternative).

The court carefully considered alternatives, including increased funding and expanded medical facilities. *See* *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. at 528 (noting California’s failure to implement less restrictive measures). The PLRA does not require courts to defer to legislative or executive inaction. *See* *Swann*, 402 U.S. at 28 (“[R]emedial plans are subject to the commands of the Constitution rather than the wishes of the authorities.”).

**IV. The Order Does Not Violate the Eleventh Amendment.**

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar the challenged relief. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), permits suits against state officials to enforce federal rights. The order runs against state officials in their official capacities, not against the State of California itself. *See* *Edelman v. Jordan*, 415 U.S. 651, 663 (1974) (distinguishing between suits against state officials and suits against the state).

Moreover, the State waived its Eleventh Amendment immunity by consenting to the receivership and the underlying remedial orders. *See* *Clark v. Barnard*, 108 U.S. 436, 447 (1883) (“A state may waive its immunity by voluntarily submitting to the jurisdiction of a federal court.”). The State’s failure to appeal the receivership order estops it from raising Eleventh Amendment defenses now. *See* *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 26 (1947) (“A party may not challenge a judgment when he has acquiesced in the proceedings.”).

**Conclusion**

The three-judge court’s order is a valid exercise of equitable authority, consistent with the PLRA and the Eleventh Amendment. It remedies systemic Eighth Amendment violations without exceeding the scope of permissible relief. This Court should affirm.
