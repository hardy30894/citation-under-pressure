The Milford Central School's exclusion of the Good News Club from its limited public forum did not abridge the First Amendment. A school district may reserve its facilities "for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics," Perry Educ. Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983), and may impose reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions, Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford's Community Use Policy opened its premises only to "social, civic and recreational meetings" and "uses pertaining to the welfare of the community" while expressly barring "religious purposes." This limitation is content-based but permissible in a nonpublic forum. See Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist., 508 U.S. 384, 391 (1993).

The Club's proposed activities—prayer, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Christ as personal Savior—constitute religious instruction and worship, not merely the discussion of morals from a religious viewpoint. See Bronx Household of Faith v. Community Sch. Dist. No. 10, 127 F.3d 207, 215 (2d Cir. 1997). Distinguishing worship from secular moral instruction does not discriminate against a viewpoint; it distinguishes subject matter. Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 830-31 (1995). Milford permitted the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club precisely because their programs addressed character without crossing into religious devotion.

Even if the exclusion implicated speech, it was justified by the Establishment Clause. Permitting the Club's meetings would have required Milford to "lend its power to one or the other side in controversies over religious authority," School Dist. of Abington Twp. v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222 (1963), and would have created an impermissible risk of government endorsement, Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971). See also Board of Educ. of Westside Cmty. Schs. v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 253 (1990) (plurality opinion) (noting that equal access must not advance religion). The district court and court of appeals correctly held that Milford could lawfully decline to subsidize religious worship on its premises.

The judgment should be affirmed.
