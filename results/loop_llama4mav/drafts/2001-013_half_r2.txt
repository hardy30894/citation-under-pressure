ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, the Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is not so comprehensive as to preempt OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels.

The Coast Guard's authority is limited to specific areas, such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation of engine and fuel tank compartments. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 391a). The courts have long recognized that the Coast Guard's authority is not exclusive, and that other federal agencies may regulate aspects of maritime employment not covered by the Coast Guard. See, e.g., Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31 (1942); NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206 (1940); International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50 (1926).

In fact, the Supreme Court has held that the Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is not necessarily exclusive, even with respect to matters related to maritime safety. See, e.g., Kelly v. Washington, 302 U.S. 1 (1937); The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558 (1874). In Kelly, the Court held that a state statute regulating the inspection of vessels was not preempted by federal law, even though the Coast Guard had authority to inspect vessels under 46 U.S.C. § 390 (now repealed). 302 U.S. at 14-15.

Moreover, the courts have recognized that the term "working conditions" in section 4(b)(1) of the Act refers to specific conditions of employment, rather than the entire range of occupational safety and health issues. See, e.g., Guss v. Utah Labor Relations Board, 353 U.S. 1 (1957); Hill v. Florida, 325 U.S. 538 (1945). In Guss, the Supreme Court held that the National Labor Relations Act did not preempt state labor laws with respect to employers not subject to the NLRA, even though the NLRA regulated some aspects of their labor practices. 353 U.S. at 10-11.

Similarly, in this case, the Coast Guard's regulation of certain aspects of maritime employment does not preempt OSHA's jurisdiction over other aspects of occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels. See, e.g., United States v. Haskins, 94 F.2d 675 (9th Cir. 1938); The Silvia, 171 U.S. 462 (1898).

The Court of Appeals' reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Those cases involved inspected vessels, and the courts' holdings were based on the comprehensive regulation of inspected vessels by the Coast Guard. See, e.g., United States v. Andrews, 207 U.S. 229 (1907); The Pennsylvania, 86 U.S. 125 (1873).

In contrast, uninspected vessels are subject to a more limited regime of Coast Guard regulation, as is evident from the statutory framework governing their operation. See, e.g., The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557 (1870); The Silvia, 171 U.S. 462 (1898). In this context, OSHA's jurisdiction is not preempted by the Coast Guard's authority.

In conclusion, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
