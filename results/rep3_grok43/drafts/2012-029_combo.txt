ARGUMENT

Federal jurisdiction over a state-law claim exists only when the claim itself is created by federal law or when the right to relief necessarily depends upon the resolution of a substantial question of federal law. Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 823 (1824); Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 379 (1821). A suit does not arise under federal law merely because the controversy may involve the construction of a federal statute. Gold-Washing & Water Co. v. Keyes, 96 U.S. 199, 203 (1877). The complaint must present a real and substantial dispute respecting the validity, construction, or effect of federal law upon which the result depends. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

Minton’s malpractice action is created by Texas common law. The elements—duty, breach, causation, and damages—are governed exclusively by state decisions. Resolution of the claim therefore does not depend upon any federal right of action. Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897) (“A suit does not arise under the patent laws merely because the validity or construction of a patent is incidentally involved.”).

Even when a federal patent question appears, jurisdiction is absent if the question is not an essential element of the plaintiff’s cause of action. American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Here the experimental-use exception would arise, if at all, only as a defense to Gunn’s summary-judgment motion. Such an anticipated defense cannot confer federal jurisdiction. The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913).

Nor is the patent issue substantial within the meaning of the statute. A question is substantial only when its resolution determines the outcome of the federal right asserted. Shoshone Mining Co. v. Rutter, 177 U.S. 505, 507 (1900). The underlying patent has already been declared invalid; the malpractice suit seeks only money damages under state law. Any patent ruling would bind no one beyond the parties and would not affect the validity or scope of any existing patent grant.

Finally, the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities precludes federal jurisdiction. Congress has not displaced state authority over attorney malpractice. Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117 (1936) (“The federal courts have no more right to enlarge or contract the scope of federal jurisdiction than they have to enlarge or contract the scope of a federal statute.”). Allowing federal courts to hear every malpractice claim that touches patent litigation would upset the division of responsibility long maintained by this Court.

The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
