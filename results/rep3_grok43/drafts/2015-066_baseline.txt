**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings to which the Sixth Amendment does not apply, and they therefore do not “violate” the Amendment. Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994).

The Sixth Amendment right to appointed counsel “does not obtain” in proceedings that do not result in imprisonment. Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979). Tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment at all. Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (“[T]he Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.”); see also United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978). Because Bryant’s tribal convictions were not secured in violation of the Sixth Amendment, Nichols permits their use to establish the existence of prior offenses. 511 U.S. at 746-47 (citing Scott).

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute that “penalize[s] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; see also United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). The Government must prove the predicate convictions beyond a reasonable doubt, In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), but that burden does not convert a valid tribal conviction into one that violates the Sixth Amendment. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that a conviction valid when rendered becomes “constitutionally infirm” when offered as a predicate, United States v. Bryant, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014)—finds no support in Nichols or Scott.

Comity principles likewise counsel against treating tribal judgments as presumptively unreliable. Federal courts extend comity to tribal-court judgments provided the tribal proceedings afforded due process under the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303. See Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir. 1997); Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987). Imposing a Sixth Amendment overlay that Congress has not required would “intrude[] needlessly on tribal self-government.” Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
