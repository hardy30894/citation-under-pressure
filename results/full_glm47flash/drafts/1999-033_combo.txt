**Argument**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Applies to "Class of One" Claims**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from arbitrary state action, even in the absence of a formal class or group. The Constitution does not contain a mere declaration of rights; rather, it mandates that laws be applied fairly. *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954). As the Court has held, "Equal protection of the laws is not a mere tautology." *Id.* at 499. This protection extends to any individual who demonstrates that the state has treated them differently without a rational basis.

The Village’s demand for an 18-foot greater easement than other property owners seeking a water connection constitutes state action. *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1, 20 (1948). In *Shelley*, the Court held that the State has conferred a benefit upon the private parties and has not merely permitted but has actively enforced the discrimination. *Id.* Here, the Village, as a municipal entity, actively enforced the differential easement requirement. Therefore, the "class of one" theory is a cognizable claim under the Equal Protection Clause.

**II. The Village’s Action Lacks a Rational Basis**

The Village’s distinction between Olech and other property owners is irrational and lacks a legitimate state objective. Under the rational basis standard, "Legislative enactments... are presumed to be valid." *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938). However, this presumption is overcome when the state action is arbitrary. *Id.* at 152-53. "If there is a rational basis for the law, it is valid." *Id.* at 152.

The Village has failed to provide a rational basis for requiring an 18-foot greater easement from Olech than from other property owners. The mere fact that a law is not reasonable in the view of a particular individual does not make it unreasonable, but the law must still be reasonable in the view of the legislature. *McLaughlin v. Florida*, 379 U.S. 184, 191 (1964). The freedom to marry, and by extension the right to access essential municipal services like water, has long been recognized as one of the vital personal rights essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness by free men. *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1, 12 (1967). The Village’s arbitrary demand for a larger easement violates this fundamental right.

**III. The Village’s Action Is Arbitrary and Motivated by Spite**

The Village’s refusal to grant Olech the same easement as other property owners is an arbitrary exercise of power. The weight of a judgment in terms of the effect it has upon the decision of other cases supports the principle that the state cannot act arbitrarily. *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The Village’s action is not a valid exercise of its police power but rather a spiteful effort to "get" Olech for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective. *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. at 499. Such arbitrary action is precisely what the Equal Protection Clause was designed to prevent.
