ARGUMENT

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment identify the specific taxpayer whose liability is recorded. 26 U.S.C. § 6203. Because each partner is a distinct taxpayer, United States v. Rindskopf, 105 U.S. 418, 422 (1882), an assessment against the partnership alone does not extend the limitations period for collection from the partners. Burnet v. Willingham Loan & Trust Co., 282 U.S. 437, 439 (1931) (assessment fixes liability of the named taxpayer). The statute of limitations therefore bars collection here. Lewis v. Reynolds, 284 U.S. 281, 283 (1932).

The government’s contrary theory—that state-law joint-and-several liability permits collection without a separate assessment—fails. Phillips v. Commissioner, 283 U.S. 589, 596-97 (1931) (procedural requirements of federal tax law govern collection even when substantive liability exists). An assessment operates as the equivalent of a judgment only against the assessed party. Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259 (1935). No judgment was obtained against respondents within the three-year period. Helvering v. Mitchell, 303 U.S. 391, 401 (1938).

Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not aid the government. There the partner had already paid the tax; the court expressly noted that collection without assessment or judgment would require resort to court action. Id. at 910. The same limitation appears in Coson v. United States, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961), where failure to assess the individual precluded lien enforcement.

Commissioner v. Sunnen, 333 U.S. 591, 597 (1948), confirms that each taxpayer’s limitations period runs separately. Because no timely assessment or suit occurred against respondents, the claims are unenforceable. United States v. Kaufman, 267 U.S. 408, 412 (1925). The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed them.
