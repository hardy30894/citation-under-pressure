# ARGUMENT

## I. The ADEA Does Not Strip Missouri of Its Sovereign Authority to Fix the Tenure of Its Own Judges

Missouri's decision to retire its judges at seventy is an exercise of the "essentially and peculiarly state power[]" to organize its own government. *Coyle v. Smith*, 221 U.S. 559, 565 (1911). In *Coyle*, this Court held that the power of a State to arrange its own internal governmental affairs is among the powers "reserved to the States," inherent in their sovereignty and not to be displaced by inference. *Id.* The selection, retention, and removal of state judges lies at the core of that reserved authority; few decisions are more central to a State's capacity to govern itself than who shall wear its judicial robes and for how long.

Because the ADEA's application to state judges would work a fundamental alteration of this traditional state prerogative, the statute should not be read to reach Missouri's judiciary absent a clear and unambiguous expression of that intent. This Court has long insisted that federal enactments not be construed to displace state authority over matters of traditional state concern unless Congress has made its purpose unmistakably clear. As the Court explained in *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947), "we start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." Nothing in the ADEA's text manifests any purpose, let alone a clear one, to reach into the States' constitutions and dictate the tenure of their judges.

Petitioners' reading of the statute rests on a mechanical parsing of the "policymaking level" exception in 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) in isolation from its context and purpose. But this Court has repeatedly cautioned against wooden literalism divorced from legislative design. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543-44 (1940), the Court explained that resort to the purpose and structure of a statute is always appropriate, "however clear the words may appear on 'superficial examination.'" Read in light of its evident purpose—to exempt from ADEA coverage those officials, however selected, who exercise the discretion and judgment characteristic of the highest levels of government—the "policymaking level" exception plainly encompasses judges, who exercise precisely that kind of discretion and judgment in resolving disputes, filling interstices in the law, and administering the courts. Nothing in the statute's text or history clearly forecloses that reading, and under *Rice* and *Coyle*, ambiguity must be resolved against displacement of the State's sovereign control of its judiciary.

## II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Age Satisfies the Rational-Basis Standard of the Equal Protection Clause

The Equal Protection Clause does not require Missouri to treat all judges, regardless of age, identically. It requires only that a classification "rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike." *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911). A classification survives that standard if "any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it." *Metropolitan Casualty Ins. Co. v. Brownell*, 294 U.S. 580, 584 (1935).

Missouri's retirement provision easily meets that test. A State may rationally conclude that the physical and mental demands of judicial office increase the risk of diminished capacity with advancing age, and that a fixed retirement age avoids the far more painful and divisive alternative of case-by-case removal proceedings against individual judges suspected of decline. The Constitution does not require Missouri to wait for embarrassing or inefficient individualized assessments when a categorical rule serves the same end. As this Court has explained, "[t]he day is gone when this Court uses the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to strike down state laws, regulatory of business and industrial conditions, because they may be unwise, improvident, or out of harmony with a particular school of thought." *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma*, 348 U.S. 483, 488 (1955). The same restraint governs equal protection review of a State's chosen method for staffing its courts.

This Court's decision in *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs*, 330 U.S. 552 (1947), confirms that States retain wide latitude to draw lines governing entry into and continuation in public and quasi-public offices, even where the line drawn is imperfect or debatable. So too here: "the Fourteenth Amendment permits the States a wide scope of discretion in enacting laws which affect some groups of citizens differently than others," and the classification offends the Constitution "only if [it] rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State's objective." *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961). Age is plainly not irrelevant to the demands of judicial service.

Finally, even if some judges retain full capacity past seventy, the Constitution tolerates such imperfection in line-drawing. Equal protection "is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination," *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944), and none exists here. Missouri's rule is a rational, evenhanded means of ensuring a competent judiciary, and it should be sustained.
