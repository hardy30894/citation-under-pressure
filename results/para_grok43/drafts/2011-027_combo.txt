I. Under the equal footing doctrine, title to the beds of navigable waters passes to a new State upon its admission to the Union. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845); Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). The doctrine "is not a matter of municipal law, but of constitutional law." Shively, 152 U.S. at 27. Consequently, the navigability of a river for title purposes must be determined as of the date of statehood. United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926); Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922).

II. Navigability in fact is the constitutional test. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). A river is navigable in fact when it is used, or susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce. Id. This inquiry focuses on the river's condition at statehood, not at some later time. Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 118 (1921); United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931).

III. The test is applied to the specific stretch of river at issue. Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. at 585-86; United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 77. Courts have long rejected any rule that would treat an entire river as a single unit when evidence shows that only discrete segments meet the navigability standard. Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 85 (1922); Barney v. Keokuk, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876). The constitutional requirement therefore obliges the trial court to examine the relevant reach as it existed when the State entered the Union.

IV. Historical evidence of use or susceptibility to use at statehood is competent to establish navigability. The Montello, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430, 441-42 (1874); Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. at 56. Such evidence may include contemporaneous accounts of boats, rafts, or other craft traversing the stretch in question. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 82-83. When that evidence demonstrates navigability at statehood, title vested in the State and remains there. Shively, 152 U.S. at 26.

These settled principles confirm that the constitutional test requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of river was navigable at the time of statehood.
