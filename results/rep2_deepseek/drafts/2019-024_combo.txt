**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by requiring that a plaintiff have “actual knowledge” both of the underlying investment *and* that it was imprudent before the three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) can begin to run. That interpretation conflicts with the statute’s text, this Court’s precedents, and the traditional principle that a party may not defeat a limitations defense by declining to read information that was fully disclosed to him.

**I. “Actual Knowledge” Means Knowledge of the Facts Constituting the Breach, Not Knowledge of the Legal Conclusion of Imprudence**

For over a century, this Court has held that statutes of limitations are triggered by knowledge of the facts giving rise to the claim, not by knowledge of the legal significance of those facts. In *The Texas & Pacific Railway Co. v. Murphy*, 111 U.S. 488, 493 (1884), the Court stated that “the knowledge required is of the facts” that constitute the injury, not “knowledge of the law relating to those facts.” Similarly, in *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 141 (1879), the Court explained that “actual knowledge” of fraud means knowledge of the “facts which constitute the fraud,” not awareness that those facts amount to a legal wrong. Accord *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874) (statute begins to run when “the fraud is discovered, not when it might have been discovered”). These cases establish a settled rule: the limitations clock starts when the plaintiff knows the underlying transaction or occurrence, even if he does not appreciate its legal consequences.

The Ninth Circuit departed from that rule. It held that for a claim under 29 U.S.C. § 1104, the plaintiff must have “actual knowledge both that those investments occurred, and that they were imprudent.” Op. 15. That second requirement—knowledge of legal impropriety—is nowhere in the statute. Congress used the term “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not “actual knowledge of the legal conclusion that the conduct was a breach.” This Court’s decision in *United States v. Oregon Lumber Co.*, 260 U.S. 290, 299-300 (1922), reinforces that the statute of limitations is a statute of repose, not a trap for the unwary who fail to integrate disclosed facts with legal rules. And this Court’s reasoning in *O’Neil v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323, 331 (1892), that “ignorance of the law is no defense,” applies with equal force here: a plaintiff who knows the facts but does not recognize they constitute a breach may not extend the limitations period.

**II. Full Disclosure Eliminates Any Genuine Dispute of Material Fact Where the Plaintiff Chose Not to Read the Disclosed Information**

Petitioners provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and other documents that plainly disclosed the asset allocations he later challenged—including the percentages invested in hedge funds and private equity, the investment strategy behind those allocations, and the associated risks. Sulyma admits he accessed some of these documents, yet claims he did not read them or does not recall reading them. This cannot defeat summary judgment.

The law long has rejected the notion that a party can avoid knowledge by failing to examine information that is openly provided. In *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. 45, 47 (1875), this Court held that a person who signs a contract “is bound by its terms, even if he did not read it, or if he did not understand its legal effect.” A plan participant who receives fiduciary disclosures is similarly charged with knowledge of their contents when he deliberately chooses not to read them. See also *Spurr v. United States*, 174 U.S. 728, 735 (1899) (willful blindness satisfies knowledge requirement). The principle that a plaintiff may not escape summary judgment by uncorroborated testimony that he “did not know” what was plainly disclosed is well established. See *Gleason v. White*, 199 U.S. 504, 511-12 (1905) (self-serving denial of knowledge insufficient to overcome documentary evidence).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach effectively rewrites the statute to require that the plaintiff have actually ingested the disclosed information, not merely that he had it in his possession and the means to know it. That reads the “actual knowledge” requirement out of the statute in cases where the defendant has done everything required to inform the plaintiff. Our case *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Rest. Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520-21 (9th Cir. 1991) (decided before this Court’s grant), recognized that actual knowledge can be established when the plaintiff “knew of the breach” even if he did not appreciate its illegality. But the Ninth Circuit here improperly added a subjective-awareness-of-imprudence element that conflates “actual knowledge” with a requirement that the plaintiff understand the legal significance of the facts.

**III. The Three-Year Limitations Period Began at the Latest When the Plaintiff Had the Information in His Possession**

The undisverted evidence shows that before October 29, 2012, Sulyma had access to multiple documents that explicitly described the very investments he now clames were imprudent. This is not a case of fraudulent concealment or missing information. In *Baily v. Glover*, 88 U.S. at 338-39, the Court recognized that where no concealment occures, the statute runs from the act, not from discovery. Congress chose a three-year “actual knowledge” period, not a general discovery rule. That choice must be given effect. See *In re Sloane*, 154 U.S. 630, 635 (1894) (statutory “actual knowledge” standard requires more than mere constructive notice but does not require the plaintiff to have read every document when the information was otherwise available to him).

The Ninth Circuit’s ruling makes the limitations period depend entirely on the plaintiff’s subjective decision to read—or not to read—materials that the defendant lawfully provided. This would permit a plaintiff to indefinitely postpone the statute by simply stating, “I did not read the disclosures,” even when the disclosures were in his hands. That result is inconcistent with the text of § 1113(2) and with the structure of ERISA, which embodies a congressional intent that participants have a duty to examine the information fiduciaries provide. See *Burnett v. New York Central R.R.*, 380 U.S. 424, 427 (1965) (statutes of limitations should be given effect consistent with congressional purpose). The purpose of § 1113(2) is to protect fiduciaries from stale clames when the plaintiff has been put on notice. That purpose is subverted when a plaintiff can avoid notice by ignoring what is openly disclosed.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for entry of summary judgment in favor of petitioners.
