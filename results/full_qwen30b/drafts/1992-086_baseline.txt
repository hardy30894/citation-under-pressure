**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment guarantees that “Congress shall make no law…abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. This protection extends to speech in public forums, including those created by government policy or practice. When a public entity opens its property to certain types of expressive activity, it must do so without discrimination based on the content or viewpoint of the speech. Here, the Center Moriches School District created a limited public forum through its consistent policy and practice of allowing non-religious community groups to use school facilities after hours. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel—whose proposed use was identical in form and purpose to those previously permitted—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech and equal access.

The District’s facilities are not a nonpublic forum. As the Second Circuit correctly acknowledged, the District’s property falls within the category of a “limited public forum,” created by policy and practice. See *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 977 F.2d 70, 74 (2d Cir. 1992). The District has permitted numerous non-religious community groups to use its auditoriums and gymnasiums for events such as lectures, musical performances, and civic meetings. These uses were not isolated or incidental; they were part of a sustained practice of opening school facilities to the public for non-curricular, non-religious purposes. The District’s own rules, including the “Rules and Regulations for Community Use of School Facilities,” explicitly permit such use, so long as it is not for religious purposes. This policy of selective exclusion—allowing non-religious groups while banning religious ones—transforms the forum into a content-based restriction, which is impermissible under the First Amendment.

The Supreme Court has made clear that in a limited public forum, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981), the Court held that a public university could not deny access to student religious groups when it had opened its campus to other student organizations. The Court emphasized that “the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and that “the First Amendment prohibits the government from excluding speech from such a forum simply because the speech is religious.” Id. at 267, 273. The same principle applies here. The District has opened its facilities to non-religious community groups—such as the Southern Harmonizers, a secular musical ensemble, and the “Psychology and the Unknown” lecture series sponsored by the public library—on multiple occasions. These events were not religious in purpose, context, or content. Yet the District denied Lamb’s Chapel, a non-profit religious organization, access to the same facilities for a film series on family values, which was similarly nonsectarian in form and open to the public.

The District’s argument that it has never allowed religious use is irrelevant. The First Amendment does not require a government entity to open a forum to all speech, but when it does open a forum, it must do so without discrimination. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). The District’s policy of excluding religious groups while allowing non-religious ones constitutes a content-based exclusion, which is subject to strict scrutiny. The government may not suppress speech merely because it is religious. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 977 F.2d at 75. The District has not shown that its exclusion serves a compelling interest, nor has it demonstrated that the restriction is narrowly tailored. Indeed, the District’s own justification—that the films “have religious connotations”—is a content-based judgment that cannot be sustained. The films were not presented as religious services, nor did they include worship or proselytizing. They were educational, family-oriented, and open to the public—just like the events the District has previously permitted.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Deeper Life* is misplaced. In *Deeper Life*, the court upheld a school district’s refusal to allow a religious group to use facilities where no prior use had been permitted. But that case is distinguishable: there, the District had never opened its facilities to any community group. Here, the District has repeatedly allowed non-religious groups to use its facilities. The forum was created by practice, not by default. The District cannot now claim it is a nonpublic forum when it has, through consistent policy and action, invited the public in.

The District’s refusal also violates the Equal Access Act, 20 U.S.C. § 4071(b), which prohibits public secondary schools from denying equal access to student groups based on the content of their speech. While the Act applies to student groups, its underlying principle—that a government-created forum must be open to all comers on equal terms—applies with equal force to non-student organizations when the forum is similarly created. The District’s selective exclusion of religious speech based on content is a violation of both the Act and the First Amendment.

Finally, the District’s claim that the denial is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is foreclosed by *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 247 (1990). There, the Court held that a public high school could not deny a student religious club access to school facilities when it had opened the forum to other non-curricular student groups. The Court rejected the argument that allowing religious speech would violate the Establishment Clause, stating that “the mere fact that a religious group seeks to use a public forum does not render the forum’s use unconstitutional.” Id. at 248. The same is true here. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a family-values film series does not advance the separation of church and state; it undermines it by discriminating against religious speech.

The First Amendment does not permit the government to create a forum and then exclude religious speech from it. The District’s policy of allowing non-religious community use while banning religious use is a content-based restriction that violates the First Amendment. The judgment of the Second Circuit must be reversed.
