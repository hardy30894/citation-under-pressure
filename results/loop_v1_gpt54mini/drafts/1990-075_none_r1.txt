The Controlled Substances Act does not violate Article I, nor does it forbid the Attorney General from acting through the DEA. Congress supplied an intelligible principle, defined the operative standards, and expressly authorized departmental delegation. Under settled precedent, that is enough.

I. Congress Validly Delegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General

The nondelegation doctrine asks whether Congress has “lay[ed] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This Court has consistently upheld delegations where Congress states a policy, identifies standards, and entrusts execution to the Executive. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 425-27 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947); Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Division of Department of Labor, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941); American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946).

The CSA fits comfortably within that line. Congress made the basic policy judgment: dangerous drugs should be controlled to protect the public. It then directed the Attorney General, before scheduling a substance, to consider specified factors, including the drug’s potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its effect, current scientific knowledge, history and pattern of abuse, risk to public health, dependence liability, and whether the substance is a precursor. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). Congress further required consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose scientific and medical evaluation is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). It also imposed notice requirements and, for temporary scheduling, a defined finding of “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and a limited duration. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). Those are not the marks of unbounded discretion; they are legislative standards. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 426; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250.

Petitioners rely on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). Those cases do not help them. In Panama Refining, the Court invalidated a statute that gave the President authority without a meaningful policy or rule to guide the decision. 293 U.S. at 430. In Schechter Poultry, the delegation was likewise condemned because Congress left the setting of codes of fair competition to private groups and the President without adequate statutory standards. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress itself determined what must be regulated, why, and by what criteria. That is precisely the kind of legislative guidance sustained in Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-93 (1892), Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813), Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), and Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-18 (1911).

The criminal consequences of drug scheduling do not alter the analysis. Congress may authorize administrative rulemaking whose violation is punishable criminally, so long as Congress itself has supplied the governing standard. Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 518-19; United States v. Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892). That is what Congress did here. The temporary scheduling provision does not create crimes out of whole cloth; it operates within a statutory scheme Congress enacted and bounded.

II. Congress Also Authorized the Attorney General to Delegate to the DEA

The second question is even easier. Congress expressly provided that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions under” the drug laws. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That language authorizes delegation within the Department. This Court has long recognized that executive power may be exercised through subordinate officers where Congress has entrusted the function to a department rather than insisting on personal action by the Secretary or Attorney General. Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 318-19 (1903); United States v. George, 228 U.S. 14, 24-25 (1913).

The delegation here was therefore doubly valid. First, Congress gave the Attorney General temporary scheduling authority subject to specific statutory limits. Second, Congress authorized the Attorney General to delegate departmental functions. The Attorney General’s regulation assigning those functions to the DEA Administrator was a permissible exercise of that express authority. In the administrative state, Congress frequently empowers agency heads to act through subordinates; that is a settled feature of federal administration, not a constitutional defect. Shurtleff, 189 U.S. at 318-19; George, 228 U.S. at 24-25.

III. The CSA’s Emergency Scheduling Scheme Is Especially Appropriate

This Court has repeatedly sustained broader delegations in fields requiring prompt and expert response. Yakus upheld a wartime price-control statute because the subject matter was technical and the need for flexibility was acute. 321 U.S. at 425-27. Fahey emphasized the importance of “well-known and generally acceptable standards” in a specialized area. 332 U.S. at 250. American Power & Light likewise sustained broad administrative authority in a complex regulatory field. 329 U.S. at 105. The CSA’s temporary scheduling provision serves the same function: when dangerous designer drugs emerge rapidly, Congress may allow the Executive to act quickly, but only within statutory bounds. That is a constitutional delegation, not an abdication. J.W. Hampton, 276 U.S. at 409; Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-27.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
