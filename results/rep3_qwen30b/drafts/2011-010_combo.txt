**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The question before this Court is whether that provision applies only when the injury occurs *on* the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit held that it does not, adopting a “substantial nexus” test that permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore. This interpretation is contrary to the plain language of the statute, the settled construction of the LHWCA, and binding precedent from this Court. The Court should reverse.

**I. The OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision applies only where the injury occurs on the outer Continental Shelf.**

The text of § 1333(b) is unambiguous: it extends LHWCA benefits to employees “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not create a jurisdictional rule based on functional or causal proximity; it is a temporal and spatial condition. An injury “occurring” on land, even if caused by offshore operations, does not fall within the statutory coverage unless the injury itself takes place on the outer Continental Shelf.

This interpretation is consistent with the long-standing principle that “the situs of the injury” determines the applicability of maritime compensation statutes. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947), the Court held that the LHWCA applies only to injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” The Court emphasized that “the place where the injury occurs is the controlling factor.” Id. Similarly, in *Barnes v. United States*, 326 U.S. 647, 650 (1946), the Court stated that “the injury must occur upon the navigable waters” to trigger LHWCA coverage. These cases establish that the *location* of the injury—not merely its causal connection to maritime activity—is the threshold requirement.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test ignores this foundational principle. It permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore, even when the injury is not “on” the outer Continental Shelf, simply because the employee was engaged in work related to offshore operations. This approach would expand the LHWCA’s reach far beyond its traditional maritime situs, undermining the clear line between maritime and land-based employment. As this Court held in *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), “the LHWCA was not intended to cover all injuries to employees engaged in maritime activities, but only those occurring upon the navigable waters.” The same principle applies to OCSLA: the statute extends LHWCA coverage *to* the outer Continental Shelf, not *from* the outer Continental Shelf.

**II. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire* confirms that the injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf.**

Although the Ninth Circuit dismissed the *Tallentire* footnote as dicta, the Court’s statement in that case is not mere obiter dictum—it is a clear and consistent application of the situs requirement. In *Tallentire*, the Court considered whether a choice-of-law provision in OCSLA applied to a helicopter crash occurring above the outer Continental Shelf. The Court held that the provision applied only to the “subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). The Court noted that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. The Court then added in a footnote: “Only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” Id. at 219 n.2.

This footnote is not mere speculation. It reflects the Court’s understanding that § 1333(b) is subject to the same *situs* requirement as the rest of OCSLA. The Court did not say that the injury must be “caused by” offshore operations; it said that the injury must occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “conducted on” refers to the location of the operations, and the injury must occur in the same place. The Court’s statement in *Tallentire* is not dicta—it is a judicial construction of the statute’s geographic scope, consistent with the Court’s prior decisions.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test conflicts with established precedent.**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. That decision was based on a misreading of *Tallentire* and ignored the clear language of the statute. The Third Circuit held that a worker injured in a car accident on his way to a helicopter was entitled to benefits under § 1333(b). But that case involved a worker who was en route to an offshore platform, not one who was injured onshore after completing his duties. The injury occurred *on* the way to the outer Continental Shelf, not *after* the work was done. The facts are distinguishable.

Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33 (1952), which reaffirmed that the LHWCA applies only to injuries occurring upon navigable waters.
