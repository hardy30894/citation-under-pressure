**Argument**

A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot excuse the procedural default of another federal habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself has first been properly exhausted and preserved in state court, or the petitioner separately establishes cause and prejudice for that default. That rule is compelled by the Court’s settled habeas jurisprudence, which requires respect for state procedural rules and gives the state courts the first opportunity to pass on federal constitutional claims. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963); *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 485-86 (1953).

The starting point is the exhaustion doctrine. A federal habeas petitioner must “fairly present” his federal claim to the state courts before seeking federal relief. *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971); *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950); *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944). The reason is comity: state courts must have the first chance to correct their own constitutional errors. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 447-48; *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447-48 (1965). If a petitioner could invoke a never-preserved ineffective-assistance claim as “cause,” he would evade the exhaustion requirement by indirection.

That is precisely what *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), forbids. The Court held that attorney error may constitute cause only when it rises to constitutionally ineffective assistance under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). But the Court also made the critical point that “the exhaustion doctrine” requires an ineffective-assistance claim to be presented to the state courts “as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause.” *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489. That rule is not a technicality; it is the necessary consequence of the Court’s insistence that federal habeas not become a forum for claims state courts never had a fair opportunity to consider.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would collapse the distinction between cause and the underlying default. A petitioner could default a trial-error claim, default the ineffective-assistance claim that supposedly explains the first default, and then ask a federal court to ignore both defaults at once. The Court has never sanctioned such a bootstrap. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly enforced procedural bars even where constitutional claims are implicated. *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 87-91; *Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 493-94 (1976); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947).

Nor do the older cases recognizing the right to counsel change the analysis. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938), held that waiver of counsel must be intentional and intelligent. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516-17 (1962), applied that principle where the record failed to show a valid waiver. But neither case suggests that a procedurally defaulted ineffectiveness claim can automatically excuse another default. That later question is governed by *Strickland* and *Carrier*, which require a properly preserved ineffective-assistance claim before it may serve as cause. *Strickland*, 466 U.S. at 687, 694; *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489.

Indeed, accepting petitioner’s position would undermine the finality and regularity that the Court has long protected in habeas. *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 88-91; *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. at 485-86. State rules of timeliness and presentation would become optional so long as the petitioner could recharacterize his omission as appellate counsel’s fault. The Court has rejected comparable efforts to evade procedural rules by reframing the issue after the fact. *Henry*, 379 U.S. at 447-48; *Picard*, 404 U.S. at 275-76.

The proper rule is straightforward. If a petitioner wishes to rely on ineffective assistance as cause for a defaulted claim, he must first have exhausted that ineffective-assistance claim in state court, or else independently excuse its default. Otherwise, the federal courts would be using a forfeited claim to erase another forfeiture, contrary to *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), and the foundational exhaustion cases. The judgment below should be affirmed.
