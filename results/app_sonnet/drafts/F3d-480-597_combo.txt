**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Failed to Prove That Nieves-Castaño Knew the Rifle Possessed the Characteristics That Made It a Machine Gun, and the Trial Court's Instructions Compounded That Failure**

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) requires proof that the defendant knew the weapon she possessed had the automatic-fire characteristics of a machine gun. This requirement flows from the longstanding presumption that Congress does not dispense with mens rea absent a clear indication otherwise. In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), the Supreme Court held that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion," *id.* at 250, and refused to read a federal criminal statute as eliminating the intent element merely because Congress omitted express intent language. The Court cautioned that mens rea should not be read out of a statute unless the offense falls within the narrow category of public-welfare regulatory crimes described in *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250 (1922), where "the emphasis of the statute is evidently upon achievement of the higher social order," *id.* at 252, rather than on individual culpability. Possession of a firearm is not such a regulatory offense; firearm ownership is lawful for most citizens, and nothing in § 922(o) signals that Congress meant to punish, without proof of knowledge, a person who innocently possessed what she believed to be an ordinary rifle.

Here, the only evidence of knowledge was Nieves-Castaño's statement that she knew the bag contained "an AK-47." That admission establishes, at most, knowledge of the weapon's make and general appearance—not knowledge of the internal mechanism that renders a weapon a "machine gun" as statutorily defined. The government offered no evidence that she knew of, or had reason to know of, any automatic-firing capability. Under *Morissette*, silence in the record on this element cannot be filled by inference from mere possession.

The error was aggravated by the district court's instructions, which permitted the jury to convict without finding that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon's automatic characteristics. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court explained that a specific-intent instruction is often what preserves a criminal statute from constitutional infirmity and ensures the jury actually finds the culpable mental state Congress required. No such instruction was given here. The jury was thus permitted to convict on a lesser showing than the statute demands.

Reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence, this Court must ask whether, "taking the view most favorable to the Government," the record contains "substantial evidence" to support the verdict. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942). Even under that generous standard, a "reasonable mind" could not "fairly conclude guilt beyond a reasonable doubt" on the knowledge element from the evidence presented. *Curley v. United States*, 160 F.2d 229, 232 (D.C. Cir. 1947). The § 922(o) conviction should be reversed.

**II. Section 922(q)(2)(A) Is Unconstitutionally Vague Because It Fails to Specify How the 1,000-Foot School-Zone Boundary Is to Be Measured**

Due process requires that a criminal statute give fair notice of the conduct it forbids. As the Supreme Court held in *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385 (1926), "a statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law." *Id.* at 391. Similarly, in *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), the Court explained that "no one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes." *Id.* at 453.

Section 922(q)(2)(A) criminalizes possession of a firearm within "1,000 feet" of a school but nowhere specifies the method of measurement—whether by straight-line distance, walking distance, or some other metric. Depending on the method chosen, a citizen standing in the same physical location could be inside or outside the zone. This is not the ordinary "matter of degree" that citizens are expected to estimate at their peril, *see Nash v. United States*, 229 U.S. 373, 377 (1913) (recognizing that some statutes permissibly require estimation of degree), because the statute supplies no fixed reference point or method from which any estimation can even begin. Unlike the regulations at issue in *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337 (1952), and *United States v. National Dairy Products Corp.*, 372 U.S. 29 (1963), where context and industry practice supplied an ascertainable standard, § 922(q)(2)(A) leaves the measurement methodology entirely to enforcement discretion. That discretion is precisely what the vagueness doctrine forbids.

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish That the Apartment Fell Within a School Zone as Properly Measured**

Even if the statute survives the vagueness challenge, the government's proof of distance was inadequate under *Glasser* and *Curley*. The government offered no evidence establishing which measurement method was used, or that any method reliably placed the third-floor apartment within thelegal zone. On this record, no reasonable jury could conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that the school-zone element was satisfied.

For these reasons, both convictions should be reversed.
