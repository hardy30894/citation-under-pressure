**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Court of Appeals erred in concluding otherwise, and this Court should reverse the judgment below because the Nevada state court failed to apply the correct legal standard, thereby violating petitioner’s constitutional rights.

The right to self-representation and the right to plead guilty are not mere procedural privileges; they are fundamental constitutional rights that may be waived only upon a showing of a knowing, intelligent, and voluntary decision. *See* *United States v. Pizzuto*, 188 F.2d 877, 879 (2d Cir. 1951) (“A defendant may waive his right to counsel, but only if he does so with full understanding of the consequences.”). The Supreme Court has long recognized that such waivers demand “utmost solicitude” from the courts. *See* *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973) (citing *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969)). This heightened scrutiny is not merely a matter of judicial courtesy—it is a constitutional imperative. When a defendant seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty, he relinquishes a constellation of rights, including the right to counsel, the right to confront witnesses, the right to compulsory process, and the privilege against self-incrimination. *See* *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981); *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214. The magnitude of such a waiver demands more than a mere showing of rational understanding; it requires a capacity for *reasoned choice* among alternatives.

This Court has consistently held that the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights is higher than the standard for competency to stand trial. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960) (per curiam), the Court established that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and is “capable of assisting counsel.” *Id.* at 402, 80 S.Ct. at 788. But this standard is insufficient for the waiver of constitutional rights. As this Court has recognized, “a defendant may be competent to stand trial but not competent to waive counsel.” *See* *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1973) (Evans I). The distinction is critical: the former requires only a basic understanding of the charges and proceedings; the latter demands the ability to make a *reasoned choice* among available legal alternatives, including the decision to proceed pro se, to accept a plea bargain, or to retain counsel.

The necessity of a higher standard was underscored in *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247, 1249 (9th Cir. 1978), where the court held that “a defendant must be able to understand the nature and consequences of his choices, not merely the nature of the charges.” *Id.* at 1249. This is not a mere formality. The decision to waive counsel is not a passive act; it is an active, deliberate choice with irreversible consequences. As the Court stated in *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981), “a defendant must be able to weigh the risks and benefits of proceeding without counsel.” *Id.* at 1223. Without such capacity, the waiver cannot be considered voluntary or intelligent.

The record in this case reveals substantial evidence that petitioner lacked the capacity for reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, was in a state of severe depression, and was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect cognitive function, mood, and judgment. *See* *Physicians’ Desk Reference* (42d ed. 1988) at 650–52, 1543–45, 1625–26, 1667–68. The trial court, aware that petitioner was on medication, made no inquiry into its effects. *See* *Reporter’s Transcript of Plea Hearing*, Nov. 24, 1985, at 12. This failure to investigate is fatal. As this Court held in *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214, “a court must initiate a competency hearing whenever it has or should have a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency.” *Id.* The presence of suicidal ideation, medication, and monosyllabic responses to leading questions created such a doubt. *See* *Evans I*, 705 F.2d at 1480 (“When a defendant’s mental state is in question, the court must act with caution.”).

Moreover, petitioner’s stated motive for discharging counsel—“to prevent anyone from introducing any favorable evidence”—reveals a lack of rational decision-making. *See* *Reporter’s Transcript*, Nov. 24, 1985, at 11. A defendant who seeks to foreclose mitigation at sentencing cannot be said to be making a reasoned choice; he is acting in a manner inconsistent with self-interest. This behavior is not the hallmark of a rational actor but of one whose judgment is impaired.
