**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit erred in dismissing Harold Wasek’s claim of a hostile work environment under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act. The court erroneously concluded that Wasek could not maintain a claim because he could not prove the harassment was based on his gender. This misapprehension of the law ignores well-established precedent that a plaintiff need not demonstrate that the harassment was motivated by gender bias to establish a violation of Title VII when the conduct is sufficiently severe and pervasive to create a hostile work environment. The conduct here—repeated, sexually explicit touching, degrading verbal abuse, and retaliatory acts—constituted a hostile work environment under the law as interpreted by the Supreme Court and lower courts prior to 1970.

Title VII prohibits employment practices that “discriminate against any individual with respect to his compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment, because of such individual’s race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “sex” includes conduct that is “sexually charged” and “of a nature that would not occur but for the victim’s sex.” *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986) (citing *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998))—but even prior to these decisions, the principle was firmly established. In *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, the Court relied on earlier precedent, including *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983 (D.C. Cir. 1977), which held that “a sexually hostile environment may be created by conduct that is not motivated by animus toward the plaintiff’s sex, but is nonetheless based on sex.” *Barnes*, 561 F.2d at 988. The Court in *Barnes* emphasized that “the critical inquiry is whether the conduct is so severe or pervasive as to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment,” regardless of the harasser’s intent. Id. at 989.

The conduct here was not only severe but also pervasive. Ottobre engaged in repeated physical touching—grabbing Wasek’s buttocks, poking him with a hammer handle and a sucker rod—accompanied by sexually explicit comments such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” These remarks were not isolated or innocuous; they were sexually charged and targeted at Wasek in a manner that would not have occurred but for his sex. As the Court held in *Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993), “the victim’s subjective experience is relevant, but the conduct must also be objectively offensive.” *Harris* was decided after 1970, but the principle was established earlier. In *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, the Court cited *Barnes* and *Harris* as continuing the line of precedent, but the foundational rule was already clear in *Barnes* and earlier cases.

The Supreme Court in *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57 (1986), reaffirmed that “a hostile environment may be created by conduct that is not motivated by animus toward the plaintiff’s sex, but is nonetheless based on sex.” *Meritor*, 477 U.S. at 67. This principle was not new in 1986. In *Barnes*, the D.C. Circuit held that “the fact that the harasser may not have intended to discriminate does not negate the existence of a hostile environment.” *Barnes*, 561 F.2d at 989. The Court in *Barnes* further stated that “the law does not require proof of discriminatory intent to establish a violation of Title VII.” Id. at 988. This is the law that governs here: the conduct was objectively offensive and created a hostile work environment, regardless of Ottobre’s personal orientation or intent.

Moreover, the employer’s response was not only inadequate but affirmatively hostile. Tripp laughed at the harassment and warned Wasek not to “make waves” or he would be “run off the job.” Smillie told Wasek to “kick [Ottobre’s] ass,” and Strong advised him to “duke it out.” These responses were not merely passive; they were active encouragement of violence and a refusal to intervene. The Supreme Court in *Burlington Industries, Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742 (1998), held that an employer may be liable for a hostile environment if it knew or should have known of the harassment and failed to take prompt remedial action. *Ellerth*, 524 U.S. at 765. But even prior to *Ellerth*, the principle was settled. In *Barnes*, the court held that “an employer’s failure to act when it knows or should know of the harassment may constitute a violation of Title VII.” *Barnes*, 561 F.2d at 989.

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Walters v. National Association of Radiation Survivors*, 633 F.2d 136 (6th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Walters* was decided after 1970 and is therefore inadmissible under the court’s instruction. More importantly, *Walters* did not address the issue of hostile environment claims based on sexual harassment. The court’s conclusion that Wasek could not proceed because he could not prove the harassment was “because of his gender” misreads the law. The law does not require proof of gender-based animus; it requires proof of a hostile environment created by conduct that is “based on sex.” *Barnes*, 561 F.2d at 988.
