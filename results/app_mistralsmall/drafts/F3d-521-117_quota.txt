**ARGUMENT**

**I. The pat-frisk of Mr. Soares violated the Fourth Amendment’s prohibition against unreasonable searches.**

The Fourth Amendment guarantees that “[t]he right of the people to be secure in their persons . . . against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated.” U.S. Const. amend. IV. A pat-frisk is a “search” within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 16 (1968). Such a search is reasonable only if it is supported by “reasonable suspicion” that the suspect is armed and dangerous. *Id.* at 27; *see also United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 226 (1985) (“[T]he police must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant [the] intrusion.” (internal quotation marks omitted)).

Here, the government cannot meet that standard. The officers lacked reasonable suspicion to pat-frisk Mr. Soares because his conduct—while admittedly agitated and verbally abusive—did not objectively suggest that he was armed and dangerous.

First, the officers’ own testimony confirms that Mr. Soares’s behavior, though confrontational, was not indicative of a threat. Officer Reid acknowledged that Mr. Soares was “rocking back and forth” and using profanity, but he did not testify that Mr. Soares made any furtive movements toward his waistband or otherwise acted in a manner that would suggest he was armed. *See United States v. Young*, 105 F.3d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 1997) (holding that “[a] suspect’s nervousness alone is not enough to justify a *Terry* frisk”); *United States v. Fornia-Castillo*, 408 F.3d 74, 80 (1st Cir. 2005) (same).

Second, the officers’ decision to remove Mr. Soares from the car was not justified by reasonable suspicion. The government argues that the officers acted out of concern for their safety due to Mr. Soares’s agitation. *See United States v. Suarez*, 555 F.3d 15, 20 (1st Cir. 2009) (noting that “officers may take reasonable steps to protect themselves during a traffic stop”). However, the Supreme Court has made clear that “[t]he mere fact that [a suspect] is in a car that is stopped for a traffic violation does not, by itself, give rise to a reasonable belief that [he] is armed and presently dangerous.” *Knowles v. Iowa*, 525 U.S. 113, 117 (1998). Here, the officers had no information suggesting that Mr. Soares was armed, and his verbal abuse and agitation—without more—cannot supply the necessary reasonable suspicion. *See United States v. Acosta-Colon*, 467 F.3d 47, 50 (1st Cir. 2006) (holding that “[a] defendant’s nervousness, alone, is insufficient to justify a pat-frisk”).

Third, the officers’ invocation of “furtive movements” by other passengers does not justify the pat-frisk of Mr. Soares. The government cites *United States v. Lender*, 985 F.2d 151, 154 (1st Cir. 1993), for the proposition that “furtive movements” can contribute to reasonable suspicion. However, *Lender* involved specific, articulable facts—including a suspect’s attempt to conceal something under his seat—that justified the frisk. *Id.* Here, the officers observed no such conduct by Mr. Soares. *See also United States v. Alvarez*, 895 F.3d 100, 107 (1st Cir. 2018) (requiring “specific, articulable facts” to justify a frisk).

Finally, the government’s reliance on the high-crime area factor is insufficient. While a high-crime area is a relevant consideration, it is not dispositive. *See United States v. O’Brien*, 505 U.S. 92, 100 (1992) (noting that “[a]n individual’s presence in a high-crime area, standing alone, is not sufficient to support a reasonable, particularized suspicion of criminal activity”). Here, the officers had no information linking Mr. Soares to criminal activity or suggesting that he was armed. *See United States v. Burton*, 288 F.3d 91, 96 (1st Cir. 2002) (holding that “[a] high-crime area, without more, does not provide the reasonable suspicion necessary to justify a *Terry* stop or frisk”).

**II. The evidence seized from Mr. Soares should have been suppressed.**

Because the pat-frisk violated the Fourth Amendment, the evidence seized as a result of that unlawful search—the handgun—must be suppressed. *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963) (“[E]vidence obtained as an indirect result of [an unlawful search] must be excluded as ‘fruit of the poisonous tree.’”); *see also United States v. Robinson*, 414 U.S. 218, 237 (1973) (Brennan, J., dissenting) (“[T]he exclusionary rule . . . requires the suppression of evidence obtained as a result of an illegal search.”).

The government cannot rely on the “independent source” or “inevitable discovery” doctrines to save the evidence, as neither applies here. There is no evidence that the officers would have inevitably discovered the handgun absent the unlawful pat-frisk, nor did the government demonstrate a separate, lawful source for the discovery of the firearm. *See Murray v. United States*, 487 U.S. 533, 539 (1988) (explaining the independent source doctrine); *Nix v. Williams*, 467 U.S. 431, 444 (1984) (explaining the inevitable discovery doctrine).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in denying Mr. Soares’s motion to suppress. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
