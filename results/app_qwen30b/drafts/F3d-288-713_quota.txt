**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Request for an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct and Bias, in Violation of Due Process Under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Riddle’s petition for habeas corpus raises a constitutional claim of prosecutorial misconduct and bias, which, if proven, would violate his right to due process under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. The district court’s denial of an evidentiary hearing on this claim was error, as it failed to recognize that Riddle’s allegations—when viewed in the light most favorable to him—raise a genuine issue of material fact that cannot be resolved on the pleadings alone.

Riddle’s claim centers on the conduct of District Attorney Danny Hill, who served as chief prosecutor in the capital murder case. Riddle testified that Hill, after Riddle’s first arraignment, told him, “he was going to do his damnest [sic] to make sure [he] got the death penalty.” This statement, if true, constitutes a direct threat to impose the death penalty, which is a form of prosecutorial overreach that undermines the fairness of the trial. See *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895) (“The duty of the prosecutor is to seek justice, not merely to convict.”). The Supreme Court has long held that a prosecutor’s personal animus or threat to impose the death penalty based on the defendant’s conduct or demeanor violates due process. *See, e.g.,* *Moore v. Illinois*, 434 U.S. 220, 225 (1977) (holding that a prosecutor’s statement that he “would see that the defendant got the death penalty” violated due process); *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453 (prosecutorial conduct must not be “influenced by passion or prejudice”).

Moreover, Hill’s continued prosecution of Riddle after making such a threat—while calling witnesses to impeach Riddle’s credibility—created a clear conflict of interest and a reasonable appearance of bias. *See* *Caperton v. A.T. Massey Coal Co.*, 556 U.S. 868, 885 (2009) (holding that “a judge’s impartiality may be questioned when a party has a direct, substantial, and personal stake in the outcome”). Although Hill was not a judge, the principle applies with equal force to prosecutors: when a prosecutor makes a threat to impose the death penalty and then proceeds to prosecute the defendant with zeal, the defendant’s right to a fair trial is compromised. *See* *United States v. Agurs*, 427 U.S. 97, 103 (1976) (prosecutorial misconduct may violate due process when it “undermine[s] the integrity of the judicial process”).

The district court erred in concluding that Riddle’s claim was “speculative” and that no evidentiary hearing was necessary. *See* *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 260 (1989) (a habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing when “the factual allegations, if proven, would entitle him to relief”). Riddle’s testimony, supported by the context of Hill’s conduct—including Hill’s use of witnesses to impeach Riddle’s credibility and the assistant prosecutor’s closing argument that Riddle was “a liar” based on Hill’s testimony—creates a triable issue of fact. *See* *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977) (a habeas petitioner must be given a hearing when the record is insufficient to resolve the claim).

The district court’s refusal to hold an evidentiary hearing also violated the procedural safeguards established in *Frazier v. Cupp*, 394 U.S. 731, 739 (1969), which held that “a defendant’s right to a fair trial is not satisfied by a mere recitation of facts” when the record is incomplete. Here, the record is insufficient to determine whether Hill’s conduct was motivated by personal animus or whether it tainted the trial. *See* *United States v. Bagley*, 473 U.S. 667, 678 (1985) (prosecutorial suppression of evidence favorable to the defendant violates due process, and the court must consider whether the evidence could have affected the outcome).

II. The District Court’s Grant of Summary Judgment Was Improper Because It Failed to Consider the Full Record and the Presumption of Innocence.

The district court’s grant of summary judgment was premature and legally flawed. *See* *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986) (summary judgment is appropriate only when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact). The record contains multiple factual disputes—particularly regarding Riddle’s version of events, the authenticity of Hill’s threat, and the credibility of Bybee’s testimony—that preclude summary judgment.

Furthermore, the district court failed to apply the presumption of innocence, which is a cornerstone of our criminal justice system. *See* *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (“The Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.”). The court’s failure to afford Riddle the benefit of this presumption when evaluating his claims undermines the integrity of the habeas process.
