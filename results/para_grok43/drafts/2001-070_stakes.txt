ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 171 (1976). A punishment is unusual if it is contrary to “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). When the Virginia Supreme Court affirmed petitioner’s death sentence, a majority of death-penalty jurisdictions still permitted capital punishment of mentally retarded offenders. Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 334 (1989). That legislative pattern supplied “powerful evidence” that such executions were not regarded as cruel and unusual. Id. at 331.

Petitioner’s claim that mental retardation categorically bars execution therefore fails under the framework applied in Penry. There the Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit execution of mentally retarded persons because “so long as sentencers can consider and give effect to mitigating evidence of mental retardation,” the sentence satisfies the Constitution. Id. at 340. Virginia’s sentencing procedures afforded precisely that opportunity. The jury heard extensive evidence of petitioner’s intellectual disability, received an instruction permitting it to consider that evidence in mitigation, and nevertheless returned a death verdict. That result is consistent with Penry’s command that mental retardation be treated as a mitigating factor rather than an absolute exemption.

Nor does the record demonstrate that petitioner’s disability rendered his punishment disproportionate in the constitutional sense. The Eighth Amendment requires individualized consideration of the offender’s culpability. See Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 605 (1978) (plurality opinion). The trial court permitted full exploration of petitioner’s mental capacity, and the jury’s determination that the aggravating circumstances outweighed any mitigating effect of his retardation is entitled to deference. See Blystone v. Pennsylvania, 494 U.S. 299, 306-07 (1990).

Because no national consensus had emerged against executing mentally retarded capital defendants at the time of petitioner’s sentencing, and because Virginia’s procedures satisfied the requirement of individualized consideration, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
