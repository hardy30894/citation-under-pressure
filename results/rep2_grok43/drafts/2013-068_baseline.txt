The Massachusetts statute, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½ (2007), is a valid time, place, and manner regulation. It restricts speech only within fixed 35-foot zones adjacent to clinic entrances, exits, and driveways, and it applies equally to all speakers without regard to the content of their message. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719-20 (2000) (holding that a similar floating buffer zone was content-neutral because it “regulates where some speech may occur” rather than “what” may be said). The statute therefore triggers intermediate scrutiny. Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989).

The Act serves the significant governmental interests of “ensuring public safety and order” and “protecting a woman’s freedom to seek pregnancy-related services.” Hill, 530 U.S. at 715. It is narrowly tailored because the buffer zones “eliminate[] the delay caused by” confrontational encounters while leaving open “alternative channels for communication.” Id. at 726-27. The record confirms that petitioners continue to speak at normal conversational distances, display signs, distribute literature, and pray outside the zones. See id. at 729 (noting that “the 8-foot zone does not ‘ban’ any form of communication”).

Petitioners’ claim that the Act is viewpoint-discriminatory because it exempts clinic employees and patients fails. The exemptions are justified by the distinct governmental interests in patient access and clinic operations, not by agreement or disagreement with any message. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 719-20. The law-of-the-case doctrine likewise bars relitigation of facial validity, as no intervening Supreme Court decision has altered the governing standard. See Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 340 (2010) (reaffirming that content-neutral restrictions on speech in traditional public forums remain subject to intermediate scrutiny).

Hill v. Colorado remains controlling precedent. Its reasoning—that narrowly drawn buffer zones around healthcare facilities are permissible when they leave open ample alternatives—has not been undermined by subsequent decisions addressing wholly different contexts. Stare decisis therefore counsels against overruling or limiting Hill. See Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey, 505 U.S. 833, 854-55 (1992).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
