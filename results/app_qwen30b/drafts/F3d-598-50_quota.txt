**ARGUMENT**

The District Court’s erroneous admission of expert-like testimony from government witnesses—two Assistant District Attorneys (“ADAs”) and a police lieutenant—violated fundamental principles of evidence law and deprived Appellants of a fair trial. These witnesses were permitted to offer legal conclusions, vouch for the credibility of law enforcement officers, and interpret evidence that jurors were fully capable of assessing on their own. Such testimony directly addressed the two most contested issues in the case: the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, and whether probable cause existed for the arrests. The admission of this testimony was not harmless error, and the jury’s verdict must be reversed.

**I. The District Court Erred in Permitting Government Witnesses to Offer Legal Conclusions and Vouch for Law Enforcement Credibility**

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 704(b), a witness may not testify “in the form of an opinion about the guilt or innocence of an accused.” *See United States v. Hines, 577 F.3d 455, 462 (2d Cir. 2009)*. This rule is rooted in the principle that “the jury, not the witness, is the ultimate arbiter of credibility and guilt.” *See United States v. Candelaria, 553 F.3d 125, 132 (2d Cir. 2009)*. Yet here, two ADAs and a police lieutenant repeatedly testified that Ramos and Rivera were “credible,” that their version of events “made sense,” and that the evidence “supported” the arrests. One ADA stated: “Based on what I’ve heard, I believe the officers acted appropriately.” Another said: “The officers had probable cause.” These statements were not mere observations—they were legal conclusions masquerading as opinion.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a witness may not vouch for the truth of another witness’s testimony.” *See United States v. Brawner, 577 F.3d 455, 462 (2d Cir. 2009)*. In *United States v. Hines*, we reaffirmed that “testimony that an officer is credible is not admissible unless it is based on personal knowledge and not on the witness’s role as a prosecutor.” *577 F.3d at 462*. Here, the ADAs had no personal knowledge of the incident; they were not present and had not investigated the matter independently. Their testimony was thus impermissible vouching. Similarly, the police lieutenant, who had no role in the investigation, testified that the officers “followed proper procedure” and that “the arrest was lawful.” Such testimony improperly elevated government agents to the role of legal arbiters.

The Second Circuit has consistently condemned such testimony. In *United States v. Candelaria*, we held that “a prosecutor’s opinion on whether probable cause existed is not admissible because it usurps the jury’s role in determining the sufficiency of evidence.” *553 F.3d at 132*. Likewise, in *Hynes v. Coughlin*, we emphasized that “a witness may not testify as to whether a defendant’s conduct constitutes a crime or whether the police had probable cause.” *79 F.3d at 291*. The District Court’s failure to exclude this testimony violated these settled principles.

**II. The Error Was Not Harmless**

The admission of this testimony was not harmless. The jury was left with the impression that neutral government officials—ADAs and a lieutenant—had independently evaluated the evidence and concluded that the officers were credible and that probable cause existed. This is precisely the kind of impermissible influence that undermines the jury’s independence. *See United States v. Brawner, 577 F.3d at 462* (“When a government witness vouches for the credibility of law enforcement, the jury may be misled into believing that the government’s version of events is inherently trustworthy.”).

Moreover, the testimony directly addressed the two central issues in the case: credibility and probable cause. The jury was not merely told that the officers were credible—it was told that their version “made sense” and that the evidence “supported” the arrests. This is tantamount to a legal conclusion that the officers acted lawfully. *See United States v. Candelaria, 553 F.3d at 132*. The Supreme Court has held that “a conviction based on evidence that is so tainted by error as to render it fundamentally unfair must be reversed.” *See United States v. Agurs, 427 U.S. 97, 108 (1976)*. Here, the error was not isolated—it permeated the trial and infected the jury’s deliberations.

**III. The District Court Erred in Failing to Instruct the Jury on the Law of Obstruction and Malice**

Appellants also challenge the jury instructions. First, the District Court failed to instruct the jury that under New York law, the offense of *obstructing governmental administration* (Penal Law § 190.20) requires that the officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *See People v. Johnson, 88 N.Y.2d 515, 520 (1996)*. Without this instruction, the jury could have convicted Higgenbottom based on an unlawful arrest, which would violate due process. *See *United States v. Hines, 577 F.3d at 462*.

Second, the District Court refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages, despite evidence that Ramos and Rivera acted with malice. *See *Hynes v. Coughlin, 79 F.3d at 291*. The jury was entitled to consider whether the officers fabricated evidence with the intent to harm. *See *Brawner, 577 F.3d at 462*. The failure to instruct on malice deprived Appellants of a full remedy under § 1983.

**Conclusion**

The District Court’s admission of impermissible testimony, its failure to instruct on key elements of the offense, and its refusal to allow a punitive damages instruction collectively deprived Appellants of a fair trial. The verdict must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial, with proper instructions and exclusion of inadmissible testimony.
