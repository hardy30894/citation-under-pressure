ARGUMENT

The district court erred in treating Furlow's South Carolina distribution conviction and Georgia arson convictions as predicates. Both the ACCA and the career-offender provision are penal enactments whose reach must be ascertained by the ordinary rules of statutory construction. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed").

Where that language leaves any doubt whether a prior offense satisfies the statutory criteria, the doubt is resolved in favor of the accused. United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) ("The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself."). The same principle of strict construction governs when a statute enumerates specific offenses. McBoyle v. United States, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) ("Although it is not likely that a criminal will carefully consider the text of the law before he murders or steals, it is reasonable that a fair warning should be given to the world in language that the common world will understand, of what the law intends to do if a certain line is passed.").

Applying these settled canons, neither the South Carolina statute nor the Georgia arson statute supplies the elements required by the federal definitions. Any residual ambiguity must therefore be resolved against the government. Bell v. United States, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955) ("When Congress leaves to the Judiciary the task of imputing to Congress an undeclared will, the ambiguity should be resolved in favor of lenity.").

The judgments of sentence should be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing without the enhancements.
