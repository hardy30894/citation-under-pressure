**ARGUMENT**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA does not qualify as an “exempt retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and therefore is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate. The Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds is not triggered by the mere fact that the funds were once part of a retirement account; rather, it depends on whether the funds are currently “retirement funds” in the hands of the debtor. As the Seventh Circuit recognized, *“an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.”* The statutory structure and purpose of the exemption make clear that Congress intended protection only for funds that serve their intended function: saving for the debtor’s own retirement.

Congress has long recognized that retirement savings must be shielded from creditors to ensure that individuals can plan for their post-working years without fear of losing their life savings to pre-retirement debts. This policy is implemented through § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), which exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The key inquiry is not whether the funds were once retirement savings, but whether they *remain* retirement funds in the debtor’s possession. As the Seventh Circuit emphasized, *“the money used to be ‘retirement funds’ but isn’t now.”* Once an IRA is inherited by a non-spouse beneficiary, its economic and legal character fundamentally changes. The beneficiary cannot contribute to it, cannot roll it over, and must begin mandatory distributions within one year of the original owner’s death—often before the beneficiary reaches retirement age. These requirements strip the account of its essential function as a vehicle for future retirement income.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), and the Eighth Circuit’s *In re Nessa*, 426 B.R. 312 (BAP 8th Cir. 2010), which the Clarks rely on, misread the statute by focusing on the *origin* of the funds rather than their *current status*. The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt assets simply because they were once retirement funds. To hold otherwise would render the exemption meaningless in the context of inherited accounts. As the Seventh Circuit observed, *“if recipients of inherited IRAs could hold the wealth until their own retirement, tax deferral might become tax exemption, as capital held in IRAs could pass down through the generations without ever being subject to income tax.”* Congress explicitly rejected that outcome by imposing strict distribution rules on inherited IRAs. The very purpose of those rules is to prevent the indefinite deferral of taxation and to ensure that the funds are not used as a perpetual tax shelter.

The Clarks’ argument—that the exemption should apply because the account is still “tax-deferred” under the Internal Revenue Code—misunderstands the statutory framework. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) does not exempt all tax-deferred accounts; it exempts only those that are “retirement funds.” The term “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but its meaning is informed by context and purpose. The Supreme Court has long held that *“no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs. Deciding what competing values will or will not be sacrificed to the achievement of a particular objective is the very essence of legislative choice.”* Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987). Congress chose to protect funds that are *currently* being saved for retirement, not those that have been transformed into vehicles for current consumption.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to the homestead exemption is instructive. Just as a debtor cannot claim a former residence as exempt simply because it once served as a domicile, a debtor cannot claim an inherited IRA as exempt simply because it once served as a retirement account. *“Exemption would depend on how Heidi used the property, not how her mother used it.”* The same principle applies here: the exemption depends on the debtor’s use of the asset, not its history.

Finally, the Clarks’ policy argument—that exempting inherited IRAs would encourage saving and bequests—does not justify a strained interpretation of the statute. The Bankruptcy Code is not a tool for promoting intergenerational wealth transfer. It is a mechanism for providing a fresh start to honest debtors, not a vehicle for shielding assets from creditors based on their provenance. The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected this argument, noting that *“it is always possible to get more of whatever objective may have prompted a given clause, but ‘no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.’”*

For these reasons, the Seventh Circuit’s decision should be affirmed. The inherited IRA is not a “retirement fund” in the hands of the Clarks, and therefore does not qualify for exemption under § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).
