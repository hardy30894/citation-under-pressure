**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s $4.5 million enhancement of the lodestar, which was justified by the exceptional quality of representation and the extraordinary success achieved in a complex, high-stakes class action involving the systemic abuse of vulnerable foster children. This Court has long recognized that while the lodestar is the presumptively reasonable fee, it is not an inflexible ceiling. In rare and exceptional cases, a court may enhance the lodestar to reflect superior performance and exceptional results, provided there is specific evidence in the record to support such an adjustment. The district court here met that standard, and the Eleventh Circuit’s affirmance is consistent with this Court’s precedent.

The foundation of this principle is *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), which established that the lodestar—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is “presumed to be the reasonable fee.” Id. at 897, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. However, *Blum* did not foreclose enhancements. The Court explicitly stated that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988” only if the fee applicant fails to offer “specific evidence” of exceptional results or superior performance. Id. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. The Court emphasized that “in the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged,” an enhancement may be justified. Id. This language is not dicta; it is a clear, conditional opening for upward adjustments in exceptional circumstances.

That opening was reaffirmed and refined in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), where the Court reiterated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” Id. at 565, 106 S.Ct. at 3098 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901, 104 S.Ct. at 1548–50). The Court in *Delaware Valley I* did not overrule *Blum* but instead applied its framework, affirming that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” but leaving open the possibility of an adjustment in “exceptional” cases. Id. at 566, 106 S.Ct. at 3098–99. The Court’s decision in *Delaware Valley I* thus confirmed that the lodestar is not a ceiling, but a starting point, and that enhancements are permissible when justified by specific, record-based evidence.

The district court in this case provided precisely that: detailed findings based on its own observation of counsel’s performance over the course of a multi-year litigation. The court noted that plaintiffs’ counsel “demonstrated extraordinary skill, dedication, and professionalism,” and that the injunction entered was “one of the most comprehensive in the history of Georgia’s foster care system.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court also observed that the hourly rates charged were at the top of the market, yet the quality of representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected.” Id. at 1290. These findings were not mere opinion; they were based on the court’s firsthand experience with the case, including the complexity of the litigation, the number of motions, the depth of discovery, and the sustained commitment of counsel. This is precisely the kind of case-specific, record-based evaluation that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require.

The dissent’s claim that the district court “testified” on behalf of the plaintiffs is legally irrelevant. The court did not introduce new evidence; it applied its institutional knowledge—its “substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta” and its “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge”—to evaluate the quality of representation. This is not improper. As this Court has recognized, “a district judge is in the best position to evaluate trial-level advocacy.” *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 437, 103 S.Ct. 1933, 1941 (1983). The judge’s observations were not testimony but a necessary component of the fact-specific inquiry into whether the lodestar fully compensated for exceptional performance.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209 (11th Cir. 2008), correctly applied this precedent. The court affirmed the enhancement because the district court had made detailed findings supported by the record, including the exceptional nature of the results and the superior quality of counsel. The court cited *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784 (6th Cir. 2004), which held that “upward adjustment for quality of representation is justified only in the rare case where there is specific evidence that the quality of service was superior in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional.” Id. at 794. The same is true here.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that “excellent results” may justify an enhancement. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 435, 103 S.Ct. at 1940. In *Hensley*, the Court stated that “where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee. Normally this will encompass all hours reasonably expended on the litigation, and indeed in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” Id. (emphasis added).
