**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is the presumptively correct measure of a “reasonable” fee under statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897, 104 S. Ct. 1541, 1548 (1984). Any deviation from the lodestar—particularly an enhancement based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained”—must be justified by specific, record-based evidence of exceptional circumstances, and even then, such enhancements are permitted only in the rarest of cases.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed a $4.5 million enhancement to the lodestar based on the district court’s self-observed “stellar performance” of counsel and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, fundamentally misapplies Supreme Court precedent. The district court’s rationale—that the “superb quality of counsel’s representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates”—is not a permissible basis for an enhancement. *Blum* made clear that “the quality of representation” and “the results obtained” cannot serve as independent grounds for increasing the lodestar unless the fee applicant offers “specific evidence” demonstrating that the performance was superior to what one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional. *Id.* at 899, 104 S. Ct. at 1549. The district court here offered no such evidence. Instead, it relied on its own subjective impressions, accumulated over 58 years of legal practice, to conclude that counsel’s performance was “stellar.” This is not evidence—it is self-testimony, and it is not reviewable.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a court may enhance a fee award based on its own evaluation of counsel’s performance, particularly when that evaluation is not grounded in the record. In *Delaware Valley Citizens Council for Clean Air v. Pennsylvania*, 478 U.S. 546, 565, 106 S. Ct. 3088, 3098 (1986), the Court reaffirmed *Blum* and emphasized that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901, 104 S. Ct. at 1548–50). The Court did not, as the Eleventh Circuit suggests, create a new category of “exceptional” cases where a judge may enhance a fee based on personal admiration. Rather, it required that the enhancement be supported by “specific evidence” in the record—evidence that can be reviewed, challenged, and verified. The district court here provided none.

The dissenting opinion in the Eleventh Circuit, authored by Judge Tjoflat, correctly identifies the fatal flaw in the majority’s reasoning: the district court’s enhancement was based on unreviewable, ex parte observations that were not part of the record and were not subject to adversarial scrutiny. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1251 (11th Cir. 2008) (Tjoflat, J., dissenting). The court’s reliance on its own “substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta” and its “observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case” constitutes a violation of due process. The defendants were not given notice or an opportunity to respond to these self-serving assessments. *See United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395, 68 S. Ct. 525, 542 (1948) (due process requires that parties be given notice and an opportunity to be heard on all material issues). The district court did not merely “find” that the performance was exceptional—it testified on behalf of the plaintiffs, effectively becoming a witness in its own case.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. In *Evergreen*, the court vacated and remanded because the district court failed to address the plaintiffs’ argument for an enhancement based on exceptional success. *Id.* at 1336–37. In *Norman*, the court reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in evaluating the results. *Id.* at 1306. Neither case held that an enhancement based on quality of performance or results is automatically justified. Indeed, both cases assumed, for the sake of argument, that such enhancements *could* be appropriate in rare cases—but they did not mandate them. The Eleventh Circuit’s interpretation of these precedents as binding authority for affirming a $4.5 million enhancement is a distortion of their holdings.

The Supreme Court has never held that a fee enhancement based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” is a permissible or even a permissible *independent* basis for increasing the lodestar. To the contrary, *Blum* explicitly rejected the idea that such factors can justify an enhancement without specific evidence. *Id.* at 899, 104 S. Ct. at 1549. The Court has consistently emphasized that the lodestar is “presumed to be the reasonable fee,” and that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar.” *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 566, 106 S.
