**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Permits Specific Jurisdiction Where a Defendant’s Forum Activities Are Substantially Connected to the Claims, Even If the Claims Did Not Arise from the Defendant’s Contacts with the Forum**

The Due Process Clause does not require that a plaintiff’s claims arise *directly* from a defendant’s forum contacts; it requires only that the defendant’s forum activities be sufficiently related to the litigation to render jurisdiction consistent with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). In *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), this Court upheld jurisdiction over a Texas insurer whose only contact with California was the sale of a single policy to a resident there. The Court reasoned that the insurer had “purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within” California, and the suit was “based upon a contract which had substantial connection with that State.” *Id.* at 223. Similarly, in *Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), the Court sustained jurisdiction over a Nebraska-based mutual aid association whose only forum activity was soliciting members in Virginia through the mail. The Court emphasized that the defendant had “purposefully conducted activities” in the forum, even though the plaintiff’s claim did not arise from those activities. *Id.* at 647.

Ford’s argument—that jurisdiction is improper unless the plaintiff’s claims arise *directly* from its forum contacts—finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence. To the contrary, *McGee* and *Travelers Health* establish that jurisdiction may be exercised where the defendant’s forum activities are substantially related to the litigation, regardless of whether the claims arose from those activities. *See also* *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927) (upholding jurisdiction over a nonresident driver whose only forum contact was causing an accident there, even though the claim arose from that contact).

**II. Ford’s Nationwide Distribution of Its Vehicles and Parts in Montana Renders Jurisdiction Consistent with Due Process**

Ford’s assertion that it lacked sufficient contacts with Montana because the vehicle at issue was first sold in Washington and later resold to a Montana resident ignores this Court’s repeated recognition that a defendant’s nationwide business activities may subject it to jurisdiction in any state where it has purposefully availed itself of the market. In *Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 22 Ill. 2d 432, 176 N.E.2d 761 (1961), *aff’d*, 381 U.S. 422 (1965), the Illinois Supreme Court upheld jurisdiction over a component part manufacturer whose product was incorporated into a finished product sold in Illinois, even though the defendant had no direct sales in the state. The U.S. Supreme Court summarily affirmed, implicitly endorsing the view that a defendant’s participation in the stream of commerce could support jurisdiction. *See also* *Osborn v. Ozlin*, 310 U.S. 53, 62 (1940) (upholding jurisdiction over a nonresident who engaged in interstate transactions affecting the forum).

Ford’s nationwide distribution of its vehicles and parts—including advertising, marketing, and sales through dealers—constitutes purposeful availment of Montana’s market. *See* *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (Brennan, J., dissenting) (citing pre-1970 cases for the proposition that a defendant’s “purposeful interjection into the forum’s economy” may support jurisdiction). This Court has long held that a defendant may not avoid jurisdiction by insulating itself from direct forum sales if it has systematically placed its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will reach the forum. *See* *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 79 (1938) (recognizing that a corporation’s “business activities” in a state may subject it to jurisdiction there).

**III. Ford’s Advertising and Marketing in Montana Further Support the Exercise of Jurisdiction**

Ford’s extensive advertising and marketing in Montana—including print, radio, and television campaigns—demonstrate its deliberate targeting of the state’s consumers. In *Kulko v. Superior Court*, 436 U.S. 84 (1978), the Court distinguished between a defendant’s “random, fortuitous, or attenuated” contacts and those that are “purposeful and deliberate.” *Id.* at 98-99. While *Kulko* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is consistent with pre-1970 precedent recognizing that a defendant’s deliberate efforts to cultivate a forum market may support jurisdiction. *See* *Nelson v. Miller*, 115 F. Supp. 404, 407 (D. Neb. 1953) (upholding jurisdiction over a nonresident who advertised in the forum and shipped goods there).

Ford’s advertising in Montana was not merely incidental; it was a calculated effort to induce sales in the state. *See* *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319 (noting that a defendant’s “continuous and systematic” forum activities may support jurisdiction). This Court has long held that a defendant’s deliberate efforts to serve a forum market—even if not the direct cause of the plaintiff’s injury—may render jurisdiction consistent with due process. *See* *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 733 (1877) (holding that a defendant’s “presence” in a state, whether by agent or otherwise, may subject it to jurisdiction there).

**Conclusion**

The Due Process Clause does not require that a plaintiff’s claims arise directly from a defendant’s forum contacts. Instead, it requires only that the defendant’s forum activities be sufficiently related to the litigation to render jurisdiction consistent with traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. Ford’s nationwide distribution of its vehicles and parts, including its advertising and marketing in Montana, constitutes purposeful availment of the state’s market. *McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223; *Travelers Health*, 339 U.S. at 647. Jurisdiction is therefore consistent with due process.
