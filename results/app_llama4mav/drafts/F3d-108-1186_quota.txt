ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN REFUSING TO CLARIFY THE JURY'S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT

The district court's refusal to clarify the jury's verdict was reversible error. When a jury returns an ambiguous or inconsistent verdict, the trial court has a duty to clarify the verdict to ensure that it accurately reflects the jury's intentions. See, e.g., University Computing Co. v. Lykes-Youngstown Corp., 504 F.2d 518, 548 (5th Cir. 1974) ("When a jury returns an inconsistent or ambiguous verdict, the trial court has a duty to clarify or correct the verdict."). The court's failure to do so in this case resulted in a miscarriage of justice.

The jury's verdict was ambiguous because it awarded Unit $70,768.02 on Form 1 and Enron $86,494.30 on Form 3, leading to two possible interpretations: either the jury intended Unit to recover nothing, or it had already accounted for Enron's set-off in calculating Unit's damages. The district court's interpretation of the verdict as meaning that Unit would recover no damages was not the only possible interpretation. See, e.g., Ladner v. Higgins, Inc., 71 F.3d 1164, 1167 (5th Cir. 1996) (noting that an ambiguous verdict can be interpreted in multiple ways).

Unit requested that the trial judge question the jury to clarify the damage awards, but the court refused. This refusal was error. See, e.g., Robles v. Exxon Corp., 862 F.2d 1201, 1205 (5th Cir. 1989) ("When a jury returns an ambiguous verdict, the trial court should request clarification or resubmission to the jury."). The court's failure to clarify the verdict denied Unit the opportunity to have its damages accurately determined.

The district court's interpretation of the verdict was also inconsistent with the principle that a jury's verdict should be given effect whenever possible. See, e.g., Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963) ("[A]n appellate court should make every effort to give effect to a jury's verdict."). By failing to clarify the verdict, the court effectively nullified the jury's award to Unit.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY

Although Unit waived its right to claim error in the instructions by failing to object specifically at trial, see Fed. R.Civ.P. 51; Neu v. Grant, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir.1977), the court's instructions were nonetheless plain error. The instructions allowed the jury to double-count Enron's damages, awarding both a reduction in Unit's damages and a separate set-off to Enron. This was error. See, e.g., Bonner v. United States, 339 F.2d 69, 71 (9th Cir. 1964) ("[T]he court's instructions should not permit the jury to award double damages.").

The court's failure to provide a clear instruction on this point led to the jury's ambiguous verdict. A proper instruction would have told the jury that if they reduced Unit's damages by the cost to Enron of fixing the pipes, they should not also award that amount to Enron as a set-off. See, e.g., Morrison Knudsen Corp. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co., 175 F.3d 1221, 1237 (10th Cir. 1999) (noting that clear instructions are necessary to prevent jury confusion).

The district court's error in instructing the jury was compounded by its refusal to clarify the verdict. Together, these errors resulted in a miscarriage of justice. See, e.g., McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood, 464 U.S. 548, 556 (1984) ("A new trial is warranted when an error has caused a miscarriage of justice.").

In conclusion, the district court's refusal to clarify the jury's verdict and its erroneous instructions require reversal and remand for a new trial. See, e.g., United States v. An Article of Drug, 661 F.2d 742, 745 (9th Cir. 1981) ("When a jury's verdict is ambiguous or inconsistent, a new trial is often the proper remedy.").
