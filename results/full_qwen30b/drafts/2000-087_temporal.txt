**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in the foundational cases governing the limits of executive power over persons, forbids indefinite detention without a clear and present justification. The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize such detention, and to construe it as doing so would violate the fundamental principles of due process and personal liberty established in this Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence.

The power of the Executive to detain individuals is not plenary. In *United States v. Jackson*, 332 U.S. 561 (1947), this Court emphasized that “the power to detain is not absolute, but is subject to constitutional limitations.” The Court held that even in the context of national security, the Government may not imprison a person without a legal basis that is both specific and temporary. The same principle applies here: the Attorney General’s authority to detain a removable alien is not unlimited, particularly when the purpose of the detention—removal—has become effectively impossible.

The Government’s argument that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention rests on a strained reading of a statute that, when viewed in light of constitutional constraints, cannot be so interpreted. In *United States ex rel. Laub v. O’Connor*, 302 U.S. 108 (1937), this Court held that “the power to detain must be exercised in accordance with the law and not in a manner that renders the individual a permanent prisoner without recourse.” The statute here does not specify a duration for post-removal detention, but silence on the matter does not authorize an indefinite extension of confinement. As this Court stated in *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), “a statute must be construed in a way that avoids constitutional infirmity, if possible.” To read § 1231(a)(6) as permitting indefinite detention would render it unconstitutional under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, which this Court has long held protects against arbitrary and indefinite imprisonment.

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. That case involved an alien who had been stopped at the border and never admitted into the country. The Court upheld his detention because he had not yet gained entry and thus was not entitled to the full protections of the Constitution. But as this Court recognized in *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 222 (1922), “the right to liberty is not forfeited by the mere fact of alienage.” Once an alien has been lawfully admitted and has resided in the United States for decades, as Zadvydas has, the Government’s power to detain him is no longer governed by the principles applicable to excludable aliens at the border. The distinction is critical: *Mezei* did not involve a resident alien with a final order of removal who had been in the country for over forty years and had no realistic prospect of deportation.

Moreover, the Government’s claim that continued detention is justified by the need to ensure removal ignores the reality that removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807), this Court held that “the power to detain must be exercised with reference to the purpose for which it was granted.” If the purpose—removal—has become impossible, the power to detain ceases. The Government has not demonstrated that Zadvydas can be removed to any country. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him. No other country has offered to do so. The Government’s efforts, while perhaps well-intentioned, have not produced a single viable pathway to removal. To detain Zadvydas beyond the point at which removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable is to transform the statute into a mechanism for indefinite imprisonment—a result this Court has consistently rejected.

The Court has long recognized that “the liberty of the individual is the norm, and detention the exception.” In *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396 (1930), the Court stated that “no person may be deprived of liberty without due process of law.” The Constitution does not permit the Government to hold a person in custody indefinitely under the guise of administrative convenience. The mere fact that an alien is removable does not justify indefinite confinement. As this Court held in *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22 (1953), “the power to detain must be exercised in a manner consistent with the fundamental rights of the individual.”

The Government’s argument that Zadvydas may be released on conditions of supervision is not a sufficient answer. While supervision may be appropriate, it cannot substitute for a constitutional right to liberty. The Constitution does not permit the Government to imprison a person simply because it is inconvenient to release him. The Court has repeatedly held that “the right to liberty is not to be sacrificed to administrative expediency.” *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 84 (1807).

In sum, the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention violates the Fifth Amendment. The Government has not shown that Zadvydas can be removed in the reasonably foreseeable future. The burden is on the Government to demonstrate that removal is still possible. It has failed to do so. Therefore, Zadvydas must be released.

The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.
