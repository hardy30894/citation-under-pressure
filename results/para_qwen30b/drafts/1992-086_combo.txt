**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel from the use of its school facilities—solely because the proposed activity is religious in nature—violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal is not a neutral regulation of time, place, or manner, but a content-based suppression of speech rooted in religious viewpoint. This Court has long held that when a government creates a forum for public expression, it may not exclude speakers based on the content of their message, particularly when that content is religious. The District’s policy of denying access to all religious groups, even when non-religious groups are permitted to use the facilities, constitutes a violation of the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause and the principle of equal protection under the law.

The foundation of this principle is *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), where the Court declared that “the free exercise of religion is a fundamental right” protected by the First Amendment. The Court emphasized that “the right to speak and to publish is not confined to the discussion of politics, but includes the discussion of religion.” Id. at 303. In that case, the Court struck down a state law that punished religious solicitation, holding that the government may not “suppress the expression of religious beliefs” merely because they are unpopular or controversial. The same principle applies here: the District may not suppress religious speech simply because it is religious.

The Court has further held that when a government opens a forum for public expression, it must do so without discrimination based on viewpoint. In *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516 (1945), the Court invalidated a state law that prohibited labor organizers from speaking in public parks unless they first registered with the state. The Court observed that “the right to speak is not a privilege granted by the state, but a fundamental liberty protected by the Constitution.” Id. at 531. The Court rejected the state’s argument that it could regulate speech based on content, stating that “the state may not, under the guise of regulation, suppress expression because it disapproves of the ideas being expressed.” Id. at 532. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities for a religious film series, while permitting non-religious groups to use the same space, is precisely such a suppression.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252 (1941), where the Court invalidated a state court’s contempt citation against a labor organizer who had spoken at a public meeting. The Court held that “the right to speak in public places is a part of the liberty protected by the First Amendment.” Id. at 263. The Court emphasized that “the state may not regulate speech in a way that discriminates against particular viewpoints.” Id. at 264. The District’s policy—denying access to religious groups while allowing secular ones—constitutes such a viewpoint-based discrimination.

The Court has also recognized that the government may not use its property to favor one religion over another or to exclude religion entirely from public discourse. In *United States v. Ballard*, 322 U.S. 78 (1944), the Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to advocate religious beliefs, even if they are unpopular or controversial.” Id. at 86. The Court rejected the notion that religious speech is inherently less worthy of protection than secular speech. The District’s policy, which treats religious speech as inherently suspect, violates this principle.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that the government may not create a forum for public expression and then exclude certain speakers based on the content of their speech. In *Bridges v. California*, the Court stated that “the state may not, under the guise of regulation, suppress expression because it disapproves of the ideas being expressed.” 314 U.S. at 264. The District’s policy of denying access to religious groups while allowing non-religious groups to use the facilities creates a limited public forum. Once a forum is opened, the government may not exclude speakers based on the content of their message. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. at 303.

The Court has also held that the government may not use its property to suppress religious speech in a way that disadvantages religious speakers. In *United States v. Seeger*, 380 U.S. 163 (1965), the Court held that “the First Amendment protects the right to advocate religious beliefs, even if they are unpopular or controversial.” Id. at 184. The Court emphasized that “the government may not, under the guise of regulation, suppress expression because it disapproves of the ideas being expressed.” Id. at 185. The District’s policy, which treats religious speech as inherently suspect, violates this principle.

The District’s policy, which treats religious speech as inherently suspect, violates this principle.

In sum, the District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities for a religious film series violates the First Amendment. The District has created a limited public forum by allowing non-religious groups to use its facilities. Once a forum is opened, the government may not exclude speakers based on the content of their speech. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. at 303. The District’s policy of denying access to religious groups while allowing non-religious groups to use the facilities constitutes a content-based suppression of speech that is prohibited by the First Amendment. The judgment of the Second Circuit must be reversed.
