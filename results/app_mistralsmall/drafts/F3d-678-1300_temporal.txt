**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Concluding that Leader2Leader® Was On Sale More Than One Year Before the Critical Date

A. The On-Sale Bar Under § 102(b) Requires a Commercial Offer for Sale, Not Merely Public Use

The on-sale bar of 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) invalidates a patent where the invention was "on sale in this country, more than one year prior to the date of the application for patent in the United States." The statute does not define "on sale," but this Court has consistently held that it requires a commercial offer for sale, not merely public use or experimentation. *Mazer v. Stein*, 347 U.S. 201, 213 (1954) ("The patent laws are not violated by the use of an invention in a manner incidental to a proper business or occupation."); *Elizabeth v. Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 137 (1877) ("The use must be public and for profit, not merely experimental.").

Here, the record does not support the conclusion that Leader2Leader® was on sale prior to December 10, 2002. The white paper presented to Wright Patterson Air Force Base in January 2002 did not constitute a commercial offer for sale. It was a proposal for a potential future transaction, not a present sale. *See De Forest Radio Tel. & Tel. Co. v. United States*, 273 U.S. 236, 242 (1927) ("An offer to sell, to be sufficient to defeat a patent, must be an offer of something which is then ready to be delivered."). The paper described the product as "already commercializing" and "operational now," but this amounted to mere advertising, not a binding offer. *See id.* at 243 ("Advertisements and negotiations looking to future sales do not constitute a sale.").

B. Leader2Leader® Was Not Ready for Patenting Prior to the Critical Date

Even if the white paper constituted an offer, it did not pertain to an invention that was ready for patenting. The on-sale bar applies only if the invention was "ready for patenting" at the time of the offer. *Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. at 134 ("The use must be of the completed invention, not of an experimental model."). The record shows that Leader2Leader® was still in development in 2002, with Lamb testifying that the product was completed "probably the 2002ish time frame." J.A. 24829. The '761 patent itself was not filed until December 2003, and the asserted claims were not finalized until issuance in 2006. Thus, the invention was not ready for patenting at the time of the alleged offer.

II. The District Court Erred in Concluding that Leader2Leader® Was Publicly Used Prior to the Critical Date

A. Public Use Requires More Than Mere Accessibility

Public use under § 102(b) requires that the invention be "used in public" in a manner that places it in the public domain. *Egbert v. Lippmann*, 104 U.S. 333, 336 (1881) ("It is not necessary that the use should be open and public in the sense that all the world may see it. It is enough that the use is public in the sense that it is not confined to the inventor and those to whom he has seen fit to disclose the results of his experiments."). Here, the alleged public use of Leader2Leader® was confined to demonstrations and proposals to potential customers, not general public access. *See id.* at 337 ("The use of an invention by the inventor himself, or of any other person under his direction, by way of experiment, and in order to bring the invention to perfection, does not constitute a public use.").

B. The Demonstrations Were Experimental and Not Public

The record shows that Leader2Leader® was demonstrated to potential customers, including Wright Patterson Air Force Base, but these demonstrations were part of Leader’s efforts to refine and commercialize the product. Such demonstrations are not public use if they are experimental. *See Pennock v. Dialogue*, 27 U.S. 1, 24 (1829) ("Experiments made for the purpose of ascertaining the sufficiency of the invention to produce its intended result, and to bring it to perfection, are not public uses."). The fact that Leader was still developing the product in 2002 further supports the conclusion that the demonstrations were experimental, not public.

III. The District Court Erred in Denying Leader’s Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law

A. The Jury’s Verdict Was Not Supported by Substantial Evidence

The jury’s verdict that the asserted claims were invalid under § 102(b) was not supported by substantial evidence. The record contains no evidence of a commercial offer for sale or public use prior to the critical date. The white paper was not an offer for sale, and the demonstrations were experimental. Thus, the verdict was against the clear weight of the evidence. *See Tennant v. Peoria & P.U. Ry. Co.*, 32 U.S. 29, 32 (1833) ("Where the evidence is so overwhelming that it would be the duty of the court to set aside the verdict as against the evidence, it is the duty of the court to do so.").

B. The District Court Should Have Granted Judgment as a Matter of Law

Given the lack of substantial evidence supporting the jury’s verdict, the district court erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law. *See Slocum v. New York Life Ins. Co.*, 228 U.S. 364, 369 (1913) ("If there is no evidence to support a verdict, the court should set it aside and direct a verdict for the other party."). The court should have concluded that Leader2Leader® was neither on sale nor publicly used prior to the critical date, and thus the asserted claims were not invalid under § 102(b).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Leader respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case with instructions to enter judgment in Leader’s favor.
