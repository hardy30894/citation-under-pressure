ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, construing all facts and drawing all reasonable inferences in favor of the non-moving party. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (the evidence of the non-movant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). Summary judgment is appropriate only where there is no genuine issue of material fact. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 247–48. Because the district court resolved disputed factual questions regarding the adequacy of Burnett's notice against him, rather than in his favor, reversal is required.

II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Burnett Failed to Provide Habitat with Adequate Notice Under the FMLA

An employee is not required to invoke the FMLA by name in order to trigger its protections. This Court has repeatedly held that "the employee need not mention the FMLA specifically" when notifying an employer of the need for leave. Aubuchon v. Knauf Fiberglass, GmbH, 359 F.3d 950, 953 (7th Cir. 2004). Rather, the employee's obligation is only to supply information sufficient to show that he may need FMLA leave. Collins v. NTN-Bower Corp., 272 F.3d 1006, 1008 (7th Cir. 2001). The "critical question" is whether the information the employee gave the employer was "reasonably adequate to apprise it" of the employee's request to take leave for a serious health condition. Byrne v. Avon Products, Inc., 328 F.3d 379, 381 (7th Cir. 2003). Whether that information was sufficient is generally a question for the trier of fact where reasonable minds could differ. See Stoops v. One Call Communications, Inc., 141 F.3d 309, 312–13 (7th Cir. 1998).

Burnett's communications to Polo and other Habitat managers, viewed in the light most favorable to him, were more than adequate to create a triable issue on this question. Beginning in October 2003, Burnett told Polo he suffered from a "weak bladder" and was seeking a diagnosis. By December 11, he had informed Polo that testing revealed a high PSA level and elevated cholesterol, that he had upcoming appointments, and that he needed to see a urologist. Five days later, at a meeting attended by multiple Habitat managers and a union representative, Burnett stated that he had been "sick," that he felt he was "getting sick or was sick," and analogized his condition to his brother-in-law's prostate cancer. Weeks later, he informed a supervisor of a scheduled prostate biopsy and provided a document expressly referencing "an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA)" as the indication for the procedure. Polo himself acknowledged the seriousness of the situation by authorizing sick leave for Burnett's medical appointments.

A jury could reasonably conclude from this sequence—rising PSA levels, a referral to a urologist, an analogy to a relative's cancer, and a scheduled biopsy—that Habitat possessed sufficient information to recognize a serious health condition within the meaning of the FMLA. The district court's contrary conclusion improperly resolved a factual dispute that belonged to the jury. See Stoops, 141 F.3d at 312–13.

III. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Burnett Failed to Provide Habitat with Adequate Notice of a Disability Under the ADA

The ADA likewise does not require an employee to recite a diagnosis with medical precision before an employer's obligations are triggered. Once an employer is aware of facts suggesting that an employee's medical condition may qualify as a disability, the employer has a duty to engage in an interactive process to determine what, if any, reasonable accommodation is warranted. Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Community Schools, 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996) (employer must help the employee determine what specific accommodations are necessary, and cannot escape that duty by insisting on a level of formality the employee has not achieved). This Court has recognized that an employee "does not have to speak any magic words" to put the employer on notice; it is enough that the employee communicates a medical condition and desire for accommodation sufficient to alert the employer that action may be needed. Hendricks-Robinson v. Excel Corp., 154 F.3d 685, 692 (7th Cir. 1998); see also Steffes v. Stepan Co., 144 F.3d 1070, 1073–74 (7th Cir. 1998).

Here, Burnett provided Habitat with escalating information: a self-reported bladder condition, an elevated PSA level, a referral to a urologist, an explicit comparison to a relative's prostate cancer, and finally a written medical document identifying prostate cancer screening as the basis for an impending biopsy. Rather than engage Burnett regarding his condition or its implications for his job duties—which included heavy lifting—Habitat's supervisors instead escalated discipline against him in the very same weeks he was disclosing this medical information, culminating in reprimands, a denied transfer, and a suspension. A reasonable jury could find that Habitat had sufficient notice of a potential disability to trigger its interactive-process obligations, and that it failed to satisfy them. See Bultemeyer, 100 F.3d at 1285.

The district court's holding that Burnett's disclosures were legally insufficient as a matter of law improperly substituted its own judgment for that of the jury on a quintessentially factual question. See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for trial on Burnett's FMLA and ADA claims.
