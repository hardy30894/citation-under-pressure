# ARGUMENT

## I. THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN

### A. The Constitution's Due Process Clause Forbids Indefinite Civil Detention

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause "applies to all 'persons' within the United States, including aliens, whether their presence here is lawful, unlawful, temporary, or permanent." *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 693 (2001) (citing *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 210 (1982)). Freedom from physical restraint "has always been at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause from arbitrary governmental action." *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Civil detention, not limited to specially dangerous individuals and potentially permanent, raises serious constitutional concerns. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690–91 (citing *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 368 (1997)).

Petitioner's detention is neither criminal punishment nor bounded civil confinement of the type this Court has sanctioned. Unlike the dangerous sex offenders in *Hendricks*, who were confined pursuant to "proof in a judicial proceeding of a special dangerousness," 533 U.S. at 691, Section 1231(a)(6) applies to a vast population of removable aliens for reasons having nothing to do with future dangerousness—including simple visa violations. The statute contains no special procedural protections, no judicial determination of dangerousness, and, critically, no limit tied to the practical possibility of removal. Where detention's "sole purpose" is to assure presence at a future removal that may never occur, the confinement becomes potentially permanent and punitive in effect. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690 (citing *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987)).

This Court has recognized that "a statute permitting indefinite detention of an alien would raise a serious constitutional problem." *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690. The reason is straightforward: "the sole procedural protections available to the alien are found in administrative proceedings, where the sole issue is whether detention continues to be justified by a strong showing of special circumstances." *Id.* at 692. No such showing is required here.

### B. Under the Canon of Constitutional Avoidance, Section 1231(a)(6) Must Be Construed to Contain an Implicit Reasonable-Time Limitation

"[W]here a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter." *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 689 (quoting *United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909)). This canon has "special force" in the immigration context, where "if one of two possible constructions would raise a multitude of constitutional problems, the other should prevail." *Id.* (quoting *Clark v. Martinez*, — and applying the reasoning of *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 300 (2001)).

Section 1231(a)(6)'s text provides no explicit temporal limit, stating only that a removable alien "may be detained beyond the removal period." 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6). But silence does not mean permission for indefinite detention. As this Court held, the statute, "read in light of the Constitution's demands, limits an alien's post-removal-period detention to a period reasonably necessary to bring about that alien's removal from the United States. It does not permit indefinite detention." *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 689.

This construction respects Congress's stated purpose. Section 1231(a)(6)'s detention authority exists "basic[ally]" to assure "the alien's presence at the moment of removal." *Id.* at 699. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves that purpose and instead becomes the very type of indefinite civil confinement the Constitution forbids. *Id.* at 690.

### C. The Fifth Circuit's "Good Faith Efforts" Standard Improperly Places an Unbearable Burden on the Alien

The court of appeals held that Petitioner's detention was lawful so long as "good faith efforts to effectuate . . . deportation continue" and Petitioner failed to show that deportation would prove "impossible." *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). This standard cannot stand. As this Court has explained, "this standard would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear." *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701.

The proper standard requires only that the alien "provide[] good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future," at which point "the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing." *Id.* at 701. Moreover, "for detention to remain reasonable, as the period of prior post-removal confinement grows, what counts as the 'reasonably foreseeable future' conversely would have to shrink." *Id.*

Applying this standard, Petitioner easily meets his burden. He was held for more than six months beyond the removal period when he filed his petition, and several years beyond it by the time of the district court's decision. Both Germany and Lithuania affirmatively refused to accept him. No other country has agreed to do so. This is not the "problematical, difficult and distant" prospect the Fifth Circuit imagined, 185 F.3d at 294, but a demonstrated, sustained impossibility that satisfies—indeed exceeds—the reasonable-foreseeability standard this Court has since articulated.

### D. Habeas Relief Under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 Remains Available

Nothing in the immigration statutes strips federal courts of habeas jurisdiction to review the legality of post-removal-period detention. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 687 (holding § 2241 habeas jurisdiction survives). The habeas court's task is to "ask whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal," measuring reasonableness "primarily in terms of the statute's basic purpose." *Id.* at 699–700. "[I]f removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute." *Id.* at 699.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that Section 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite post-removal-period detention, and that Petitioner—having demonstrated no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future—is entitled to release under appropriate conditions of supervision.
