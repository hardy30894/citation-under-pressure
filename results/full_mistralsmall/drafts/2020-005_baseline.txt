**ARGUMENT**

**I. Montana’s Exercise of Specific Jurisdiction Over Ford Comports With Due Process**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945); *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). This standard is met where the defendant’s forum conduct is sufficiently connected to the litigation. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of Cal., San Francisco Cty.*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017) (citing *Kadence v. Morgeson*, 319 U.S. 358, 360 (1943)).

Here, Ford’s Montana contacts—its extensive marketing, sales, and service of vehicles and parts within the state—are inextricably linked to the claims at issue. *See Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 788 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where defendant’s forum conduct was “purposefully directed” at the forum and gave rise to the suit). Ford’s nationwide distribution system, including Montana dealerships and parts suppliers, ensured that the very vehicle involved in the accident was available for purchase and service in Montana. *See Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014) (jurisdiction exists where defendant’s forum activities “create a substantial connection” to the litigation). The fact that the vehicle reached Montana through a chain of private sales does not sever this connection. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 775 (1984) (jurisdiction may be grounded in forum activities that “foreseeably” give rise to the suit).

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction requires a causal nexus between its Montana contacts and the accident is foreclosed by precedent. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985) (jurisdiction exists where defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum”). Ford’s systematic exploitation of the Montana market—through advertising, dealerships, and parts distribution—establishes the necessary relationship between its forum conduct and the litigation. *See J. McIntyre Mach., Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 880 (2011) (plurality) (jurisdiction exists where defendant’s forum activities are “purposefully directed” at the state).

**II. Ford’s “No-Cause” Theory Misreads Due Process Jurisprudence**

Ford contends that jurisdiction is improper because none of its Montana contacts “caused” the accident. This argument conflates causation with jurisdiction. *See Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 920 (2011) (jurisdiction is not a “causation” inquiry). The Due Process Clause does not require a direct causal link between forum contacts and the injury; it requires only that the defendant’s forum activities be sufficiently related to the litigation. *See Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 445 (1952) (jurisdiction exists where defendant’s forum activities are “continuous and systematic”).

Ford’s reliance on *BMS* is misplaced. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of Cal., San Francisco Cty.*, 137 S. Ct. at 1780. In *BMS*, the Supreme Court rejected jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum contacts were unrelated to the claims. Here, by contrast, Ford’s Montana activities—its marketing, sales, and service of the very vehicle involved—are directly tied to the litigation. *See Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 141 S. Ct. 1017, 1025 (2021) (noting that Ford’s forum activities “relate to” the claims).

**III. Ford’s “Stream of Commerce” Theory Does Not Limit Jurisdiction**

Ford argues that jurisdiction is improper under *J. McIntyre*, 564 U.S. at 880. But *McIntyre* does not impose a categorical bar on jurisdiction where a defendant’s products reach a forum through the “stream of commerce.” *See id.* at 881 (plurality) (jurisdiction exists where defendant’s forum activities are “purposefully directed” at the state). Ford’s extensive marketing and service network in Montana—including dealerships and parts suppliers—demonstrates that it purposefully availed itself of the Montana market. *See Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 472.

**Conclusion**

Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction over Ford is consistent with due process. Ford’s systematic exploitation of the Montana market—through advertising, dealerships, and parts distribution—establishes the necessary relationship between its forum conduct and the litigation. Ford’s “no-cause” theory misreads due process jurisprudence and is foreclosed by precedent. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
