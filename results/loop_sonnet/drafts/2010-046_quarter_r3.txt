# ARGUMENT

## I. The Population Reduction Order Satisfies the PLRA's Requirements Because Federal Courts Possess Broad Equitable Authority to Remedy Ongoing Constitutional Violations Through Structural Relief.

This Court has long recognized that a court sitting in equity possesses inherent and expansive authority to fashion whatever relief is necessary to eliminate an established constitutional violation. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), equitable jurisdiction has traditionally been characterized by flexibility rather than rigidity, giving the chancellor authority to shape a decree to fit the particular necessities of the case before it. *Id.* at 329-30. That flexible authority extends to structural remedies that reach beyond individual relief when the violation itself is systemic. *See In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593 (1895) (recognizing the broad power of a federal court to enter injunctive relief adequate to secure compliance with federal law). The three-judge court's population order is a direct application of this settled principle: having found, after extensive fact-finding, that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the ongoing Eighth Amendment violations in California's prisons, the court was entitled to order the only remedy the record showed would work.

The PLRA's requirement that relief be narrowly drawn and represent the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), does not displace this traditional equitable framework; it codifies it. Courts have always been required to calibrate relief to the scope of the violation, but calibration does not mean impotence. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court confirmed that a court retains continuing power to supervise and, if necessary, adjust its decree "in adaptation to changed conditions," *id.* at 114, precisely the kind of ongoing, calibrated supervision the three-judge court exercised here through its extensive findings, its consideration of alternatives, and its retention of jurisdiction to modify the order as conditions changed.

## II. This Court's Structural-Remedy Precedents Confirm That District Courts May Order Specific, Quantifiable Relief When Necessary to Eliminate a Constitutional Violation.

The State's suggestion that a population cap is inherently too intrusive a remedy cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated approval of specific, quantifiable remedial orders in analogous contexts involving entrenched, systemic constitutional violations. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court held that a district court could order a local government to levy taxes and reopen schools to remedy segregation, explaining that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out," *id.* at 234, and that a district court has authority to compel local officials to exercise powers of government to eliminate the constitutional violation, even where doing so requires the officials to raise and expend public funds. *Id.* at 233. Similarly, in *Watson v. City of Memphis*, 373 U.S. 526 (1963), this Court rejected the argument that a court-ordered timetable for desegregating public facilities was too intrusive, holding that further delay was intolerable once the constitutional violation was established.

In *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court confirmed that school boards and courts alike bore an "affirmative duty to take whatever steps might be necessary" to eliminate the constitutional violation "root and branch." *Id.* at 437-38. And in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), this Court made clear that where state officials' own conduct has perpetuated a constitutional violation, the remedial obligation falls squarely on the courts to ensure compliance, notwithstanding the burdens such compliance imposes on the State. *Id.* at 4.

These precedents establish that when, as here, a coordinate branch of state government has for years failed to remedy a proven constitutional violation, a federal court does not exceed its authority by ordering specific, quantifiable relief—whether expressed as tax levies, timetables, or, as in this case, a population ceiling calibrated to design capacity. The order below is materially indistinguishable from the remedial decrees this Court has consistently sustained.

## III. The Order Is a Proportionate Response to an Established and Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment's Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment.

The gravity of the underlying violation confirms the propriety of the remedy. The Eighth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), draws its meaning from "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long recognized that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause forbids the infliction of needless suffering upon those in state custody. *See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments involving "torture or a lingering death" or the wanton infliction of pain). A prison system so overcrowded that inmates are deprived of adequate medical and mental health care, as the district court found here, inflicts exactly the kind of needless suffering the Eighth Amendment forbids. Where state officials' own conduct has perpetuated the constitutional violation, *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. at 4, the remedial obligation falls squarely on the courts, and the State cannot now claim that a remedy tailored to the violation it created exceeds the PLRA's bounds.

## CONCLUSION

The population order is narrowly drawn, is the least intrusive means of correcting a violation the State itself allowed to fester, and is fully consistent with this Court's settled equitable and Eighth Amendment jurisprudence. The judgment below should be affirmed.
