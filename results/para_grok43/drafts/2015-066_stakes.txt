The Sixth Amendment does not bar the use of Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court convictions as predicates under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Those convictions were obtained in proceedings that complied with the Indian Civil Rights Act and therefore “do not violate the Sixth Amendment.” Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994) (citing Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979)). Because the convictions are constitutionally valid, their use to establish an element of a subsequent federal offense raises no Sixth Amendment objection.

The Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes.” Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008); see also United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978). Tribal-court proceedings are therefore governed by the ICRA, not by the Sixth Amendment. A conviction obtained in compliance with the ICRA cannot be treated as though it “violate[d] the Sixth Amendment.” Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-47. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary premise—that a tribal conviction is “constitutionally infirm” unless it affords the full panoply of federal constitutional rights—finds no support in this Court’s precedent.

Nichols itself confirms that a prior uncounseled conviction that is valid under Scott may be used in a later proceeding. There the Court held that such a conviction could be used to enhance a sentence, because “an uncounseled conviction valid under Scott may be relied upon to enhance the penalty for a subsequent offense.” 511 U.S. at 746-47. The same logic applies when the prior conviction is an element rather than a sentencing factor. Section 117(a) “penalize[s] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Id. at 747; see also United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). The government must prove the predicates beyond a reasonable doubt, but that burden does not convert a valid prior conviction into an invalid one.

The reliability concerns expressed in Nichols are satisfied here. The tribal convictions were final judgments rendered by courts of competent jurisdiction. Nothing in the Sixth Amendment requires a federal court to disregard those judgments simply because the tribal proceedings did not replicate every federal procedural safeguard. See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (recognizing comity owed tribal-court judgments).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. Ant predates Nichols and rests on the same erroneous assumption—that a conviction valid when rendered becomes constitutionally defective when offered as a predicate. Nichols rejected that assumption. The decision below therefore conflicts with Nichols and with the holdings of the Eighth and Tenth Circuits that have applied Nichols to § 117(a) predicates. See United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592 (8th Cir. 2011); United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993 (10th Cir. 2011).

Because Bryant’s tribal-court convictions were obtained in conformity with applicable law and do not violate the Sixth Amendment, their use as predicates under § 117(a) is consistent with the Constitution. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
