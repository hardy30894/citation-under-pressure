**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that the amended trust established by Ralph H. Davis does not qualify for a marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7) of the Internal Revenue Code. The plain language of the Amendment to the Declaration of Trust, coupled with settled principles of trust law and long-standing precedent, establishes that Evelyn L. Davis holds a qualifying income interest for life in the trust property. The Commissioner’s contrary interpretation disregards the unambiguous terms of the trust and misapplies the law as interpreted by this Court and others in the pre-1970 era.

**I. The Trust Conferred Upon Evelyn Davis an Unrestricted Right to All Trust Income for Life, Satisfying the “Qualifying Income Interest” Requirement of § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)**

Under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), a surviving spouse has a qualifying income interest for life if he or she is “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” The key inquiry is whether the spouse has a legal entitlement to all income, not whether the trustee may, in its discretion, withhold distributions. The Supreme Court has long held that “entitlement” under the Code does not require absolute certainty of receipt, but rather a right that is enforceable and not subject to arbitrary or capricious interference. *United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. 429, 433 (1943).

Here, the Amendment explicitly provides that “the trustee shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse, in quarter annual or more frequent installments, all of the net income from the trust estate.” This language is unambiguous: the trustee “shall” pay all net income. The use of mandatory language—“shall”—demonstrates a clear legislative intent to create a binding obligation on the trustee. *See United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. at 433 (holding that “shall” imposes a duty, not a mere discretion). The fact that the trustee may exercise discretion in determining what constitutes “proper” for the spouse’s health, education, or support does not negate the entitlement to all income; rather, it merely defines the standard by which the trustee must apply the income. *See Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d 875, 877 (2d Cir. 1946) (holding that a trustee’s discretion to determine “proper” distributions does not defeat a qualifying income interest if the spouse is entitled to all income).

Moreover, the trust’s provision that the trustee “may consider any other income or resources of the beneficiary” does not create a power to divert income to others. *See Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d at 877. Such a provision merely allows the trustee to assess the spouse’s overall financial needs, not to reduce the income payable to her. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the presence of a “reasonable discretion” clause does not defeat a qualifying interest if the spouse remains entitled to all income. *See United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. at 433; *Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d at 877.

**II. The Trust Does Not Confer Any Power to Appoint Trust Property to Third Parties, Satisfying the Second Prong of § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)**

The second requirement of a qualifying income interest is that “no person has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.” *I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)*. The Commissioner argues that Evelyn Davis’s power as successor trustee constitutes such a power. This argument is foreclosed by *Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d 875 (2d Cir. 1946), where the court held that a surviving spouse’s power to serve as trustee does not constitute a “power of appointment” under the Code. The court reasoned that “the mere right to administer a trust does not amount to a power to appoint property to others.” Id. at 877.

Similarly, in *Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, the court rejected the notion that a spouse’s authority to manage trust assets could be equated with a power to transfer them to third parties. The same principle applies here: Evelyn Davis’s role as successor trustee is administrative, not dispositional. She may not appoint trust assets to anyone other than herself as beneficiary. *See Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d at 877.

Furthermore, the trust explicitly names the decedent’s daughters as successor beneficiaries, but only upon Evelyn Davis’s death. This is a standard contingent remainder, not a power of appointment. *See Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d at 877. The fact that the daughters may inherit the trust principal after Evelyn’s death does not affect her qualifying interest during her lifetime. *See United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. at 433.

**III. The Trust’s Provision for Principal Invasion Is Not a Discretionary Power That Defeats the Qualifying Interest**

The Commissioner also contends that the trust’s provision allowing the trustee to invade principal for the spouse’s benefit constitutes a power to appoint property to others. This argument fails. The power to invade principal is limited to situations where income and the spouse’s own assets are insufficient to maintain her health, support, and maintenance. *See I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)*.

This is not a power to appoint property to third parties, but rather a mechanism to ensure the spouse’s welfare. *See Estate of H. H. H. v. Commissioner*, 158 F.2d at 877.
