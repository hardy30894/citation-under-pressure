**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS FOR COLLECTION FROM THAT PARTNER.**

The Internal Revenue Code provides that the IRS may collect a tax liability by levy or judicial proceeding only if it acts “within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). An “assessment” is defined as the recording of a taxpayer’s liability, and the IRS’s regulations require that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203; 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The statutory and regulatory framework thus makes clear that an assessment must be made against a specific taxpayer to extend the limitations period for collection from *that* taxpayer.

This Court has long held that an assessment must identify the taxpayer against whom it is made. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership but did not name Coson, a purported partner, in the assessment. The district court held that the lien was invalid because the assessment did not identify Coson as the taxpayer, and this Court affirmed. *Id.* at 458. The Court explained that “[t]his court is of the opinion that such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to §§ 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Id.* (citing *Coson v. United States*, 169 F.Supp. 671, 675-76 (S.D. Cal. 1958)). The Court’s reasoning applies with equal force here: the IRS’s assessment against the Partnership did not identify the individual partners as taxpayers, and thus the assessment did not extend the limitations period for collection from them.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund because he was liable for the partnership’s tax debt under state law, but the court did not hold that the IRS could collect the tax from the partner without an individual assessment. *Young*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054. The court explicitly stated that the IRS would have needed to resort to “court action” or an individual assessment to collect the tax from the partner. *Id.* at 76,050. This Court affirmed, and its opinion did not contradict the district court’s reasoning. *Young*, 283 F.2d at 910. Thus, *Young* does not support the IRS’s position that an assessment against the partnership extends the limitations period for collection from the partners.

**II. CALIFORNIA PARTNERSHIP LAW DOES NOT ALLOW THE IRS TO COLLECT FROM INDIVIDUAL PARTNERS WITHOUT FIRST OBTAINING A JUDGMENT AGAINST THEM.**

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); *Witkin, Summary of California Law* § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001). To collect a partnership debt from a partner’s personal assets, the creditor must first obtain a judgment against the partner. *Id.* The IRS has not obtained such a judgment against the respondents, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Thus, the IRS’s claim in bankruptcy is unenforceable.

The IRS cites cases from other circuits for the proposition that it may collect partnership taxes from individual partners under state partnership law. *See, e.g., United States v. Texas, 507 U.S. 529 (1993); United States v. Dack*, 286 F.3d 222 (5th Cir. 2002). These cases are inapposite because they involve situations where the IRS had already obtained a judgment against the partners or where the partners had been individually assessed. *See Texas*, 507 U.S. at 532; *Dack*, 286 F.3d at 225. Here, by contrast, the IRS has neither assessed the respondents nor obtained a judgment against them. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Galletti* is consistent with these authorities and correctly applies the law to the facts of this case.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s assessment against the Partnership did not extend the limitations period for collection from the individual partners because the partners are separate taxpayers who must be individually assessed. Moreover, California law requires the IRS to obtain a judgment against the partners before collecting from them, which the IRS has failed to do. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS’s claims, and the Ninth Circuit’s decision should be affirmed.
