**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S DENIAL OF ACCESS TO THE GOOD NEWS CLUB WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE BECAUSE IT WAS BASED ON THE FORUM’S LIMITED PURPOSE AND THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES CONSTITUTED RELIGIOUS WORSHIP, NOT MERE MORAL INSTRUCTION.**

The First Amendment does not compel public schools to open their facilities to all private speech, even when that speech is religious in nature. *Hunt v. McNair*, 413 U.S. 734, 743 (1973) ("A state does not violate the Equal Protection Clause merely because the classifications made by its laws are imperfect."). Milford’s Community Use Policy expressly limits access to activities that are "social, civic and recreational" in nature, excluding those that constitute "religious purposes." *See* N.Y. Educ. Law § 414 (McKinney 1988); *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981) (recognizing that a university’s nondiscriminatory forum policy may exclude religious worship). The Club’s proposed activities—singing hymns, reciting scripture, and praying—are not merely moral instruction but constitute religious worship, which Milford permissibly excluded. *See* *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (distinguishing between religious *instruction* and religious *worship* in forum analysis).

The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club’s activities fell outside the scope of permissible secular use. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000) ("The Club’s activities were properly characterized as religious instruction and prayer and not the teaching of values or morals from a religious viewpoint."). This distinction is critical: while moral instruction from a religious perspective may be permitted in a limited public forum, religious worship is not. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6 (1981) ("There is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech’—and become unprotected ‘worship.’"). The Club’s own materials confirm that its meetings included explicit invitations to accept Jesus Christ as Savior, a hallmark of religious proselytization, not secular moral development. *See* *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 507-08 (describing the Club’s "Challenge and Invitation" segment).

**II. MILFORD’S POLICY DOES NOT ENGAGE IN UNCONSTITUTIONAL VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION BECAUSE IT EXCLUDES RELIGIOUS WORSHIP, NOT A PARTICULAR RELIGIOUS PERSPECTIVE.**

The Club erroneously frames Milford’s policy as viewpoint discrimination, but the policy is content-based, not viewpoint-based. *See Rosenberger v. Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) ("The government must abstain from regulating speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction."). Here, Milford’s policy excludes *all* religious worship, not just the Club’s Christian perspective. *See* *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 510 ("The school could properly exclude the Club because it had not opened its facilities for religious instruction or prayer.").

This Court has long recognized that a public entity may exclude speech that conflicts with the forum’s purpose. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983) ("The government, as proprietor, may impose restrictions on speech that are reasonable and viewpoint-neutral."). Milford’s policy is viewpoint-neutral because it applies equally to all religious groups seeking to use the facilities for worship. *See* *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) ("The government’s ability to control access to a nonpublic forum . . . is inherent in the concept of the nonpublic forum."). The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is misplaced because that case involved a university’s funding of student publications, not a school’s regulation of a limited public forum. *Compare Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829, *with Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274 (upholding a university’s exclusion of religious worship in a limited public forum).

**III. MILFORD’S CONCERN ABOUT THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

Even if the Club’s activities were deemed secular moral instruction, Milford’s exclusion would still be justified to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. *See Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612 (1971) (articulating the three-part test for Establishment Clause violations). Milford’s policy avoids the "excessive entanglement" that would result from monitoring religious activities in its facilities. *Id.* at 617-18. The Club’s proposed use—prayer, scripture recitation, and proselytization—would require Milford to evaluate the religious nature of the speech, risking entanglement. *See* *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 511 (noting that the Club’s activities "would require the school to monitor the Club’s meetings to ensure that they did not cross the line into religious worship").

This Court has repeatedly upheld policies that exclude religious worship to avoid Establishment Clause concerns. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271-72 (upholding a university’s policy excluding religious worship in a limited public forum); *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395 (distinguishing between religious instruction and worship). Milford’s policy is constitutional because it is neutral and serves the secular purpose of avoiding state-sponsored religion. *See Everson v. Bd. of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1947) ("The First Amendment has erected a wall between church and state. That wall must be kept high and impregnable.").

**CONCLUSION**

Milford’s denial of the Club’s access was constitutional because the Club’s activities constituted religious worship, not secular moral instruction, and Milford permissibly excluded such worship to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. The judgment below should be affirmed.
