ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act supplies an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority. In Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), the Court held that Congress may delegate authority when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized is directed to conform. The Act meets this standard by requiring the Attorney General to consider the history, pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse together with the risk to public health before finding an imminent hazard. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (1988).

This guidance is comparable to that sustained in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420 (1944), where Congress directed the Price Administrator to fix prices that effectuate the purposes of preventing inflation. It likewise parallels the delegation upheld in Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), which authorized rules for associations in accordance with the standards set forth in the statute.

The decision invalidating a delegation is distinguishable. In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the statute left the President without any declared policy or governing criterion. Here the enumerated factors and the imminent-hazard limitation supply the missing criteria.

Congress may also authorize the Attorney General to subdelegate. In Union Bridge Co. v. United States, 204 U.S. 364, 377 (1907), the Court approved the transfer of functions vested in the Secretary of War to subordinate officers. Earlier, Wayman v. Southard, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825), recognized that Congress may commit the details to those charged with execution. Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904), In re Kollock, 165 U.S. 526, 533 (1897), and United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516 (1911), confirm that administrative officers may promulgate rules carrying criminal consequences when acting within statutory bounds.

The temporary scheduling provisions therefore rest on a valid delegation of authority that the Attorney General lawfully transmitted to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration.
