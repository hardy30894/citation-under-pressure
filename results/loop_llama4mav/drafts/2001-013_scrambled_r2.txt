ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, the Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels is not so comprehensive as to preempt OSHA's jurisdiction over uninspected vessels.

The Coast Guard's authority is limited to specific areas, such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation of engine and fuel tank compartments. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 391a). The courts have long recognized that the Coast Guard's authority is not exclusive, and that other federal agencies may regulate aspects of maritime employment not covered by the Coast Guard. See, e.g., Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31 (1942); NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206 (1940); International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50 (1926).

In fact, the Supreme Court has held that state laws and regulations can coexist with federal maritime law, as long as they do not conflict with it. See, e.g., Just v. Chambers, 312 U.S. 383 (1941); The Lottawanna, 88 U.S. 558 (1874). In Just, the Court held that a state wrongful death statute could be applied to a maritime tort, even though federal maritime law also governed the claim. 312 U.S. at 391-392.

Moreover, the courts have recognized that the term "working conditions" in section 4(b)(1) of the Act refers to specific conditions of employment, rather than the entire range of occupational safety and health issues. See, e.g., Bethlehem Steel Co. v. NLRB, 120 F.2d 641 (D.C. Cir. 1941); Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. Jackson, 185 F.2d 74 (8th Cir. 1950). In Bethlehem Steel, the court held that the NLRB had jurisdiction to regulate certain aspects of an employer's labor practices, even though other federal agencies regulated other aspects of the employer's activities. 120 F.2d at 645-646.

Similarly, in this case, the Coast Guard's regulation of certain aspects of maritime employment does not preempt OSHA's jurisdiction over other aspects of occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels. See, e.g., United States v. Mead, 155 F.2d 508 (5th Cir. 1946); United States v. Haskins, 94 F.2d 675 (9th Cir. 1938); United States v. Hana, 153 F.2d 536 (7th Cir. 1946).

The Court of Appeals' reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Those cases involved inspected vessels, and the courts' holdings were based on the comprehensive regulation of inspected vessels by the Coast Guard. See, e.g., The Pennsylvania, 86 U.S. 125 (1873); The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557 (1870).

In conclusion, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act to issue citations to uninspected vessels. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
