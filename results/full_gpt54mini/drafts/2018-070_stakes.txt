**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule cannot be squared with the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act’s text, structure, or controlling precedent. OCSLA does not ask whether state law “pertains” to the subject matter and is merely not preempted in the ordinary Supremacy Clause sense. It asks a distinct question: whether state law is “applicable” and “not inconsistent with” federal law, and only then does it become “the law of the United States” on covered structures. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court has long treated that language as a gap-filling provision, not a free-standing invitation to borrow any compatible state rule. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-66 (1969); Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473, 480-84 (1981); Secretary of the Interior v. California, 464 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1984); Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 217-19 (1986).

Rodrigue is the starting point. There, the Court explained that OCSLA was enacted “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the Shelf, and it held that state law was incorporated because federal admiralty law did not govern the accident. 395 U.S. at 355, 366. That reasoning presupposes a need to determine what federal law already covers and what remains to be supplied. OCSLA was designed to furnish a governing regime for “federal enclaves” on the Shelf, not to duplicate every state labor regulation merely because it concerns the same topic. Id. at 366.

Tallentire confirms that OCSLA’s incorporation of adjacent state law is limited to the statute’s own terms and purpose. The Court held that Louisiana wrongful-death law applied only because it was adopted as surrogate federal law under § 1333(a)(2)(A), not because state law generally survived absent ordinary preemption. 477 U.S. at 217-19. Tallentire emphasized that OCSLA seeks to create a “complete” federal regime by borrowing state law only where Congress has so provided. Id. at 217-18. The Ninth Circuit’s approach would invert that design by making state law the default unless some federal enactment expressly displaces it.

Gulf Offshore and Secretary of the Interior likewise reject the notion that OCSLA turns on a bare compatibility inquiry untethered to federal coverage. Gulf Offshore held that OCSLA “extends the general maritime law” only where it applies “of its own force,” and otherwise state law is adopted as surrogate federal law under § 1333(a)(2)(A). 453 U.S. at 480-84. Secretary of the Interior likewise described OCSLA as adopting state law only “as surrogate federal law” when the federal regime is deficient. 464 U.S. at 316-17. Those cases are incompatible with a rule that any nonconflicting state law “pertains” to the lawsuit and therefore applies.

The Ninth Circuit also disregarded the statute’s text. Congress did not say that adjacent state law applies whenever it addresses the same subject matter and is not preempted. It said state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). “Applicable” is not surplusage. It limits incorporation to the law that actually fills a federal gap in the governing regime on the Shelf. As this Court has recognized in analogous incorporation statutes, Congress may borrow state law only for the portions of federal law it has left unaddressed. See United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 292-93 (1958); Lewis v. United States, 523 U.S. 155, 164-66 (1998).

Newton’s reliance on the FLSA’s savings clause does not alter the OCSLA analysis. The FLSA preserves more generous state wage laws from ordinary preemption in the employment context, see 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but that clause is not an OCSLA incorporation command. The question here is not whether California law is independently valid on the mainland; it is whether Congress, through OCSLA, made it federal law on fixed platforms on the Shelf. The answer depends on § 1333(a)(2)(A), not on the FLSA’s separate preemption rules. See Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd. v. Newton, 139 S. Ct. 1881, 1891-95 (2019).

That conclusion also accords with federal labor law’s comprehensive scope. The FLSA establishes nationwide minimum wage, overtime, and related rules, and Congress invited states to enact more protective standards for employment generally. See 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207, 218(a). But where Congress has already supplied a federal rule for Shelf workers, OCSLA does not authorize courts to layer on adjacent state rules simply because they are not inconsistent. The Ninth Circuit’s “pertains to the subject matter” test would allow state law to govern whenever federal law does not expressly forbid it, collapsing OCSLA into ordinary conflict preemption and stripping “applicable” of independent meaning.

Because Newton’s claims arise from wage-and-hour disputes already covered by federal law, California’s meal-period and overtime rules do not become surrogate federal law under OCSLA. The judgment should be reversed.
