**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits Montana to exercise specific jurisdiction over Ford because Ford’s continuous and systematic business activities in the State relate to the claims at issue. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945) (jurisdiction exists when a defendant has “certain minimum contacts with [the forum] such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice’”).

Ford’s extensive Montana operations—selling vehicles, parts, and services through authorized dealers—establish the requisite connection. *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (a single contract having “substantial connection” with the forum suffices when the defendant has “purposefully availed” itself of the privilege of conducting activities there). These activities are not isolated; they reflect Ford’s deliberate decision to serve the Montana market on a regular basis. *Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia ex rel. State Corp. Comm’n*, 339 U.S. 643, 647 (1950) (solicitation of business through regular channels justifies jurisdiction).

The claims “relate to” those contacts even though the particular Explorer was not first sold in Montana. *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 445 (1952) (continuous corporate activities in the forum support jurisdiction over claims having a substantial connection to those activities). The vehicle reached Montana through the stream of commerce that Ford itself created and maintained. *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927) (use of state highways by nonresidents engaged in interstate commerce subjects them to jurisdiction for resulting injuries).

Due process requires only that the defendant’s conduct and connection with the forum make it reasonable to require defense of the suit there. *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940) (“[T]he defendant’s presence within the territorial jurisdiction of court was prerequisite to its rendition of a judgment personally binding him.”). Ford’s Montana presence satisfies this standard. *Kane v. New Jersey*, 242 U.S. 160, 167 (1917) (state may condition use of its highways upon submission to jurisdiction for accidents occurring within its borders).

Ford’s purposeful availment distinguishes this case from situations in which jurisdiction rests on “random, fortuitous, or attenuated contacts.” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). Here the contacts are neither random nor attenuated; they are the direct result of Ford’s sustained commercial strategy. *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 720 (1878) (due process is satisfied when the defendant has been brought within the territorial power of the court by its own acts).

Because Ford’s Montana activities relate to the claims and render the exercise of jurisdiction fair, the judgments below should be affirmed.
