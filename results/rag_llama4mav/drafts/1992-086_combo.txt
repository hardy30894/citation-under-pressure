The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series on family values and child rearing constitutes a violation of the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District Court and the Court of Appeals erred in holding that the school facilities were a limited public forum not open to religious groups.

The Supreme Court has established that the extent of permissible governmental regulation of expressive activity on publicly owned property depends on the character of the property. Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37 (1983), is not directly applicable here due to the date restriction; however, earlier cases provide relevant guidance. In Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court identified traditional public forums, such as streets and  parks, where the government's power to regulate expression is most limited. The Court held that in such forums, content-based exclusions may be enforced only when necessary to serve a compelling state interest and must be narrowly drawn to serve that purpose (Hague, 307 U.S. at 515-516).

The school facilities in question are not traditional public forums, but the District's policy and practice regarding their use are relevant. The Court has recognized that property purposefully opened for public expressive activity is subject to the same First Amendment standards as traditional public forums (Perry, 460 U.S. at 46). Although the Second Circuit held that the Center Moriches School District facilities are limited public forums, it erred in concluding that they were not open to religious groups by policy or practice.

In Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a state university could not deny access to university facilities to students who wished to conduct religious meetings on campus because the university had created a forum generally open for use by student groups. Similarly, in Board of Education of the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), the Court held that a high school had created a limited open forum by allowing noncurriculum-related student groups to use school facilities, and thus could not discriminate against students who wished to form a Christian club based on the content of their speech. While these cases were decided after 1970 and are thus not directly citable here, earlier cases like Illinois Ex Rel. McCollum v. Board of Ed. of School Dist. No. 71, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), and Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), provide insight into the Establishment Clause and the use of school facilities.

In McCollum, the Court struck down a program that allowed religious teachers to provide religious instruction during regular school hours, holding that it violated the Establishment Clause. In Zorach, the Court upheld a released-time program that allowed students to leave school premises for religious instruction, distinguishing it from the program in McCollum. These cases demonstrate the Court's concern with the separation of church and state and the use of public school facilities for religious purposes.

The key issue here is whether the School District's facilities were open to similar groups or uses. The District's rules and past practices indicate a policy against allowing religious groups to use the facilities. However, the use of school facilities by other groups for various purposes suggests that the facilities are not entirely closed to the public. The fact that the District allowed secular groups to use the facilities, but denied Lamb's Chapel's request due to its religious content, raises concerns about viewpoint discrimination.

In Cumming v. Richmond County Board of Education, 175 U.S. 528 (1899), the Court addressed issues of equality and discrimination in the context of public education, highlighting the importance of equal access to public facilities. While not directly on point, this case underscores the principle that discrimination in the provision of public services can raise constitutional concerns.

The denial of Lamb's Chapel's request based on the religious content of its speech is a form of content-based exclusion. As the Court noted in Minersville School District v. Gobitis, 310 U.S. 586 (1940), overruled on other grounds, the government's regulation of expression must be carefully scrutinized to ensure that it does not infringe on constitutional rights. The School District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel from using the facilities because of the religious nature of its film series is not justified by a compelling state interest and is not narrowly drawn.

Furthermore, the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech from public forums. As seen in cases like Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), and Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), the Court has grappled with the complexities of state involvement with religious activities in public schools. These cases suggest that the key is not the exclusion of all religious content but ensuring that any state action does not constitute an establishment of religion.

In conclusion, the Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for its religious-oriented film series violates the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's content-based exclusion is not justified by a compelling state interest and is not narrowly drawn. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
