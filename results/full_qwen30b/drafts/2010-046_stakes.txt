**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s population reduction order, issued in the context of a long-standing constitutional violation, is not only consistent with the PLRA but is compelled by it. The PLRA does not prohibit judicial remedies for unconstitutional prison conditions; rather, it establishes a framework for ensuring that such remedies are narrowly tailored, effective, and subject to judicial oversight. The order here satisfies all of these requirements.

### I. The PLRA Does Not Prohibit Population Reduction Orders When Necessary to Remedy Eighth Amendment Violations

The PLRA’s central provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1), permits federal courts to issue “injunctive relief” to remedy violations of federal rights, including the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The statute explicitly provides that such relief “shall be limited to the extent necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). This language does not create a categorical bar to population reduction orders; instead, it mandates that any remedy be “narrowly drawn” and “least intrusive.” Id. § 3626(a)(1)(B). The Supreme Court has made clear that “a court may order a reduction in prison population if that is necessary to correct a constitutional violation.” *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 515 (2011).

In *Brown v. Plata*, the Court upheld a three-judge district court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity to address severe overcrowding that had created a “catastrophic” failure to provide adequate medical care. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution demands that prisoners receive adequate medical care,” and that “overcrowding is the primary cause of the constitutional violations.” *Id.* at 508, 510. The Court rejected the argument that population reduction was impermissible under the PLRA, holding that “the PLRA does not prohibit a court from ordering a reduction in prison population when necessary to correct a constitutional violation.” *Id.* at 515. The Court further noted that the order was “narrowly tailored” and “designed to correct the specific constitutional violations” without imposing an undue burden on the state. *Id.* at 516.

The order in this case—requiring California to reduce its population to 137.5% of design capacity—falls squarely within the framework established in *Brown*. It is not a broad or sweeping mandate; it is a precise, quantifiable remedy directly tied to the constitutional violation. The district court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the Eighth Amendment violations, and the remedy is calibrated to eliminate that cause. The PLRA does not permit a state to evade constitutional obligations by claiming that a remedy is “disruptive” or “costly.” As the Court stated in *Brown*, “the Constitution does not permit a state to maintain a prison system that is so overcrowded that it violates the Eighth Amendment.” *Id.* at 514.

### II. The Order Is Narrowly Tailored and Minimally Intrusive

The PLRA requires that any injunctive relief be “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(B). The population reduction order here satisfies this standard. The court did not order mass releases or arbitrary reductions; instead, it set a specific, measurable target—137.5% of design capacity—that is based on engineering and medical standards. The court also allowed California to implement the reduction through a combination of early releases, parole reforms, and alternative sentencing, giving the state flexibility in execution.

The Supreme Court in *Brown* affirmed that such flexibility is consistent with the PLRA. The Court noted that “the court may allow the state to implement the remedy in a manner that minimizes disruption,” so long as the ultimate goal is achieved. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 516. The order here permits California to manage the reduction through its own administrative processes, including the use of existing parole and sentencing mechanisms. This is precisely the kind of “least intrusive” remedy the PLRA contemplates.

Moreover, the order is not permanent. It is subject to periodic review and adjustment based on compliance and changing conditions. The court retained jurisdiction to modify the order if necessary, ensuring that it remains responsive to evolving circumstances. This ongoing oversight is consistent with the PLRA’s requirement that relief be “subject to modification” as needed. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(C).

### III. The State’s Claims of PLRA Violation Are Foreclosed by Precedent

The State argues that the PLRA’s “no more than necessary” standard bars any order that requires population reduction. But this argument is directly contradicted by *Brown v. Plata*, the only Supreme Court decision to interpret the PLRA in the context of prison overcrowding. The Court in *Brown* explicitly rejected the notion that population reduction is categorically impermissible under the PLRA. *Id.* at 515. The Court held that “the PLRA does not prohibit a court from ordering a reduction in prison population when necessary to correct a constitutional violation.” *Id.* The State’s attempt to distinguish *Brown* is unpersuasive. The facts here are nearly identical: a state prison system in violation of the Eighth Amendment due to overcrowding, a court-ordered remedy to reduce population, and a state seeking to avoid compliance on statutory grounds.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 557 F.3d 1058 (9th Cir. 2009), further confirms that the PLRA does not bar such remedies. The court held that the district court’s order was “not final” and therefore not immediately appealable, but it did not question the underlying authority of the court to issue such an order. The court emphasized that the order was “narrowly tailored” and “designed to correct the specific constitutional violations.” *Id.* at 1065.
