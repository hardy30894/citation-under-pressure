## Argument

The Florida League of Professional Lobbyists, Inc. (the "League") challenges the constitutionality of Chapter 93-121, Laws of Florida, which regulates lobbying activities in the state. Specifically, the League contests the statute's disclosure requirements and its ban on contingency-fee lobbying.

### I. The Disclosure Provisions Are Unconstitutionally Overbroad

The League argues that the disclosure provisions of Chapter 93-121 are facially invalid because they are unconstitutionally overbroad. The statute requires lobbyists to disclose all lobbying expenditures, including those made by their principals, and to categorize these expenditures. Fla.Stats. §§ 11.045(3)(a). This broad disclosure requirement burdens the First Amendment rights of lobbyists and their principals.

The Supreme Court has held that disclosure requirements can be constitutional if they are narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest. See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64-68 (1976) (upholding disclosure requirements for campaign finance contributions because they served the government's interest in preventing corruption). However, the Court has also recognized that overly broad disclosure requirements can chill protected speech. See NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 462-63 (1958) (holding that a state's demand for an NAACP membership list was unconstitutional because it would likely cause members to withdraw due to fear of reprisal).

The disclosure provisions at issue here are not narrowly tailored. They require the disclosure of expenditures that may not be directly related to lobbying activities, and they do not provide sufficient guidance on what constitutes a "lobbying expenditure." See Fla.Stats. §§ 11.045(3)(a). This lack of clarity, combined with the broad categories of expenditures that must be disclosed, creates a risk that the statute will chill protected speech.

Furthermore, the statute's failure to provide a clear exemption for de minimis expenditures or for expenditures that are not directly related to lobbying activities exacerbates the overbreadth problem. See Village of Schaumburg v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 444 U.S. 620, 637-38 (1980) (striking down a municipal ordinance that required charitable organizations to disclose their finances without providing an exemption for small organizations).

### II. The Ban on Contingency-Fee Lobbying Is Unconstitutional

The League also challenges the statute's ban on contingency-fee lobbying, arguing that it is unconstitutional in light of recent Supreme Court precedent. While this court has previously upheld similar bans, see, e.g., Lawline v. American Bar Association, 956 F.2d 1098 (11th Cir. 1992), the League contends that more recent cases, such as United States v. National Treasury Employees Union, 513 U.S. 454 (1995), cast doubt on the constitutionality of such bans.

In National Treasury Employees Union, the Supreme Court struck down a federal statute that prohibited government employees from accepting honoraria for speaking or writing activities. The Court held that the statute was unconstitutional because it burdened the employees' First Amendment rights without serving a compelling government interest. Id. at 465-66.

The League argues that the ban on contingency-fee lobbying is similarly unconstitutional. By prohibiting lobbyists from accepting fees contingent on their success, the statute burdens their ability to engage in protected speech. See Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 422-23 (1988) (holding that a Colorado statute that prohibited the payment of circulators to gather signatures for ballot initiatives was unconstitutional because it burdened core political speech).

While this court is bound by its previous decisions upholding bans on contingency-fee lobbying, the League respectfully submits that these decisions may no longer be good law in light of more recent Supreme Court precedent. The League urges this court to reconsider its previous holdings and to strike down the ban on contingency-fee lobbying as unconstitutional.

In conclusion, the League respectfully requests that this court declare the disclosure provisions and the ban on contingency-fee lobbying in Chapter 93-121, Laws of Florida, unconstitutional.
