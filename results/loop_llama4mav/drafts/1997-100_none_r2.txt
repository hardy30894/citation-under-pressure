The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969), bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, as stated in North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), and rooted in Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163 (1873), and United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896).

The Clause's protections extend beyond traditional trials to proceedings that are "in substance and effect, a trial." In re Nielsen, 131 U.S. 176, 188 (1889). California's statutory scheme for determining prior conviction allegations under Penal Code section 1025 provides a trial-like process, complete with procedural guarantees such as the right to a jury trial and the requirement that the prosecution prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. This trial-like process is akin to the trial on guilt or innocence, and thus triggers double jeopardy protections.

While Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), established that due process does not require a trial-like procedure for sentencing, it does not preclude the application of double jeopardy protections when a state chooses to provide such a process. In fact, the Court has recognized the importance of protecting a defendant's rights during sentencing proceedings involving prior convictions, as seen in Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912), and Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957).

The respondent's reliance on Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), is misplaced. Stroud does not preclude the application of double jeopardy protections in sentencing proceedings where the initial sentence is based on a factual determination, such as the existence of a prior conviction. Instead, the Court's precedents, including Benton, North Carolina v. Pearce, Ex parte Lange, United States v. Ball, Green, Graham, In re Nielsen, and Williams, collectively support the application of double jeopardy protections in this context.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. Therefore, the petitioner is entitled to relief under the Double Jeopardy Clause.
