The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The court held that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence to trigger the statute of limitations. However, this interpretation is not supported by pre-ERISA case law.

In Brown v. Bullock, 294 F.2d 415 (2d Cir. 1961), the court discussed the importance of disclosure in the context of investment management. While not directly addressing the "actual knowledge" requirement, the court's emphasis on the need for clear disclosure suggests that a plaintiff's knowledge of the underlying transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations.

Furthermore, in Winger v. Chicago City Bank & Trust Co., 325 Ill. App. 459, 60 N.E.2d 560 (1945), the court held that a beneficiary's claim against a trustee was barred by the statute of limitations because the beneficiary had knowledge of the trustee's actions, even if they did not know that those actions were improper. This decision supports the idea that knowledge of the transaction alone is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations.

Similarly, in Denike v. Denike, 198 Minn. 401, 270 N.W. 145 (1936), the court held that a plaintiff's claim against a trustee was barred by the statute of limitations because the plaintiff had knowledge of the trustee's actions more than the relevant time period before filing the action. The court did not require the plaintiff to have knowledge of the alleged impropriety of the trustee's actions.

These pre-ERISA cases suggest that the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) is triggered when a plaintiff has knowledge of the underlying transaction, not when they have knowledge of both the transaction and its alleged impropriety. The Ninth Circuit's decision, which requires a plaintiff to have actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence, is inconsistent with this precedent.

Therefore, we urge the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the "actual knowledge" requirement is satisfied when a plaintiff has knowledge of the underlying transaction, regardless of whether they understand its implications or alleged impropriety. This interpretation is consistent with pre-ERISA case law and will provide clarity and consistency in the application of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2).
