## ARGUMENT

### I. Congress’s Delegation of Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Standard.

The Constitution does not forbid Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, provided Congress “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), supplies such a principle.

First, Congress expressly limited the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling power to situations where he finds that the substance “poses an imminent hazard to the public safety.” § 811(h)(1). That phrase is not a blank check; it is a substantive standard that confines executive discretion. *Cf. Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944) (upholding delegation where statute required the Administrator to fix prices that would be “fair and equitable”).

Second, Congress enumerated specific factors the Attorney General must consider before temporarily scheduling a substance: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of that abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. § 811(h)(3); *see also* § 811(c)(4)–(6). These factors channel the Attorney General’s discretion far more narrowly than the open-ended delegations struck down in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). In *Panama Refining*, the statute “declared no policy as to the transportation of the excess production” and “established no criterion to govern the President’s course.” 293 U.S. at 415. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President could approve “codes of fair competition” without any “standard” beyond the “general aim of rehabilitating industry.” 295 U.S. at 531–32. Here, by contrast, Congress specified both the triggering condition (“imminent hazard”) and the criteria for evaluation.

Third, Congress imposed procedural safeguards: the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit his proposal to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be taken into account. § 811(h)(4). The temporary scheduling order lasts at most one year, with a possible six-month extension. § 811(h)(2). These limits prevent the Attorney General from acting arbitrarily or indefinitely. *Cf. Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation where the regulated field had “well-known and generally acceptable standards” and the delegatee’s actions were subject to “judicial review”).

The fact that the delegation authorizes the Attorney General to define criminal conduct does not alter the analysis. In *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506 (1911), the Court upheld a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to make rules governing the use of national forests, the violation of which was a crime. The Court reasoned that Congress had “specified the act to be done” and left to the Secretary only the “details” of implementation. *Id.* at 521. So too here: Congress specified the criteria and the standard; the Attorney General merely applies them to particular substances.

Because § 811(h) provides an intelligible principle, the delegation is constitutional.

### II. The Attorney General Lawfully Subdelegated the Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Drug Enforcement Administration.

The Controlled Substances Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the Act to “any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General exercised that authority by promulgating 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), which assigns to the Administrator of the Drug Enforcement Administration “[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.”

Subdelegation of executive functions is presumptively valid unless Congress expressly forbids it. *See Runkle v. United States*, 122 U.S. 543, 557 (1887) (recognizing that “the head of a department” may “delegate to a subordinate” duties that are “ministerial” in nature). The temporary scheduling power is not a purely legislative act; it is an executive application of the statutory standard. *Cf. Buttfield v. Stranahan*, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904) (upholding delegation to the Secretary of the Treasury to fix tea grades because Congress had “prescribed the rule” and the Secretary merely “ascertain[ed] the facts”). The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator, who is an officer of the Department of Justice, falls squarely within the authority granted by § 871(a).

Moreover, the subdelegation does not violate any express prohibition in the Act. Congress did not require that the Attorney General personally exercise the temporary scheduling power. To the contrary, the Act’s broad delegation language in § 871(a) contemplates that the Attorney General may assign his functions to subordinates. *See* 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) (“The Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.”).

### Conclusion

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision supplies an intelligible principle and adequate procedural safeguards, satisfying the nondelegation doctrine. The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA Administrator was authorized by statute and consistent with long-standing precedent. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
