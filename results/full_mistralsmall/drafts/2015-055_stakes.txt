**ARGUMENT**

I. **Johnson’s Rule Regarding the Residual Clause Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion). The Court has further explained that a rule applies retroactively if it is “a watershed rule of criminal procedure implicating the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding.” *Id.* at 311. The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) is unconstitutionally vague—is precisely such a rule.

In *Johnson*, the Supreme Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA, which defined a “violent felony” to include any offense that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it proscribed and invited arbitrary enforcement. *Id.* at 2556-57. The Court concluded that the residual clause violated the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment because it was impermissibly vague. *Id.* at 2557. This holding was a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure that “alter[ed] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004) (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 301).

The rule announced in *Johnson* is a “watershed” rule that implicates the fundamental fairness of criminal proceedings. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311. As the Court explained in *Johnson*, the residual clause’s vagueness “produce[d] more unpredictability and arbitrariness than the Due Process Clause tolerates.” 135 S. Ct. at 2557. This unpredictability undermined the reliability of sentences imposed under the ACCA, as defendants could not know whether their prior convictions would trigger the enhanced penalty. *Id.* The rule thus “necessarily [cast] a serious retrospective doubt” on the fairness of sentences imposed under the residual clause. *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 390 (1994).

Because *Johnson* announced a new constitutional rule that is retroactive under *Teague*, Welch is entitled to relief. The Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply *Johnson* retroactively to his collateral challenge to his ACCA sentence.

II. **Welch’s Florida Robbery Conviction Does Not Qualify as a “Violent Felony” Under the ACCA**

Even if *Johnson* did not apply retroactively, Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” to include any offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). Florida’s robbery statute, however, criminalizes conduct that does not necessarily involve the use of physical force. *See* Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996).

Under the categorical approach, courts must look to the elements of the prior offense, not the facts underlying the conviction, to determine whether it qualifies as a “violent felony.” *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990). The Florida robbery statute criminalizes the taking of property from a person “by the use of force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1). This language sweeps more broadly than the ACCA’s elements clause, as it includes conduct that does not involve the use of physical force. *See* *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2563 (noting that the residual clause’s breadth was a reason for its unconstitutionality).

Florida courts have recognized that the state’s robbery statute criminalizes conduct that does not involve physical force. For example, in *Goldsmith v. State*, 573 So. 2d 445 (Fla. 2d Dist. Ct. App. 1991), the court held that the “slight force used ... to remove the bill from [the victim’s] hand” was insufficient to constitute robbery. *Id.* at 445. Similarly, in *A.J. v. State*, 561 So. 2d 1198 (Fla. 3d Dist. Ct. App. 1990), the court held that the “degree of force used to [grab a camera hanging from the victim’s shoulder] was insufficient to constitute robbery.” *Id.* at 1198. These decisions demonstrate that Florida’s robbery statute criminalizes conduct that does not involve the use of physical force, and thus does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in concluding that Welch’s Florida robbery conviction qualified as a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The categorical approach requires courts to look to the elements of the prior offense, not the facts underlying the conviction, and Florida’s robbery statute criminalizes conduct that does not involve the use of physical force. *Taylor*, 495 U.S. at 602. The court’s conclusion to the contrary was erroneous and warrants reversal.
