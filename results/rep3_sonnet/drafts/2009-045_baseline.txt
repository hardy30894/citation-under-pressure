# ARGUMENT

## I. This Court's Precedents Establish That the Lodestar May Be Enhanced in Rare and Exceptional Cases Supported by Specific Evidence.

The reasonable fee owed under a federal fee-shifting statute like 42 U.S.C. § 1988 is not invariably capped at the lodestar. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the lodestar figure, while "presumed to be the reasonable fee," may be adjusted upward where the circumstances warrant. *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens Council for Clean Air* ("*Delaware Valley I*"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986). In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), the Court explained that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee," and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435. This principle was not abandoned in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984); it was refined. The *Blum* Court held that quality of representation and results obtained "are presumably fully reflected in the lodestar amount," *id.* at 897, but it did not hold that enhancement is categorically foreclosed. To the contrary, the Court expressly contemplated that an upward adjustment could be proper in "the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged" and produced results that were themselves exceptional. *Id.* at 899.

*Delaware Valley I* confirmed rather than repudiated this framework. Although the Court cautioned that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar," it simultaneously reaffirmed that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." 478 U.S. at 565–66 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). Had the Court intended categorically to eliminate enhancements for performance and results, it would have said so; instead it preserved a narrow but real avenue for district courts to award more than the presumptive lodestar when the record demonstrates that the lodestar undervalues the service actually rendered.

## II. Every Court of Appeals to Consider the Question Agrees That Enhancement Is Permissible in the Rare and Exceptional Case.

The uniform judgment of the courts of appeals confirms this reading. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits have all held that a district court may enhance a lodestar fee for superior performance and exceptional results when the record contains the specific evidentiary support *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* require. *See, e.g.*, *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004) (concluding that *Delaware Valley I* permits enhancements "in rare and exceptional cases"); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999) (affirming enhancement "on account of the exceptional results obtained"); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993) (upward adjustments proper where "supported by specific evidence on record and detailed findings by lower courts"). No circuit has held the opposite. This settled consensus, built directly on this Court's own language in *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*, should not be disturbed.

## III. The District Court's Enhancement Here Rested on Precisely the Kind of Specific Evidence and Detailed Findings This Court's Precedents Require.

This case is the rare and exceptional one that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* anticipated. The district court did not rely on generalized praise. It made detailed, case-specific findings that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." The court drew on its direct observation of counsel's performance over years of complex litigation involving the State's foster-care system, and on the comprehensive injunctive relief actually secured for the class—relief that reformed a statewide system of care for thousands of vulnerable children. These are the kinds of "detailed findings by the lower courts" that *Delaware Valley I* demands, 478 U.S. at 565, and they are precisely the sort of "specific evidence" contemplated in *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, that the hourly rate alone did not capture.

To hold that enhancement can *never* rest on quality of performance and results, no matter how exceptional and no matter how well documented, would read out of *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* the very language those decisions used to preserve the possibility of enhancement in extraordinary circumstances. It would also strip district courts—who alone observe counsel's performance from the filing of the complaint to the final judgment—of the discretion this Court has consistently entrusted to them in fee-shifting cases. See *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
