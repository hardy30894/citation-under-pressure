The district court erred in refusing to resubmit the verdict for clarification before discharging the jury. When a verdict is ambiguous or inconsistent, the trial court must attempt to reconcile it or seek clarification rather than impose its own construction. *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd.*, 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962); *Johnson v. Chicago & Nw. Ry. Co.*, 156 F.2d 617, 619 (8th Cir. 1946). This duty is especially acute where, as here, the forms permitted the jury to reduce Unit’s recovery for substantial performance and then separately award the same sum as a set-off. *See* *Zhang v. Am. Gem Seafoods, Inc.*, 339 F.3d 1020, 1038 (9th Cir. 2003); *Ticor Title Ins. Co. v. Brown*, 511 U.S. 117, 121 (1994) (per curiam).

Unit preserved the issue by objecting before discharge and requesting clarification. *See* *Jarvis v. Commercial Union Assurance Cos.*, 823 F.2d 675, 678-79 (1st Cir. 1987). Even absent a perfect objection, the court’s refusal constituted plain error because it produced a judgment contrary to the jury’s evident intent. *United States v. Simmonds*, 931 F.2d 685, 687-88 (10th Cir. 1991); *Neu v. Grant*, 548 F.2d 281, 287 (10th Cir. 1977).

The court further erred by subtracting the set-off from the damages award rather than entering judgment for the net amount the jury had already calculated. Verdicts must be read to uphold the jury’s findings if reasonably possible. *Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963); *Miller v. Royal Netherlands S.S. Co.*, 508 F.2d 978, 982-83 (5th Cir. 1975). The jury’s use of Form 1 for $70,768.02 after the set-off instruction demonstrates it had already performed the subtraction.

Finally, the instructions themselves were prejudicially confusing because they permitted double reduction of the same damages. A party is entitled to instructions that fairly present its theory without misleading the jury. *Hopper v. Evans*, 456 U.S. 625, 628 (1982); *United States v. Grissom*, 44 F.3d 1507, 1512 (10th Cir. 1995). The district court’s refusal to clarify or correct the resulting judgment requires reversal.
