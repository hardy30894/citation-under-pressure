The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it could review the entire trial record, including matters outside what was presented to the jury, to determine whether the error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. Under plain-error review, an appellate court may correct an error not raised at trial if it is plain, affects substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). 

To determine whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights, the court must consider whether it "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." Id. at 734. In making this determination, the Eleventh Circuit properly looked beyond the evidence presented to the jury to assess the strength of the evidence against Greer. This approach is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), where the Court held that, in assessing prejudice under plain-error review, a court should consider "all the circumstances" of the case, including "the overall strength of the Government's case." Id. at 83, 85.

Greer's argument that the Eleventh Circuit erred by looking outside the trial record is unpersuasive. The Court has never held that an appellate court is limited to the evidence presented to the jury when assessing prejudice under plain-error review. In fact, in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 138 S. Ct. 1897 (2018), the Court considered the defendant's "criminal history" and other facts not presented to the jury in determining whether to exercise its discretion to correct a plain error. Id. at 1908-09.

Furthermore, the Eleventh Circuit's review of the entire record is consistent with the principle that plain-error review is a fact-specific inquiry. See United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 16 (1985) ("[T]he plain-error exception to the contemporaneous-objection rule is to be 'used sparingly, solely in those circumstances in which a miscarriage of justice would otherwise result.'") (quoting United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 163 n.14 (1982)). By considering all relevant circumstances, including Greer's prior convictions and other evidence not presented to the jury, the Eleventh Circuit properly assessed whether the error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
