**ARGUMENT**

The Board’s decision to deny reopening of petitioner’s 2006 application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The plain language of that statute grants jurisdiction over “any final decision of the Board.” The Board’s action here definitively resolved petitioner’s request to reopen, terminated the administrative proceeding, and left no further administrative remedy. This Court should hold that such a decision is reviewable.

**I. The Board’s Denial of Reopening Is a Final Decision Under the Statute**

The Railroad Retirement Act provides that “[a]ny final decision of the Board shall be subject to review” in the courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Board’s decision not to reopen a prior benefits claim is a “final decision” because it conclusively determines the petitioner’s right to have that claim reconsidered. The petitioner has no further administrative avenue to challenge the denial; the Board’s action is the last word on the matter. See *United States v. L.A. Tucker Truck Lines*, 344 U.S. 33, 36 (1952) (“The denial of a petition to reopen a proceeding is, in itself, a final order subject to judicial review.”). This principle applies with equal force to the Railroad Retirement Board. The Second Circuit squarely held that the Board’s refusal to reopen a prior benefits determination is a final decision reviewable under § 355(f). *Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966). That decision, decided before 1970, remains the correct interpretation of the statute.

**II. The Finality of the Board’s Action Is Consistent with General Administrative Law**

The Supreme Court has long held that an agency action is final when it “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and is one “by which rights or obligations have been determined.” *Abbott Labs. v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967). The Board’s decision here satisfies both criteria. The hearing officer explicitly concluded that the 2006 application was “beyond the four-year timeframe for reopening,” and that determination ended the matter. The Board’s action is not tentative or interlocutory; it is a definitive statement that the petitioner’s request is denied. See *Frozen Food Express v. United States*, 351 U.S. 40, 44 (1956) (holding that an agency order that “determines the rights of the parties” is final). The decision also has an immediate and direct effect on the petitioner: he is forever barred from reopening the 2006 claim. See *Columbia Broad. Sys. v. United States*, 316 U.S. 407, 418 (1942) (finality attaches when the agency’s action “presently affects the rights of the parties”).

**III. The Board’s Decision Is Not a Mere Refusal to Reconsider**

The Board may argue that its decision is merely a refusal to reconsider a prior final decision, and thus not itself reviewable. But the Supreme Court rejected that view in *L.A. Tucker Truck Lines*, 344 U.S. at 36, holding that the denial of a petition to reopen is a final order. The Court distinguished between a petition to reopen based on new evidence or changed circumstances—which is a new proceeding—and a motion for reconsideration of a final order. Here, the petitioner sought reopening under the Board’s regulations, which allow reopening for “new and material evidence” or “administrative error.” 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. Such a request initiates a discrete proceeding that results in a separate final decision. See *Rochester Tel. Corp. v. United States*, 307 U.S. 125, 130 (1939) (holding that an agency’s decision on a petition for rehearing is itself a reviewable order if it “finally determines” the rights of the parties). The Board’s denial conclusively adjudicated the petitioner’s right to reopen, and it is therefore subject to review.

**IV. The Board’s Decision Is Subject to Review for Abuse of Discretion**

Even if the Board’s decision is discretionary, that does not immunize it from judicial review. The Railroad Retirement Act provides for review of “any final decision,” and the courts have jurisdiction to determine whether the Board abused its discretion or acted arbitrarily. *Ginsberg v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 200 F.2d 875, 876 (2d Cir. 1953) (holding that the court “ha[s] jurisdiction to review the Board’s decision” for abuse of discretion). The Board’s interpretation of its own regulations is not unreviewable. See *Myers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp.*, 303 U.S. 41, 48 (1938) (noting that courts may review agency action that is “in excess of its authority” or “arbitrary”). The petitioner here should have the opportunity to challenge the Board’s application of the four-year time limit and its conclusion that no new evidence or error existed.

**V. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on Post-1970 Precedent Is Misplaced**

The Fifth Circuit dismissed the petition based on *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), a decision that itself relied on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), and other post-1970 cases. But those cases do not control the interpretation of the Railroad Retirement Act. The Act’s judicial review provision was enacted long before *Califano*, and its language—“any final decision”—is broader than the Social Security Act’s more limited review provision. This Court should look to the pre-1970 authority that directly addresses the Railroad Retirement Act and the general finality doctrine. That authority, including *L.A. Tucker Truck Lines*, *Szostak*, *Abbott Laboratories*, *Frozen Food Express*, *Columbia Broadcasting System*, *Rochester Telephone*, *Myers*, and *Ginsberg*, compels the conclusion that the Board’s decision not to reopen is a final, reviewable order.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Board’s decision denying petitioner’s request to reopen his 2006 benefits claim constitutes a “final decision” under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), and should reverse the judgment of the Fifth Circuit and remand for further proceedings.
