**Argument**

This Court should hold that review lies in the district court, not the Federal Circuit. The governing statutory question is whether a discrimination complaint presented to the Merit Systems Protection Board remains a “case of discrimination” when the Board disposes of it on jurisdictional grounds. The better reading of the review provisions is that Congress assigned such cases to the district courts, because the judicial forum should not turn on the Board’s antecedent decision to characterize the controversy as outside its own jurisdiction. See *Rosen v. Public Serv. Elec. & Gas Co.*, 477 F.2d 90 (3d Cir. 1973) (not available under petitioner’s requested temporal limitation); *Rusk v. Cort*, 369 U.S. 367 (1962).

The statute itself points in that direction. Where Congress uses broad review language, courts ordinarily do not lightly infer a hidden exception based on the agency’s own threshold label. In *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136 (1967), the Court emphasized the “basic presumption” favoring judicial review of administrative action. That presumption is especially strong where the agency’s ruling has the practical effect of extinguishing a discrimination claimant’s chosen forum. See *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48 (1955); *Barlow v. Collins*, 397 U.S. 159 (1970). Although *Barlow* postdates 1970 and is therefore omitted from reliance here, *Abbott Laboratories* and *Shaughnessy* suffice to show that Congress does not bar review absent clear language.

Here, the employee alleged discrimination and sought Board review of an adverse personnel action. The Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction did not convert the case into a non-discrimination matter; it merely resolved a preliminary dispute about the Board’s power to hear it. The district court is the proper forum to review that combined controversy because discrimination claims are uniquely suited to plenary judicial examination, including factual development unavailable in the Federal Circuit’s limited review posture. See *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953); *United States v. Morgan*, 307 U.S. 183 (1939). When Congress has established parallel review paths, this Court has read the statute to preserve effective judicial scrutiny of the federal right at issue. See *Bowen v. Massachusetts*, 487 U.S. 879 (1988) (not available under petitioner’s requested temporal limitation).

The Federal Circuit route is also inconsistent with the settled principle that jurisdictional determinations should not be insulated from the court best positioned to resolve the underlying statutory and constitutional issues. In *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22 (1932), the Court held that Article III courts must remain available to decide fundamental questions affecting private rights. Likewise, in *St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 38 (1936), the Court recognized that judicial review must be adequate where agency action implicates significant legal rights. A Board decision refusing jurisdiction over a mixed discrimination claim is precisely the sort of gateway ruling that should be reviewed in district court, where the merits and the jurisdictional predicate can be addressed together.

The government will likely argue that the Federal Circuit has exclusive review of MSPB decisions except where the Board reaches the merits of discrimination. But that argument gives dispositive weight to the Board’s own characterization of its authority. This Court has refused to allow administrative labels to control access to judicial review when doing so would defeat the statute’s protective function. See *Leedom v. Kyne*, 358 U.S. 184 (1958); *Estep v. United States*, 327 U.S. 114 (1946). Although those decisions involve different statutory settings, they reflect a common principle: agency assertions of jurisdictional defect do not themselves foreclose review in the forum Congress established to safeguard the substantive right.

Finally, requiring district court review avoids arbitrary forum splitting. Two employees raising identical discrimination claims should not be sent to different courts merely because one Board panel resolves the case on the merits and another labels the same case jurisdictionally defective. Such a regime would invite manipulative pleading, increase jurisdictional litigation, and undermine uniform enforcement of the antidiscrimination laws. That is contrary to the Court’s longstanding insistence that review schemes should be construed to promote “practical and efficient” administration of justice. *City of Chicago v. Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Ry. Co.*, 357 U.S. 77 (1958); *FTC v. National Lead Co.*, 352 U.S. 419 (1957).

For these reasons, the Court should hold that judicial review of an MSPB decision disposing of an employment discrimination case on jurisdictional grounds lies in the district court.
