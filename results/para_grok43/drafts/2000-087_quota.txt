The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of removable aliens. As the Court has long recognized, “all persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection” of the Due Process Clause. Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). That protection extends to resident aliens facing removal. Landon v. Plasencia, 459 U.S. 21, 32-33 (1982). Detention beyond the removal period therefore must be justified by a legitimate governmental purpose and adequate procedural safeguards. United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 746 (1987).

Indefinite detention raises grave constitutional concerns. Civil detention may not continue once its justifying purpose—here, effectuating removal—has become unattainable. Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 79 (1992). The government may not detain an alien “solely because of” dangerousness or flight risk when removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 358 (1997). Nor may detention rest on procedures that fail to provide individualized review. Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976).

These concerns compel a narrowing construction. Statutes should be read to avoid serious constitutional questions when fairly possible. Reno v. Flores, 507 U.S. 292, 314 n.9 (1993). Section 1231(a)(6) therefore contains an implicit temporal limitation measured by the period reasonably necessary to secure removal. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 222 (1953) (Jackson, J., dissenting) (emphasizing that detention must remain tied to a pending proceeding). Once removal ceases to be reasonably foreseeable, continued detention exceeds statutory authority.

The government’s contrary reading would permit detention without foreseeable end, a result the Due Process Clause does not tolerate. Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). The statute must therefore be construed to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to bring about the alien’s removal.
