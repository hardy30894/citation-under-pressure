**ARGUMENT**

I. The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) incorporates state law only to fill significant voids or gaps in federal law.

The district court correctly held that California’s wage and hour laws do not apply to Newton’s claims because the Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to govern wage and hour disputes on the Outer Continental Shelf (“OCS”). The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates state law only “to the extent that [it] is applicable and not inconsistent with ... Federal laws and regulations.” The Fifth Circuit has long interpreted this provision to require a “significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may be applied. *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance that federal enclave law is generally displaced by federal law unless state law is necessary to fill a void. *Silas v. Silas*, 175 U.S. 182, 186 (1899) (“[W]here Congress has legislated ... the state law is superseded only so far as the state law is inconsistent with the federal legislation.”).

The Fifth Circuit’s “gap-filling” test has been repeatedly reaffirmed. In *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990), the court explained that state law applies on the OCS only where federal law is “silent or insufficient.” Similarly, in *Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc.*, 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014), the court held that state law governs only where federal law does not address the subject matter. This approach ensures that federal law remains the primary source of law on the OCS, with state law serving as a supplementary remedy only where necessary. *See also Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366 (1969) (state law applies where federal law is “deficient”).

II. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no significant void or gap for state wage and hour laws.

The FLSA is a detailed, comprehensive statute that regulates wages, hours, and overtime for covered employees. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. It includes a savings clause allowing more protective state wage and overtime laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218, but this does not transform state law into a gap-filling mechanism. The Supreme Court has recognized that the FLSA’s savings clause preserves state law where it is more protective, but it does not authorize the application of state law where federal law is otherwise comprehensive. *See Cipriano v. City of Houma*, 395 U.S. 701, 706 (1969) (state law may supplement federal law only where federal law is not comprehensive).

The Department of Labor’s regulations further confirm that the FLSA is a complete regulatory scheme. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 784.10 (exemptions under the FLSA are narrowly construed). The Ninth Circuit has deferred to these regulations in interpreting the FLSA’s scope. *Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004). Because the FLSA addresses wage and hour disputes comprehensively, there is no gap for state law to fill.

III. The OCSLA does not authorize the application of state law merely because it is “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law.

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state law applies whenever it is “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law. The plain text of § 1333(a)(2)(A) incorporates state law only “to the extent that [it] is applicable and not inconsistent with ... Federal laws.” The Fifth Circuit has consistently interpreted this provision to require a gap in federal law before state law may apply. *See PLT Engineering*, 895 F.2d at 1047; *Breton Energy*, 764 F.3d at 398. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Rodrigue* supports this interpretation, as it applied state law only where federal law was deficient. 395 U.S. at 366.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach would render the FLSA’s savings clause superfluous, as it would allow state law to apply even where federal law is comprehensive. This conflicts with the Supreme Court’s admonition that savings clauses do not authorize the application of state law where federal law is otherwise complete. *See Cipriano*, 395 U.S. at 706.

IV. The FLSA’s savings clause does not authorize the application of California’s wage and hour laws.

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, permits state wage and overtime laws that are more protective than federal law. However, this clause does not transform state law into a gap-filling mechanism. The Supreme Court has held that savings clauses preserve state law where it is more protective, but they do not authorize the application of state law where federal law is comprehensive. *See Cipriano*, 395 U.S. at 706. Because the FLSA addresses wage and hour disputes comprehensively, the savings clause does not permit the application of California’s wage and hour laws.

V. Conclusion

The OCSLA incorporates state law only to fill significant voids or gaps in federal law. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to govern wage and hour disputes on the OCS. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to the contrary conflicts with the Fifth Circuit’s well-established precedent and the Supreme Court’s guidance. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that California’s wage and hour laws do not apply to Newton’s claims.
