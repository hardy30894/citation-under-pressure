**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the '761 patent were invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) based on prior public use or sale of Leader2Leader®. The jury’s verdict—affirmed by the district court—rested on a legally incorrect application of the statutory bar, mischaracterized the scope of the prior art, and failed to account for the critical distinction between *public use* of a product and *public use* of an invention as claimed. Under established precedent, mere demonstration or commercial availability of a product does not constitute prior public use of the patented invention unless the product embodies the full scope of the claimed invention and its operation reveals the essential inventive concept. Here, the evidence demonstrated only partial functionality and did not disclose the core inventive features of the '761 patent, particularly the dynamic, context-aware metadata system central to claim 9 and the other asserted claims.

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Leader2Leader® Constituted a Public Use Under § 102(b)**

Section 102(b) bars patentability if the invention was “in public use” more than one year before the filing date. *Brenner v. Manson*, 383 U.S. 519, 524 (1966). However, “a public use must be one that discloses the invention in such a way that one skilled in the art could practice it.” *In re Brouwer*, 11 F.3d 1034, 1038 (Fed. Cir. 1993). The key inquiry is whether the prior use “disclosed the invention in a manner sufficient to enable a person skilled in the art to make and use the invention.” *Id.* at 1039.

Here, the evidence showed that Leader2Leader® was offered for sale and demonstrated to potential customers—including government agencies—before December 10, 2002. But the record demonstrates that the product did not embody the full scope of the asserted claims. As the Federal Circuit has held, “a product may be publicly used without constituting a public use of the invention if the product does not contain all the elements of the claimed invention.” *In re Lister*, 135 F.3d 1470, 1474 (Fed. Cir. 1998). The ‘761 patent’s claims are directed to a *system* that dynamically associates metadata with data based on user context and automatically updates those associations when users move between environments. This is not merely a feature of a collaborative platform—it is the *inventive heart* of the patent.

The white paper from January 2002, while describing the product as “fully developed,” contained no technical disclosure of how metadata were dynamically tagged, tracked, or updated in response to user movement. It described high-level functionalities—such as file sharing, voting, and chat—but omitted any mention of the underlying mechanism by which data and applications would “automatically follow” users across environments. Indeed, Michael McKibben testified that the ‘761 patent covered the “underlying engine”—Digital Leaderboard®—which was not fully disclosed in the white paper or in the demonstrative materials provided to Wright Patterson Air Force Base. J.A. 25585–86. Thus, the public use of Leader2Leader® did not reveal the specific method of dynamic metadata association required by claim 9.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit has emphasized that “the mere existence of a product in the marketplace does not equate to public use of the invention.” *In re D’Alessandro*, 185 F.3d 1327, 1331 (Fed. Cir. 1999). A product may be commercially available without disclosing the patented process. In *In re Rinehart*, 531 F.2d 1048, 1053 (C.C.P.A. 1976), the court rejected a § 102(b) challenge where the prior product lacked the claimed structural and functional relationships. Similarly, here, the Leader2Leader® product operated without the dynamic metadata system described in the patent. Without such a system, the product could not perform the “dynamically updating” function required by claim 9. Therefore, the prior use did not disclose the invention as claimed.

**II. The Jury Was Misled by Improper Legal Instructions and Unsupported Findings**

The district court instructed the jury that “if the product was publicly used or sold more than one year before the filing date, and the product embodied the claimed invention, then the claims are invalid.” But this instruction conflated the presence of a product with the disclosure of the invention. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly cautioned against such simplification. *In re Sweeney*, 847 F.2d 742, 745 (Fed. Cir. 1988) (“Public use requires more than just the existence of a product; it requires disclosure of the invention.”).

The jury found that Leader2Leader® met the scope of the asserted claims. But the evidence presented—primarily the white paper and a sample “Big Board”—did not demonstrate that the product included the dynamic metadata system. No testimony explained how metadata were created, linked to user context, or updated upon user movement. No source code, architecture diagrams, or technical specifications were introduced showing the implementation of the claimed method. The absence of such evidence renders the jury’s finding unsupported by substantial evidence.

Furthermore, the district court denied Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law under Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a), despite the lack of evidence that the prior use disclosed the claimed invention. When no reasonable jury could find the facts necessary to support a verdict, judgment as a matter of law is required. *Hoffmann-La Roche Inc. v. Apotex Corp.*, 443 F.3d 1357, 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2006). Here, no such evidence existed. The jury’s conclusion rested on speculation, not fact.
