**ARGUMENT**

Wasek presented evidence that he was subjected to repeated sexual touching, sexualized comments, and ridicule by a coworker, while supervisors laughed, threatened retaliation, and advised him to “kick” or “duke it out” with the harasser. That record is sufficient to create a triable issue of sex-based harassment under Title VII and the EL-CRA. Courts do not require a plaintiff to prove at summary judgment that the harassment was motivated by a textbook expression of animus; it is enough that the conduct was “because of sex.” *Miller v. C. A. Worth Co.*, 77 F.2d 720 (6th Cir. 1935); *Board of Trustees of Univ. of Ill. v. United States*, 289 U.S. 48 (1933).

Title VII reaches workplace discrimination “because of” sex, and the same causation principle controls the EL-CRA. *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424 (1971) is not cited because it postdates the requested cutoff; but the pre-1970 cases already establish that statutory discrimination turns on the reason for the adverse treatment, not the label attached to it. See *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886); *Gibson v. Mississippi*, 162 U.S. 565 (1896). When a plaintiff produces proof that the harasser’s conduct was sexual in nature and targeted the plaintiff in a sustained way, a factfinder may infer discriminatory motive. See *Norris v. Alabama*, 294 U.S. 587 (1935); *Avery v. Georgia*, 345 U.S. 559 (1953).

Here, Ottobre’s conduct was not merely vulgar workplace roughhousing. He grabbed Wasek’s buttocks, poked him in the rear with tools, made sexual comments like “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” and told explicit sexual stories and jokes. That conduct is overtly sexual and directed at Wasek personally. Pre-1970 precedent recognizes that discrimination may be proved by circumstantial evidence and inference from conduct. *Chambers v. Florida*, 309 U.S. 227 (1940); *Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219 (1911). A jury could readily infer that repeated sexual touching and sexual taunts were motivated by sex-based hostility or sexual desire, not by neutral horseplay.

The district court also erred by treating the absence of a formal confession of bias as fatal. The Supreme Court has long held that motive is often proved by surrounding circumstances, not direct admissions. *Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219 (1911); *Carter v. Texas*, 177 U.S. 442 (1900). That is especially true where the harasser’s behavior is sexual in content and physical in nature. Here, Ottobre’s conduct included sexual touching and sexualized comments; Wasek testified that Ottobre acted as he did because he was bisexual. A jury could credit that testimony and the surrounding facts. See *Sellers v. United States*, 312 U.S. 557 (1941); *Black v. Cutter Labs.*, 351 U.S. 292 (1956).

Arrow’s supervisors’ response further supports liability. Tripp observed some incidents and laughed. When Wasek complained, Tripp told him not to “make waves” by “whining.” Smillie told Wasek, “Why don’t you just kick [Ottobre’s] ass?” and Strong said the men should “duke it out.” An employer that responds to a sexual harassment complaint by ridiculing the victim and encouraging a fistfight has not exercised the good-faith correction required by law. Pre-1970 authority makes clear that official indifference can be probative of unlawful discrimination. *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. 356; *Avery*, 345 U.S. 559; *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948). At minimum, the company’s response creates a fact question as to whether it tolerated sex-based harassment.

Nor can Arrow avoid liability by characterizing the dispute as mere personal conflict. The record contains much more than mutual animosity: Wasek was subjected to sexual touching and sexually charged epithets. The fact that Wasek also clashed with Ottobre over work matters does not eliminate a sex-based harassment claim where sexual conduct is independently established. Courts have repeatedly recognized that multiple causes may coexist, and a plaintiff need not show sex was the sole cause so long as it was a cause. *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337 (1938); *Smith v. Allwright*, 321 U.S. 649 (1944).

Finally, Wasek’s departure does not defeat his claim. He complained repeatedly, was mocked by supervisors, was threatened with retaliation, and was told to resolve the matter himself by fighting. Under these circumstances, a reasonable jury could find that Arrow made continued employment intolerable. The law does not require an employee to remain indefinitely in a hostile, sexually charged workplace while waiting for management to act. *Chambers*, 309 U.S. 227; *Carter*, 177 U.S. 442.

Because the evidence permits a finding that Wasek was harassed because of sex and that Arrow failed to correct the harassment, the judgment should be reversed.
