**ARGUMENT**

**I. Mens Rea That the Weapon Was a Machine Gun**

The government’s evidence failed to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun, and the district court’s instructions permitted conviction without such knowledge. This Court has long recognized that criminal liability requires a guilty mind. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (“The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion.”). Where conduct is not inherently wrongful, due process demands actual knowledge of the facts that make it criminal. *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225, 229 (1957). The mere act of possessing a rifle is innocent; the criminal character depends on the weapon’s automatic-fire capability. The jury should have been instructed that the government must prove Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle could fire automatically. *See Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 104 (1945) (requiring specific intent to protect the innocent). The evidence showed only that she knew it was an AK-47, not that she knew its firing mechanism. That is insufficient. *United States v. Romano*, 382 U.S. 136, 141 (1965) (mere presence or proximity is not enough).

**II. The School-Zone Statute Is Void for Vagueness**

Section 922(q)(2)(A) criminalizes possession of a firearm “in a school zone,” defined as within 1,000 feet of school grounds. The statute fails to specify how the 1,000 feet is to be measured—by straight line, by pedestrian route, or by property boundary. A statute that “forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application violates the first essential of due process of law.” *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). “No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes.” *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). The “standard of certainty is higher in criminal cases.” *Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 515 (1948). Here, a resident of a housing project abutting a school cannot know whether her balcony is 999 or 1,001 feet from the schoolhouse door. The statute provides no measurement method, no fixed reference point, and no guidance for law enforcement. It is therefore void. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952) (statute must give fair warning).

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient on the School-Zone Count**

Even if the statute were not vague, the government failed to prove the distance. The prosecution offered no evidence of the method used to measure the 1,000 feet, no survey, and no testimony establishing the relevant boundary of the school grounds. The jury was left to speculate. A conviction must rest on proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943). The government’s failure to establish the measurement standard requires reversal. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) (verdict must be supported by substantial evidence).

**Conclusion**

The convictions must be reversed. The machine-gun conviction fails for lack of proof of mens rea and for erroneous instructions. The school-zone conviction fails because the statute is unconstitutionally vague and the evidence was insufficient.
