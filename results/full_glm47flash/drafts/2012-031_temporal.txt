**Argument**

**I. The District Court Erred by Certifying the Class Because the Plaintiffs Failed to Prove That the Damages Expert’s Methodology Was Applicable to the Entire Class.**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that the common questions of law and fact which predominate over any questions affecting only individual members are capable of proof on a class-wide basis. *Cox v. American Tobacco Co.*, 327 F.2d 459, 462 (4th Cir. 1964). The district court found that Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, had developed a common methodology to measure damages. However, the expert’s model was tailored to the specific circumstances of the named plaintiffs and did not account for the diversity of the class. The district court failed to ensure that the methodology was applicable to the class as a whole. *Klein v. General Motors Corp.*, 359 F.2d 649, 652 (2d Cir. 1966). The court must be satisfied that the common questions are capable of proof on a class-wide basis, not just that a methodology exists in theory. The district court’s finding was clearly erroneous because it did not verify that the expert’s calculations could be applied to the class members without individualized proof. The court’s reliance on the “rigorous analysis” standard does not excuse this failure. *Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 511 (1947). The court must look at the evidence to determine if the common questions are capable of proof on a class-wide basis. Here, the evidence showed that the expert’s methodology was not applicable to the class as a whole.
