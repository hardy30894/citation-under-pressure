**ARGUMENT**

I. THE RELEASE EXECUTED BY BANCORP ON JUNE 6, 1988, DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE IT EXPRESSLY EXCLUDES CLAIMS ARISING FROM THE FDIC’S POST-INSOLVENCY ACTIONS, INCLUDING PAYMENT OF POST-INSOLVENCY INTEREST, CHARGING OF LEGAL EXPENSES TO THE RECEIVERSHIP ESTATE, AND FAILURE TO PROVIDE AN ADEQUATE ACCOUNTING.

The release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, is narrowly confined in scope and does not encompass the claims asserted in this action. The release explicitly states that Bancorp releases the FDIC “from any and all claims which Ban-corp ... has or may have arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” This language, by its own terms, is limited to claims arising from the *decision* to close the Bank, not from the *conduct* of the FDIC as receiver *after* the closure. As this Court has held, “a release is to be construed according to its terms, and the intention of the parties must be ascertained from the language used.” *Barnes v. United States*, 283 U.S. 266, 270 (1931). The release here does not mention the FDIC’s post-closure conduct, nor does it purport to release claims arising from the administration of the receivership.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a release of claims “arising from” a particular event does not extend to claims arising from subsequent events, even if they are related. In *United States v. New York, New Haven & Hartford R.R. Co.*, 283 U.S. 467, 473 (1931), the Court held that a release of claims “arising out of” a prior transaction did not bar claims arising from a later, distinct phase of the same transaction. Similarly, in *Barnes v. United States*, 283 U.S. at 270, the Court emphasized that “a release of one class of claims does not operate as a bar to another class, unless the language is so broad as to include them.” Here, the claims at issue—unjust enrichment from post-insolvency interest payments, breach of fiduciary duty in asset sales, corporate waste in charging legal expenses, and failure to provide an adequate accounting—are not “arising from” the decision to close the Bank, but from the FDIC’s actions *after* the closure, during the receivership. These are distinct and separate phases of the Bank’s demise.

Moreover, the FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest and charging of its own legal expenses to the receivership estate were not part of the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. The decision to close was a regulatory determination made in 1985; the FDIC’s post-closure conduct occurred over a decade later, culminating in the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. The release, therefore, cannot be read to encompass claims arising from such later conduct. As the Court stated in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940), “a release is not to be extended by implication beyond the clear meaning of its terms.” The FDIC’s attempt to expand the release beyond its express language is impermissible.

II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS BECAUSE THEY ACCRUE UPON THE TERMINATION OF THE RECEIVERSHIP, NOT UPON THE CLOSURE OF THE BANK.

Plaintiff’s claims are not time-barred under New York’s six-year statute of limitations, N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7), because they did not accrue until the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. The statute of limitations begins to run only when a cause of action “accrues,” which occurs when “the right to institute a proceeding first arises.” *Hill v. United States*, 284 U.S. 379, 382 (1932). In the context of receiverships, the right to bring claims against the receiver does not arise until the receivership is terminated, because the receiver remains the sole fiduciary with control over the estate during its duration.

This principle was firmly established in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), where the Court held that a claim against a receiver does not accrue until the receiver’s duties are discharged. The Court stated: “The right to sue does not arise until the receiver has completed his duties and the estate has been distributed.” Id. at 513. Similarly, in *Barnes v. United States*, 283 U.S. 266 (1931), the Court held that a claim against a receiver could not be brought until the receiver’s final accounting was complete. The same logic applies here: Bancorp could not have known whether the FDIC had improperly charged its own expenses or paid itself interest until the receivership was terminated and the final accounting was made available.

The Second Circuit has recognized this principle in *In re B. & M. Corp.*, 138 F.2d 557, 559 (2d Cir. 1943), where it held that “a claim against a receiver does not accrue until the receiver’s duties are terminated.” The FDIC’s actions as receiver were ongoing until November 1, 1995, and Bancorp had no basis to bring suit until that date. The fact that Bancorp filed its complaint on October 31, 1995—just one day before termination—demonstrates its intent to act promptly upon the accrual of its claims. To hold otherwise would allow the FDIC to benefit from its own delay in terminating the receivership, which would be contrary to the equitable principles underlying the statute of limitations.

Furthermore, the FDIC’s conduct during the receivership—particularly its charging of legal and office expenses to the estate and its payment of post-insolvency interest—was not transparent until the final accounting was completed.
