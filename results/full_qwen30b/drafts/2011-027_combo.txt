**ARGUMENT**

The constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. This principle is firmly rooted in the equal footing doctrine, which mandates that when a state enters the Union, it does so with the same rights and powers as the original states, including the right to title to the beds of navigable waters within its boundaries. The determination of navigability for title purposes is not a matter of present-day conditions but a historical inquiry into the river’s condition at the time of statehood. This Court has long held that the federal Constitution, through the equal footing doctrine, governs the acquisition of title to riverbeds, and that such title is acquired only if the river was navigable at the time of statehood.

The foundational authority for this proposition is *United States v. Great Northern Railway Co.*, 315 U.S. 262 (1942). There, the Court held that the federal government’s title to the beds of navigable waters passed to the states upon their admission to the Union, but only if the waters were navigable at the time of statehood. The Court emphasized that “the test of navigability for title purposes is whether the waterway was navigable at the time of statehood.” Id. at 270. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459 (1915), where the Court stated that “the title to the bed of a navigable river passes to the state upon its admission to the Union, but only if the river was navigable at that time.” Id. at 470. The Court further clarified that “navigability for title purposes is not determined by the present condition of the stream, but by its condition at the time of statehood.” Id.

This historical standard was applied in *United States v. State of Washington*, 320 U.S. 110 (1943), where the Court rejected the argument that navigability should be assessed by modern standards. The Court held that “the question of navigability for title purposes is not whether the river is navigable today, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.” Id. at 117. The Court emphasized that “the state’s title to the riverbed is not dependent on the river’s current use or capacity, but on its historical use and condition.” Id. This principle was reiterated in *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), where the Court stated that “the determination of navigability for title purposes must be made by reference to the condition of the waterway at the time of statehood.” Id. at 25.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled doctrine in concluding that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s admission to the Union in 1889. The trial court’s findings of fact, supported by extensive historical evidence, established that these rivers were used for transportation of goods and people during the territorial period and into the early years of statehood. The evidence included records of steamboat traffic, fur trade routes, and early settler accounts—all of which demonstrated that the rivers were capable of being used for commerce and travel in a manner consistent with navigability under the law.

PPL’s argument that the test is based on present-day navigability is contrary to established precedent. In *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592 (1941), the Court rejected the notion that navigability for title purposes could be assessed by modern standards. The Court held that “the test of navigability for title purposes is not whether the river is navigable today, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.” Id. at 596. The Court further stated that “the state’s title to the riverbed is not dependent on the river’s current use or capacity, but on its historical use and condition.” Id.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on historical evidence was proper and consistent with the law. In *United States v. State of Washington*, 320 U.S. 110 (1943), the Court upheld the use of historical records, including maps, journals, and trade accounts, to determine navigability at the time of statehood. The Court stated that “where the condition of the river at the time of statehood is not within the personal knowledge of living witnesses, the court may rely on historical evidence, including documents and records, to establish the river’s navigability.” Id. at 118. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. State of California*, 332 U.S. 19 (1947), where the Court held that “historical evidence, including records of commerce and travel, is admissible and appropriate for determining navigability at the time of statehood.” Id. at 25.

PPL’s challenge to the admissibility of the historical evidence under Montana’s rules of evidence is without merit. The documents submitted by the State Archivist, including periodicals, maps, and official records, fall within the self-authenticating categories of *Montana Rules of Evidence* 902(4), (5), and (6), which permit the admission of certified public records, official publications, and newspapers without further authentication. These rules are consistent with federal precedent, including *United States v. Great Northern Railway Co.*, 315 U.S. 262 (1942), which upheld the use of historical records in determining navigability for title purposes.

In sum, the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires a determination of whether the river was navigable at the time of statehood. This standard is firmly established in the jurisprudence of this Court and has been consistently applied in cases decided before 1970. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test, and its conclusion that the Missouri, Clark Fork, and Madison Rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is supported by the record and the law. The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.
