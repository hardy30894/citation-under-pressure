**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The district court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal, in violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2), was harmless because petitioner knew of that right and deliberately chose not to appeal. Habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 requires a showing of actual prejudice, and petitioner cannot make that showing.

**I. The Rule 32 Violation Is Subject to Harmless-Error Review**

Rule 32(a)(2) is a procedural rule, not a constitutional mandate. Nonconstitutional errors are harmless unless they “affect substantial rights.” *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946). Even constitutional errors may be harmless if the government proves beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the outcome. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967). A fortiori, a mere procedural omission is subject to harmless-error review.

This Court has already applied that principle to Rule 32. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), the Court held that a trial court’s failure to ask a defendant whether he wished to speak before sentencing—a violation of Rule 32(a)—was not “of itself a fundamental defect” and could not be raised for the first time in a § 2255 motion. The same reasoning governs here. The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is no more fundamental than the failure to invite allocution. It is a procedural irregularity, not a defect that undermines the integrity of the proceeding.

**II. Petitioner Cannot Show the Prejudice Required for Habeas Relief**

Section 2255 is not a substitute for direct appeal. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954). A prisoner seeking collateral relief must demonstrate that the alleged error deprived him of a right guaranteed by federal law. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963). Petitioner cannot do so.

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that petitioner knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is amply supported by the record. Because petitioner had actual knowledge, the court’s failure to repeat the advice did not deprive him of any opportunity. He could have appealed; he chose not to. That choice was a knowing and voluntary waiver. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right”).

Petitioner’s claim is also barred by his failure to pursue a direct appeal. A defendant who knowingly declines to appeal cannot later use a procedural omission to obtain collateral relief. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (a motion to vacate cannot be used to review questions that could have been raised on appeal). And a constitutional right may be forfeited by the failure to assert it at the proper time. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944). Here, petitioner had the right to appeal, knew of it, and forfeited it by his own choice.

**III. The Purpose of Rule 32 Was Satisfied**

Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant is informed of his right to appeal. Its purpose is to protect the defendant’s ability to make a knowing decision about whether to appeal. When the defendant already knows of that right, the rule’s purpose is fully served. Procedural rules are “devised to promote the ends of justice, not to defeat them.” *United States v. Hark*, 320 U.S. 531, 536 (1944). To vacate a sentence because a court failed to recite advice the defendant already possessed would exalt form over substance and defeat the rule’s purpose.

Petitioner admitted that he knew he could appeal. The district court credited that testimony. The omission therefore had no effect on petitioner’s decision. It did not cause him to miss an appeal he would otherwise have taken. It did not prejudice him in any way.

**IV. The Courts Below Correctly Applied Harmless-Error Review**

The court of appeals held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review and that the error was harmless because petitioner knew of his right. That conclusion is consistent with *Kotteakos*, *Chapman*, and *Hill*. The district court’s factual finding of actual knowledge is not clearly erroneous and is dispositive. Because petitioner suffered no prejudice, there was no “fundamental defect” warranting collateral relief. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any violation of Rule 32, regardless of the defendant’s knowledge, requires vacatur of a sentence. That rule would turn a procedural safeguard into a technical trap, rewarding defendants who knowingly decline to appeal and later seek a second chance. It would also ignore the settled principle that habeas relief is reserved for actual prejudice, not formal error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
