**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims That Merely Require Application of Federal Patent Law.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim that requires a court to apply federal patent law necessarily “arises under” federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. The Supreme Court has long held that federal question jurisdiction under § 1338(a) extends only to cases “in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). The mere presence of a federal issue is insufficient. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313 (2005).

**A. The Federal Patent Issue Is Not “Substantial” in the Context of This State Malpractice Claim.**

Under *Grable*, a federal issue is “substantial” only if its resolution is important to the federal system as a whole, not merely to the parties. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313. Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies—is case-specific and fact-bound. It does not implicate the validity or scope of any patent; it merely asks whether Minton’s attorneys should have raised a particular defense. As the dissent below correctly observed, “the federal issue is not substantial because it is not important to the federal system as a whole.” *Gunn v. Minton*, No. 10-0123, slip op. at 18 (Tex. 2012) (Willett, J., dissenting). The resolution of this issue will not affect the rights of any patent holder or alter the uniformity of patent law. *See Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 388 (5th Cir. 2008) (holding that a state malpractice claim involving patent law did not raise a substantial federal issue because “the outcome of this case will not affect the validity of any patent”).

**B. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities.**

The *Grable* test requires courts to consider whether federal jurisdiction would “disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Congress has not indicated that state legal malpractice claims should be swept into federal court merely because they touch on patent law. To the contrary, states have a strong interest in regulating attorney conduct and adjudicating malpractice claims. *See New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 343 (Neb. 2005) (holding that “when patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action, federal courts do not have jurisdiction”). Allowing federal jurisdiction here would open the floodgates to countless state malpractice cases, overwhelming federal dockets and undermining state sovereignty. *See E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516, 525 (Cal. Ct. App. 2010) (finding no federal jurisdiction because “to the extent that the subject matter of patent law is relevant to the determination of the professional negligence claim, it does not present a question of patent law that is substantial”).

**C. The Federal Circuit’s Contrary Precedent Is Inconsistent with Supreme Court Authority.**

The Federal Circuit’s decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), are not binding on this Court and are inconsistent with *Grable*. Those decisions failed to conduct the federalism analysis required by the Supreme Court. *See Davis v. Brouse McDowell, L.P.A.*, 596 F.3d 1355, 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (applying *Air Measurement* without citing *Grable*). This Court should not follow precedent that ignores the Supreme Court’s clear directive to balance federal and state interests.

**II. The State Court Properly Exercised Jurisdiction Over This Malpractice Claim.**

Because the federal patent issue here is neither substantial nor necessary to the federal system, and because exercising federal jurisdiction would disrupt the balance of state and federal responsibilities, the state courts have concurrent jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim. The Supreme Court of Texas erred in dismissing the case for lack of jurisdiction. This Court should reverse and remand for consideration of the merits.
