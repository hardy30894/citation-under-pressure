## ARGUMENT

The statute governing post-removal-period detention, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The text, structure, and constitutional backdrop compel a limiting construction: detention is permitted only for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal.

**I. The Plain Language and Structure of § 1231(a)(6) Do Not Clearly Authorize Indefinite Detention.**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain categories of aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period.” It does not specify any temporal limit. But silence on duration does not imply infinite authority. The statute is part of a carefully calibrated scheme. Section 1231(a)(1) mandates detention during the 90-day removal period. Section 1231(a)(3) provides for supervised release if removal is not accomplished within that period. The structure envisions removal as the norm; detention beyond 90 days is an exception, not an open-ended grant. See *Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 380 (2005) (reading § 1231(a)(6) to contain a reasonable-time limitation to avoid constitutional questions). When Congress intended to authorize indefinite detention, it did so explicitly. Compare 8 U.S.C. § 1226a(a) (detention of suspected terrorists with periodic review), and *Demore v. Kim*, 538 U.S. 510, 531 (2003) (upholding mandatory detention during removal proceedings because it is “limited” in duration). The absence of similar provisions in § 1231(a)(6) is telling.

**II. Reading § 1231(a)(6) to Permit Indefinite Detention Would Raise Serious Constitutional Concerns.**

This Court has long recognized that “freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that [the Due Process] Clause protects.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). For resident aliens who have been admitted to the United States, “[t]heir presence in the United States is not the type of ‘temporary, narrow, or minimal’ invasion of property rights that the Court has held may be allowed without due process.” *Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982). An alien who has “gained admission to the United States” is entitled to due process protections, including the right not to be subjected to indefinite civil detention. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 693.

Indefinite detention of a resident alien after a final removal order—when removal is not reasonably foreseeable—serves no legitimate regulatory purpose. It is not punishment, because the removal order is already final. *Id.* at 690. It is not prevention of flight, because there is no prospect of removal. *Id.* at 699. And it is not a valid exercise of the immigration power to detain at the border, because the resident alien has already entered. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 235 (1896) (holding that the immigration power does not permit “imprisonment at hard labor” without judicial trial). Continued detention when no country will accept the alien “would be potentially permanent,” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 691, and thus unconstitutional under *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (holding that civil detention must be linked to a legitimate purpose and cannot be indefinite).

**III. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires a Limiting Construction.**

Where a statute is ambiguous and a construction that raises serious constitutional questions is plausible, this Court adopts the construction that avoids those questions. *United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909). The canon is “a tool for choosing between competing plausible interpretations of a statutory text.” *Clark*, 543 U.S. at 381. Here, the statute is ambiguous because it does not specify a maximum duration. The government’s interpretation—that detention may continue indefinitely as long as good-faith removal efforts persist—is not the only plausible reading. The Fifth Circuit erred by requiring the alien to show that removal is “impossible,” a standard that “demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 705. A more natural reading is that detention is authorized only for a period “reasonably necessary to bring about the alien’s removal from the United States.” *Id.* at 699.

**IV. The Proper Standard Is Reasonable Foreseeability of Removal.**

The statute’s basic purpose is “assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” *Id.* at 699. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention ceases to serve that purpose and becomes arbitrary. The habeas court must determine whether “removal is reasonably foreseeable” and whether the detention has exceeded “a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” *Id.* at 699-700. To provide guidance, a presumptively reasonable period of six months is appropriate; after that, the alien may show that there is “no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future,” and the government must rebut with evidence. *Id.* at 701.

This approach respects Congress’s intent while avoiding constitutional infirmity. It does not forbid detention of dangerous aliens; it simply requires that detention be tied to a realistic prospect of removal. The government retains authority to supervise released aliens under § 1231(a)(3) and to return them to custody if removal becomes foreseeable or if release conditions are violated. *Id.* at 700.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Section 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention. The case should be remanded with instructions to grant the writ unless the government demonstrates that removal is reasonably foreseeable.
