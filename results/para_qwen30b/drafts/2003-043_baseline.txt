**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government must assess each individual partner for a tax debt in order to extend the statute of limitations against them—requires a straightforward application of statutory text, legislative intent, and binding precedent. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS may not collect unpaid partnership taxes from individual partners without first making individual assessments or obtaining judgments against them. Because no such assessments were made within the three-year window prescribed by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the statute of limitations has run as to each partner. The IRS’s attempt to circumvent this requirement by relying on an assessment against the partnership alone fails under both statutory construction and settled case law.

**I. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS ON ASSESSMENT APPLIES INDIVIDUALLY TO EACH TAXPAYER, INCLUDING PARTNERS**

Under the Internal Revenue Code, the government may collect a tax deficiency only if it has assessed the tax against the taxpayer within three years after the return was filed. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). This limitation is not merely procedural—it is jurisdictional. *United States v. D’Amico*, 347 F.3d 1089, 1092 (9th Cir. 2003) (“The three-year period for assessment is strictly construed and cannot be extended by implication.”). The statute makes clear that the assessment must be made “against” the taxpayer. Id. The term “taxpayer,” as defined in § 7701(a)(14), includes “an individual,” which plainly encompasses a partner. Thus, each partner is a distinct taxpayer entitled to the protections of § 6501(a).

The IRS argues that because the partnership is a “taxpayer,” an assessment against it suffices to trigger collection authority against its members. But this argument misreads the statute. Section 6502(a) permits collection *only* where “the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) (emphasis added). The phrase “properly applicable” refers to the time limit governing the specific taxpayer being assessed. Since the IRS never assessed the Gallettis or the Briguglios individually, the three-year period for assessing them had expired. The assessment against the partnership did not alter that fact. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed, “the assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership, but it left unaltered the limitations period applicable to Debtors.” *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1155–56.

This interpretation is consistent with the regulatory framework. Treasury Regulation § 301.6203-1 requires that an assessment “provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. An assessment naming only the partnership fails to identify the individual partners as taxpayers. Without such identification, the assessment does not satisfy the statutory requirement. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (“[T]he assessment at issue names only the partnership and ‘an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers.’”) (quoting district court opinion). The failure to identify a taxpayer renders the assessment invalid as to that person. *Id.* at 458.

**II. CASE LAW CONFIRMS THAT INDIVIDUAL ASSESSMENTS ARE REQUIRED FOR COLLECTION FROM PARTNERS**

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the court upheld the denial of a refund to a partner who had paid his share of the partnership’s tax liability. The court acknowledged that the IRS could not have collected the tax from the partner absent an individual assessment or judgment. *Young*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050. The court stated: “It is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment in order to create liability; their liability arises as a matter of state law.” *Id.* at 76,054. But this language applies only to the *existence* of liability—not to the *procedural means* of collection. The court explicitly recognized that “the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals’ names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” *Id.* (emphasis added). Thus, *Young* confirms that while state law may impose joint and several liability, the federal government still must follow federal procedures to enforce that liability.

Similarly, in *Coson*, we affirmed a district court ruling that the IRS could not enforce a lien against a partner’s property because there had been no individual assessment. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458. The court emphasized that “a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” *Id.* at 458. To reach a partner’s assets, the government must obtain a separate judgment against the partner. *Id.* at 458–59. The IRS’s failure to do so here bars enforcement.

The IRS also cites *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), but that case is distinguishable. There, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *Wright*, 868 F.Supp. 1070, 1071 & n. 1 (S.D.Ind. 1994). Because assessments were made against the partners, the statute of limitations was tolled for them. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562. The Seventh Circuit did not address whether an assessment against the partnership alone would suffice. Indeed, the court noted that the issue before it was whether the statute of limitations should be tolled during bankruptcy proceedings—a question not raised here. *Id.* at 562–63. Thus, *Wright* provides no support for the IRS’s position.
