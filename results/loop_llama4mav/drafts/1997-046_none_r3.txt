The respondent, Eugene Woodard, has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is inapplicable to clemency proceedings, as they are not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. The Supreme Court has long recognized that clemency is an act of executive discretion, as seen in Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925), where the Court characterized clemency as an act of "mercy." This characterization highlights the fundamentally non-judicial nature of clemency decisions.

The Court has consistently held that clemency decisions are not subject to judicial review. In United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871), the Court stated that the President's power to grant reprieves and pardons is "unlimited" and "cannot be controlled or modified by Congress." Similarly, in Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 603-04 (1896), the Court described a pardon as a "private" act of the executive, not subject to due process requirements. The distinction between the judicial process and clemency proceedings is further underscored by United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. 150, 161 (1833), which characterized a pardon as "an act of grace" separate from the judicial process.

The creation of a clemency procedure by the state does not create a protected liberty interest. As established in Murray v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. 272, 277 (1856), a statutory procedure does not necessarily create a constitutional right. Furthermore, Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. 307, 311 (1856), emphasized that the executive's power to grant clemency is a constitutional duty not subject to judicial oversight. These precedents demonstrate that clemency proceedings are not integral to the adjudicatory process and do not implicate due process concerns.

The respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is also meritless. The Ohio clemency procedure does not compel the respondent to waive his Fifth Amendment rights, as participation in the clemency interview is voluntary. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 111-12 (1908), held that the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated when a person is not compelled to testify against themselves. The respondent remains free to assert his Fifth Amendment privilege if he chooses to participate in the interview.

The Court of Appeals' remand for further proceedings is premature and unnecessary. Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496, 500-01 (1941), established that a federal court should not intervene in a state proceeding unless there is a clear constitutional violation. In this case, there is no such violation, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims was proper.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims should be reinstated.
