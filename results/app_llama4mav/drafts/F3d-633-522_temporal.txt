The district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL Express Inc. (USA) and Deutsche Post AG, as the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 and signed by the appellant class members did not waive their WARN Act claims in a manner consistent with the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA) amendments to the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 626(f). Although the OWBPA does not directly govern WARN Act claims, courts have looked to it for guidance in assessing the validity of waivers of WARN Act claims. 

In determining whether a waiver is valid, we look to the principles established in cases such as Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), where the Supreme Court held that a waiver of a statutory right must be "knowing and voluntary." The Court emphasized that a waiver is not valid if it is "extracted" from an employee under duress or coercion. Id. at 704. Here, the appellants were faced with a plant closure and mass layoff, and were given a short period to decide whether to accept the severance package, which included a waiver of their WARN Act claims. Drivers who opted for the ten-week plan had to make their decision by December 11, 2008, just two days after the agreements became operative on December 9, 2008. This tight deadline, combined with the requirement that they simultaneously complete a job rebid form, raises concerns about the voluntariness of their waivers.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a waiver of a statutory right must be "clear and unmistakable." See Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Kennedy, 301 U.S. 389, 393 (1937). While the "General Waiver and Release" signed by the appellants explicitly mentions the WARN Act, the language is boilerplate and does not specifically inform the signers of the nature of their WARN Act claims or the consequences of waiving them. 

In Curtis v. Loether, 415 F.2d 702 (7th Cir. 1969), this Court held that a waiver of a statutory right must be "intentional and informed." The circumstances surrounding the execution of the waivers in this case raise questions about whether the appellants made an informed decision to waive their WARN Act claims. The short time frame for making a decision, combined with the lack of specific information about the WARN Act claims, suggests that the waivers may not have been "intentional and informed."

For these reasons, we urge the Court to reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings to determine whether the appellants' waivers were knowing and voluntary.
