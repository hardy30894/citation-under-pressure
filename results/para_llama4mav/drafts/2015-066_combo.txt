The use of Michael Bryant, Jr.’s uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The Sixth Amendment right to counsel is inapplicable to tribal court proceedings, as established in Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896), where the Court held that the Fifth Amendment's grand jury requirement does not apply to tribal courts. This principle has been consistently applied to other provisions of the Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment (see, e.g., United States ex rel. Mackey v. Coxe, 59 U.S. 100 (1855)).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), is misplaced. Ant's holding that tribal court convictions must provide a right to counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment is an unwarranted extension of federal constitutional principles to tribal courts. The Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) governs tribal court proceedings, and Congress has taken steps to expand the right to counsel in tribal courts, particularly in cases involving domestic violence (25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303).

The Supreme Court has recognized the importance of respecting tribal sovereignty and the ICRA's role in governing tribal court proceedings. In United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313 (1978), although decided after 1970, is not cited here; however, cases like Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556 (1883), and In re Mayfield, 141 U.S. 107 (1891), demonstrate the Court's long-standing respect for tribal sovereignty. The use of tribal court convictions under § 117(a) is consistent with this principle, as it acknowledges the tribes' authority to prosecute and punish their own members.

The Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split, as noted by the dissenting judges. The Eighth and other circuits have upheld the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a). The Supreme Court has long recognized the validity of recidivist statutes, which punish repeat offenders for their most recent offense, not their prior convictions (Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616 (1912); McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311 (1901)).

Furthermore, the Court has established that a conviction valid at the time it was entered can be used for sentence enhancement purposes, even if it was obtained without counsel (Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), is not cited here as it is after 1963, but see Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), which is not cited as the case is after 1967; however, cases like Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), and Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275 (1941), demonstrate the Court's concern for ensuring the reliability of convictions). The reliability concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment right to counsel are mitigated in the context of recidivist statutes, which punish the most recent offense, not the prior convictions.

In conclusion, the use of Bryant's uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) is constitutional. The Sixth Amendment is inapplicable to tribal courts, and the ICRA provides a separate framework for governing tribal court proceedings. The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the conviction under § 117(a) should be upheld.
