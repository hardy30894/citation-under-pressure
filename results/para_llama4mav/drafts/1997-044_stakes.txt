The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to those who commit fraud. The question presented here is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The plain language of this provision indicates that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable. The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held that the debt owed by Edward S. Cohen to Hilda de la Cruz and other tenants is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because it was obtained through Cohen's actual fraud.

To prevail under § 523(a)(2)(A), a creditor must show that the debtor obtained money or property through a material misrepresentation, with knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard for the truth, and with the intent to deceive. In re Poskanzer, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992). The bankruptcy court found that Cohen had committed actual fraud by charging rents in excess of those permitted under the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance, and that he had done so with reckless disregard for the truth. This finding is supported by the fact that Cohen failed to inform himself about the rent control ordinance, despite being aware of its existence, and that he ignored the Rent Leveling and Stabilization Board's notice that he was overcharging tenants.

The fact that Cohen made no affirmative representation to the tenants does not preclude a finding of misrepresentation. As the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit held, Cohen's silence, coupled with the rental amounts he fixed, constituted an implicit representation that the rents were lawful. Furthermore, Cohen's reckless disregard for the truth is evident from his failure to investigate the rent control ordinance and his disregard for the Board's notice.

The award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is also nondischargeable. While the Ninth Circuit has held that punitive damages are dischargeable because they are not "debts obtained by fraud," In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), the Eleventh Circuit has taken the opposite view, holding that punitive damages are nondischargeable because they are part of the "debt" incurred through the debtor's fraudulent conduct, In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993). The latter view is more persuasive, as it is consistent with the "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code, which is intended to benefit "honest but unfortunate debtors," Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991).

In this case, the treble damages award is particularly appropriate because it was mandated by statute, making it foreseeable to Cohen at the time he committed the fraud. Therefore, the debt owed by Cohen to the tenants, including the treble damages award, is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
