**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to workers injured on land. The text of OCSLA, its structure, and this Court’s precedents compel the conclusion that the situs of the injury must be on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Plain Language of OCSLA Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision states that LHWCA benefits extend to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” defines not merely the source of the injury but the geographical locus of the covered event. This Court has long held that “[w]here the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

The statutory phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” cannot bear the Ninth Circuit’s construction. The word “occurring” modifies “injury,” establishing a direct link between the physical location of the injury-producing event and the shelf. If Congress had intended to cover all injuries with any connection to shelf operations, it would have said so—as it did in other provisions of OCSLA. *Compare* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (extending state law to artificial islands and fixed structures), *with* § 1333(b) (limiting coverage to injuries occurring as the result of operations conducted on the shelf). The deliberate distinction in phrasing demonstrates congressional intent to confine compensation coverage to the shelf itself.

**II. This Court’s Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Coverage Turns on Situs**

In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), this Court stated that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Acknowledging that § 1333(b) is the “[o]nly one provision of OCSLA [that] superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement,” the Court identified that situs requirement as a geographic limitation. *Id.* at 219 n.2. That statement reflects the consistent understanding of this Court. *See also United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947) (recognizing federal jurisdiction over submerged lands within the defined shelf area).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. *Curtis* conflicts with this Court’s instruction that OCSLA contains an explicit geographic limitation. *See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 423 (1985). In *Herb’s Welding*, this Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* That geographic limitation bars compensation for injuries occurring onshore.

**III. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Is Consistent with the Statutory Scheme**

OCSLA was enacted to govern activities on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act’s purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf’s natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers’ compensation program for offshore workers. Extending OCSLA to onshore injuries would disregard the Act’s limited territorial reach. *Cf. Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (statutory construction must respect jurisdictional boundaries).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test—requiring only a connection between the injury and shelf operations—improperly expands OCSLA beyond its text. This Court has rejected such open-ended standards when Congress has spoken clearly. *See United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) (“The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is founded on the tenderness of the law for the rights of individuals; and on the plain principle that the power of punishment is vested in the legislative, not in the judicial department.”).

**IV. Applying the Correct Situs Standard, Valladolid’s Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility located on the California coast. Because his injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s remand for application of its “substantial nexus” test was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
