**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Prohibit the Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders.**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted by reference to “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). In assessing whether a particular punishment violates this prohibition, the Court has long looked to objective indicia of national consensus, including legislative enactments and jury sentencing practices. *See, e.g., Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551, 563-64 (2005).

At the time of this Court’s decision in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), only two States had statutes expressly prohibiting the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and no State had ever executed a mentally retarded offender since the Court’s decision in *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238 (1972). *Id.* at 334-35. The Court therefore concluded that there was insufficient evidence of a national consensus to support a categorical prohibition. *Id.* at 335.

Since *Penry*, the legislative landscape has not materially changed. As of 2002, only twelve States had statutes prohibiting the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and only five States had executed mentally retarded offenders in the past decade. *See Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311-12 (2002). This does not demonstrate a “consensus” sufficient to support a categorical Eighth Amendment rule. *See id.* at 312-13.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly cautioned against interpreting the Eighth Amendment “in a vacuum.” *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977). The Eighth Amendment does not prohibit all punishments that are disproportionate to the offense committed; rather, it prohibits only those punishments that are “cruel and unusual” in the constitutional sense. *See, e.g., Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994-95 (1991) (plurality opinion). The execution of mentally retarded offenders is not inherently disproportionate to the crime of capital murder. *See Penry*, 492 U.S. at 335.

**II. The Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment’s “Evolving Standards of Decency.”**

The Court has recognized that the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted in light of “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. However, the Court has also cautioned that this principle does not permit the Eighth Amendment to be interpreted “in a vacuum” or to reflect the personal predilections of individual Justices. *Coker*, 433 U.S. at 592.

In *Penry*, the Court concluded that the execution of mentally retarded offenders did not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments.” 492 U.S. at 335. The Court noted that the legislative trend at the time was not sufficient to demonstrate a national consensus against the practice. *Id.* at 334-35. Since *Penry*, the legislative trend has not materially changed. As of 2002, only twelve States had statutes prohibiting the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and only five States had executed mentally retarded offenders in the past decade. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311-12.

Moreover, the Court has recognized that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit all punishments that are disproportionate to the offense committed; rather, it prohibits only those punishments that are “cruel and unusual” in the constitutional sense. *See, e.g., Harmelin*, 501 U.S. at 994-95 (plurality opinion). The execution of mentally retarded offenders is not inherently disproportionate to the crime of capital murder. *See Penry*, 492 U.S. at 335.
