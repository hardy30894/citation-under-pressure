**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s Subjective-Awareness Standard Contradicts the Text of § 1113(2) and This Court’s Precedent**

Section 1113(2) bars ERISA claims commenced more than three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). “Actual knowledge” does not require that a plaintiff appreciate the legal significance of disclosed facts. Rather, knowledge is actual when the plaintiff possesse the operative facts underlying the claim—regardless of whether he subjectively appreciated their import. *See Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633, 648–49 (2010) (applying analogous “actual knowledge” standard under the PSLRA and holding that knowledge of facts, not legal conclusions, triggers limitations). The Ninth Circuit inverted this settled principle by requiring that Sulyma have been “actually aware” that the investments were “imprudent”—a legal conclusion—before the clock began to run.

This Court has long instructed that statutes of limitations begin when the plaintiff “has knowledge of facts sufficient to put a reasonable person on inquiry.” *United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 122 (1979). ERISA is no different. In *Martin v. Consultants & Administrators, Inc.*, 506 U.S. 100, 108 (1992), the Court explained that ERISA’s limitations provisions “reflect a congressional decision that the rights of plan participants and beneficiaries are better served by a limitations period that begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach.” The Court emphasized that “actual knowledge” is distinct from “constructive knowledge” and that Congress “meant what it said” in requiring only knowledge of the breach, not knowledge of the law. *Id.*

**II. Congress’s Repeal of the Constructive-Knowledge Provision Confirms That Disclosure of Relevant Information Triggers Limitations**

When Congress enacted ERISA in 1974, it included both an “actual knowledge” prong and a “constructive knowledge” prong triggered by the filing of reports from which a plaintiff “could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976). Congress repealed the constructive-knowledge prong in 1987, leaving only the actual-knowledge provision. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. This repeal signaled Congress’s intent that plans should not be subject to “protracted, open-ended liability” merely because participants ignored disclosures. *See Brock v. Writers Guild of America, West, Inc.*, 762 F.2d 1349, 1354 (9th Cir. 1985) (recognizing that limitations periods serve “the important policy of quieting titles and stabilizing business relationships”). The Ninth Circuit’s reading resurrects the constructive-knowledge regime Congress deliberately eliminated: under its standard, defendants cannot rely on disclosures unless the plaintiff actually read and cognitively processed them, effectively requiring the near-impossible proof of a plaintiff’s mental state.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with the Weight of Circuit Authority**

Every other circuit to have addressed the question has held that “actual knowledge” is satisfied when a plaintiff knows the underlying facts—even if he does not understand their legal significance or does not recall reading them. *See Maher v. Strachan*, 914 F.2d 250, 1990 WL 137846, at *4 (10th Cir. 1990) (unpublished table decision) (knowledge of transactions triggering claim is sufficient); *Glaziers Local 252 Pension Fund v. Newberry Corp.*, 2005 WL 2039175, at *5 (3d Cir. Aug. 24, 2005) (unpublished) (same). In *Smith v. Sydnor*, 184 F.3d 356, 362 (4th Cir. 1999), the Fourth Circuit held that a plaintiff has “actual knowledge” when “he knew the underlying facts that put him on notice of the breach.” And in *Rogers v. Baxter International Inc.*, 710 F.3d 733, 741 (7th Cir. 2013), the Seventh Circuit held that ERISA plaintiffs need not “know that the conduct violated ERISA” for limitations to run. The Ninth Circuit’s outlier standard—which requires subjective awareness of imprudence—creates a circuit split and invites forum shopping.

**IV. Sulyma’s Failure to Read or Recall the Disclosures Cannot Defeat Summary Judgment**

The undisputed record shows Intel provided Sulyma with Fund Fact Sheets, Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notices, Summary Plan Descriptions, and Annual Disclosures—all of which stated that the funds included alternative investments, described the strategy behind those investments, and disclosed the resulting performance lag. Sulyma concedes he accessed some of this information online during his employment. What he lacked, he says, was “awareness” that his accounts were invested in hedge funds and private equity. That is not a failure of knowledge; it is a failure of engagement. The statute does not reward plaintiffs who “cho[o]se not to read or could not recall having read the information.”

This Court has never required that a limitations trigger be tied to a plaintiff’s self-serving testimony about his own obliviousness. To the contrary, in *Kubrick*, the Court held that a plaintiff need not understand the medical or legal nuance of his injury; it is enough that he is on notice of the facts. *Kubrick*, 444 U.S. at 122–23. Where, as here, the defendant’s disclosures put the plaintiff on notice of the allegedly imprudent investments, the statute begins to run. *See also Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB*, 522 U.S. 359, 378 (1998) (“knowledge” can be inferred where a party “has been exposed to information” that would prompt inquiry).

**V. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Is Unworkable and Defeats ERISA’s Purposes**

ERISA’s limitations period exists to ensure that “the rights of plan participants are not subject to indefinite delay,” *Martin*, 506 U.S. at 108, and to protect fiduciaries from “stale claims” that arise long after documents were disclosed. The Ninth Circuit’s subjective standard makes limitations dependent on a plaintiff’s degree of attention and memory—facts that can be manufactured through deposition testimony. That approach rewards willful ignorance and imposes impossible burdens on defendants, who can only disclose, not compel, reading. *See In re WorldCom, Inc. ERISA Litigation*, 263 F. Supp. 2d 745, 766 (S.D.N.Y. 2003) (adopting objective disclosure standard to avoid “reward[ing] participants who deliberately ignore plan materials”). The judgment below should be reversed.
