**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an inherited IRA qualifies as an exempt “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12)—turns on the meaning of “retirement funds” in the context of the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption scheme. The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that such funds are not exempt merely because they are no longer being used for the current owner’s retirement. That interpretation disregards the plain language of the statute, the settled principles of statutory construction, and the well-established rule that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be construed liberally in favor of the debtor.

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt from the reach of creditors “any retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code.” The phrase “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, but the legislative history and judicial interpretation of similar statutory language confirm that the term refers to funds set aside for future retirement, not merely those currently being used for that purpose. The critical inquiry is not whether the funds are currently being reserved for the debtor’s retirement, but whether they were originally intended for that purpose and remain in a vehicle designed to serve that end.

The Internal Revenue Code, under section 408, establishes the framework for IRAs, including inherited IRAs. Section 408(d)(3)(C) provides that an inherited IRA is not subject to income tax until distributions are made, and that the beneficiary must begin withdrawals within one year of the original owner’s death. This provision reflects Congress’s intent to preserve the tax-deferred status of retirement savings even after the original owner’s death. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw the funds within a limited time does not strip the account of its character as a retirement fund. Rather, it reflects a policy choice to prevent indefinite tax deferral while still allowing the funds to serve their original purpose—providing for the beneficiary’s future financial security.

This interpretation is consistent with the principle that “the meaning of a statute is to be gathered from the language used, and not from the supposed intent of the legislature.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The Bankruptcy Code does not require that the funds be “used” for retirement at the time of bankruptcy; it requires only that they be “retirement funds” in a tax-exempt account. The inherited IRA satisfies both conditions. It was originally established as a retirement fund by the decedent, and it remains in a vehicle that is exempt from taxation under section 408 of the Internal Revenue Code. The fact that the beneficiary must withdraw the funds within a fixed period does not alter the fund’s essential nature.

The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the idea that the funds are “no one’s retirement funds” after the decedent’s death is legally unsound. The law does not require that the current owner be the original saver in order for the funds to retain their exempt status. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court held that “the character of property is not changed by a mere transfer of ownership.” Similarly, the character of retirement funds is not altered by the fact that they pass to a beneficiary. The beneficiary inherits not only the assets, but also the statutory protections that accompany them. The fact that the beneficiary must begin withdrawals earlier than the original owner does not mean the funds are no longer “retirement funds.” It means only that the time for distribution has been accelerated—just as a pension plan may require distributions at age 70½, but that does not make the pension non-retirement funds.

The Seventh Circuit’s analogy to a gift of cash from a decedent to a beneficiary is inapposite. In that scenario, the funds are no longer in a tax-deferred account and are not subject to the same statutory protections. But an inherited IRA remains within the framework of section 408, which governs the tax treatment of retirement accounts. The funds are not “freely used for current consumption” as the court suggests. They are subject to mandatory distributions and are taxed upon withdrawal. The beneficiary cannot spend the funds at will; they are bound by the rules of the Internal Revenue Code. To treat them as ordinary assets would undermine the very purpose of the tax-deferred retirement account.

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s interpretation creates an absurd result. It allows a debtor to claim exemption for a retirement account that was never intended for their own retirement, while denying exemption to an account that was originally intended for retirement and remains in a tax-deferred vehicle. This contradicts the fundamental purpose of the exemption: to protect the debtor’s future financial security. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the purpose of a statute is not to be defeated by a strained construction.” The purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is to protect funds set aside for retirement, regardless of who originally contributed them.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the principle that exemptions in bankruptcy are to be construed in favor of the debtor. In *In re H. L. H. Co.*, 292 U.S. 379, 383 (1934), the Court held that “the exemptions provided by the Bankruptcy Act are to be liberally construed in favor of the debtor.” This principle is especially strong where the exemption is based on a federal statute, as here. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the policy of the law is to relieve the debtor from the burden of his debts and to give him a new start in life.” *In re S. S. Kresge Co.*, 297 U.S. 107, 110 (1936). To deny exemption to an inherited IRA—despite its continued tax-deferred status and its original purpose—undermines this policy.
