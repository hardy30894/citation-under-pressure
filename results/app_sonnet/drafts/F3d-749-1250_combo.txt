**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Committed Plain Error by Admitting Agent Jones's Opinion Testimony That Invaded the Jury's Exclusive Province to Judge Credibility.**

It has long been settled that "the credibility of witnesses, the effect and value of evidence, are questions exclusively for the jury." *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. 76, 88 (1891). No principle is more fundamental to the jury's function than its sole authority to decide whom to believe. Agent Jones's testimony trampled that principle. He told the jury that Stanley's demeanor and word choice—his invocation of God, his statement that he would rather die than face charges, his manner of answering questions—were themselves proof of guilt: "Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person," and statements invoking faith are "deceptive statements." This was not evidence of a fact; it was a naked opinion, dressed in expert clothing, that Stanley was lying and therefore guilty.

This Court's predecessor doctrine is squarely illustrated by *Quercia v. United States*, 289 U.S. 466 (1933). There, the trial judge told the jury that the defendant's manner of testifying—wiping his hands while on the stand—showed "that he is not telling the truth." *Id.* at 468. The Supreme Court reversed, holding that such commentary on demeanor as proof of falsehood exceeded the bounds of permissible comment and invaded the jury's function of weighing credibility. *Id.* at 470. If a *judge* may not tell a jury that a defendant's physical conduct while testifying betrays deception, an FBI agent, testifying with the imprimatur of "expert" status, certainly may not do so through the vehicle of "special tactics" training that purports to divine truth from a suspect's invocation of faith or fear of prosecution. The vice is identical: substituting the witness's judgment of credibility for the jury's own.

Nor was Jones's methodology shown to rest on any reliable, testable foundation. Courts have long insisted that expert opinion be grounded in a discipline whose validity can be tested, not mere say-so about interrogation "experience." *See Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923) (excluding expert testimony based on a technique that had not "gained general acceptance" in the relevant field). Agent Jones offered nothing but his own subjective conviction that certain phrases and behaviors are "common among the criminal element." That is not expertise; it is vouching, precisely the kind of overreach condemned when a government representative "overstep[s] the bounds of that propriety and fairness which should characterize the conduct of such an officer in the prosecution of a criminal offense." *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 84 (1935). The prosecutor's elicitation of this testimony from a federal agent—an official cloaked with governmental authority—compounded the prejudice, for the jury was invited to substitute the government's assessment of guilt for its own.

**II. The Error Is Reviewable, and Reversal Is Required, Even Absent a Contemporaneous Objection.**

Although trial counsel did not object, appellate courts retain authority to correct errors that were not raised below when the interests of justice so require. "In exceptional circumstances, especially in criminal cases, appellate courts, in the public interest, may, of their own motion, notice errors to which no exception has been taken." *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). This is such a case. The improperly admitted testimony went to the very heart of the contested issue—whether Stanley, as opposed to Dejuan, was inside the bank or merely present nearby—and an FBI agent's declaration that Stanley's protestations of innocence were themselves badges of guilt could not have been more prejudicial. Where "the substantial rights of the accused" are affected, this Court should not turn a blind eye simply because trial counsel failed to object. *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189, 200 (1943).

**III. The Error Was Not Harmless and Affected Stanley's Substantial Rights.**

The proper inquiry is "not merely whether there was enough to support the result, apart from the phase affected by the error," but "whether the error itself had substantial influence" on the verdict. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946). Where "the error may have prejudiced petitioner in the eyes of the jury," reversal is warranted. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 83 (1942). Here, the government's own case shifted twice as to Stanley's role, the physical and eyewitness evidence connecting him to the interior of the bank was thin, and the case turned largely on the jury's assessment of Stanley's credibility during his post-arrest statements. As the Supreme Court has recognized, "the jury's estimate of the truthfulness and reliability of a . . . witness may well be determinative of guilt or innocence." *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959). By allowing a federal agent to tell the jury, in effect, that Stanley's own statements proved his guilt, the district court did not merely admit improper opinion evidence—it allowed the government to usurp the jury's core function. That error cannot be deemed harmless, and Stanley's convictions must be reversed.
