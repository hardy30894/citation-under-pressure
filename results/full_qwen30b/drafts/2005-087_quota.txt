**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right to introduce expert evidence about his mental state to rebut the prosecution’s proof of criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the scope of Clark’s expert testimony to the insanity defense, consistent with settled precedent and the state’s legitimate interest in maintaining the integrity of its criminal trial process. Clark’s claim fails because he was not deprived of any constitutional right—his due process rights were fully respected, and the state’s evidentiary rules governing the use of mental health testimony were neither arbitrary nor irrational.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a defendant’s right to present a defense, but that right is not absolute. As this Court has long held, “the right to present evidence is not a license to introduce any and all evidence, regardless of its relevance or reliability.” *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973). The Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert testimony on mental state to negate criminal intent unless such evidence is relevant to a defense that the state recognizes. *See United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (holding that the Constitution does not require a court to admit expert testimony on the reliability of a polygraph test, even if relevant, where the state has a legitimate policy against such evidence).

Arizona law, like the laws of many states, treats the insanity defense as a distinct, affirmative defense that requires the defendant to prove by clear and convincing evidence that he lacked the capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the law. *See Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501*. The state is entitled to define the scope of that defense and to limit the use of expert testimony to that specific purpose. *See *State v. Hines*, 145 Ariz. 477, 481 (1985) (holding that expert testimony on mental illness may be admitted only to support an insanity defense, not to negate mens rea generally). This is not a denial of due process; it is a reasonable exercise of state authority to structure its criminal justice system.

Clark’s argument rests on a fundamental misreading of *Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984*, 18 U.S.C. § 17, which applies only to federal prosecutions and does not bind state courts. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 594 F.2d 221, 230 (D.C. Cir. 1978) (rejecting the notion that federal standards govern state insanity defenses). The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that states may define the insanity defense as they see fit, so long as they do not violate fundamental fairness. *See *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 409 (1986) (recognizing that states may define the scope of the insanity defense, so long as the process is not arbitrary or capricious).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has rejected the idea that a defendant has a constitutional right to introduce expert testimony to negate mens rea unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. In *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 654 (1984), the Court emphasized that “the right to present a defense” is not violated unless the defendant is “prevented from introducing evidence that is both relevant and material to the defense.” Here, Clark was not barred from presenting expert testimony on his mental state; he was simply prohibited from using it to prove a lack of criminal intent outside the framework of the insanity defense. That limitation is neither arbitrary nor irrational.

The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision in *State v. Hines*, 145 Ariz. 477 (1985), is directly on point. There, the court held that expert testimony on mental illness may be admitted only to support an insanity defense and not to establish a general lack of intent. The court reasoned that allowing such testimony to negate mens rea would “undermine the distinction between the insanity defense and the general defense of lack of intent,” which is a critical component of the state’s criminal law structure. *Id.* at 481. This reasoning is sound and consistent with due process.

Clark also cites *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), to argue that the prosecution must prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including criminal intent. But *Jackson* does not require the defendant to be allowed to introduce expert evidence to rebut that burden unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. The burden of proof remains on the prosecution, and the defendant’s right to present a defense does not extend to forcing the state to accept evidence outside the framework of its own legal rules.

Finally, the Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld state rules that limit the use of expert testimony in criminal trials. In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993), the Court affirmed that “the trial judge acts as a gatekeeper” in determining the admissibility of expert testimony. The Arizona trial judge here properly exercised that gatekeeping function by limiting the use of Clark’s expert testimony to the insanity defense, a recognized and constitutionally permissible defense.

For these reasons, Clark was not deprived of due process. The state’s evidentiary rules were reasonable, consistent with precedent, and did not prevent him from presenting a meaningful defense. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
