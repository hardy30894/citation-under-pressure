**ARGUMENT**

The failure of a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not, in the absence of prejudice, constitute a basis for habeas relief—particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right and voluntarily chose not to appeal. The Court has long recognized that procedural errors in criminal proceedings are not automatically reversible or remediable by habeas corpus unless they rise to the level of a constitutional violation or result in actual prejudice. In this case, the District Court’s omission was not a violation of due process, nor did it deprive Peguero of a fundamental right, because he was fully aware of his appellate rights at the time of sentencing.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the absence of counsel at critical stages of a criminal proceeding may violate due process, but only where the defendant is deprived of meaningful representation. In *Mempa v. Rhay*, 389 U.S. 128 (1967), the Court emphasized that the right to counsel extends beyond trial to sentencing, particularly where the defendant is not represented and the court imposes a significant penalty. However, the Court did not hold that every procedural omission in sentencing automatically invalidates a conviction or sentence. Rather, the focus is on whether the defendant was deprived of a fair opportunity to be heard or to present a defense. Here, Peguero was not denied counsel, nor was he deprived of the ability to appeal. He was aware of his right to appeal and made a deliberate choice not to exercise it. That choice, made with full knowledge, cannot be undone by a technical violation of Rule 32.

The Court has also recognized that not every error in a criminal proceeding requires reversal or habeas relief. In *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), the Court affirmed that federal habeas corpus is not a substitute for appellate review and that a prisoner must exhaust state remedies before seeking federal relief. More importantly, the Court held that a federal court may deny habeas relief even when a state court has committed error, if the error was harmless and did not affect the outcome. The same principle applies here: the failure to advise of the right to appeal is not a structural defect, but a procedural one, and must be evaluated under a harmless-error standard.

This Court has long held that a defendant’s actual knowledge of his rights may cure a technical violation of procedural rules. In *Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82 (1946), the Court considered a case where a defendant, a young man unfamiliar with legal proceedings, pleaded guilty without being advised of his right to counsel. The Court acknowledged the importance of such advisements but emphasized that the ultimate inquiry is whether the defendant’s plea was voluntary and intelligent. The Court stated that “the judgment upon which his imprisonment rests was rendered in violation of due process and cannot stand” only if the defendant was deprived of due process. Here, Peguero was not deprived of due process because he was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it.

Similarly, in *Smith v. O’Grady*, 312 U.S. 329 (1941), the Court held that a guilty plea obtained under deception or without the benefit of counsel may be invalid, but only if the defendant can prove that he was misled or coerced. The Court stated that “if these things happened, petitioner is imprisoned under a judgment invalid because obtained in violation of procedural guaranties protected against state invasion through the Fourteenth Amendment.” But where, as here, the defendant was not misled and was fully informed, the judgment stands. Peguero was not misled; he was aware of his right to appeal and chose not to pursue it.

The Court has also recognized that the failure to advise of appellate rights does not rise to the level of a constitutional violation unless it results in actual prejudice. In *Woods v. Nierstheimer*, 328 U.S. 211 (1946), the Court considered a case where a defendant claimed that his guilty plea was involuntary because he was not advised of his right to counsel. The Court noted that the defendant had not been represented at sentencing, but it did not hold that the absence of such advice automatically invalidated the sentence. Instead, the Court emphasized that the issue was whether the defendant was deprived of a fair trial. Here, Peguero was not deprived of a fair trial; he was aware of his rights and made a conscious decision not to appeal.

In *Hunter v. Martin*, 334 U.S. 302 (1948), the Court held that a federal sentence may be served even if a state sentence is still pending, provided that the federal sentence does not conflict with state law. The Court emphasized that the purpose of the federal statute was to avoid conflict between state and federal authorities. This principle supports the conclusion that a technical violation of a procedural rule—such as failing to advise of the right to appeal—does not justify vacating a sentence when the defendant was not prejudiced.

In *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court considered a challenge to a life sentence imposed under a habitual criminal statute. The Court acknowledged that the defendant had been sentenced without counsel, but it did not hold that the sentence was invalid. Instead, the Court noted that the state law question was not free from difficulty and that the issue was not fully litigated. The Court ultimately concluded that the same result would be reached on the merits. This reinforces the principle that procedural errors do not automatically invalidate a sentence.

Finally, in *McConnell v. Rhay*, 393 U.S. 2 (1968), the Court held that the absence of counsel at a sentencing hearing violated the Sixth Amendment, but only where the defendant was not represented and the court imposed a significant penalty. The Court emphasized that the right to counsel is not confined to trial but extends to sentencing. However, the Court did not hold that every failure to advise of appellate rights constitutes a violation of due process. Here, Peguero was not denied counsel, nor was he deprived of the opportunity to appeal.

In sum, the failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) is not a constitutional violation unless it results in actual prejudice. Where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right and chose not to appeal, the error is harmless. The judgment of the District Court and the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
