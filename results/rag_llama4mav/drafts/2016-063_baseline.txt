The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction to review the Merit Systems Protection Board's dismissal of Anthony Perry's appeal for lack of jurisdiction. According to the Civil Service Reform Act, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2), judicial review of the Board's decisions in "mixed cases" lies in federal district court. A mixed case is defined as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal laws, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), held that when the Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court.

Although Kloeckner involved a procedural dismissal, its reasoning applies equally to jurisdictional dismissals. The Court's decision turned on the interpretation of "mixed cases" under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), which includes cases where an employee alleges discrimination and the action is "appealable to the MSPB," Kloeckner, 133 S.Ct. at 604. Perry argues that this language is not limited to procedural dismissals, and that jurisdictional dismissals also fall within the exception.

The statutory text supports Perry's argument. The phrase "affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A), focuses on the nature of the action taken against the employee, not the Board's ultimate decision on jurisdiction. In the case of a jurisdictional dismissal, the action itself may not be appealable, but the employee's claim is still a mixed case because it alleges discrimination.

Furthermore, the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not as clear-cut as the Federal Circuit suggests. As Justice Kagan noted during oral argument in Kloeckner, "there seem[ed] to be a good deal of difference between the question, what happens to something that is clearly a mixed case, and alternatively, the question of whether something is a mixed case," Transcript of Oral Argument at 23-24, Kloeckner, 133 S.Ct. 596. However, this distinction does not necessarily dictate different outcomes for procedural and jurisdictional dismissals.

The practical implications of the Federal Circuit's rule also support Perry's position. Requiring review of jurisdictional dismissals to be in the Federal Circuit, while review of procedural dismissals is in district court, creates unnecessary complexity and uncertainty. As Perry notes, the statute contains various deadlines that depend on knowing whether a case is a mixed case, and the proper forum for judicial review should not vary based on the Board's ultimate decision on jurisdiction.

The D.C. Circuit's decision in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), which held that review of jurisdictional dismissals lies in the Federal Circuit, is not controlling. Kloeckner's reasoning undermines the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, and Perry's arguments, although not novel, gain new traction in light of the Supreme Court's decision.

In conclusion, the Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction to review the Board's dismissal of Perry's appeal. The correct forum for judicial review is the federal district court, as mandated by 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2) and supported by the Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis.
