## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Exist Regarding Erdman’s FMLA Eligibility

Review of a summary judgment is plenary, and all facts must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmovant. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). Summary judgment is proper only when “no genuine issue as to any material fact” exists. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 250 (1986). Here, the district court erroneously resolved factual disputes concerning the hours Erdman actually worked—a material issue that must be tried.

#### A. Erdman’s “Hours of Service” Include All Compensated Time Actually Worked

Under the FMLA, an eligible employee must have “at least 1,250 hours of service” during the preceding 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The Department of Labor’s regulations define “hours of service” by reference to the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)—meaning “all hours actually worked” that are compensable under the FLSA. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c) (2009). This includes overtime and “comp” time if the employer compensates such hours. *See Bush v. Cheaptickets, Inc.*, 425 F.3d 336, 340 (7th Cir. 2005) (“[T]he 1,250 hours requirement incorporates the FLSA’s definition of ‘hours worked.’”). Compensated hours are counted even if the employer later claims they were unauthorized. *Reich v. New York City Transit Auth.*, 45 F.3d 646, 650 (2d Cir. 1995) (FLSA counts all work “suffered or permitted,” regardless of employer approval). *Accord Calderon v. Witvoet*, 999 F.2d 1101, 1104 (7th Cir. 1993).

#### B. The Record Creates a Genuine Dispute Over Whether Erdman Accumulated 1,250 Hours

Erdman worked for Nationwide for over two decades, including a part-time schedule. Her supervisor, Patty Sarno, consistently authorized and paid her for extra hours worked outside the office, and allowed Erdman to use those hours as “comp” time. When a new supervisor, Stella Getgen, later questioned the practice, Erdman sought clarification. But Getgen did not prohibit comp time until February 10, 2003—and even then, she did not dispute Erdman’s past use. A jury could reasonably infer that Erdman’s compensated extra hours during the 12 months preceding her April 2003 leave request satisfied the 1,250-hour threshold.

The district court erred by ignoring this evidence and by crediting Nationwide’s assertion that Erdman’s extra hours were “unapproved.” At summary judgment, courts cannot weigh evidence or make credibility determinations. *See Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255. Moreover, even if some hours were later deemed unauthorized, they still count under FLSA principles because Nationwide “suffered or permitted” the work and compensated it. *See Millea v. Metro-North R.R. Co.*, 658 F.3d 154, 163 (2d Cir. 2011) (hours paid for work actually performed are “hours of service” under the FMLA). *See also Darby v. Bratch*, 287 F.3d 673, 679 (8th Cir. 2002) (same).

#### C. The District Court’s Error Requires Reversal of Both FMLA Claims

Because a genuine issue exists as to Erdman’s eligibility, the district court’s grant of summary judgment on her FMLA interference and retaliation claims cannot stand. *See Callison v. City of Philadelphia*, 430 F.3d 117, 124 (3d Cir. 2005) (interference requires proof of eligibility); *Sommer v. The Vanguard Group*, 461 F.3d 397, 399 (3d Cir. 2006) (retaliation requires same). Nationwide fired Erdman just weeks after she requested FMLA leave and after it had denied her vacation request—a classic factual dispute for the jury.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
