## ARGUMENT

### I. Section 1231(a)(5) Does Not Reach Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. “No law shall be construed to have a retrospective operation unless its words are so clear, plain, and unequivocal that they admit of no other meaning.” *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch (7 U.S.) 399, 413 (1806). The statute’s text speaks of an alien who “has reentered” and directs reinstatement of “the prior order,” but it nowhere states that it applies to reentries completed before its enactment. That silence is dispositive.

“The presumption against retroactive legislation is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence.” *Shwab v. Doyle*, 258 U.S. 529, 534 (1922). Where Congress has not clearly expressed an intent that a deportation statute apply to prior conduct, the statute operates prospectively only. *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284 (1922) (deportation is “a harsh penalty” and statutes imposing it must be strictly construed). Longstanding rule likewise holds that “statutes are to be construed as prospective only unless a contrary intention is clearly expressed.” *Claridge v. Evans*, 137 F.2d 347, 349 (7th Cir. 1943). Congress easily could have written “who reentered at any time” if it intended to reach pre-1997 reentries. It did not. The Tenth Circuit therefore erred in holding that Section 1231(a)(5) applies to petitioner.

### II. Retroactive Application Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, that application would attach new legal consequences to conduct completed long before the statute’s enactment. That is the very evil the presumption against retroactivity forbids. *United States v. Schooner Peggy*, 1 Cranch (5 U.S.) 103, 110 (1801). “The presumption is that a statute is not intended to operate retroactively.” *Shwab*, 258 U.S. at 534. “The presumption against retroactive legislation is a fundamental rule of statutory construction.” *United States v. Chambers*, 291 U.S. 217, 222 (1934). No clear congressional intent to apply the statute retroactively exists here.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the immigration laws permitted an alien in his position to apply for adjustment of status if he later married a United States citizen. The 1997 reinstatement statute retroactively extinguished that avenue for relief, barring all forms of relief for aliens subject to a reinstated order. This is precisely the type of retrospective change that the canon against retroactivity is designed to prevent: a new law that “impairs rights a party possessed when he acted.” *Shwab*, 258 U.S. at 534. Petitioner’s reentry was made in reliance on the legal regime then in effect, and his subsequent marriage and application for adjustment were concrete steps taken under that regime. The government cannot now wipe away those settled expectations without a clear command from Congress. *Schooner Peggy*, 1 Cranch at 110; *Heth*, 3 Cranch at 413.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status disregards these principles. Deportation is “a harsh penalty.” *Ng Fung Ho*, 259 U.S. at 284. This Court has long held that deportation statutes must be construed in favor of the alien wherever any doubt remains. *United States ex rel. Volpe v. Smith*, 62 F.2d 808, 810 (7th Cir. 1933) (“It is a well-settled rule that deportation statutes are to be construed in favor of the alien.”). The right to seek relief under the law as it existed at the time of reentry is precisely the kind of settled expectation that the presumption against retroactivity protects. *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 713 (1893) (the power to deport must be exercised “in accordance with law”). A contrary holding would permit Congress to impose new deportation penalties for conduct that was lawful or remediable when performed, merely by altering the statute afterward. That is not permissible.

Because the reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date, and because applying it retroactively would be impermissibly retroactive, the petition for review should be granted.
