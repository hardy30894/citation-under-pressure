The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it was not limited to the trial record when determining whether the error affected Greer's substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. Under plain-error review, an appellate court may consider matters outside the trial record to assess the impact of an error. See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 59 (2002) (holding that an appellate court may consider the entire record, not just the trial record, when reviewing for plain error); see also United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993) (noting that the plain-error inquiry is not limited to the trial record).

In Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 138 S. Ct. 1897 (2018), the Supreme Court emphasized that the plain-error inquiry is a fact-specific analysis that requires consideration of the entire record. Id. at 1908. The Court in Rosales-Mireles looked beyond the trial record to determine whether the error affected the defendant's substantial rights. Id. at 1909. Similarly, in United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), the Court considered the entire record when assessing the impact of an error on the defendant's substantial rights. Id. at 83.

The Eleventh Circuit's consideration of Greer's prior convictions, which were not presented to the jury, was proper under plain-error review. See United States v. Reed, 941 F.3d 1018, 1021 (11th Cir. 2019) (holding that an appellate court may consider a defendant's prior convictions when assessing the impact of an error on their substantial rights). The court may take judicial notice of public records, including prior convictions, when reviewing for plain error. See United States v. Berrojo, 628 F. App'x 1002, 1005 (11th Cir. 2015) (per curiam) (taking judicial notice of a defendant's prior convictions when reviewing for plain error).

Furthermore, the Eleventh Circuit's decision is consistent with the principle that an appellate court should not second-guess the jury's verdict unless the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. See United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 633 (2002) (holding that an appellate court should not notice plain error unless it "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings"). The court properly considered the entire record, including Greer's prior convictions, to determine that the error did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard by considering matters outside the trial record. The court's decision is supported by the Supreme Court's precedents in Vonn, Olano, Rosales-Mireles, and Dominguez Benitez, as well as the Eleventh Circuit's own precedent in Reed. The judgment should be affirmed.
