**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—is governed by settled Supreme Court precedent that permits the use of prior uncounseled misdemeanor convictions in recidivist statutes, so long as those convictions are valid under the law of the jurisdiction in which they were obtained. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to bar the use of Bryant’s tribal court convictions, even though they were obtained in accordance with the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) and were not constitutionally infirm under the Sixth Amendment, conflicts with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 114 S.Ct. 1921, 128 L.Ed.2d 745 (1994), and undermines Congress’s intent in enacting § 117(a) to combat domestic violence in Indian country.

The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24, 98 S.Ct. 1079, 55 L.Ed.2d 303 (1978), reaffirmed that Indian tribes retain inherent sovereign authority to punish tribal offenders, and that the Bill of Rights does not extend to tribal courts. *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337, 128 S.Ct. 2709, 171 L.Ed.2d 457 (2008). Thus, a tribal court conviction is not subject to the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel. The fact that a tribal court did not appoint counsel for Bryant in his prior proceedings is not a constitutional defect, but a reflection of the distinct legal regime governing tribal justice systems.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), is misplaced. *Ant* predates *Nichols* and was decided under a different legal framework. More critically, *Ant* held that tribal court misdemeanor convictions could not be used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions because they lacked a right to counsel coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. But *Nichols* explicitly rejected that reasoning. In *Nichols*, the Court upheld the use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction—obtained in a state court where the Sixth Amendment did not apply under *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 99 S.Ct. 1158, 59 L.Ed.2d 383 (1979)—to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. The Court emphasized that the *reliability* of the prior conviction, not its constitutional pedigree under the Sixth Amendment, was the relevant inquiry. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746–47, 114 S.Ct. 1921. The Court concluded that the sentencing court could rely on such a conviction so long as it was “valid” and “not constitutionally infirm,” a standard that *Ant* failed to recognize.

The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Nichols* on the ground that it was a sentencing case is legally unsound. The Supreme Court has consistently held that recidivist statutes punish only the *current* offense, not the defendant’s status as a repeat offender. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747, 114 S.Ct. 1921; *United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386, 128 S.Ct. 1783, 170 L.Ed.2d 719 (2008). The use of prior convictions as an element of a new offense does not violate due process so long as those convictions are reliable and valid under the law of the jurisdiction in which they were obtained. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364, 90 S.Ct. 1068, 25 L.Ed.2d 368 (1970). The government need not prove the prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt when they are used to establish an element of a new offense—only that they are valid and reliable. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748, 114 S.Ct. 1921.

The Ninth Circuit’s insistence that tribal court convictions must meet a Sixth Amendment standard is inconsistent with the ICRA, which governs tribal court proceedings. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)–(c). The ICRA provides that tribal courts must afford defendants “the right to counsel” in cases where the maximum penalty exceeds one year, but it does not require that the right be coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that tribal court convictions be “coextensive” with the Sixth Amendment right to counsel imposes a federal constitutional standard on tribal courts that Congress never intended. This judicial overreach undermines tribal sovereignty and contradicts the principle of comity that requires federal courts to respect tribal judicial processes. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir.1997); *Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15, 107 S.Ct. 971, 94 L.Ed.2d 10 (1987).
