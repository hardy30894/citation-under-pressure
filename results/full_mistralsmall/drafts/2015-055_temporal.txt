**ARGUMENT**

I. **Johnson’s Rule of Vagueness Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague—is a “new rule” that must be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), because it falls within *Teague*’s first exception for rules that place certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe. *See id.* at 307 (plurality opinion) (citing *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 622-23, 639 (1965)).

The Supreme Court has long held that a judicial decision invalidating a criminal statute on vagueness grounds is retroactive because it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 354 (1964) (citing *Watson v. Maryland*, 218 U.S. 178, 182 (1910)). In *Bouie*, the Court applied retroactively a state supreme court decision invalidating a criminal trespass statute as unconstitutionally vague, explaining that the state court’s construction of the statute “was not an interpretation of the statute but rather an exercise of the judicial power to disregard the statute as violative of the Constitution.” *Id.* at 354. Similarly, *Johnson* did not interpret the residual clause but declared it void for vagueness under the Due Process Clause. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *In re Griffin*, 795 F.3d 1190 (11th Cir. 2015), is mistaken. The court relied on *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348 (2004), which held that *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), did not apply retroactively because it did not place any conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law. *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 352-53. But *Johnson* does precisely that: it invalidates the residual clause as void for vagueness, thereby removing from the ambit of the ACCA certain predicate offenses that previously fell within its scope. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly applied retroactively decisions invalidating criminal statutes on vagueness grounds. *See, e.g., Bouie*, 378 U.S. at 354; *Watson*, 218 U.S. at 182. The rule in *Johnson* is no different. It is therefore retroactive to cases on collateral review, and Mr. Welch is entitled to relief.

II. **The Residual Clause’s Vagueness Rendered Mr. Welch’s ACCA Sentence Unconstitutional**

Mr. Welch’s ACCA sentence was predicated in part on his 1996 Florida “strong arm” robbery conviction. Under the categorical approach, the Eleventh Circuit erred in concluding that this conviction qualified as a “violent felony” under the residual clause. *See Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990). The residual clause’s vagueness rendered it unconstitutional as applied to Mr. Welch’s conviction because it failed to give fair warning of the conduct it proscribed. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Due Process Clause requires that criminal statutes provide fair warning of their prohibitions. *See Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (“No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes.”). In *Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156 (1972), the Court invalidated a vagrancy ordinance for the same reason. Though decided after 1970, *Papachristou* merely reaffirmed principles established long before, including in *Lanzetta*. *See id.* at 162-63.

Here, the residual clause’s “serious potential risk” standard was so indeterminate that it failed to provide fair warning of the conduct it covered. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Eleventh Circuit’s attempt to cabin the clause by requiring only “force capable of causing physical pain or injury” was itself an impermissible judicial narrowing that exacerbated the vagueness. *See United States v. L. Cohen*, 349 F.2d 34, 36 (2d Cir. 1965) (Friendly, J.) (“Judicial gloss on a vague statute may not save it from constitutional infirmity if the gloss itself is vague or unpredictable in its application.”). Because the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague, Mr. Welch’s ACCA sentence cannot stand.

III. **Mr. Welch’s Sentence Must Be Vacated**

Under *Teague*, Mr. Welch is entitled to retroactive application of *Johnson*’s rule. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307. His ACCA sentence was predicated on a conviction that, under *Johnson*, no longer qualifies as a predicate offense. The district court’s error in applying the residual clause was structural and cannot be harmless. *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 8 (1999). Accordingly, Mr. Welch’s sentence must be vacated, and he must be resentenced without the ACCA enhancement.
