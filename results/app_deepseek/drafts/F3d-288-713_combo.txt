**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Relief Because the Prosecutor’s Dual Role as Witness and Advocate Deprived Riddle of a Fair Trial in Violation of Due Process.**

The state trial court’s decision to permit District Attorney Hill to testify as a rebuttal witness and then continue as chief prosecutor violated clearly established federal law. A prosecutor’s duty is to seek justice, not merely to convict. *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). That duty is incompatible with serving as a witness whose credibility the jury must assess. When a prosecutor testifies, he becomes a “witness against the accused” and his subsequent advocacy “inevitably carries the weight of his office,” undermining the impartiality of the proceeding. *See In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955) (due process requires a “fair trial in a fair tribunal”); *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 532 (1927) (any “tribunal” with a personal interest in the outcome violates due process). Here, Hill testified that Riddle’s claim of a threat was false, then personally argued Riddle’s credibility was destroyed. That dual role rendered Riddle’s trial fundamentally unfair.

Moreover, the prosecutor’s use of his own testimony to label Riddle a liar is a form of knowing use of false or misleading evidence. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935); *Pyle v. Kansas*, 317 U.S. 213, 215-16 (1942); *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264, 269 (1959). By injecting his personal knowledge into the case, Hill implied that he possessed special information about Riddle’s veracity, inviting the jury to rely on the prosecutor’s official position rather than the evidence. *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88-89. No harmless error analysis can salvage this structural defect. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 23 (1967) (some errors “affect[] the framework within which the trial proceeds”).

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle an Evidentiary Hearing, as the State Court’s Factual Findings Were Not Adequately Developed.**

The district court granted summary judgment without allowing Riddle to develop the record regarding the prosecutor’s misconduct and its impact on the jury. Under *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963), a federal habeas court must hold an evidentiary hearing when the petitioner alleges facts that, if proved, would entitle him to relief and the state court’s factual determinations are not fairly supported by the record. *See also Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 460 (1953) (habeas courts must consider “the whole record” to determine if a constitutional violation occurred). Here, the state habeas court summarily rejected Riddle’s claim without any hearing or factual development. The district court then granted summary judgment, relying on the same inadequate state-court record. Riddle’s proffered evidence—including the prosecutor’s own testimony and the closing argument—demonstrates a clear due process violation, yet no court has ever allowed him to question witnesses or introduce evidence of the prosecutor’s bias. This contravenes the fundamental principle that “the right to be heard” is “the very essence of due process.” *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948). An evidentiary hearing is essential to ensure that the constitutional violation is fully aired and justly resolved.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of habeas relief and remand with instructions to grant the writ or, in the alternative, to hold an evidentiary hearing.
