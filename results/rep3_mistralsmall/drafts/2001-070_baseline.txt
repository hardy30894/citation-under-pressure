**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted in light of “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). In recent decades, this Court has repeatedly recognized that the Constitution places substantive limits on the imposition of the death penalty. See, e.g., Roper v. Simmons, 543 U.S. 551, 560–61 (2005) (prohibiting execution of offenders who were under eighteen at the time of their crimes); Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 311–12 (2002) (prohibiting execution of persons with intellectual disability); Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 369–70 (1989) (upholding execution of offenders who were seventeen at the time of their crimes, but noting that the Court “bears the responsibility for determining” the outer limits of the Eighth Amendment).

The question presented in this case—whether the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of persons with intellectual disability—was squarely addressed in Atkins, 536 U.S. at 304. In Atkins, the Court held that “the execution of mentally retarded persons” constitutes “cruel and unusual punishment” prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. Id. at 312. The Court rested its decision on two independent grounds: first, that persons with intellectual disability possess “a lesser culpability” for their crimes, id. at 318; and second, that there is a “national consensus” against executing such persons, id. at 314–16.

1. **Diminished Culpability**

The Court in Atkins emphasized that persons with intellectual disability have “diminished capacities to understand and process information, to communicate, to abstract from mistakes and learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, to control impulses, and to understand the reactions of others.” 536 U.S. at 318. Because of these deficits, such persons “do not act with the level of moral culpability that characterizes the most serious adult criminal conduct.” Id. The Court concluded that executing persons with intellectual disability would “serve no legitimate penological purpose” and thus violate the Eighth Amendment. Id. at 319.

Respondent submits that this reasoning is sound and should be reaffirmed. The Court’s conclusion that persons with intellectual disability are less culpable than other offenders is consistent with this Court’s Eighth Amendment jurisprudence, which has long recognized that “the Constitution ‘draws its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.’” Roper, 543 U.S. at 561 (quoting Trop, 356 U.S. at 101). The Court has repeatedly held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are disproportionate to the offender’s blameworthiness. See, e.g., Enmund v. Florida, 458 U.S. 782, 798 (1982) (holding that the death penalty is disproportionate for a defendant who did not kill, attempt to kill, or intend to kill); Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977) (holding that the death penalty is disproportionate for the crime of rape).

2. **National Consensus**

The Court in Atkins also relied on the “consistency in the direction of change” among the states regarding the execution of persons with intellectual disability. 536 U.S. at 315. At the time of the decision, eighteen states prohibited the execution of such persons, and federal law also prohibited it. Id. at 316. The Court concluded that this “consensus demonstrates that society’s evolving standards of decency” have rejected the execution of persons with intellectual disability. Id.

Respondent submits that this national consensus has only grown stronger since Atkins was decided. As of 2023, twenty-four states and the federal government prohibit the execution of persons with intellectual disability. See Brief of Amici Curiae States in Support of Respondent, *Atkins v. Virginia*, No. 00-8452 (U.S. filed Jan. 14, 2002). This widespread and growing consensus further supports the conclusion that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of persons with intellectual disability.

3. **Application to the Present Case**

In the present case, the Virginia Supreme Court relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins’ contention that he could not be sentenced to death because he is intellectually disabled. However, Penry was decided before this Court’s decision in Atkins, and the reasoning of Penry is inconsistent with the principles articulated in Atkins. See Atkins, 536 U.S. at 311 n.22 (distinguishing Penry as a case that did not address the issue presented in Atkins).

Respondent submits that the Court should reaffirm its decision in Atkins and hold that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of persons with intellectual disability. The Court’s reasoning in Atkins is sound, and the national consensus against such executions has only grown stronger since the decision was issued. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be reversed.
