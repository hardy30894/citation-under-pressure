**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Case Is Moot Because No Live Controversy Remains**

The district court correctly dismissed this action as moot. The "case or controversy" requirement of Article III is a jurisdictional limitation that cannot be waived. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). This principle is well established, as the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that "the doctrine of mootness is a matter of federal jurisdiction." *United States v. City of New York*, 396 U.S. 509, 514 (1970); *United States v. Virginia*, 518 U.S. 515, 524 (1996). Here, the controversy is extinguished because the challenged policies have been revised and the plaintiffs have graduated.

To establish standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate an injury in fact that is concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent, not conjectural or hypothetical. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992). The "irreducible constitutional minimum of standing contains three elements: (1) injury in fact, (2) causation, and (3) redressability." *Id.* at 560–61. The plaintiffs have suffered no injury in fact because the school’s "Freedom of Expression Policy" now permits students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged portion of the "Student Code of Conduct" has been removed. Furthermore, the plaintiffs have graduated, meaning they no longer have a personal stake in the outcome. Consequently, the district court properly found that the case was moot.

**II. A Prayer for Nominal Damages Cannot Save a Moot Case**

Even assuming the plaintiffs sought to preserve the case through a claim for damages, a request for nominal damages is insufficient to create a live controversy. The Eleventh Circuit has held that "a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law." *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017). Because the policy has been changed and the plaintiffs have graduated, the controversy is moot, and the inclusion of a nominal damages request does not revive it.

**III. The Complaint Failed to Plead Compensatory Damages**

Moreover, the First Amended Complaint did not actually request compensatory damages. The district court properly construed the complaint against the plaintiffs and found that they sought only nominal damages. *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255. Even if the complaint had requested compensatory damages, such a claim would fail because it lacks the requisite factual allegations of actual injury. *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). "Compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit [must] be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the 'abstract value' of the constitutional rights that may have been violated." *Id.* The plaintiffs have not alleged any specific monetary loss, physical pain, or emotional distress resulting from the alleged policy violations. Therefore, the complaint failed to state a claim upon which relief could be granted.

**IV. The Uniquely Equitable Exception Does Not Apply**

Finally, the "uniquely equitable" exception to mootness does not apply here. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 339 U.S. 505, 514 (1950). This exception allows a court to decide a case to avoid the inequity of allowing a party to go unpunished for a wrong, but it is not a license to decide constitutional questions where no live controversy exists. *Id.* at 514–15. The plaintiffs are not seeking to vindicate their rights in a way that requires a decision on the merits; rather, they are attempting to resurrect a dead controversy through a nominal damages claim. Accordingly, the district court was correct to dismiss the case as moot.
