**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s “violent felony” definition is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This Court has long held that a new rule of constitutional law is retroactive if it is “substantive” rather than “procedural.” *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). A substantive rule “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Id.* at 353. *Johnson* is precisely such a rule: it held that the residual clause “fails to give ordinary people fair notice of the conduct it punishes” and “invites arbitrary enforcement.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 595. By declaring the residual clause void for vagueness, *Johnson* “place[d] particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish.” *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 136 S. Ct. 718, 728 (2016). Accordingly, *Johnson* must apply retroactively.

**I. *Johnson* Announced a Substantive Rule That Is Retroactive Under *Teague*.**

Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. *Id.* at 310. But substantive rules—those that “narrow the scope of a criminal statute” or “place particular conduct beyond the government’s power to punish”—are retroactive. *Montgomery*, 136 S. Ct. at 728. *Johnson* is substantive because it “removed the residual clause from the statute entirely,” thereby “narrow[ing] the scope of the ACCA.” *Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1265 (2016). Before *Johnson*, a defendant could be sentenced under the residual clause based on a judge’s assessment of the “hypothetical risk” posed by an “abstract generic version” of the prior offense. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. After *Johnson*, that clause is a nullity. The rule thus “alters the range of conduct that the law punishes” by eliminating an entire category of predicate offenses. *Summerlin*, 542 U.S. at 353.

**II. *Johnson* Is Not a Procedural Rule That Would Be Subject to *Teague*’s Bar.**

The government may argue that *Johnson* is procedural because it changes the method by which courts determine whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony. But this Court has consistently held that rules “prohibiting punishment of certain conduct” are substantive, even if they also affect the procedures used to impose punishment. *Montgomery*, 136 S. Ct. at 732. In *Montgomery*, the Court held that *Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460 (2012)—which prohibited mandatory life without parole for juvenile offenders—was substantive because it “rendered a certain category of punishment for juveniles unconstitutional.” *Montgomery*, 136 S. Ct. at 732. Similarly, *Johnson* rendered a certain category of punishment—sentences enhanced under the residual clause—unconstitutional. The fact that *Johnson* also altered the analytical framework for evaluating predicate offenses does not make it procedural. *See id.* at 731 (“A rule is substantive rather than procedural if it alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.”).

**III. *Johnson* Satisfies the Exception for “Watershed” Procedural Rules, But That Is Unnecessary.**

Even if *Johnson* were deemed procedural, it would still be retroactive under the second *Teague* exception for “watershed rules of criminal procedure” that are “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311. The void-for-vagueness doctrine is a core due process protection that “guarantees that individuals receive fair notice of what conduct is prohibited” and “protects against arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 595. A rule that invalidates an entire statutory provision as unconstitutionally vague is surely “central to an accurate determination of innocence or guilt.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 313. But this Court need not reach that question because *Johnson* is substantive.

**IV. *Johnson* Applies Retroactively to Mr. Welch’s Case.**

Mr. Welch was sentenced under the residual clause based on a Florida robbery conviction that, at the time, was deemed a “violent felony” because it “otherwise involve[d] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). After *Johnson*, that clause is void. Mr. Welch’s sentence—a mandatory minimum of 15 years—was imposed under an unconstitutional statute. Because *Johnson* is retroactive, Mr. Welch is entitled to resentencing without the residual clause. *See Welch*, 136 S. Ct. at 1268 (holding that *Johnson* “announced a substantive rule that has retroactive effect in cases on collateral review”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing.
