ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of authority to regulate activities on state, Native, corporate, or private lands merely because those lands lie inside the exterior boundaries of a Park System unit. The statute must be read as a whole, in light of Congress’s long-recognized power to protect federal reservations and to preserve the very purposes for which parks and preserves are established. See United States v. Gratiot, 39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 526, 538 (1840); Fort Leavenworth R.R. Co. v. Lowe, 114 U.S. 525, 531-32 (1885); Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 535-37 (1911); Hunt v. United States, 278 U.S. 96, 100 (1928).

Congress has repeatedly recognized that the federal government may regulate conduct on nonfederal lands when necessary to effectuate federal purposes. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 524-25 (1897), the Court upheld federal regulation of privately owned land because Congress may prevent private use of property from defeating public land policy. Likewise, in United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927), the Court sustained federal protection of national forest lands against conduct occurring on adjoining private lands. And in Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States, 243 U.S. 389, 404-05 (1917), the Court confirmed that federal authority over public lands is not limited to the title line where regulation of surrounding conduct is needed to protect federal interests. These cases refute the suggestion that mere nonownership is dispositive.

ANILCA § 103(c) does not speak in the sweeping terms petitioner urges. It provides that only “public lands” within conservation system units are administered as part of the unit, but it does not say that state or private lands inside those boundaries become regulatory enclaves immune from otherwise valid federal rules. Congress knows how to displace federal authority when it wishes to do so; absent such unmistakable language, courts do not infer a relinquishment of federal power. Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1894); Mann v. Tacoma Land Co., 153 U.S. 273, 276-77 (1894); Hynes v. Grimes Packing Co., 337 U.S. 86, 110-11 (1949). Petitioner’s reading would create precisely the sort of implied surrender of federal authority that these decisions reject.

Nor does petitioner’s rule comport with the settled understanding that Congress may impose regulations necessary to preserve a federal reservation’s integrity even where the regulated conduct has effects on nonfederal parcels within or adjacent to the reservation. In Light, 220 U.S. at 536-37, the Court upheld federal restrictions on livestock grazing in a national forest notwithstanding surrounding private interests. In Hunt, 278 U.S. at 100, the Court sustained federal measures necessary to protect a federal preserve from outside interference. And in Palazzolo v. Rhode Island, 42 U.S. (1 How.) 709, 734-35 (1843), the Court recognized that public-rights regulations may burden private use where the public interest demands it. ANILCA should be read consistently with that tradition, not against it.

The statutory structure confirms the point. Congress did not write § 103(c) to disable every federal rule whose geographic reach touches nonfederal land inside a park boundary. Rather, the provision preserves the distinction between federal administration of “public lands” and the continued existence of other lands within a unit’s exterior lines. That distinction is fully compatible with general regulatory measures of the kind at issue here, especially those aimed at conduct that directly affects the use and enjoyment of the federal unit itself. See Camfield, 167 U.S. at 524-25; Alford, 274 U.S. at 267; Utah Power & Light, 243 U.S. at 404-05.

Finally, petitioner’s interpretation would produce absurd and unstable results. It would mean that the federal government could not even enforce general park-wide safety or resource-protection rules on a state-owned river segment, a Native allotment, or a private inholding simply because the parcel lies inside park boundaries. Congress enacted ANILCA against a background of broad federal authority over public reservations, not to create fragmented jurisdictions that undermine the very conservation system it established. Fort Leavenworth, 114 U.S. at 531-32; Light, 220 U.S. at 535-37; Hynes, 337 U.S. at 110-11.

Because ANILCA does not withdraw the National Park Service’s authority to regulate conduct on nonfederal lands and waters physically within park boundaries where such regulation is otherwise authorized by federal law, the judgment below should be affirmed.
