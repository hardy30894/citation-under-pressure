## ARGUMENT

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’s 35-foot buffer zone as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation that leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), directly controls and should not be limited or overruled.

### I. The Massachusetts Law Is a Valid Time-Place-Manner Regulation

The Act regulates only the *location* of speech on public sidewalks adjacent to reproductive health care facilities. It does not target any viewpoint or message. A regulation is content-neutral if it “serves purposes unrelated to the content of expression” and “was adopted because of” those neutral purposes. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The Act’s purpose is to ensure “safe passage” of patients and staff and to prevent obstruction, not to suppress any particular message. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. The exemptions—for persons entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and those passing through—apply equally to all speakers regardless of viewpoint. Content neutrality is therefore satisfied.

Because the Act is content-neutral, it need only satisfy intermediate scrutiny: it must be “narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest” and “leave open ample alternative channels for communication.” *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The government’s interests in public safety, patient access, and protecting individuals from harassment and intimidation are “unquestionably legitimate and significant.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715; *see also Madsen v. Women’s Health Center*, 512 U.S. 753, 767–68 (1994) (recognizing similar interests).

The Act is narrowly tailored. It does not ban speech entirely; it merely moves it 35 feet away from clinic entrances. This is far less restrictive than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill*. The zone is limited to the area immediately adjacent to entrances, exits, and driveways—the precise locations where congestion and confrontation are most acute. The Act “targets and eliminates no more than the exact source of the ‘evil’ it seeks to remedy.” *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799 (internal quotation marks omitted). Petitioners remain free to speak, leaflet, pray, and display signs from just outside the buffer zone. They can still engage in “close, personal, and consensual” conversations with willing listeners. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 729.

Ample alternative channels exist. Petitioners can communicate through signs, literature, amplified speech, and one-on-one conversations from the sidewalk beyond the zone. They can also use the internet, mail, and other media. The First Circuit correctly found that the Act “leaves open ample alternative channels of communication” because petitioners “may still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signs, and pray” from outside the buffer zone. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 180 (1st Cir. 2009). The fact that petitioners cannot achieve the same “effectiveness” as before does not render the regulation unconstitutional. *See Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 295 (1984) (alternative channels need not be “perfect” or “the most effective”).

### II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled

*Hill* upheld a Colorado statute that created a 100-foot buffer zone around health care facility entrances, prohibiting “knowingly approaching” within eight feet of another person without consent for purposes of “protest, education, or counseling.” 530 U.S. at 707. The Court held the statute content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaving open ample alternatives. *Id.* at 725–30. The Massachusetts Act is even less restrictive: it imposes a fixed 35-foot zone with no “approach” restriction, and it exempts persons entering or leaving the facility. *Hill* is directly on point.

Petitioners may argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled. But *stare decisis* demands “special justification” for overruling a precedent. *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984). No such justification exists. *Hill* has been repeatedly cited and applied by lower courts. The decision did not create a new category of speech restrictions; it applied well-established time-place-manner principles. The factual context—sidewalk protests outside abortion clinics—remains unchanged. The Court should not disturb a precedent that has provided clear guidance for over a decade.

Moreover, *Hill* does not conflict with later decisions like *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), which struck down a content-based restriction on pharmaceutical data mining. *Sorrell* involved a law that “disfavor[ed] specific speakers” and “burden[ed] disfavored speech.” *Id.* at 564. The Massachusetts Act, by contrast, applies neutrally to all speakers. *Hill* remains good law and fully supports the Act.

### III. The First Circuit Correctly Applied *Hill* and Rejected Petitioners’ As-Applied Challenge

The First Circuit properly held that the Act, as applied to the three clinics, leaves open adequate alternative channels. Petitioners can still communicate with patients who walk on the public sidewalk; they can also reach patients arriving by car through signs and literature placed at the edge of the buffer zone. The record shows that petitioners have continued to engage in counseling and have achieved some success. The Act does not “foreclose[] a speaker’s ability to reach an intended audience.” *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 734.

The court also correctly applied the law-of-the-case doctrine to the facial challenge, which had been resolved in *McCullen I*. Petitioners’ reliance on later Supreme Court decisions is misplaced because those cases involved content-based or speaker-based restrictions, not neutral time-place-manner regulations.

For these reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
