**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of subjective intent. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “true threats” fall outside First Amendment protection because they are not speech but “a type of true (criminal) conduct.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (quoting *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam)). In *Black*, the Court explicitly held that the First Amendment permits statutes criminalizing cross burnings when they are done with “an intent to intimidate.” *Id.* at 363. The Court explained that “a burning cross is not always intended to intimidate,” and thus, “the First Amendment permits [a] State to ban cross burnings carried out with the intent to intimidate.” *Id.* at 365. This holding necessarily applies to all “true threats,” not just cross burnings, because the Court grounded its reasoning in the nature of true threats as conduct rather than speech. *See id.* at 359–60.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat—impermissibly dispenses with the requirement of subjective intent. *See United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991). This objective standard conflicts with *Black*’s holding that the First Amendment requires proof of intent to intimidate. *See Black*, 538 U.S. at 363. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the First Amendment protects speech even when it causes fear or offense, absent a specific intent to threaten. *See, e.g., Hess v. Indiana*, 414 U.S. 105, 108 (1973) (per curiam) (“[P]rofane and abusive language” is protected unless “intended and likely to produce imminent lawless action.”); *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (en banc) (holding that speech may be punished only if it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.”).

The Third Circuit’s rule also undermines the First Amendment’s protection of hyperbole, jest, and artistic expression. *See Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708 (per curiam) (“Political hyperbole . . . is only punishable ‘where such statements are clearly not intended to be taken seriously.’”). Elonis’s posts—modeled after rap lyrics—were quintessential examples of protected speech that cannot be criminalized without proof of subjective intent. *See id.* The Third Circuit’s objective standard risks punishing speakers for statements that a reasonable person might find threatening, even when the speaker subjectively intended no such meaning. This is precisely the danger the First Amendment seeks to avoid.

II. The Third Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s precedents requiring proof of mens rea in criminal statutes.

This Court has repeatedly held that criminal statutes require proof of mens rea unless Congress clearly indicates otherwise. *See, e.g., United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 70 (1994) (“[W]e have read into the text of § 2252 a mens rea requirement as to the sexually explicit nature of the material.”); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (“The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil.”). Section 875(c) does not expressly dispense with mens rea, and thus, the Court should require proof that Elonis subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

The government’s contrary position—that § 875(c) requires only negligence—would render the statute unconstitutionally vague. *See Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972) (“[A] law fails to give fair warning ‘when it is so vague and standardless that it leaves the public uncertain as to the conduct it prohibits.’”). Without a mens rea requirement, speakers could be convicted for statements that a reasonable person might construe as threatening, even when the speaker subjectively intended no harm. This would chill protected speech and violate the First Amendment.

III. The Third Circuit’s holding is unnecessary to protect victims from fear of violence.

The government argues that an objective standard is necessary to protect individuals from the fear of violence. *See* Brief for United States at 42–43. But the First Amendment does not permit the suppression of speech based solely on the fear it might cause. *See Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“[A] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger.”). The government’s concern is addressed by existing tort and criminal laws that punish harassment, stalking, and other conduct that genuinely threatens harm. *See, e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 2261A* (interstate stalking); *18 U.S.C. § 875(b)* (ransom threats). The First Amendment does not permit the government to criminalize speech based on the subjective reactions of listeners.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s judgment and remand the case with instructions to vacate Elonis’s conviction.
