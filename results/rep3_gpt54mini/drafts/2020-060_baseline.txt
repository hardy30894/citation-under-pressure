**Argument**

The decision below should be reversed because Guam’s claim is a timely CERCLA § 107 cost-recovery action, not an untimely § 113(f) contribution claim. CERCLA supplies different remedies for different circumstances, and this Court has repeatedly held that a plaintiff may proceed under § 107 unless it has the kind of qualifying settlement or judgment that triggers § 113(f). See *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 161–62, 167 (2004); *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 136–39 (2007).

1. **Section 107 remains available unless § 113(f) is actually triggered.**  
Section 107 authorizes recovery of “necessary costs of response” incurred by “any other person.” 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). Section 113(f), by contrast, creates a contribution action only “during or following” a civil action under § 106 or § 107, or after a party has “resolved its liability” in an administrative or judicially approved settlement. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(1), (3)(B). In *Atlantic Research*, this Court held that § 107 and § 113 “provide two 'clearly distinct' remedies,” and that § 107 is available to a PRP that “has incurred cleanup costs” outside the context of a § 113-triggering event. 551 U.S. at 138–39. The Court rejected the notion that § 113 quietly displaces § 107 in every case involving a PRP. Id. at 139–40. The D.C. Circuit’s rule—that any settlement compelling remedial work necessarily forces the plaintiff into § 113—is inconsistent with *Atlantic Research*’s text-based distinction between incurred costs under § 107 and contribution following a qualifying resolution of liability under § 113. Id. at 139.

2. **The 2004 consent decree did not “resolve” Guam’s liability for response costs within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B).**  
Section 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). This Court has emphasized that “resolved” means an actual resolution of CERCLA liability, not merely an agreement to undertake work. See *United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. at 139 n.6; *Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 167–68. A settlement must do more than impose obligations; it must settle the party’s liability for a response action. *Id.* The 2004 decree here arose in a Clean Water Act case, not a CERCLA enforcement action, and it expressly disclaimed any “finding or admission of liability.” That language is inconsistent with a resolution of CERCLA liability. See *United States v. Charter Int’l Oil Co.*, 83 F.3d 510, 518–20 (1st Cir. 1996) (settlement triggers contribution only where it resolves liability for the response action); *Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y., Inc. v. UGI Utils., Inc.*, 423 F.3d 90, 96–98 (2d Cir. 2005).

3. **A Clean Water Act decree cannot be converted into a CERCLA contribution trigger absent clear CERCLA resolution.**  
CERCLA’s contribution provisions are specific and limited. Section 113(f)(3)(B) addresses administrative or judicial settlements resolving liability “for some or all of a response action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Courts interpreting that language have required a settlement to resolve CERCLA-relevant liability, not merely to require remediation under some other statute. See *Niagara Mohawk Power Corp. v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc.*, 596 F.3d 112, 127–29 (2d Cir. 2010); *Agere Sys., Inc. v. Advanced Envtl. Tech. Corp.*, 602 F.3d 204, 226–27 (3d Cir. 2010). Here, the decree imposed cleanup measures under the Clean Water Act and did not purport to determine CERCLA liability. Because § 113(f)(3)(B) is not triggered, § 107 remains available under *Atlantic Research*. 551 U.S. at 139.

4. **The Government’s limitations defense cannot rewrite CERCLA’s remedial scheme.**  
The Government’s position would create the very trap *Atlantic Research* avoided: a party compelled to incur cleanup costs would be denied § 107 relief even though it lacks a valid § 113 contribution claim. The Court refused to read CERCLA that way because the statute’s text “adopts a clear distinction between costs of response incurred voluntarily and costs of response incurred pursuant to a civil action.” *Atlantic Research*, 551 U.S. at 139. Where Congress has made contribution available only after a qualifying resolution, courts may not enlarge that trigger by implication. *Cooper Indus.*, 543 U.S. at 168–69.

Because Guam’s 2004 decree did not resolve its CERCLA liability, Guam did not forfeit § 107. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary rule should be reversed, and Guam’s cost-recovery action allowed to proceed.
