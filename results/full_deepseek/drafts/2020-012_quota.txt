**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Board Order Declining To Reopen a Prior Claim Is a “Final Decision” Under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)**

The Railroad Retirement Act makes Board decisions subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), which grants courts of appeals jurisdiction over “final decisions” of the Board. See 45 U.S.C. § 231g. Nothing in that provision limits review to initial merits determinations, and nothing conditions review on the label the Board attaches to its ruling. An order refusing to reopen a prior claim ends the administrative process for that claimant. It is not tentative or interlocutory; it is the Board’s last word, with immediate financial consequences.

That understanding comports with this Court’s general finality framework. Agency action is final when it “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and is one “from which legal consequences will flow.” Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154, 178 (1997). Here, the Board’s refusal to reopen determined that Salinas’s 2006 application would not be revisited under the reopening standards in 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. That ruling extinguished any chance of an earlier annuity start date. Rights were determined, and consequences flowed.

**II. Califano v. Sanders Does Not Dictate the Result Below**

The Fifth Circuit invoked its decision in Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), in which it “joined several of our sister circuits in determining that we have no jurisdiction to review the Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim for benefits.” Id. at 140. The Fifth Circuit’s rule was not rooted in the text of § 355(f). Indeed, the court declared that “the jurisdiction of the federal courts of appeals is limited to the review of Board decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted.” Id. But the words “merits” and “exhausted” appear nowhere in the statute.

The Roberts court borrowed that limitation from Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), “find[ing] the reasoning of the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh and Tenth circuits persuasive” in light of that decision. Roberts, 346 F.3d at 141. Califano, however, turned on statutory language that has no analogue in the Railroad Retirement Act. The Social Security Act permits review of a “final decision of the Secretary made after a hearing.” 42 U.S.C. § 405(g); see Califano, 430 U.S. at 108. Because denials of reopening are not made after hearing, this Court held they are outside that statute. Id.

Section 355(f) contains no “made after a hearing” requirement. The D.C. Circuit relied on precisely that textual distinction when it rejected the majority view: “the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations.” Stovic v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 826 F.3d 500, 502 (D.C. Cir. 2016). The absence of a hearing requirement makes Califano composite. Id. at 504.

**III. The Majority Circuits’ Reliance on Agency Discretion Is Misplaced**

The majority view cannot be defended as an application of the “committed to agency discretion” exception to judicial review. Harris v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999); Abbruzzese v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995); Gutierrez v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990); Steebe v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983). This Court has recognized that relief from finality may be committed to unreviewable discretion in some statutory schemes. ICC v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers, 482 U.S. 270, 282 (1987). But that principle has no application where, as here, the agency’s regulations confine its reopening authority to specific grounds—“new and material evidence” or “administrative error”—within a specified period. See 20 C.F.R. § 261.2. When an agency’s discretion is bounded by law, courts have “law to apply” and review is appropriate. Citizen to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe, 401 U.S. 402, 410 (1971). The Board’s decision rested on nothing more than its interpretation of the four-year regulatory deadline. That is a legal question, and legal questions are what courts review.

**IV. The Minority Circuits Understand the Act’s Remedial Purpose**

The Second and Eighth Circuits have long recognized jurisdiction over reopening denials. Szostak v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966); Sones v. R.R. Retirement Bd., 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991). Their approach is consistent with the Act’s function as a remedial scheme for railroad workers. If reopening denials are categorically shielded from review, the Board could insulate legal errors—including misapplications of its own regulations—from any correction. The finality and exhaustion policies recognized in the Social Security context serve administrative efficiency, not the protection of legal error. Weinberger v. Salfi, 422 U.S. 749, 765 (1975). Here, Salinas received a hearing, and the hearing officer issued a considered decision on his reopening request. That is precisely the kind of final administrative action that § 355(f) makes reviewable.

For these reasons, the Fifth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed.
