**ARGUMENT**

**THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT GIVE RISE TO A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF DOES NOT ALLEGE MEMBERSHIP IN A CLASS OR GROUP.**

The text, history, and purpose of the Equal Protection Clause confirm that it protects individuals only from discrimination that burdens a protected class or infringes a fundamental right. This Court has never recognized a “class-of-one” theory untethered to group-based discrimination, and the decision below—which permitted such a claim—cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents.

**I. The Equal Protection Clause, by Its Terms and Tradition, Addresses Group-Based Discrimination.**

The Equal Protection Clause provides that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. From its inception, this Clause was understood to prohibit discriminatory classifications that disadvantage a defined group. *Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1880) (holding that the Clause bars laws that “discriminate against” a “class of persons”). This Court has consistently applied the Equal Protection Clause to claims that a law or government action singles out a group for unequal treatment. *See, e.g., City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 440 (1985) (applying rational-basis review to discrimination against the mentally disabled); *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 109 (1949) (upholding a law that distinguished between “those who do and those who do not” engage in a particular activity).

The “class-of-one” theory, by contrast, dispenses with any requirement of group membership. It allows a single individual to challenge government action as irrational, even if the action does not classify or burden any identifiable group. This Court has never endorsed such a theory. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Equal Protection Clause is “essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 216 (1982). That formulation presupposes a comparison between the plaintiff and others who are similarly situated—a comparison that necessarily involves a class or group.

**II. This Court’s Precedents Reject a Free-Standing “Class-of-One” Claim.**

In *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), this Court held that the “class-of-one” theory does not apply in the public employment context, reasoning that such claims would “convert the Equal Protection Clause into a general tort of malicious or arbitrary government action.” *Id.* at 608. The Court explained that the Clause is “not a guarantee against incorrect or ill-advised government decisions.” *Id.* at 599. Although *Engquist* left open the possibility that “class-of-one” claims might exist in other contexts, the Court’s reasoning applies with equal force here. Allowing a plaintiff to challenge government action solely on the ground that it was motivated by “spite” or “ill will” would invite federal courts to second-guess routine administrative decisions, undermining the principle that “the Constitution does not require that all government actions be rational.” *Id.* at 603.

Moreover, this Court has never upheld a “class-of-one” claim in the absence of an allegation that the plaintiff was treated differently from others who are identically situated. *See Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, 564 (2000) (per curiam) (assuming without deciding that such a claim is cognizable, but requiring a showing that the plaintiff was “intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated”). The decision below, however, permitted the claim to proceed based solely on an allegation of “spiteful” motive, without requiring any showing of differential treatment. That approach is inconsistent with the fundamental requirement that an equal protection plaintiff must demonstrate that she was “treated differently from others similarly situated.” *City of Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439.

**III. Recognizing a “Class-of-One” Claim Would Open the Floodgates to Litigation.**

The practical consequences of the decision below are staggering. If every government action that is allegedly motivated by “spite” or “ill will” gives rise to an equal protection claim, then virtually any zoning decision, permit denial, or regulatory enforcement action could be challenged in federal court. This would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a “font of tort law,” *Paul v. Davis*, 424 U.S. 693, 701 (1976), and would “require federal courts to review the reasonableness of almost every administrative act.” *Engquist*, 553 U.S. at 604. The Constitution does not countenance such a result.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
