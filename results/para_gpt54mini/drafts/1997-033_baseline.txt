**Argument**

Section 1407(a) does not permit a transferee district court to keep an MDL case for trial by invoking § 1404(a). Congress directed that actions transferred for multidistrict proceedings “shall be remanded by the panel” to the transferor court “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that where statutory text is clear, courts must give effect to it. *Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). Here, the statute’s command is not merely clear; it is mandatory. *Lexecon, Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 35-40 (1998).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach depended on the view that § 1404(a) supplies an exception because a transferee judge may “transfer” the case to itself for trial. But § 1404(a) authorizes transfer only “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” and it presupposes a transfer from one district to another. 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a); *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964). A court cannot “transfer” a case to the very district in which it already sits. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 41-42. That is not venue transfer; it is an assignment for trial under another name.

The Supreme Court in *Lexecon* squarely rejected the transferee-court self-assignment practice, holding that “the Panel must remand the action to the transferor court after the MDL proceedings have ended.” *Id.* at 35. The Court explained that § 1407(a)’s remand requirement is “obligatory” and that “an order assigning a transferred case to the same district court for trial” cannot be squared with the statute. *Id.* at 40-41. The Court further held that the transferee court may not use § 1404(a) to circumvent remand, because doing so would nullify Congress’s explicit command. *Id.* at 42-43.

That conclusion is consistent with the structure and purpose of § 1407. Congress enacted multidistrict transfer to promote coordinated or consolidated **pretrial** proceedings, not to alter the forum for trial. *In re Korean Air Lines Disaster of September 1, 1983*, 829 F.2d 1171, 1174 (D.C. Cir. 1987). Once common pretrial proceedings are complete, the case returns to the district selected by the usual venue rules and by the plaintiff’s initial filing choice. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 36-40. As the Court explained, if Congress had intended transferee judges to retain cases for trial, it would have said so expressly; instead, it required remand. *Id.* at 38-40.

The contrary rule also creates serious administrability problems. If a transferee court may retain a case for trial under § 1404(a), the mandatory remand language becomes discretionary, and the transferee judge becomes the gatekeeper of the very transfer Congress assigned to the Panel. The Supreme Court rejected that reading because it would allow the “pretrial” court to decide the case’s ultimate forum, undermining the limited role Congress gave MDL transferee courts. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 43.

Nor does the fact that some courts historically allowed self-assignment save the judgment. Prior practice cannot overcome the statute’s text. *Id.* at 40-43; *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). And while convenience may favor keeping a familiar judge on the case, convenience cannot override a command Congress made mandatory. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40-41.

The judgment below therefore rests on legal error. Under § 1407(a), the Arizona court was required to remand Lexecon’s case to the Northern District of Illinois once pretrial proceedings concluded. It had no authority to invoke § 1404(a) to keep the case and assign it to itself for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 41-43. The Court should reverse and hold that MDL transferee courts may not retain transferred actions for trial by using § 1404(a).
