ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY'S CLAIMS UNDER MASS. GEN. LAWS CH. 93A AND CH. 244.

The district court improperly dismissed Foley’s claims under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244, as the complaint sufficiently alleged that Wells Fargo failed to comply with the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement. The settlement agreement imposed specific obligations on Wells Fargo to consider Foley for a MAP2R modification, including providing clear, written explanations of modification denials and notifications in foreclosure-related communications. See Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99, 103 (1945) (emphasizing the importance of considering the obligations imposed by a settlement agreement).

Wells Fargo's failure to provide Foley with a HAMP application in a timely manner and its subsequent failure to consider him for a MAP2R modification constitute a breach of the settlement agreement. The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo's actions were unfair and deceptive, in violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A. See York v. Sullivan, 201 Mass. 32, 35 (1909) (holding that a defendant's actions can be considered unfair and deceptive under ch. 93A if they cause harm to the plaintiff).

Furthermore, Wells Fargo's actions may have also violated Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, which governs foreclosure proceedings. The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to comply with the statutory requirements for foreclosure, including providing Foley with proper notice. See Way v. McLellan, 147 Mass. 296, 298 (1888) (emphasizing the importance of strict compliance with statutory foreclosure requirements).

II. FOLEY'S CLAIMS ARE NOT PREEMPTED BY FEDERAL LAW.

Wells Fargo argues that two of Foley's claims are preempted by the Home Owners' Loan Act ("HOLA"), 12 U.S.C. § 1461 et seq. However, the Supreme Court has held that HOLA preemption is not absolute and that state laws may be enforced in certain circumstances. See First Nat'l Bank v. Commonwealth, 264 U.S. 426, 434 (1924) (holding that state laws may be enforced unless they conflict with federal law).

In this case, Foley's claims under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A and ch. 244 do not conflict with HOLA, as they are based on Wells Fargo's failure to comply with the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement and its unfair and deceptive practices. See Missouri Pac. Ry. Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885) (holding that state laws may be enforced if they are not in conflict with federal law).

Furthermore, the settlement agreement itself contemplates the application of state law to Settlement Class B members, including Foley. See Bank of Augusta v. Earle, 38 U.S. 519, 589 (1839) (emphasizing the importance of considering the terms of a contract or agreement).

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY'S COMMON LAW CLAIMS.

The district court also erred in dismissing Foley's common law claims, as the complaint sufficiently alleged that Wells Fargo breached its contractual and fiduciary duties to Foley. See Davis v. Coburn Corp., 227 Mass. 226, 229 (1917) (holding that a defendant's breach of contractual duties can give rise to liability).

Wells Fargo's actions, as alleged in the complaint, demonstrate a clear breach of its obligations under the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement and its duty of good faith and fair dealing. See Clark v. State St. Trust Co., 270 Mass. 140, 153 (1930) (emphasizing the importance of good faith and fair dealing in contractual relationships).

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Foley's claims, and this Court should reverse the dismissal and remand the case for further proceedings. See Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (emphasizing the importance of applying state law in diversity cases). 

Additionally, as seen in Swift v. Tyson, 41 U.S. 1, 18 (1842), the court has the authority to consider the laws of the state in which the case is being heard. In this case, Massachusetts law should be applied. 

The court should also consider the principles established in Gelpcke v. City of Dubuque, 68 U.S. 175, 206 (1863), where it was held that a party's rights under a contract should be protected.

By reversing the district court's dismissal, this Court can ensure that Foley receives the consideration he is entitled to under the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement and Massachusetts law.
