**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive the right to counsel and plead guilty. This standard, distinct from the competency-to-stand-trial inquiry, ensures that a defendant possesses the mental capacity to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this standard in reversing the district court’s denial of habeas relief. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Due Process Clause Demands a Heightened Standard for Competency to Waive Rights**

The Due Process Clause protects an accused from being "deprived of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This protection extends to the right to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of constitutional rights, including the right to counsel and the right to plead not guilty. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 396 (1993) (citing *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975)). To ensure such waivers are valid, courts must assess whether the defendant possesses the requisite mental capacity to understand the nature of the rights being waived and the consequences of that decision.

This Court has long recognized that competency to waive constitutional rights requires more than a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that a defendant must have the capacity to consult with counsel and assist in his defense—a standard distinct from the "reasoned choice" inquiry. However, when a defendant seeks to waive fundamental rights, such as the right to counsel or to plead guilty, the inquiry is more exacting. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399-400 (citing *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 313-14 (1966) (per curiam)).

The Ninth Circuit correctly articulated this heightened standard in *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263 (9th Cir. 1992), aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds, 509 U.S. 389 (1993). The court held that a defendant must possess the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the alternatives available to him when waiving constitutional rights. *Id.* at 267 (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871 (1988)). This standard ensures that the waiver is not the product of confusion, impairment, or incapacity. *Id.* (citing *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986)).

**II. The Trial Court Failed to Apply the Heightened Standard**

The trial court in this case did not apply the "reasoned choice" standard when Moran sought to waive his right to counsel and plead guilty. Instead, the court relied solely on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s competency to stand trial—not his capacity to make a reasoned decision about waiving his constitutional rights. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 270-71. This was error.

The competency-to-stand-trial standard, as articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), requires only that a defendant have "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and a "rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. By contrast, the "reasoned choice" standard demands more: it requires that the defendant understand the alternatives available to him and the consequences of his decisions. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 267 (citing *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856).

Here, the trial court accepted Moran’s waiver without inquiring into whether he possessed the capacity to make a reasoned choice. The record reflects substantial evidence calling Moran’s competency into doubt: he had recently attempted suicide, was heavily medicated, and provided monosyllabic responses to the court’s questions. *Id.* at 265-66. Under these circumstances, the trial court had an affirmative duty to initiate a competency hearing sua sponte. *Id.* at 267 (citing *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973)).

**III. Post-Conviction Proceedings Cannot Cure the Error**

The state post-conviction court compounded the error by applying the wrong legal standard. It relied on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s competency to stand trial, not his capacity for reasoned choice. *Id.* at 270-71. This was reversible error. As this Court has held, "a state court’s determination of competency to plead guilty is entitled to no presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)" when the wrong legal standard is applied. *Creech v. Arave*, 947 F.2d 873, 879 n.5 (9th Cir. 1991), cert. granted, 503 U.S. 976 (1992).

Moreover, the post-conviction hearing occurred years after the plea, and the psychiatric evaluations did not address Moran’s capacity to make a reasoned choice at the time of the plea. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 271. The trial court’s failure to apply the heightened standard at the time of the plea cannot be cured retroactively. *Id.* at 272 (citing *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963)).

**IV. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Applied the Law**

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the trial court’s failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard violated due process. The court held that due process requires a trial court to initiate a competency hearing whenever it has or should have a "good faith doubt" about the defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights. *Id.* at 267 (citing *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856). This standard is consistent with this Court’s precedents, which require heightened scrutiny when fundamental rights are at stake. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399-400.

**CONCLUSION**

The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive the right to counsel and plead guilty. The trial court in this case failed to apply this standard, and the post-conviction proceedings did not cure the error. The Ninth Circuit correctly reversed the district court’s denial of habeas relief. This Court should affirm.
