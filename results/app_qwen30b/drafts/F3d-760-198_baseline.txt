**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BASED ON A NARROW, FORMULAIC INTERPRETATION OF “SEXUALLY OFFENSIVE ACTS.”

The district court erred in concluding that Moll’s hostile work environment claim failed because she did not allege any “sexually offensive acts” within the applicable statute of limitations. That conclusion rested on a fundamental misreading of the law: the Second Circuit has long held that a hostile work environment claim under Title VII may be based on a *pattern* of conduct, including facially sex-neutral incidents, when those incidents are “sufficiently severe or pervasive to create a work environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive,” and when they are “motivated by sex.” See *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002). The court’s error was not merely procedural—it was substantive, as it failed to consider the totality of the circumstances, including non-sexually offensive conduct that, when viewed in context, supports a claim of sex-based hostility.

The Second Circuit has made clear that “sexually offensive” acts need not be overtly sexual in nature. *Id.* at 375. Instead, “the critical inquiry is whether the conduct was motivated by sex.” *Id.* Thus, conduct that is facially neutral—such as being denied a vacation, being prohibited from working remotely, or being excluded from social events—may still constitute a hostile work environment if it is part of a broader pattern of discrimination based on sex. See *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 988 (D.C. Cir. 1977) (holding that “a hostile environment may be created by conduct that is not overtly sexual but is nonetheless motivated by sex”).

Here, Moll alleged a sustained pattern of sex-based discrimination that, when viewed in its totality, created a hostile work environment. The district court improperly isolated the sexually suggestive notes and phone calls—some of which predated the limitations period—and dismissed the entire claim, ignoring the broader context of discriminatory treatment that occurred within the limitations period. Moll alleged that after Irving became her supervisor in 2001, he restricted her communication to in-person meetings, denied her the ability to work from home despite male colleagues being permitted to do so, and denied her vacation requests while granting them to male employees with less tenure. These incidents, though facially neutral, were part of a consistent pattern of differential treatment that, when combined with the sexually suggestive conduct, support a reasonable inference of a hostile environment motivated by sex.

Moreover, Moll was excluded from work-related social events—such as professional hockey games—events that were attended by her male colleagues. Exclusion from such events, particularly when it occurs systematically and disproportionately against women, can contribute to a hostile work environment. See *Barnes*, 561 F.2d at 988 (“The cumulative effect of a series of discriminatory acts may be sufficient to create a hostile environment.”). The district court’s failure to consider these incidents in context rendered its analysis legally flawed.

The Second Circuit has repeatedly emphasized that courts must evaluate hostile work environment claims “in the context of the entire record,” not by “isolating individual incidents.” *Alfano*, 294 F.3d at 375. The district court’s approach—focusing only on the absence of sexually offensive acts within the limitations period—violated this principle. It effectively required Moll to prove a claim based on a single, isolated category of conduct, contrary to established precedent.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING MOLL’S MOTION TO COMPEL PRODUCTION OF DOCUMENTS.

The district court also erred in denying Moll’s motion to compel production of documents related to the transfer of the Buffalo Sales Engineers to Syracuse. Moll sought documents showing the rationale for the transfer, including internal communications, performance evaluations, and records of the severance package offered to her. These documents were critical to her claim that the transfer was retaliatory and part of a broader pattern of discrimination.

Verizon’s refusal to produce these documents, without a valid privilege or good-faith basis, constituted a failure to comply with discovery obligations under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 37. The district court’s denial of the motion to compel, without a clear explanation or finding of good cause, undermined Moll’s ability to present a complete defense and to establish a prima facie case of retaliation.

The Second Circuit has held that “a party may not defeat discovery by simply asserting a privilege without providing a sufficient basis for the claim.” *In re Grand Jury Subpoena*, 228 F.3d 105, 111 (2d Cir. 2000). Here, Verizon offered no such basis. The documents sought were directly relevant to Moll’s claim that the transfer was retaliatory and that she was forced into a disadvantageous position due to her protected activity. The denial of discovery, therefore, prejudiced Moll’s ability to litigate her claims and warrants reversal.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE PAY DISPARITY CLAIM.

The district court granted summary judgment on Moll’s pay disparity claim under the Equal Pay Act and Title VII, concluding that Moll failed to show that she was paid less than male employees for equal work. However, Moll presented evidence that she was the lowest-paid Sales Engineer in the Buffalo office in 2002, while two male colleagues were promoted during the same period. This evidence, when viewed in context, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether the pay disparity was based on sex.

The Equal Pay Act requires only that the plaintiff show that she performed equal work under similar working conditions. *29 U.S.C. § 206(d)(1)*. Moll’s allegations, supported by her own testimony and the comparative pay data, create a triable issue of fact. The district court’s failure to consider this evidence in the light most favorable to Moll was error.
