ARGUMENT

A district court's omission of the Rule 32 advice concerning appeal rights furnishes no ground for collateral relief when the defendant already knew of the right and declined to exercise it. Hill v. United States, 368 U.S. 424, 428-29 (1962). Collateral attack remains an extraordinary remedy, available only when the error is jurisdictional or constitutes a fundamental defect that inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice. Sunal v. Large, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947); United States v. Morgan, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954).

Harmless-error principles govern even constitutional claims on collateral review. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946); Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967). Where the record establishes that the defendant possessed independent knowledge of the right to appeal, the omission causes no prejudice and the judgment stands. Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 275 (1942).

A knowing and intelligent waiver of appellate review bars later collateral challenge. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938); Waley v. Johnston, 316 U.S. 101, 104 (1942). The evidentiary finding that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to pursue it therefore precludes relief. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963).

Federal courts have long declined to disturb final criminal judgments on the basis of technical violations that inflict no actual injury. Ex parte Parks, 93 U.S. 18, 23 (1876); Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 375 (1879); Goto v. Lane, 265 U.S. 393, 401 (1924). The same restraint applies here. Because the purpose of the rule was fulfilled by Peguero's actual knowledge, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
