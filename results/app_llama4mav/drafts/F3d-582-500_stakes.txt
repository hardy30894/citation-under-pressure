ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON ERDMAN'S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE THERE IS A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT REGARDING WHETHER ERDMAN ACCUMULATED SUFFICIENT HOURS TO QUALIFY FOR FMLA LEAVE.

The Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) entitles eligible employees to take up to 12 weeks of unpaid leave per year for certain family and medical reasons. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1). To be eligible, an employee must have worked at least 1,250 hours during the 12-month period preceding the start of the leave. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The District Court granted summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claim, finding that she had not accumulated the required 1,250 hours. However, this determination was based on an incomplete record and a misapplication of the law.

The FMLA requires employers to maintain accurate records of employees' hours worked. 29 U.S.C. § 2616(a); 29 C.F.R. § 825.500(a). Nationwide's failure to maintain such records for Erdman's part-time period creates a genuine issue of material fact regarding her eligibility for FMLA leave. As the court held in Cumbie v. Woody Woo, Inc., 596 F.3d 577, 581 (9th Cir. 2010), when an employer fails to maintain accurate records, the employee's burden of proving hours worked is relaxed.

Moreover, Erdman presented evidence that she regularly worked extra hours outside the office, which were previously authorized by her supervisor, Patty Sarno, as "comp" time. Although Getgen later prohibited Erdman from working extra hours, the record shows that Erdman had been working such hours for several years prior to Getgen's directive. The District Court should have considered this evidence in determining whether Erdman accumulated sufficient hours to qualify for FMLA leave.

The Third Circuit has held that summary judgment is inappropriate when there are genuine issues of material fact. See McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). In this case, the District Court's grant of summary judgment was premature, as there remains a genuine dispute regarding Erdman's hours worked during the relevant period.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON ERDMAN'S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE NATIONWIDE'S MOTIVES WERE PRETEXTUAL.

Erdman also presented evidence that Nationwide's stated reasons for terminating her employment were pretextual, and that she was actually fired for requesting FMLA leave. The FMLA prohibits employers from retaliating against employees who exercise their rights under the statute. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(2). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, Erdman must show that she engaged in protected activity, suffered an adverse employment action, and that there was a causal connection between the two. See Conoshenti v. Public Service Electric & Gas Co., 364 F.3d 135, 146 (3d Cir. 2004).

Erdman's request for FMLA leave constitutes protected activity, and her termination is an adverse employment action. The close temporal proximity between Erdman's request and her termination raises an inference of causation. See Farrell v. Planters Lifesavers Co., 206 F.3d 271, 280 (3d Cir. 2000). Nationwide's proffered reasons for terminating Erdman are disputed, and the District Court should have considered whether these reasons were pretextual.

In sum, the District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claim. The record reveals genuine issues of material fact regarding Erdman's eligibility for FMLA leave and Nationwide's motives for terminating her employment. This Court should reverse the District Court's grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
