**Argument**

The Board’s polling rule is not rational under the Act because it divorces the employer’s right to test union support from the very evidentiary showing that makes testing necessary. The National Labor Relations Act protects employees’ freedom of choice in selecting or rejecting representation, and it permits the Board to fashion procedures that safeguard that freedom. See *NLRB v. A.J. Tower Co.*, 329 U.S. 324, 330-31 (1946); *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226-27 (1940). But agency discretion ends when a rule becomes arbitrary. See *NBC v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 224-25 (1943). Here, the Board allows an employer to poll employees only after objective evidence already justifies withdrawal of recognition—yet the very purpose of a poll is to determine whether that evidence is sufficient. That rule is internally inconsistent and cannot be squared with the Act’s policy of informed employee choice. See *A.J. Tower*, 329 U.S. at 330-31; *Waterman*, 309 U.S. at 226-27.

This Court’s precedents show that the Board may insist upon orderly procedures, but not upon standards that make lawful testing of majority status practically impossible. In *A.J. Tower*, the Court upheld the Board’s authority to regulate election mechanics because the Act committed to the Board the responsibility to secure “fair and free” choice. 329 U.S. at 330-31. In *Mayo v. Lakeland Highlands Canning Co.*, 309 U.S. 310, 321-22 (1940), the Court likewise recognized the Board’s power to adopt means reasonably adapted to the Act’s policies. But the Board’s polling rule is not reasonably adapted to those policies. It allows polling only when the employer already has enough proof to act without polling. That does not protect employee free choice; it suppresses a procedure that would reduce uncertainty before the employer makes a legally consequential decision. A rule that eliminates the practical value of a lawful inquiry is not a rational regulation of that inquiry. See *NBC*, 319 U.S. at 224-25.

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The substantial-evidence standard requires that findings rest on “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate.” *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). It is not enough that the Board prefer one inference over another where the record as a whole points the other way. See *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488, 491, 496-97 (1951); *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300, 305-10 (1939).

Here, the record showed that employees made direct statements indicating that the union had lost majority support. A poll was conducted by secret ballot and revealed a 19-to-13 vote against the union. The Board discounted those facts and treated only a subset of employee statements as probative. But substantial evidence review requires consideration of the whole record, not selective counting. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488, 491. The question is whether the evidence would compel a reasonable trier of fact to conclude that the employer genuinely doubted majority support. *Consolidated Edison*, 305 U.S. at 229. On this record, the Board’s conclusion that Allentown lacked such doubt is not supportable.

The Board’s treatment of employee expressions of dissatisfaction also cannot stand. This Court has long distinguished between protected sentiments and the weight to be given particular evidence under the Act. See *NLRB v. Hudson Motor Car Co.*, 128 F.2d 528, 532-33 (6th Cir. 1942), aff’d, 132 F.2d 952 (6th Cir. 1943). When employees say they do not want union representation, the Board may not dismiss that evidence by recharacterizing it as mere dissatisfaction with union administration if, taken in context, it bears directly on majority support. See *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488, 491. Nor may the Board ignore corroborating statements simply because each item, standing alone, is not conclusive. The Act asks whether the employer had objective grounds for doubt, not proof beyond dispute. *Columbian Enameling*, 306 U.S. at 300, 305-10.

Finally, the Board’s order rests on a premise foreign to the Act: that uncertainty must be resolved against the employer even when the employer seeks only to test the union’s status through a fair poll. The Act protects representative stability, but it also protects employees from being bound to an organization that no longer enjoys majority support. See *A.J. Tower*, 329 U.S. at 330-31; *Mayo*, 309 U.S. at 321-22. Allentown’s evidence, including the employee statements and the carefully supervised poll, was more than enough to create a reasonable doubt. Because the Board’s contrary finding is unsupported by substantial evidence and its polling rule is irrational, the order should be set aside.
