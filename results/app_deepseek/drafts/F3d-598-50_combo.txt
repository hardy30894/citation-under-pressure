**ARGUMENT**

The district court committed reversible error by permitting two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant to testify to their opinions on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, on the existence of probable cause, and on the strength of the evidence. Such testimony invaded the province of the jury and violated fundamental evidentiary rules.

It is well established that a witness may not express an opinion on the credibility of another witness. *United States v. Rappy*, 157 F.2d 964, 967 (2d Cir. 1946) (error to allow witness to testify that another witness was truthful). The ADAs and lieutenant directly vouched for Ramos and Rivera, telling the jury that the officers were credible. This usurped the jury’s exclusive role. *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 519 (1943) (“The credibility of a witness is for the jury to determine.”); *United States v. Bender*, 218 F.2d 869, 871 (7th Cir. 1955) (witness cannot vouch for another’s truthfulness).

Nor may a witness testify to a legal conclusion, such as whether probable cause existed. *United States v. 5 Cases*, 179 F.2d 519, 522 (2d Cir. 1950) (expert cannot testify that product is “misbranded” because that is a legal conclusion). The ADAs and lieutenant opined that probable cause supported the arrests and prosecution—a pure legal determination for the jury. *United States v. 88 Cases*, 187 F.2d 967, 971 (3d Cir. 1951) (opinion that product is “adulterated” is inadmissible legal conclusion). The ultimate issue of probable cause is for the jury, not for witnesses. *United States v. Spaulding*, 293 U.S. 498, 506 (1935) (opinion on ultimate issue inadmissible if it invades province of jury).

A witness may not interpret evidence for the jury, as that is the jury’s exclusive function. *United States v. Allied Stevedoring Corp.*, 241 F.2d 925, 930 (2d Cir. 1957) (witness cannot give opinion on meaning of documentary evidence). Here, the ADAs and lieutenant explained how certain evidence strengthened or weakened Appellants’ case, effectively telling the jury what to conclude from the record.

These errors were not harmless. The case turned on credibility and probable cause—the very subjects of the improper testimony. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (error is not harmless if it may have influenced the jury). The photographs did not definitively resolve the dispute, so the jury likely relied on the ostensible neutrality of the government witnesses. A new trial is required.

Additionally, the district court erred in its jury instructions. On remand, the court should instruct that under New York law, obstructing governmental administration requires that the officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *People v. Williams*, 25 N.Y.2d 86, 89 (1969). The court should also give a punitive damages instruction because Appellants’ evidence of fabrication supports an inference of malice. These errors, combined with the evidentiary errors, compel reversal.
