## Argument

### I. The district court erred in holding that Pakistan is immune under the FSIA because the complaint alleges conduct falling within the commercial-activity exception.

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act provides that a foreign state is not immune where “the action is based . . . upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has instructed that the FSIA’s commercial-activity inquiry turns on the “nature” of the conduct, not its purpose. Saudi Arabia v. Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 360-61 (1993); Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). A state acts commercially when it engages in “the type of action by which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce.” Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614. By contrast, sovereign acts such as expropriation, police power, and detention are not commercial merely because they are accompanied by later negotiations or compensation disputes. Nelson, 507 U.S. at 361-62.

But Beg’s claim is not limited to the original taking. He alleges that after Pakistan expropriated his land, the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States and offered him substitute land in exchange for the expropriated property, that the exchange was accepted by the Lahore High Court, and that Pakistan later refused to honor or complete the transaction. Those allegations describe a commercial land exchange and a settlement-style negotiation, not a uniquely sovereign act. A private party can buy, sell, and exchange real property; a sovereign’s participation in such a transaction is commercial when it acts as a market participant. See Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614; Connecticut Bank of Commerce v. Republic of Congo, 309 F.3d 240, 253-54 (5th Cir. 2002) (sovereign’s contractual obligations may constitute commercial activity where the state acts “in the manner of a private player in the market”); de Sanchez v. Banco Central de Nicaragua, 770 F.2d 1385, 1392-93 (5th Cir. 1985) (commercial activity includes acts “in which a private party engages in trade and traffic or commerce”).  

The district court focused on the initial taking and concluded that Pakistan “had not engaged in any commercial activity.” That analysis is too narrow. Under the FSIA, the court must look to the gravamen of the suit and the particular acts on which the action is based. Nelson, 507 U.S. at 357; Guevara v. Republic of Peru, 468 F.3d 1289, 1295-96 (11th Cir. 2006) (en banc). Here, Beg seeks relief not only for expropriation but also for Pakistan’s later refusal to carry out a land-for-land exchange and its failure to compensate him after the government initiated and accepted a specific replacement transaction. A sovereign does not shed immunity merely because a commercial dealing is related to an expropriation. Rather, when the challenged conduct itself is commercial in nature, immunity does not attach. Weltover, 504 U.S. at 614; Guevara, 468 F.3d at 1296-97.

### II. The complaint also satisfies the third clause of § 1605(a)(2) because Pakistan’s conduct caused a direct effect in the United States.

A “direct effect” under § 1605(a)(2) is one that follows “as an immediate consequence of the defendant’s . . . activity.” Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618. The effect need not be substantial or foreseeable; it need only be legally and immediately caused by the foreign sovereign’s act. Id.; Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 941 F.2d 145, 152-53 (2d Cir. 1991), aff’d, 504 U.S. 607 (1992). The Eleventh Circuit has likewise recognized that the direct-effect requirement is satisfied when the foreign state’s conduct has a concrete, legally operative consequence in this country. Vermeulen v. Renault, U.S.A., Inc., 985 F.2d 1534, 1548-49 (11th Cir. 1993).

Beg alleges that a representative of Punjab came to the United States to offer a substitute parcel of land, thereby creating an interstate nexus between Pakistan’s refusal to compensate and Beg’s position in the United States. At minimum, the allegations permit an inference that Pakistan’s conduct was aimed at resolving the property dispute through a transaction with a United States-based effect: Beg’s loss of the promised substitute property or compensation while he pursued relief from the United States. See Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618; Vermeulen, 985 F.2d at 1548-49. The district court therefore erred in resolving the jurisdictional issue against Beg at the pleading stage.

### III. At the jurisdictional stage, Beg was entitled to have his well-pleaded allegations accepted and to proceed to discovery if necessary.

When subject matter jurisdiction turns on disputed FSIA facts, a plaintiff need only allege facts that, if true, would defeat immunity. Butler v. Sukhoi Co., 579 F.3d 1307, 1312-13 (11th Cir. 2009); Phoenix Consulting, Inc. v. Republic of Angola, 216 F.3d 36, 39-40 (D.C. Cir. 2000). The district court’s dismissal effectively resolved factual ambiguities against Beg, despite allegations that Pakistan’s officials negotiated a substitute land exchange and that the dispute centered on a compensatory arrangement that the government failed to honor. Because those allegations plausibly invoke § 1605(a)(2), dismissal was premature. Butler, 579 F.3d at 1312-13; Phoenix Consulting, 216 F.3d at 39-40.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
