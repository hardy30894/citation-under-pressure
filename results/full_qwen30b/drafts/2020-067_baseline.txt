**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may consider matters outside the trial record when reviewing for plain error under Rule 52(b)—is answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied settled precedent in concluding that a reviewing court may look beyond the jury’s verdict and the trial record to assess whether an error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceeding. This is not an expansion of plain error review; it is a faithful application of the rule’s purpose and the Supreme Court’s own jurisprudence.

Rule 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct “plain errors or defects affecting substantial rights” even when no objection was made at trial. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The rule is “not a license to reweigh the evidence, reevaluate the credibility of witnesses, or substitute the appellate court’s judgment for that of the trial court.” Id. But it is also not a straitjacket that confines appellate review to the narrow confines of the trial record. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings” may be compromised by errors that are not apparent from the record alone. *Id.* at 736. To determine whether such a risk exists, courts must consider the full context of the case, including facts that may not have been presented to the jury but are nonetheless relevant to the defendant’s rights.

In *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019), the Court held that the government must prove both that a defendant knew he possessed a firearm and that he knew he was a felon at the time of possession. The Court did not limit its inquiry to the jury instructions or the evidence presented at trial. Instead, it emphasized that “the jury must be instructed on the elements of the offense,” and that “the government must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt.” *Id.* at 1355. The Court’s analysis rested on the structure of the statute and the constitutional requirement of notice and proof—concerns that transcend the trial record. The Court did not require the defendant to show that the jury was misled by the instruction; it required the government to prove the defendant’s knowledge of his status. That inquiry necessarily involves assessing the totality of the circumstances, including the defendant’s background, prior convictions, and the nature of the offense.

The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority in considering Greer’s prior convictions and the full factual context of his case. The court’s review was not a de novo determination of guilt or innocence. It was a narrow, fact-specific inquiry into whether the failure to instruct on the *Rehaif* element—knowledge of status—affected Greer’s substantial rights. The court examined the record, including Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions under Ohio law, to assess whether the error was likely to have influenced the outcome. This is precisely the kind of inquiry Rule 52(b) permits. As the Supreme Court has stated, “the existence of plain error is not to be determined by a mechanical application of a formula, but by a careful examination of the entire record.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736.

Moreover, the court’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions was not improper. Those convictions were part of the plea agreement and were used to trigger the ACCA enhancement. They were not introduced for the purpose of proving guilt but to assess the impact of the instructional error. The court did not speculate or rely on hearsay. It considered only facts that were already before the district court and that were relevant to the question of prejudice. This is consistent with *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), where the Court held that appellate courts may consider “the entire record” in determining whether an error affected substantial rights. The Court emphasized that “the plain error standard is not a rigid rule,” but a “flexible” one designed to “prevent miscarriages of justice.” *Id.* at 61.

Greer’s argument that the court should have confined its review to the jury’s verdict and the evidence presented at trial misreads the scope of plain error. The rule is not a procedural technicality; it is a remedial tool to preserve the integrity of the judicial process. If a court were barred from considering any fact outside the jury’s view, it would be unable to assess whether an error was truly “plain” or whether it “affected substantial rights.” That would render the rule toothless in cases where the error is not apparent from the record alone—precisely the kind of case *Rehaif* presents.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with the broader tradition of appellate review. Courts routinely consider sentencing records, plea agreements, and other documents not presented to the jury when assessing the impact of errors. *See, e.g., United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984) (appellate court may consider record to determine whether counsel’s performance was deficient). The rule does not require the appellate court to “reconstruct” the trial, but to “evaluate the effect of the error in light of the entire record.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736.

For these reasons, the Eleventh Circuit did not err in reviewing matters outside the trial record. Its conclusion—that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice or that the error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial—was supported by the record and consistent with precedent. The judgment should be affirmed.
