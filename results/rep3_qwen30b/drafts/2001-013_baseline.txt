**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations occurring aboard the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation *per se* preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act, regardless of whether the vessel is inspected or uninspected. This interpretation misreads both the statutory text and controlling precedent, and would create an impermissible, categorical exemption that Congress never intended.

**I. The OSH Act’s jurisdiction extends to uninspected vessels, including drilling barges, where the workplace is not otherwise subject to exclusive federal regulation.**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the Act’s reach: it applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is broadly defined to include “any building, structure, facility, or piece of equipment where employees are employed.” *Barnes v. OSHRC*, 737 F.2d 1288, 1291 (5th Cir. 1984). The *MR. BELDON* was a fixed, non-self-propelled drilling barge engaged in continuous industrial operations—drilling an oil well—on navigable waters within Louisiana’s territorial jurisdiction. It was not a vessel in navigation in the traditional sense, but rather a floating industrial platform. The Administrative Law Judge correctly found that the *MR. BELDON* constituted a “workplace” under the Act. See *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, OSHRC Docket No. 98-1012 (2000). The fact that the barge was not self-propelled and was moored in place for extended periods distinguishes it from vessels in active navigation, and supports its classification as a workplace under the OSH Act.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard’s authority is “exclusive” over all seamen on vessels in navigation, regardless of inspection status, misapplies *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983). In *Clary*, this Court held that OSHA regulations do not apply to “working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” 609 F.2d at 1122. But the Court did not purport to define the scope of “vessels in navigation” or to exclude uninspected vessels from OSHA’s reach. Indeed, the *Clary* opinion makes no mention of inspection status, and the barge in that case was not described as inspected. The Court’s holding was grounded in the *functional* distinction between vessels engaged in navigation and fixed industrial platforms—precisely the distinction at issue here.

The Fifth Circuit’s categorical rule—that *any* vessel with seamen is beyond OSHA’s reach—contradicts the statutory framework. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that OSHA’s jurisdiction is preempted only “with respect to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This is not a blanket exclusion; it is a narrow, case-specific preemption triggered only when another agency has *statutory authority* to regulate the *specific working conditions* at issue. *See United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 102 (2000) (“The preemption provision is not a general bar to OSHA jurisdiction, but a limitation on it.”).

**II. The Coast Guard does not have exclusive authority over the working conditions of employees on uninspected drilling barges such as the *MR. BELDON*.**

The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 2 is broad, but not unlimited. It “shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States covering all matters not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department.” 46 U.S.C. § 2. However, this authority does not extend to all occupational safety and health matters on vessels. The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels—such as those governing fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and emergency alerting systems—focus on *maritime safety*, not *occupational health*. See 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. These are not comprehensive occupational safety standards; they do not address, for example, exposure to hazardous chemicals, machine guarding, or emergency response procedures for industrial accidents.

In *Donovan*, this Court emphasized that the Coast Guard’s authority is “comprehensive” *only* in the context of maritime safety, not in the broader realm of occupational health. 720 F.2d at 826. The Court recognized that “OSHA’s geographic limitations” make it ill-suited to regulate the entire maritime industry uniformly. *Id.* at 829. But that very concern underscores why OSHA’s jurisdiction is not preempted in cases like this: the Coast Guard’s regulations do not cover the full spectrum of occupational safety issues that arise in industrial operations on fixed platforms. The explosion on the *MR. BELDON* involved a blowout preventer failure and inadequate emergency procedures—issues that fall squarely within OSHA’s expertise and are not addressed by Coast Guard regulations for uninspected vessels.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation would create a dangerous loophole for employers of seamen on uninspected vessels.**

If the Fifth Circuit’s rule stands, employers could avoid OSHA oversight simply by operating uninspected vessels, even when those vessels are engaged in high-risk industrial work. This would allow companies to circumvent federal safety standards by exploiting the Coast Guard’s limited regulatory scope. Congress did not intend such a result.
