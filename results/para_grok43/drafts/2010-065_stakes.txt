ARGUMENT

I. A Private Defendant Lacks Standing to Assert That a Federal Statute Violates the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States or the people those powers not delegated to the United States. U.S. Const. amend. X. A criminal defendant prosecuted under a validly enacted federal statute may not invoke that reservation to challenge Congress’s authority. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies contended that federal operation of power facilities invaded powers reserved to the States. The Court held that the companies had “no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment,” because “[t]here is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states.” Id. at 144.

That holding controls here. Bond, a private citizen, asserts that 18 U.S.C. § 229 intrudes upon powers reserved to the States. No State has objected. Under Tennessee Electric, she therefore “absent the states or their officers, have no standing” to litigate the Tenth Amendment claim. 306 U.S. at 144.

The same principle appears in Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83 (1998), which requires courts to resolve jurisdictional questions, including standing, before reaching the merits. Id. at 101-02. Because Bond’s Tenth Amendment objection presents a question of “whether the action of the United States is an invasion of the reserved powers of the states,” Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 144, and because the States have not asserted any invasion, the claim lies outside Article III.

The Fifth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Second Circuits have applied this rule to deny private-party standing. See, e.g., United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004). Although two circuits have permitted such challenges, those decisions cannot override the Supreme Court’s explicit statement that private litigants lack standing to enforce the Tenth Amendment when the affected States remain silent.

Bond’s prosecution rests on legislation enacted to implement a treaty. Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920). Whether that legislation is necessary and proper is a question the States alone may raise. Because no State has done so, Bond may not litigate the federalism issue. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
