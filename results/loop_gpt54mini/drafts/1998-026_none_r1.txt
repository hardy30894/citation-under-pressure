### Argument

The judgment should be affirmed because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal does not warrant habeas relief where the defendant already knew of that right and chose not to appeal. Habeas corpus is not a vehicle for correcting every procedural omission; it lies only for errors that produce a fundamental defect or a miscarriage of justice. See *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178, 181 (1947) (habeas is not a substitute for appeal); *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (collateral relief is unavailable for a mere failure to comply with the formal requirements of a rule); *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202–03 (1830) (collateral review is limited to jurisdictional or similarly fundamental defects).

Rule 32(a)(2) was designed to ensure that a defendant who might otherwise be unaware of the right to appeal is informed in time to act. That purpose is fully satisfied when the defendant already knows of the right. The omission of the formal advisement therefore causes no prejudice. This Court has long rejected automatic reversal for errors that do not affect substantial rights. See *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760, 764–65 (1946) (error warrants relief only if it had a substantial influence on the judgment); *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963) (asking whether the error had any effect on the result); *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896) (declining to reverse for technical error absent prejudice). The same principle controls here: once the district court found that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at sentencing, the court’s failure to repeat the warning did not impair any opportunity for review.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would convert a notice requirement into an automatic vacatur provision, even where the omission caused no harm. That approach is inconsistent with the Court’s repeated insistence that criminal judgments should not be set aside for nonprejudicial irregularities. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942), instructs that claimed errors must be evaluated in context. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), likewise reflects the settled rule that only errors affecting substantial rights justify relief. And *Riddle v. Dyche*, 262 U.S. 333, 335–36 (1923), confirms that collateral relief is not available for defects that do not infect the legality of the conviction or sentence.

The finality interests underlying habeas law further support affirmance. Once a defendant had actual knowledge of his right to appeal and declined to exercise it, there is no injustice to remedy by reopening the sentence years later. See *Baldwin v. Traveling Men’s Assn.*, 283 U.S. 522, 525 (1931) (litigation must end after a fair opportunity to be heard); *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 468–69 (1938) (collateral relief is reserved for fundamental constitutional infirmities, not every procedural omission); *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (factual findings are accepted unless clearly erroneous). Here, the district court’s factual finding that Peguero knew of his appeal right forecloses any claim of prejudice.

In short, Rule 32(a)(2) ensures notice, not a windfall. Where the defendant already knew of the appeal right and elected not to appeal, the rule’s purpose was satisfied, the omission was harmless, and habeas relief is unavailable. The Court should therefore affirm the judgment below.
