**ARGUMENT**

The decision below must be reversed. The District Court certified a class without requiring adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This Court has consistently held that a class action cannot be maintained where individualized proof of damages predominates over common questions. The District Court’s acceptance of a damages model untethered to the sole theory of liability it permitted was an abuse of discretion, and the Court of Appeals erred in affirming.

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Certifying a Class Without a Damages Model Capable of Proving Class-Wide Injury**

The predominance inquiry under Rule 23(b)(3) requires a district court to determine whether “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” This determination demands a rigorous analysis of the evidence, not merely a review of the pleadings. *See Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 45 (1940) (holding that a class action cannot be maintained where the issues are “not common to the class”); *Snyder v. Harris*, 394 U.S. 332, 340 (1969) (emphasizing that Rule 23 requires a common question of law or fact that predominates over individual issues).

Here, the District Court accepted Plaintiffs’ expert Dr. McClave’s damages model even though that model was not tethered to the sole theory of antitrust impact the court had permitted—deterrence of overbuilder entry. The model calculated damages based on a benchmark of cable prices from other markets, but it did not isolate the effect of overbuilder deterrence from other potential causes of higher prices, such as increased programming costs or improved service quality. A damages model that does not measure the impact of the specific anticompetitive conduct alleged cannot satisfy Rule 23(b)(3). *See Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931) (holding that while damages need not be proven with “mathematical precision,” there must be a “just and reasonable inference” from the evidence); *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (requiring that the evidence “afford a reasonable basis for the inference” of damages). A model that assumes the answer—that all price differences are attributable to the challenged conduct—cannot provide such an inference.

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on an Unsupported Damages Model Was Clearly Erroneous and an Abuse of Discretion**

The District Court’s finding that Dr. McClave’s model could measure class-wide damages was clearly erroneous. The model did not measure the impact of the alleged anticompetitive conduct—deterrence of overbuilders—but instead measured the difference between Comcast’s prices and prices in other markets, without accounting for any non-antitrust factors. This approach conflates correlation with causation. *See Standard Oil Co. v. United States*, 221 U.S. 1, 62 (1911) (rejecting a damages theory that failed to isolate the effect of the challenged conduct from other market factors). A district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class based on a damages model that is so riddled with flaws that it cannot possibly establish a common method of proving injury. *See Albrecht v. Herald Co.*, 390 U.S. 145, 154 (1968) (requiring that antitrust damages be proven with “reasonable certainty” and not through “speculation or guesswork”).

Moreover, the District Court failed to require that the model be capable of application to each class member’s individual circumstances. This Court has long recognized that a class action cannot be maintained where the issues are “not common to the class.” *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 45. Here, the District Court’s analysis was anything but rigorous; it accepted the expert’s model at face value without scrutinizing whether it could actually prove class-wide injury.

**III. The Court of Appeals Erred by Applying an Overly Deferential Standard of Review**

The Court of Appeals applied an abuse-of-discretion standard and then deferred to the District Court’s factual findings under a “clearly erroneous” standard. But a finding is clearly erroneous when it is “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support.” *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). The District Court’s acceptance of Dr. McClave’s model was devoid of such support because the model did not measure the impact of the only theory of liability the court had permitted. The Court of Appeals therefore erred in affirming.

This Court has long recognized that a district court abuses its discretion when it certifies a class without ensuring that common questions predominate. *See Snyder*, 394 U.S. at 340. Here, the individual issues surrounding damages—whether each class member was actually injured by overbuilder deterrence, and to what extent—plainly predominate over any common questions. The District Court’s certification thus cannot stand.

**IV. The District Court’s Certification Violated the Requirement That Antitrust Impact Be Proven by Common Evidence**

Antitrust impact—the fact of injury—must be proven by common evidence in a class action. *See Klor’s, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 207, 213 (1959) (requiring that antitrust plaintiffs prove “actual injury” to competition). Here, the District Court permitted Plaintiffs to proceed on a single theory—deterrence of overbuilders—but the evidence of impact was not common to all class members. Some class members may have been affected by overbuilder deterrence; others may not have been, depending on where they lived and whether an overbuilder was actually deterred from entering that specific area. The District Court’s finding that impact could be proven by common evidence was therefore clearly erroneous. *See United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, 395 (1956) (holding that antitrust impact must be proven by evidence that “relates to the market as a whole,” not by evidence that varies by individual customer).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to decertify the class.
