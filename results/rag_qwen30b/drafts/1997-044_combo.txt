**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud may be discharged under the Bankruptcy Code—requires this Court to interpret the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The Third Circuit’s holding that treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable because they are “debt[s] obtained by fraud” misreads the statutory text and contradicts settled precedent defining the limits of fraud-based exceptions to discharge. The Court must reverse.

**I. The Debt Was Not “Obtained by Actual Fraud” Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

The Bankruptcy Code does not discharge debts “obtained by actual fraud.” *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1878), establishes that “fraud” in the bankruptcy context means “positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong, as does embezzlement; and not implied fraud, or fraud in law, which may exist without the imputation of bad faith or immorality.” This distinction is critical. The debt here arose not from a deliberate misrepresentation, but from a failure to comply with a rent control ordinance—a regulatory violation, not a fraudulent act.

The debtor did not make any affirmative misrepresentation to the tenants. He did not claim that the rents were lawful. He did not state that he had consulted the Rent Control Board. He did not affirmatively mislead anyone. The Third Circuit’s conclusion that silence constituted a “misrepresentation” is contrary to *Neal*. There, the Court held that a debt was not “created by fraud” where the bankrupt had not engaged in “intentional wrong” or “moral turpitude.” Id. at 709. Here, the debtor’s conduct—though negligent—was not fraudulent in the constitutional sense. He believed he was complying with the law, and he had no reason to suspect otherwise. His failure to consult the Board or read the ordinance was not a “false pretense” or “false representation,” but a failure of diligence. *Miller v. Guasti*, 226 U.S. 170, 174 (1912), confirms that a discharge is not defeated by mere negligence. There, the Court held that a debtor who failed to inform creditors of his bankruptcy proceedings was not barred from discharge because the failure was not “fraudulent” in nature. The same principle applies here: the absence of intent to deceive, coupled with a lack of affirmative misrepresentation, defeats the claim of “actual fraud.”

**II. Treble Damages Are Not “Debt[s] Obtained by Fraud”**

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable because they were “obtained by fraud.” This reasoning is flawed. The damages were not “obtained” by fraud; they were imposed by statute. The debt arose not from the debtor’s fraudulent conduct, but from the legislature’s decision to penalize violations of consumer protection law. The distinction is fundamental.

*Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick*, 43 U.S. 202, 208 (1844), holds that a discharge “shall, in all courts of justice, be deemed a full and complete discharge of all debts, contracts, and other engagements of such bankrupt, which are provable under the act.” The key inquiry is whether the debt is “provable” under the Bankruptcy Act. A debt is not provable if it arises from a penalty or forfeiture, which are not “engagements” in the ordinary sense. *Moore v. Bay*, 284 U.S. 4, 10 (1931), affirms that the Bankruptcy Act supersedes state law, and that claims “which for want of record or for other reasons would not have been valid liens as against the claims of the creditors of the bankrupt shall not be liens against his estate.” Treble damages under the New Jersey Act are not compensatory; they are punitive in nature. They are not “obtained” by fraud, but are a statutory penalty for violating a regulatory scheme.

*Bluthenthal v. Jones*, 208 U.S. 64, 70 (1908), reinforces this principle. There, the Court held that a debt was not discharged where it was “created by fraud,” but emphasized that the fraud must be “positive fraud” and not mere negligence. The Court noted that the prior bankruptcy proceeding had been denied because of fraud, but the debt in question was not “created by fraud” in the same sense. The same logic applies here: the debt was not “created by fraud,” but by a statutory penalty for noncompliance with a rent control ordinance.

**III. The “Fresh Start” Policy Requires Discharge of Statutory Penalties**

The Bankruptcy Code’s purpose is to provide an “honest but unfortunate debtor” with a “fresh start.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991), citing *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1878). This policy is undermined when statutory penalties—imposed by state law, not by the debtor’s fraudulent conduct—are made nondischargeable. The debtor here did not defraud the tenants. He did not misrepresent his financial condition. He did not obtain money through false pretenses. He simply failed to comply with a complex regulatory scheme.

*Scott v. Ellery*, 142 U.S. 381, 387 (1892), holds that a creditor who proves a debt in bankruptcy waives the right to pursue a deficiency decree. The Court emphasized that the bankruptcy court’s discharge “surrendered the right to proceed” in state court. This principle applies with equal force here: the debt was not “obtained by fraud,” and thus should not be excepted from discharge. To hold otherwise would allow state law to override the federal policy of discharge.
