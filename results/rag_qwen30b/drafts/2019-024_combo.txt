**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that a plaintiff may avoid the limitations period merely by asserting he did not read or recall reading disclosures, even when those disclosures were available and contained the very information he now claims constituted a breach. The Court must reject this interpretation, which would render the statute of limitations meaningless and allow plaintiffs to delay litigation indefinitely by passive inattention. The proper standard, grounded in settled principles of equitable tolling and the common law of statutes of limitations, is that actual knowledge requires more than mere availability of information—it requires that the plaintiff *actually knew* of the breach or violation. This standard is consistent with the Supreme Court’s long-standing jurisprudence, which has repeatedly held that a plaintiff cannot avoid the statute of limitations by failing to investigate or by choosing not to read information that was plainly accessible.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that “actual knowledge” means only awareness of the *nature* of the alleged breach—without requiring knowledge of its *imprudence*—is contrary to the settled law of this Court. In *Curtis v. Connly*, 257 U.S. 260 (1921), the Court held that a cause of action for breach of fiduciary duty by bank directors accrued when the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the wrongful acts, not merely when the acts occurred. The Court emphasized that “the statute of limitations begins to run when the cause of action accrues,” and that “the cause of action accrues when the wrong is done.” Id. at 264. Here, the alleged breach—imprudent investment in alternative assets—was not a mere transaction; it was a fiduciary failure requiring a judgment of imprudence. The plaintiff’s mere awareness of the investment mix, without knowledge that it was imprudent, does not constitute actual knowledge of the breach. To hold otherwise would allow a plaintiff to delay suit indefinitely by refusing to read disclosures, even when those disclosures explicitly warned of risks and poor performance.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. That case, decided before the 1987 amendment to ERISA, held that knowledge of the transaction itself triggered the limitations period. But *Blanton* involved fiduciaries who were parties to the transaction and thus had actual knowledge of its substance. The Court in *Blanton* did not hold that knowledge of a transaction alone suffices when the plaintiff is not a party and has no reason to suspect impropriety. In *American Surety Company v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 160 (1898), the Court held that a cause of action for fraud does not accrue until the plaintiff discovers the fraud, not when the fraud was committed. The Court stated: “The cause of action does not arise until the wrong is discovered.” Id. at 174. This principle is directly applicable here: Sulyma did not discover the alleged imprudence of the investments until after he learned of their poor performance. His failure to read disclosures does not equate to discovery.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also conflicts with *Peak v. United States*, 353 U.S. 43 (1957), where the Court held that a cause of action for death benefits accrued when death occurred, not when the claimant learned of it. The Court rejected the argument that the statute of limitations should be postponed based on a legal presumption of death. The Court stated: “If death occurred in 1943, the cause of action accrued at that time, and is therefore barred after six years.” Id. at 46. Similarly, here, the cause of action for imprudent investment accrued only when Sulyma had actual knowledge of the imprudence—not when the investments were made or when disclosures were issued. The mere availability of information does not trigger the limitations period.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s rule would undermine the purpose of the statute of limitations, which is to prevent stale claims and ensure that evidence remains fresh. In *Exploration Co. v. United States*, 247 U.S. 435 (1918), the Court held that a suit to vacate a patent was barred by the statute of limitations, even though the fraud was concealed for years. The Court stated: “The statute was intended to bar all actions after six years from the date of the issuance of the patent.” Id. at 442. The Court rejected the argument that concealment should toll the statute, noting that “if for six years the Government has failed to discover the fraud, no matter what its diligence in that respect may be, its action against the guilty parties is forever barred.” Id. This principle applies with equal force here: Intel disclosed the investments and their risks. Sulyma had the opportunity to read the disclosures. His failure to do so cannot be excused by a rule that treats mere availability as actual knowledge.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also contradicts *United States v. A. S. Kreider Co.*, 313 U.S. 443 (1941), where the Court held that a refund action was timely only if brought within the statutory period, regardless of when the government discovered the error. The Court emphasized that “the action was commenced well within the applicable period of limitation,” relying on the date of the claim, not the date of discovery. Id. at 450. Here, the claim was filed more than three years after Sulyma had access to the disclosures. The fact that he did not read them does not excuse the delay.

Finally, *Jaben v. United States*, 381 U.S. 214 (1965), reaffirmed that the statute of limitations begins to run when the cause of action accrues, not when the plaintiff learns of it. The Court held that the filing of a complaint satisfying Rule 3 does not toll the statute unless probable cause is established. Id. at 220. This reinforces the principle that a plaintiff cannot delay litigation by failing to act. Similarly, Sulyma cannot delay suit by failing to read disclosures that were available and accessible.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow plaintiffs to “sit on their rights” indefinitely, relying on the defendant’s disclosure as a shield against the statute of limitations.
