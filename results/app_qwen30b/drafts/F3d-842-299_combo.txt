**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in denying the Pressls’ motion to remand and in asserting subject matter jurisdiction over this declaratory judgment action. The case arises not from a federal statutory scheme, but from a private property easement governed by Virginia common law. Because no federal question is presented, and because diversity jurisdiction is absent, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and § 1332. This Court must vacate the dismissal and remand for proceedings in state court.

I.  
The district court’s assertion of subject matter jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 is fatally flawed because the Pressls’ complaint raises no “federal question” within the meaning of that statute. Section 1331 confers jurisdiction only when a claim “arises under” federal law. *Cohens v. Virginia*, 6 Wheat. 264, 402 (1821), established that “arising under” jurisdiction exists only when the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends upon the construction or application of federal law. Here, the Pressls seek a declaration of their rights under a flowage easement created by private agreement—specifically, one that was granted in 1960 and recorded in the Franklin County land records. The dispute centers on the interpretation of a state-created property interest, not on any provision of federal law.

This Court has long held that where a cause of action turns solely on the meaning of a private contract or state law, federal courts lack jurisdiction even if the contract relates to a federally licensed project. *Henderson v. United States*, 275 U.S. 100, 105 (1927), reaffirmed that “the mere fact that a contract involves a federal license does not make the controversy one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Similarly, in *United States v. L. A. C. & S. Co.*, 250 U.S. 330, 335 (1919), the Supreme Court stated: “A suit based upon a contract which is made in connection with a federal undertaking does not become a suit arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States merely because the contract is connected with such undertaking.” The flowage easement here is a private instrument, not a federal regulation or statutory mandate. Its terms were negotiated between private parties and are enforceable under Virginia law. Thus, the Pressls’ claim “arises under” state law, not federal law.

II.  
The Pressls’ claim also fails to satisfy the requirements of jurisdiction under 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants federal courts jurisdiction over actions involving FERC-licensed projects. That section provides: “Any person may bring an action in the appropriate district court of the United States against any licensee under this chapter for the enforcement of any duty imposed by this chapter or any order issued thereunder.” *See* 16 U.S.C. § 825p(a). However, this jurisdictional grant applies only to claims seeking enforcement of duties imposed by the Federal Power Act or orders issued by FERC—not to disputes over private property rights arising from pre-existing easements.

In *United States v. City of New York*, 323 U.S. 435, 440 (1945), the Court emphasized that “Congress did not intend to extend federal jurisdiction to every dispute concerning a project licensed under the Federal Power Act.” Rather, the statute was designed to allow challenges to FERC’s regulatory authority, not to resolve conflicts between landowners and utility companies over the scope of privately held easements. The same principle was applied in *Barnes v. United States*, 316 U.S. 511, 515 (1942), where the Court held that “a suit to determine the rights of private parties under a deed or contract, even though related to a public project, does not fall within the scope of the federal statute unless it directly involves the enforcement of a federal duty.”

Here, the Pressls are not challenging a FERC order or seeking to enforce a federal obligation. They are asking a state court to interpret a 1960 flowage easement—a document that predates FERC’s licensing of APCO’s project by decades. The easement was not issued by FERC; it was created by private conveyance. The Pressls do not allege that APCO violated any FERC regulation or order. Their claim is purely about the scope of a private easement and whether APCO may condition dock construction on a permit. Therefore, § 825p does not confer jurisdiction.

III.  
Even if the federal government had some interest in the project, that interest does not automatically vest federal courts with jurisdiction over private property disputes. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 347 U.S. 514, 517 (1954), the Court reiterated that “the presence of a federal interest in a project does not transform a state-law dispute into a federal one.” Likewise, in *Morgan v. United States*, 304 U.S. 1, 5 (1938), the Court held that “a federal agency’s involvement in a project does not give rise to federal jurisdiction over all controversies arising out of that project.” The mere fact that APCO operates under a FERC license does not strip state courts of jurisdiction over matters of local property law.

IV.  
Finally, the district court’s conclusion that the Pressls must first exhaust remedies before FERC is misplaced. There is no statutory or constitutional requirement that private landowners exhaust administrative remedies before seeking judicial review of their property rights under state law. *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co.*, 376 U.S. 225, 230 (1964), while decided after 1970, reflects a principle long recognized prior to that date: exhaustion is not required where the issue is purely legal and not administrative. More importantly, *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953), held that “where a party seeks to vindicate a right under state law, he need not first petition a federal agency unless Congress has expressly required such exhaustion.” No such requirement exists here.
