**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S DENIAL OF ACCESS TO THE GOOD NEWS CLUB WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE BECAUSE IT WAS BASED ON THE FORUM’S CHARACTERIZATION OF THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES AS RELIGIOUS WORSHIP, NOT VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION.**

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club was not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination but rather permissible subject-matter discrimination. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 512 (2d Cir. 2000). The Club’s proposed activities—singing religious songs, reciting scripture, hearing Bible lessons, and praying—constituted religious worship, not merely moral instruction from a religious perspective. *Id.* at 510-11. This distinction is critical because a public school may exclude speech that constitutes worship while permitting speech that merely discusses religion from a secular viewpoint. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (noting that a school may permissibly exclude religious worship from its facilities).

The Club’s own materials confirm that its meetings were not merely moral instruction but religious worship. The lesson plans explicitly sought to lead children to "believe on the Lord Jesus Christ" and "give God first place in [their] life." *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 507. The Club’s activities included prayer, scripture recitation, and an invitation to accept Jesus as Savior—hallmarks of religious worship rather than secular moral instruction. *Id.* at 506-07. This Court has recognized that a school may exclude speech that constitutes worship even if it permits discussions of religion from a secular perspective. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 270 n.7 (1981) (noting that a university could exclude religious worship while permitting religious discussion).

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), is misplaced. In *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university’s denial of funding to a religious student publication constituted viewpoint discrimination because the publication’s speech was not inherently religious worship but rather a discussion of secular topics from a religious perspective. *Id.* at 831-32. Here, by contrast, the Club’s speech was not merely religious discussion but religious worship, which Milford permissibly excluded. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512.

**II. MILFORD’S POLICY WAS JUSTIFIED BY THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE BECAUSE PERMITTING THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES WOULD HAVE CONSTITUTED AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL ENDORSEMENT OF RELIGION.**

Even if the Club’s speech were deemed secular, Milford’s exclusion was justified by the Establishment Clause. The Second Circuit correctly held that permitting the Club’s activities would have violated the Establishment Clause because it would have amounted to state endorsement of religion. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 513-14. This Court has recognized that a public school may avoid endorsing religion by excluding religious worship from its facilities. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395.

The Club’s activities would have sent a message of endorsement to young, impressionable children. The Club’s meetings included prayer, scripture recitation, and an invitation to accept Jesus as Savior—activities that a reasonable observer would perceive as state endorsement of religion. *See County of Allegheny v. ACLU*, 492 U.S. 573, 594 (1989) (holding that a crèche displayed on government property violated the Establishment Clause because it endorsed religion). Milford’s policy of excluding religious worship from its facilities was a permissible means of avoiding such endorsement. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 514.

The Club’s reliance on *Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), is unavailing. In *Mergens*, the Court held that a school’s denial of access to a religious student club violated the Equal Access Act because the club’s speech was not inherently religious worship but merely religious discussion. *Id.* at 247-48. Here, by contrast, the Club’s speech was religious worship, which Milford permissibly excluded to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 514.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of access to the Good News Club was constitutionally permissible. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
