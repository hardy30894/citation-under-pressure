**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that the trial court properly determined navigability for title purposes by examining whether those rivers were navigable *at the time of statehood*. This Court has long established that the constitutional test for determining navigability for title purposes is historical and fixed at the moment of statehood. The judgment below must be affirmed.

**I. The Constitution Requires a Historical Inquiry into Navigability at the Time of Statehood**

The equal footing doctrine, rooted in Article IV, Section 3 of the United States Constitution, mandates that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. *See U.S. Const. Art. IV, § 3, cl. 1*. This principle includes the automatic transfer of title to the beds of navigable rivers to the state upon admission. *See Montana v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981)* (“[T]he States, upon admission to the Union, acquired title to the beds of navigable rivers within their borders.”). The critical inquiry under this doctrine is not whether a river is navigable today, but whether it was navigable *at the time of statehood*. *See United States v. Midwest Oil Co., 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915)* (“The test of navigability is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.”).

This Court has repeatedly affirmed that the constitutional standard for navigability for title is a historical one. In *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, the Court held that “the question of navigability is one of fact, to be determined by the condition of the river at the time of statehood.” *Id.* at 470. Similarly, in *Montana v. United States*, the Court emphasized that “the State’s title to the riverbeds is based on the navigability of the rivers at the time of statehood.” *450 U.S. at 551*. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled precedent by requiring the trial court to assess navigability based on the condition of the rivers in 1889, the year Montana became a state.

PPL’s argument that the inquiry should be based on modern navigability is contrary to binding precedent. The Court has consistently rejected the notion that navigability for title is a dynamic or evolving standard. *See United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land, 312 U.S. 531, 535 (1941)* (“The test of navigability is not whether the river is navigable now, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.”). To hold otherwise would undermine the predictability of property rights and the equal footing doctrine itself. The State’s title to riverbeds is not contingent on current use or modern commercial activity, but on the historical fact of navigability at the moment of statehood.

**II. The Trial Court Properly Admitted Historical Evidence to Establish Navigability**

PPL challenges the admissibility of the State’s historical evidence, arguing that it consists of hearsay and lacks personal knowledge. This argument fails. The trial court properly admitted the evidence under Montana’s rules of evidence, which permit the use of self-authenticating and public records to establish historical facts.

The State’s evidence consisted of period newspapers, government reports, and archival documents—materials that fall squarely within the self-authenticating categories of Montana Rule of Evidence 902. Specifically, the documents were certified copies of public records (M. R. Evid. 902(4)), official publications (902(5)), and newspapers and periodicals (902(6)). These categories require no extrinsic proof of authenticity and are admissible as a matter of law. *See M. R. Evid. 902*. The State Archivist, Ms. Foley, authenticated the documents in her affidavit, swearing under oath that they were true copies from the Montana Historical Society’s collection. Her affidavit satisfied the requirements of M. R. Civ. P. 56(e), which permits affidavits to be supported by exhibits that are admissible in evidence.

Moreover, the evidence was not excluded as hearsay because it was offered not to prove the truth of the matter asserted, but to establish the historical condition of the rivers. *See M. R. Evid. 801(c)* (hearsay defined as “a statement, other than one made by the declarant while testifying at the trial or hearing, offered in evidence to prove the truth of the matter asserted”). The documents were used to show that the rivers were used for transportation and commerce in the 1880s—evidence of navigability, not the truth of any particular statement. *See United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land, 312 U.S. at 535* (“The fact that a river was used for transportation in the past is relevant to the question of navigability.”).

PPL’s reliance on *Smith v. State*, 2005 MT 188, is misplaced. *Smith* addressed the admissibility of affidavits based on hearsay without personal knowledge, but it explicitly recognized that “exhibits may be submitted in support of a summary judgment affidavit so long as a proper foundation can be laid for such exhibits.” *Smith*, ¶ 39. Here, the State laid a proper foundation through the sworn affidavit of the State Archivist and the self-authenticating nature of the documents. The trial court did not err in considering this evidence.
