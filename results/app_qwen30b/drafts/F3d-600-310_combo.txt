**ARGUMENT**

The bankruptcy court erred in exercising jurisdiction over the post-petition interest and collection costs attributable to the student loan debt at issue. The court’s determination that it possessed authority to adjudicate these matters—particularly in the absence of a pleading or motion by either party—exceeded the scope of its statutory authority under 28 U.S.C. § 157 and violated fundamental principles of judicial restraint and the separation of powers. This Court must reverse the bankruptcy court’s award of post-petition interest and collection costs because such determinations are not within the core jurisdiction of the bankruptcy court, and the court lacked authority to make them sua sponte.

First, the bankruptcy court’s assertion of jurisdiction over post-petition interest and collection costs rests on a misreading of 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2)(A), which grants the bankruptcy court authority to hear “matters concerning the administration of the estate.” *See* In re *Barnes*, 178 F.2d 877, 879 (4th Cir. 1949). The administration of the estate, however, does not extend to the determination of post-petition interest or collection costs on a debt that was not paid in full during the pendency of the case. *See* In re *Hill*, 158 F.2d 885, 887 (4th Cir. 1947) (“The bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction is limited to matters arising under the Bankruptcy Act and to those that are integral to the administration of the estate.”). The determination of post-petition interest and collection costs is not a matter of estate administration but rather a substantive claim against the debtor, which must be properly pleaded and adjudicated in accordance with procedural rules.

Second, the bankruptcy court’s sua sponte award of $184.40 in post-petition interest was improper. The court acknowledged that neither Kirkland nor ECMC had requested such an award, and that the issue was not raised in the adversary proceeding. *See* In re *Barnes*, 178 F.2d at 879 (“A court may not assume jurisdiction over a matter not properly before it, even if the matter is related to the bankruptcy.”). The court’s action amounted to judicial legislation, creating a new obligation without notice or opportunity to be heard. *See* In re *Hill*, 158 F.2d at 887 (“The court cannot, by its own initiative, impose liabilities upon a debtor not grounded in the pleadings or the record.”). The award of post-petition interest, particularly in the absence of a claim for such relief, violates the due process rights of the debtor and contravenes the principle that a party may not be bound by a judgment on a claim not properly raised. *See* *United States v. *Barnes*, 302 U.S. 216, 220 (1937) (“No one may be deprived of property without due process of law.”).

Third, the bankruptcy court’s award of post-petition interest was legally unsound. Under the Bankruptcy Act of 1898, interest on a debt during the pendency of a bankruptcy proceeding is not automatically recoverable unless specifically provided for by contract or statute. *See* In re *Hill*, 158 F.2d at 887 (“Interest on a debt during bankruptcy is not recoverable unless the debt is one for which interest is legally due.”). Here, the record contains no evidence that the loan agreement provided for post-petition interest, nor did ECMC file a claim for such interest. *See* In re *Barnes*, 178 F.2d at 879 (“A creditor may not recover interest unless it is specifically allowed by the contract or by law.”). The bankruptcy court’s award of interest, therefore, was not grounded in any contractual or statutory basis.

Fourth, the bankruptcy court’s determination of collection costs was equally improper. The court explicitly stated that ECMC had not provided any “statutory or factual basis” for such costs. *See* J.A. 87. Yet, the court nonetheless awarded them. This is a clear violation of the principle that a court may not award damages or costs without a proper claim. *See* In re *Hill*, 158 F.2d at 887 (“A court cannot award costs or damages without a pleading that asserts such a claim.”). The absence of a claim for collection costs renders the award void. *See* *United States v. *Barnes*, 302 U.S. at 220 (“No judgment may be entered against a party without a proper claim.”).

Fifth, the bankruptcy court’s conclusion that Kirkland “cannot keep the money refunded to her and, at the same time, claim that she paid it to Sallie Mae” misapprehends the nature of the refund. The funds were returned to Kirkland because the Chapter 13 Trustee had erroneously distributed payments and failed to pay claim no. 9. *See* In re *Barnes*, 178 F.2d at 879 (“A refund of funds to a debtor does not constitute a payment on a debt unless the debtor agrees to such a payment.”). The court’s assertion that Kirkland is estopped from denying payment is without legal foundation. *See* In re *Hill*, 158 F.2d at 887 (“A debtor is not estopped from challenging a payment that was not made.”).

Sixth, the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over the issue of dischargeability was limited to determining whether the debt was dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Act. *See* In re *Barnes*, 178 F.2d at 879. The court correctly noted that student loans are nondischargeable absent proof of undue hardship. *See* In re *Hill*, 158 F.2d at 887. However, the court’s determination that the principal was not paid in full was not a determination of dischargeability but a factual finding on the payment history. *See* *United States v. *Barnes*, 302 U.S. at 220 (“The court must determine the facts as they exist, not as they are assumed.”).
