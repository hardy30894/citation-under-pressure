The district court’s certification order cannot stand. Under the governing standards for representative suits, a class may proceed only when the asserted rights are common to the group and susceptible of uniform adjudication. Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921). Where, as here, the claim for damages turns on individualized proof, that requirement is not satisfied.

The Sherman Act demands that each plaintiff prove “actual damages” caused by the violation. Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931). Those damages must be established “by facts from which their existence is logically and legally inferable,” not by “speculation or conjecture.” Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946). When the measure of injury varies from subscriber to subscriber—depending on the particular service package, the timing of the subscription, and the presence or absence of alternative providers—no single formula can supply the required proof for every class member.

Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32 (1940), confirms that due process forbids binding absent persons to a judgment unless their interests have been adequately represented. Adequate representation is impossible when the central element of relief—quantification of damages—remains an individual question. The record contains no admissible evidence demonstrating that damages are measurable on a common basis; the proffered methodology rests on assumptions that do not hold for the entire class. Under these circumstances, the predominance of common questions required for representative treatment is absent, and certification exceeds the district court’s authority.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
