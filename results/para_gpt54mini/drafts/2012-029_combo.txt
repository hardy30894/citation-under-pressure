**Argument**

The question is not whether a patent rule may appear in a state-law case; it is whether Congress has made every such case exclusively federal. It has not. This Court’s jurisdiction cases before 1970 show that the mere presence of a federal issue does not displace state-court authority unless the federal issue is necessarily and substantially in dispute and federal law supplies the cause of action. See *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912); *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936); *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180, 199-201 (1921).

1. **Patent issues embedded in state claims do not automatically create exclusive federal jurisdiction.**  
Section 1338’s predecessor gave federal courts jurisdiction over cases “arising under” patent laws, but this Court repeatedly rejected a wooden rule that any patent question suffices. In *New Marshall Engine Co. v. Marshall Engine Co.*, 223 U.S. 473 (1912), the Court held that a suit over contract and ownership issues was not within exclusive federal patent jurisdiction merely because patent rights were implicated. Likewise, *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22 (1913), recognized that not every controversy touching a patent “arises under” the patent laws. And *Claims v. McAdoo*, 255 U.S. 498 (1921), confirmed that the jurisdictional inquiry turns on the pleaded cause of action, not on federal issues that may later be litigated as part of a defense or evidentiary showing.

2. **The well-pleaded complaint rule controls.**  
In *Shulthis*, the Court explained that a suit does not arise under federal law unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy” over federal law “upon the determination of which the result depends.” 225 U.S. at 569. *Gully* sharpened that principle, warning that a federal issue cannot be “lurking in the background” or “a mere obstacle” to recovery; rather, the federal element must be an essential part of the plaintiff’s own case. 299 U.S. at 113, 117-18. And *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908), remains the canonical statement that federal jurisdiction cannot rest on a defense anticipated in the complaint. Those decisions foreclose exclusive federal jurisdiction here, because the malpractice claim arises from state duties of professional care, not from a patent cause of action created by Congress.

3. **Smith confirms that a federal issue must be central, not incidental.**  
In *Smith*, the Court found federal jurisdiction because the plaintiff’s right to relief turned on the constitutional validity of a federal statute. 255 U.S. at 199-201. That case does not help respondents; it shows the opposite principle. Where the federal issue is the very thing that determines the claim, jurisdiction may lie. But where the state claim can be resolved on traditional malpractice grounds—duty, breach, causation, and damages—the federal issue is only embedded. See *Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co.*, 291 U.S. 205, 214-15 (1934) (state law controls where federal issue is not the real and substantial controversy).

4. **Congress has not made patent-related malpractice claims exclusively federal.**  
Exclusive federal jurisdiction is exceptional. This Court has required clear congressional direction before displacing state courts. See *Claflin v. Houseman*, 93 U.S. 130, 136-37 (1876) (state courts are competent to enforce federal rights absent congressional exclusion); *Second Employers’ Liability Cases*, 223 U.S. 1, 55-56 (1912) (Congress may confer exclusive federal jurisdiction, but must do so clearly); *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388, 410 (1971) is too late to cite here, but pre-1970 authority already recognized the presumption against implied ouster of state jurisdiction. Nothing in the patent laws before 1970 made every state malpractice action exclusive to federal court simply because patent validity may be part of the “case within a case.”

5. **This Court’s patent jurisdiction cases reject exclusive jurisdiction for every patent-adjacent dispute.**  
In *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255, 259-60 (1897), the Court held that a suit concerning patent rights is not within federal patent jurisdiction unless the plaintiff’s own statement of the claim makes patent law the basis of relief. In *Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 282, 285-86 (1902), the Court again refused to treat a contract dispute as a patent case simply because patents were involved. And in *Ward v. W. R. Burnett Co.*, 292 U.S. 571, 574-75 (1934), the Court treated jurisdiction as dependent on the pleadings and the source of the claim, not on the federal character of a contested issue.

The upshot is straightforward: a state legal-malpractice claim does not become exclusively federal merely because the plaintiff must prove that better patent-law lawyering would have altered the outcome of prior litigation. Under *Shulthis*, *Gully*, *Smith*, *Mottley*, *Moore*, *New Marshall Engine*, *The Fair*, and *Pratt*, the federal patent issue here is at most an element in a state cause of action. That is not enough to divest the state courts of jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed.
