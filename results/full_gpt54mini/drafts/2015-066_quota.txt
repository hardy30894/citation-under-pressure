## Argument

The Sixth Amendment bars the government from using a prior conviction as the functional equivalent of an element of a later felony unless that prior conviction was obtained with the constitutional protections the Amendment requires in criminal prosecutions. Here, the government relied on Michael Bryant’s prior tribal-court domestic-violence convictions to satisfy 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)’s predicate-offense requirement and thereby convert new assault charges into a felony. That use of uncounseled tribal convictions violates the Sixth Amendment.

1. **Section 117(a) uses prior convictions to prove guilt, not merely to increase punishment.**  
The Constitution imposes the highest procedural safeguards when the government must prove an element of a crime. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). By contrast, sentencing factors are treated differently because they are found after guilt has already been established. Id.; see also Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738, 747-48 (1994). Section 117(a) is not a sentencing guideline. It defines a separate felony offense and requires proof that the defendant “has a final conviction on at least 2 separate prior occasions” for qualifying domestic-violence offenses. The prior convictions are thus part of the government’s case in chief. Under Winship, the government may not satisfy that burden with convictions whose use would be constitutionally defective if they had been obtained in a federal or state prosecution. See Medley v. Runnels, 506 F.3d 857, 862 (9th Cir. 2007).

2. **Nichols does not authorize the government to use uncounseled convictions as elements of a new offense.**  
Nichols upheld the use of a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction at sentencing where the conviction itself was valid under Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979). The Court emphasized that the defendant was not imprisoned for the prior conviction and that the challenged use occurred in sentencing, not in the guilt phase. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-48. That distinction is decisive. Nichols did not hold that an uncounseled conviction may be used to establish guilt in a later prosecution. To the contrary, Nichols rested on the different standards governing sentencing and conviction. Id. at 748. A statute that makes prior convictions an element of a felony prosecution is subject to Winship, not Nichols.

3. **Alabama v. Shelton confirms that the Sixth Amendment turns on the reliability of the conviction, not on metaphysics about when imprisonment is imposed.**  
Shelton held that a suspended prison sentence may not rest on an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel applies when a conviction leads to imprisonment. Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654, 662-68 (2002). The Court’s reasoning reflected the right-to-counsel guarantee’s role in ensuring reliability in criminal adjudication. Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963). If an uncounseled conviction cannot support imprisonment in the ordinary state-felony setting, it cannot constitutionally serve as the essential predicate for a federal felony prosecution under § 117(a).

4. **Tribal-court convictions obtained without counsel are not equivalent to convictions obtained in state or federal court.**  
It is true that the Sixth Amendment does not directly apply to tribal prosecutions. United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978); see also Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). But Congress did not write § 117(a) to permit any conviction, however procedurally limited, to serve as the basis for a federal felony. It required prior convictions for offenses that would be domestic-violence offenses “if subject to Federal jurisdiction.” That cross-reference reflects a federal-law judgment about the quality of the predicate offense. Congress does not lightly incorporate convictions entered under systems that lack the core adversarial safeguard the Sixth Amendment treats as fundamental. See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987); Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 65-71 (1978).

5. **Using uncounseled tribal convictions as felony predicates also conflicts with settled recidivism principles.**  
Although recidivist statutes punish the latest offense, not the prior conduct, the prior convictions still must be legally usable for the statutory purpose. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; see also United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). A conviction invalid for lack of counsel in one context does not become constitutionally adequate merely because Congress relabels it a predicate element. The Court has never sanctioned that kind of bootstrapping. See Baldasar v. Illinois, 446 U.S. 222 (1980), overruled in part by Nichols, 511 U.S. 738 (1994).

6. **The Ninth Circuit’s rule is consistent with the Constitution’s central concern for fair adjudication.**  
The government may not prove a felony by relying on prior convictions whose use would undermine the reliability guarantees that the Sixth Amendment secures. See Gideon, 372 U.S. at 344; Shelton, 535 U.S. at 662; Winship, 397 U.S. at 364. Because § 117(a) makes prior convictions an element of the offense, and because Bryant’s tribal convictions were uncounseled, the indictment should have been dismissed.

The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
