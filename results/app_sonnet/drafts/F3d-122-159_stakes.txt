ARGUMENT

I. ROUSSOS'S CONVICTION FOR CONSPIRACY TO DISTRIBUTE A CONTROLLED SUBSTANCE IS NOT A "CRIME OF VIOLENCE" UNDER THE CATEGORICAL APPROACH THAT 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3) AND 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 REQUIRE.

The BOP regulation implementing 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) excludes an inmate from early-release eligibility only if his "current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)." 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. Section 924(c)(3) defines that term by reference to the elements and nature of the offense itself, not the facts developed at sentencing: a crime of violence is a felony that "has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force," or that "by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force ... may be used in the course of committing the offense." 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).

This is the categorical approach that the Supreme Court has mandated whenever a statute asks whether a "conviction" qualifies as a particular category of offense. In Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), the Court held that a sentencing court "must look only to the fact of conviction and the statutory definition of the prior offense," 495 U.S. at 602, rather than to the underlying facts of the defendant's conduct, in deciding whether a prior conviction constitutes a predicate "violent felony." The Court reasoned that a facts-based inquiry would be unworkable and unfair, requiring relitigation of conduct years after the fact. Id. That reasoning applies with equal force here: conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance under 21 U.S.C. § 846 contains no element of force, and distribution offenses do not, "by their nature," involve a substantial risk that force will be used. The gun found in Roussos's vacation home was not an element of his offense of conviction; it was the basis for a two-level Specific Offense Characteristic enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1, a purely sentencing-stage fact that has nothing to do with the elements of § 846.

The Ninth Circuit confronted this precise question and reached the same conclusion. In Davis v. Crabtree, 109 F.3d 566 (9th Cir. 1997), the court considered Program Statement 5162.02's treatment of a § 846 or § 841 conviction as a crime of violence whenever the sentencing court applied a weapon enhancement. The court held that this approach improperly collapses the categorical, elements-based definition of "crime of violence" in § 924(c)(3) into a facts-based sentencing inquiry, and that a drug-trafficking conviction does not become a crime of violence merely because a firearm happened to be present at some point connected to the offense. The same is true here. Roussos's guns were not used, brandished, or even present at the time of his arrest; they were found during a search of a residence, and their relevance was solely to a guideline enhancement he accepted as part of a plea agreement. Under the categorical approach of Taylor and Davis, that fact cannot convert a nonviolent drug conspiracy into a "crime of violence."

II. THE BOP'S PROGRAM STATEMENT, INSOFAR AS IT REDEFINES A NONVIOLENT DRUG OFFENSE AS A CRIME OF VIOLENCE BASED ON A SENTENCING ENHANCEMENT, CONFLICTS WITH THE STATUTE AND ITS OWN REGULATION AND IS NOT ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE.

Program Statement 5162.02 is not a legislative rule promulgated through notice-and-comment rulemaking; it is an internal agency directive. The Supreme Court has held that such Program Statements receive a lesser measure of deference than full Chevron deference affords to formal regulations. See Reno v. Koray, 515 U.S. 50, 61 (1995) (BOP Program Statement is "akin to an interpretive rule" and is therefore "entitled to some deference," rather than the deference due a regulation promulgated after notice and comment) (citing Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984)). That lesser deference is appropriate only where the Program Statement offers a reasonable reading of an ambiguous statutory or regulatory term. Here, however, the Program Statement does not merely interpret an ambiguity in § 924(c)(3); it displaces the categorical, elements-based test that Congress and the BOP's own regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, adopted, substituting a facts-of-sentencing test that Congress never authorized. An agency directive that conflicts with the plain terms of the statute and regulation it purports to implement is owed no deference at all. See Chevron, 467 U.S. at 842-43 (deference is due only where "Congress has not directly addressed the precise question at issue" and the agency's construction is "permissible").

Because § 924(c)(3) and 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 unambiguously require a categorical, elements-based inquiry, Program Statement 5162.02's contrary rule—treating any § 846 or § 841 conviction enhanced for weapon possession as a per se crime of violence—cannot stand. Roussos's drug conspiracy conviction remains, as a matter of law, a nonviolent offense.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court denying Roussos's petition for a writ of habeas corpus should be reversed, and the BOP should be directed to consider him eligible for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).
