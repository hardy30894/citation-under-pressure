## ARGUMENT

Milford Central School did not violate the First Amendment. Its community-use policy created a limited public forum for secular activities, and the Good News Club’s proposed use—religious instruction and prayer—fell outside that forum. Even if the exclusion implicated viewpoint discrimination, it was justified by Milford’s compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.

### I. Milford’s Policy Created a Limited Public Forum for Secular Uses, and the Club’s Activities Were Excluded as Subject-Matter Discrimination

When the government opens a nonpublic forum for limited purposes, it may restrict speech to those purposes as long as the restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s policy permitted use of school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but expressly prohibited use for “religious purposes.” This created a limited public forum for secular activities. *See Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983) (a public body may reserve a forum for certain groups or topics).

The Club’s meetings were not a secular discussion of morals from a religious viewpoint; they were religious instruction and prayer. The Club’s lesson plans included a “challenge and invitation” segment urging unsaved children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” and to pray for salvation. The meetings opened with prayer, included Bible lessons, and closed with prayer. This is not the teaching of values from a religious perspective—it is the very religious worship and instruction that Milford’s policy excludes. *Cf. Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (noting difficulty of distinguishing religious speech from worship, but recognizing that a forum may be limited to secular topics).

Because Milford had not opened its facilities for religious instruction, it could permissibly exclude the Club without engaging in viewpoint discrimination. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393–94 (1993) (a school may exclude religious worship while allowing secular uses). The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious” and thus fell outside the forum’s boundaries.

### II. The Exclusion Was Viewpoint Neutral Because the Club’s Activities Changed the Subject Matter, Not Merely the Viewpoint

Viewpoint discrimination occurs when the government permits discussion of a subject but disfavors one perspective on that subject. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Here, the subject matter was not “morals and character” generally; Milford allowed groups like the Boy Scouts and 4-H to teach secular moral development. The Club, however, did not simply offer a religious viewpoint on morals—it engaged in religious exercises: prayer, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Christ. That is a different subject matter, not a different viewpoint. *See Hazelwood Sch. Dist. v. Kuhlmeier*, 484 U.S. 260, 270 (1988) (schools may regulate speech that is “inconsistent with its basic educational mission”).

The Club’s activities were indistinguishable from the religious worship that Milford’s policy forbids. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “there is a crucial difference between government speech endorsing religion, which the Establishment Clause forbids, and private speech endorsing religion, which the Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses protect.” *Board of Educ. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (plurality opinion). But that protection does not require a school to open its limited forum to religious worship. Milford’s exclusion was based on the *subject* of the Club’s meetings, not on the religious viewpoint of an otherwise permissible subject.

### III. Even If the Exclusion Was Viewpoint Based, It Was Justified by Milford’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation

A school’s interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation is a compelling interest that can justify content-based restrictions on speech. *Capitol Square Review & Advisory Bd. v. Pinette*, 515 U.S. 753, 761–62 (1995) (plurality opinion). Permitting the Good News Club to meet on school premises—with its overtly evangelical message directed at young children—would create a reasonable perception of school endorsement of religion. *See Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (the government may not “place its imprimatur on a particular religious creed”). The Club’s meetings were held after school but on school grounds, and the school provided bus transportation to the Club’s off-site meetings. Allowing the Club to meet on campus would blur the line between private religious speech and school sponsorship, particularly for impressionable elementary students. *See Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308 (2000) (school endorsement of religious speech is unconstitutional when it “coerces” students to participate).

Milford’s policy was a reasonable effort to avoid such entanglement. The school did not ban all religious speech; it merely excluded religious worship and instruction from a limited forum designed for secular community uses. That decision was both reasonable and viewpoint neutral, and it was amply justified by the school’s constitutional obligations.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
