**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS must assess the individual partners—not merely the partnership—if it wants the benefit of the Code’s ten-year collection period against those partners. The statute’s text, structure, and this Court’s partnership precedents all confirm that result.

Under the Internal Revenue Code, assessment is made against the “taxpayer,” 26 U.S.C. § 6203, and the taxpayer is the person whose liability is recorded. Section 6501(a) gives the government three years to assess a tax; only if the assessment is timely does § 6502(a) extend the time for collection to ten years after assessment. The government’s position rewrites those provisions by treating an assessment against one taxpayer—the partnership—as automatically extending limitations as to other taxpayers—the individual partners. That is not what the statute says. As the Ninth Circuit explained, a “partnership” is a taxpayer, but so is each individual partner, and “the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified ‘taxpayer.’” United States v. Galletti, 291 F.3d 1170, 1175-76 (9th Cir. 2002), amended by 298 F.3d 1107 (9th Cir. 2002).

This Court has long recognized that a partnership and its partners are distinct legal entities for federal tax and bankruptcy purposes. In Forsyth v. Woods, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 484, 492 (1871), the Court explained that “the partnership is a distinct thing from the partners themselves.” Likewise, in Francis v. McNeal, 228 U.S. 695, 698-99 (1913), the Court treated the partnership and individual estates as separate for bankruptcy administration, warning of the anomaly of allowing a debt to be collected in full from joint debtors while ignoring the separate estates. And in Liberty Nat. Bank of Roanoke, Va. v. Bear, 276 U.S. 215, 217-18 (1928), the Court again distinguished partnership property from the individual property of the partners. Those cases defeat the government’s premise that a partnership assessment is the legal equivalent of an assessment against the partners.

The government’s reliance on state-law joint and several liability does not alter the federal limitations question. Even if partners are substantively liable for partnership debts, the Code still requires timely assessment or suit against the person from whom the IRS seeks collection. The Ninth Circuit properly relied on United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), where the court recognized that a valid lien on a partner’s property depends on an assessment against that person. See id. at 458, 462-63. The same principle governs here: liability may exist, but the government’s procedural path to collection is time-barred.

Nor does Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), help the IRS. The Ninth Circuit correctly explained that Young involved a refund claim after payment, not an attempt to collect from a partner without an individual assessment or judgment. Galletti, 291 F.3d at 1177-78. Young therefore did not decide whether the government may invoke § 6502(a)’s ten-year period against partners who were never assessed. Nor did United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), answer that question; as the Ninth Circuit noted, the Seventh Circuit had no occasion to consider whether an assessment against the partnership alone extends limitations against individual partners. Galletti, 298 F.3d 1107, 1156 (9th Cir. 2002).

The government’s position also conflicts with the basic limits Congress placed on collection actions. Section 6502(a) authorizes collection only where “the assessment of any tax” has been made within the limitations period, and then only for ten years “after the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). That language ties the extended collection period to the assessed taxpayer and the assessed tax. It does not permit the IRS to bootstrap a separate taxpayer’s exposure from another entity’s assessment. When Congress wants one event to affect multiple liabilities, it says so expressly. It did not do so here.

Finally, bankruptcy law confirms the Ninth Circuit’s result. A claim is allowable only if it is enforceable against the debtor under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Here, California law provides that “[a] judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS obtained no judgment against respondents, and the time to do so had expired. Galletti, 291 F.3d at 1179-80. Because the claim was unenforceable when filed, it was properly disallowed.

The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
