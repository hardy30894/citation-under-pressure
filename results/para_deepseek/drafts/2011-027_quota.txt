## ARGUMENT

The constitutional test for determining navigability for title purposes requires a court to assess whether the relevant stretch of a river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test, and its judgment should be affirmed.

### I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Mandates a Statehood-Date Navigability Inquiry

Under the equal footing doctrine, a State admitted to the Union acquires title to the beds of waters that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). This principle is “firmly established.” *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894). The critical temporal benchmark is the date of statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931) (“the question of navigability … is to be determined as of the time of the admission of the State into the Union”); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (same). A river is navigable for title purposes if it was “used, or [was] susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 10 Wall. 557, 563 (1871). This definition applies at statehood. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922).

### II. The Navigability Determination Must Be Made Segment by Segment

The constitutional test requires a stretch-by-stretch analysis, not a generalized inquiry into the river’s entire length. In *United States v. Utah*, this Court examined separate segments of the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers, concluding that “navigability … is not a continuous condition” and that “a river may be navigable in part and not in another part.” 283 U.S. at 77. Similarly, in *United States v. Oregon*, the Court analyzed discrete sections of the Klamath River. 295 U.S. at 14-15. The trial court here properly followed this approach, evaluating the navigability of each relevant stretch of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as of 1889. The Montana Supreme Court affirmed that determination.

### III. The Trial Court Correctly Relied on Historical Evidence of Navigability at Statehood

The trial court considered extensive historical evidence—including government surveys, newspaper accounts, and shipping records—to determine whether each river segment was used or susceptible to use for commerce in 1889. This is precisely the type of evidence this Court has endorsed. *See United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926) (navigability may be proved by “evidence of the use of the stream for commercial purposes” at the relevant time); *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77, 87 (1922) (same). The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that such historical evidence is admissible and sufficient to establish navigability at statehood. *See Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (recognizing that Montana acquired title to beds of navigable rivers at statehood).

### IV. Petitioner’s Contrary Arguments Are Unsupported by Precedent

Petitioner contends that the constitutional test does not require a statehood-date navigability inquiry, but that position is irreconcilable with this Court’s decisions. *United States v. Utah* expressly rejected the argument that “navigability … may be determined by present conditions.” 283 U.S. at 75. The test is historical, not prospective. *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14. Moreover, the segment-by-segment analysis is not a discretionary choice but a constitutional requirement. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 77. The trial court’s meticulous findings on each river stretch complied with that requirement.

### V. The Montana Supreme Court’s Decision Is Consistent with This Court’s Precedents

The Montana Supreme Court applied the correct legal standard: it required the State to prove that each relevant river segment was navigable at the time of statehood. The court then affirmed the trial court’s factual findings based on substantial historical evidence. This Court has repeatedly held that such factual determinations are entitled to deference. *See Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 56-57; *Brewer-Elliott*, 260 U.S. at 87-88. The decision below should be affirmed.

### Conclusion

The constitutional test for navigability for title requires a court to determine whether the relevant stretch of a river was navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied that test. The judgment should be affirmed.
