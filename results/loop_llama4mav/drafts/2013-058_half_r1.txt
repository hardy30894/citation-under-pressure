The Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the U.S. Bankruptcy Code. The plain meaning of the statute, combined with a long line of judicial precedent, supports this conclusion.

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under section... 408 ... of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C). The key issue is whether an inherited IRA constitutes "retirement funds" for the beneficiary. As the Seventh Circuit correctly observed, an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary because it is not held for their retirement.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that exemptions in bankruptcy law should be narrowly construed. In Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), the Court discussed the interpretation of exemptions, stating that the court below had erred in its construction of the relevant statute. Id. at 190.

Similarly, in Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), the Court emphasized the importance of adhering to established principles when interpreting the Bankruptcy Act. The Court held that a discharge in bankruptcy is not a plea in bar to a suit upon a debt, but rather a personal privilege of the debtor. Id. at 562.

Other courts have also recognized the importance of narrowly construing exemptions. In Re Lippitt, 229 F. 854 (D.C. Ohio 1915), the court discussed the principles governing exemptions in bankruptcy. See also Schwartz v. Holzman, 69 F.2d 814 (2d Cir. 1934); In re White, 61 F.2d 966 (2d Cir. 1932); Lockwood v. Exchange Bank, 190 U.S. 294 (1903); Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181 (1902); Swarts v. Fourth National Bank, 117 F. 1 (8th Cir. 1902).

In Hanover National Bank v. Moyses, the Supreme Court noted that the Bankruptcy Code is designed to provide a fair and equitable distribution of a debtor's assets among creditors. 186 U.S. at 190. Exempting inherited IRAs from the bankruptcy estate would be inconsistent with this goal, as it would allow debtors to shield assets that are not truly retirement funds.

The Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with this long line of precedent. By holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt from the bankruptcy estate, the court ensured that the Bankruptcy Code is applied in a fair and equitable manner. The decision should be affirmed.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit's decision in Clark v. Rameker is supported by a long line of judicial precedent and is consistent with the plain meaning of the Bankruptcy Code. The decision should be affirmed, and inherited IRAs should not be exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy.
