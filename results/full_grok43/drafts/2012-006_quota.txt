**ARGUMENT**

Withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period negates an essential element of the offense, placing the burden of persuasion on the government to prove the defendant's membership during the relevant period beyond a reasonable doubt. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 399 (1957) (limitations period runs from last overt act); *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910) (conspiracy is "continuing offense").

Once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, the prosecution must establish continued participation. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) (affirmative act required to withdraw); *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 645 (1946) (liability attaches only to acts within scope of agreement). The government cannot rely on the initial agreement alone when the limitations clock has run. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 755 (1946) (each conspirator's liability measured by personal involvement).

Requiring the defendant to prove withdrawal by a preponderance would invert the constitutional allocation of burdens. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) ("proof beyond a reasonable doubt" required for every fact necessary to constitute the crime); *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 704 (1975) (State may not shift burden on element of offense). Withdrawal directly negates the element of membership during the limitations period. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 215 (1977) (distinguishing elements from affirmative defenses).

This Court has consistently treated the government's proof of timely membership as its affirmative obligation. *Blumenthal v. United States*, 332 U.S. 539, 557 (1947) (evidence must connect defendant to conspiracy as it existed within limitations period); *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 253 (1940) (government must prove continuing agreement). Placing the ultimate burden on the prosecution ensures that convictions rest on evidence rather than presumptions of perpetual membership. *Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1, 8 (2006) (government bears burden on elements even when defendant raises defense).

The court of appeals therefore erred in relieving the government of its burden once withdrawal evidence was introduced.
