ARGUMENT

I. The Temporary Scheduling Provision Supplies an Intelligible Principle.

Article I vests legislative power in Congress, U.S. Const. art. I, § 1, yet “Congress may delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). Section 811(h) meets that standard. It authorizes temporary placement of a substance on Schedule I only upon a finding that scheduling “is necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), after consideration of three enumerated statutory factors, id. § 811(h)(3), and after notice to the Secretary of Health and Human Services whose comments must be considered, id. § 811(h)(4). These constraints are “far more specific” than those upheld in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 420, 426 (1944), and are comparable to the detailed directives sustained in Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 374-77.

The availability of judicial review of the resulting criminal prosecution further cabins the delegation. See Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 544 (1952). Because the statute supplies an intelligible principle, petitioners’ facial challenge fails.

II. Subdelegation to the DEA Administrator Is Lawful.

Congress expressly empowered the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter” to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General’s regulation transferring temporary-scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), is therefore authorized by statute. This Court has repeatedly approved such internal executive subdelegations when Congress has granted the requisite power. See, e.g., United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 512-14 (1974); Fleming v. Mohawk Wrecking & Lumber Co., 331 U.S. 111, 120-21 (1947).

Petitioners’ contention that criminal rulemaking may never be subdelegated is unsupported. The intelligible-principle test applies equally whether the delegatee is the Attorney General or an agency head acting under his direction; nothing in the Constitution distinguishes the two. See Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 372-73.

III. The Nondelegation Doctrine Does Not Require Heightened Scrutiny for Temporary Criminal Provisions.

Petitioners urge stricter review because temporary scheduling creates criminal liability. The Court has never adopted a different nondelegation standard for statutes that authorize criminal sanctions. See, e.g., Yakus, 321 U.S. at 418-19 (price-control regulations enforceable by criminal penalties). The same intelligible-principle inquiry governs. Because section 811(h) satisfies that inquiry, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
