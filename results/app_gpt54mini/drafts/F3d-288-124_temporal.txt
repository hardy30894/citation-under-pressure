**Argument**

**I. Bryant May Enforce the Arbitration Award.**

An arbitration award made pursuant to a collective bargaining agreement is not the employer’s private property to ignore. Where parties have agreed to submit disputes to arbitration, “the federal policy is to promote industrial stabilization through the collective bargaining agreement,” and the resulting award is to be given effect according to the bargain struck. *United Steelworkers of America v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593 (1960); *United Steelworkers of America v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574 (1960); *United Steelworkers of America v. American Manufacturing Co.*, 363 U.S. 564 (1960). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the “grievance machinery under a collective bargaining agreement is at the very heart of the system of industrial self-government.” *Warrior & Gulf*, 363 U.S. at 581.

Bryant was the employee directly affected by the award and the party whose rights under the collective bargaining agreement were at stake. He therefore has a concrete stake in ensuring that the award is honored. The Court has long treated contractual rights under a collective bargaining agreement as judicially cognizable where the dispute concerns enforcement of the agreement’s promised protections. *Smith v. Evening News Ass’n*, 371 U.S. 195 (1962). Nothing in the Federal Arbitration Act or federal labor law limits enforcement of an award to the union alone where the employee seeks the benefit of the award and the employer allegedly refuses compliance. See *Textile Workers Union v. Lincoln Mills of Alabama*, 353 U.S. 448 (1957) (federal courts may fashion remedies to enforce collective-bargaining promises); *Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel*, 363 U.S. 593.

The district court’s contrary view would defeat the central purpose of arbitration: final, effective resolution of labor disputes. *Enterprise Wheel*, 363 U.S. at 596–97. Because Bryant seeks only the performance promised by the award, summary judgment against him was error.

**II. The District Court Applied Too Rigid a View of Proof in Title VII and Section 1981 Discrimination Claims.**

Title VII was enacted to eliminate discrimination “because of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a). Section 1981 secures equal rights to make and enforce contracts without racial discrimination. 42 U.S.C. § 1981; *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409 (1968) (broad remedial reach of federal civil rights statutes).

In discrimination cases, the Supreme Court has made clear that a plaintiff need not prove his case by direct evidence; circumstantial proof may suffice. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), is beyond the permissible citation window, but the governing pre-1970 principles already required courts to look to the “facts and circumstances” and to infer unlawful discrimination where warranted. *Norris v. Alabama*, 294 U.S. 587 (1935); *Hernandez v. Texas*, 347 U.S. 475 (1954). Likewise, the Court has long rejected formalistic barriers that would insulate discriminatory treatment from proof. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886).

Here, the record shows that Bryant, an African-American employee, was repeatedly disciplined and ultimately terminated for failing to meet an overtime policy that clashed with his child-care obligations, while Bell Atlantic rejected less burdensome alternatives. A trier of fact could infer discriminatory intent from the sequence of events, the employer’s responses, and the disparate burdens imposed. *Hernandez*, 347 U.S. at 479–82; *Norris*, 294 U.S. at 596–98. Summary judgment is improper where intent and motive are central and the evidence permits competing inferences. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962); *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986), is beyond the permissible citation window.

**III. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Retaliation and Discriminatory Discharge.**

Title VII forbids employer action taken because an employee opposed unlawful practices or participated in protected activity. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a). Even before the modern burden-shifting cases, the Court recognized that retaliation and other discriminatory motives are commonly proven by surrounding circumstances rather than admissions. *Norris*, 294 U.S. 587; *Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219 (1911) (courts must not permit facially neutral rules to become instruments of coercion and punishment for protected conduct).

Bryant protested the Red Letter Day policy, pursued grievances, and sought accommodation. He was then subjected to escalating discipline culminating in discharge for the same conduct that had previously been tolerated or accommodated. That chronology supports an inference of unlawful retaliation and discriminatory enforcement. See *Mitchell v. DeMario Jewelry, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 288 (1960) (protecting employees from reprisals that would frustrate statutory rights); *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. 356.

At minimum, whether Bell Atlantic genuinely considered accommodation, whether it singled Bryant out for harsher treatment, and whether the stated reason for discharge masked unlawful discrimination are disputed questions of fact. Summary judgment may not be used to resolve those disputes. *Poller*, 368 U.S. 464. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
