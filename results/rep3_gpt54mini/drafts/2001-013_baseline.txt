**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels strips OSHA of jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Act withdraws OSHA authority only when another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” over the relevant working conditions. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That text requires actual exercise of authority over the specific hazards at issue, not a general assertion of maritime responsibility. See *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 243–46 (2002); *Southern Railway Co. v. Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission*, 539 F.2d 335, 339–40 (4th Cir. 1976).

This Court has consistently read § 4(b)(1) to require a concrete regulatory overlap before OSHA is displaced. In *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386 (5th Cir. 1976), the court explained that § 4(b)(1) is triggered only where another agency has exercised authority over the “particular working conditions” at issue. Similarly, in *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119, 124–25 (2d Cir. 1978), the Second Circuit held that a general regulatory presence does not preempt OSHA absent standards directed to the same occupational hazards. And in *Southern Railway*, the Fourth Circuit rejected a claim of blanket preemption where the other agency’s regulations did not address the cited conditions. 539 F.2d at 339–40.

That is dispositive here. The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited and selective. Congress has given the Coast Guard authority to promulgate certain safety requirements for uninspected vessels, including fire extinguishers, life preservers, backfire flame control, ventilation, and certain emergency equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. pts. 24–26. But limited regulation is not comprehensive regulation. As this Court later held in this very case, “the Coast Guard’s regulations concerning uninspected vessels do not address all of the hazards” at issue, and therefore do not bar OSHA under § 4(b)(1). *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 248.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with the statute. Section 4(b)(1) speaks in present, specific terms: other agencies must “exercise” authority to regulate “working conditions” before OSHA is displaced. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that preemption under the Act is not based on a mere possibility of agency regulation, but on actual regulation of the relevant workplace hazard. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243–45; *Southern Railway*, 539 F.2d at 339–40. The fact that the Coast Guard has jurisdiction to investigate maritime casualties, or authority to regulate some safety features on uninspected vessels, does not mean it has exercised authority over every occupational hazard on those vessels. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 245–46.

Nor does the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on its earlier cases justify exclusion of OSHA here. In *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), the court addressed a seaman’s attempt to invoke OSHA standards in a negligence action; it did not decide that the Coast Guard had actually promulgated standards covering the specific hazards at issue on every drilling barge. See *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 246–48. And in *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), the court stated broadly that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation,” id. at 826, but that formulation cannot override the statutory requirement that another agency must actually regulate the pertinent conditions. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 247–48. To the extent *Clary* and *Donovan* suggest a categorical vessel-based exemption, they conflict with the text of § 4(b)(1) and the Court’s interpretation of it. See *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243–46.

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s broad exclusion would create the very regulatory gaps Congress rejected. Section 4(b)(1) is a limited preemption provision, not a free-standing maritime exemption from OSHA. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243. When the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over the particular hazard on an uninspected vessel, OSHA remains available to protect workers. *Id.* at 248–49. That rule best honors the Act’s text and purpose and avoids leaving workers on uninspected vessels with no federal protection at all.

The judgment should be reversed.
