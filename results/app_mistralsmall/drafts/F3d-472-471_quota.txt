**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S FMLA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS NEED FOR LEAVE.**

The FMLA requires an employee to provide notice of the need for leave “as soon as practicable.” 29 U.S.C. § 2612(e)(1). Notice need not be in any particular form, and an employee need not expressly assert rights under the FMLA. *Davis v. Michigan Dept. of Treasury*, 568 F.3d 974, 978 (7th Cir. 2009) (citing *Cobb v. Contract Transport, Inc.*, 452 F.3d 543, 549 (6th Cir. 2006)). Rather, notice is sufficient if the employer is “informed of the employee’s need for leave for a qualifying reason.” *Id.* (quoting *Callison v. City of Philadelphia*, 430 F.3d 117, 119 (3d Cir. 2005)).

Burnett’s communications with Habitat in December 2003 and January 2004 were sufficient to apprise Habitat of his need for leave. In *Davis*, the Seventh Circuit held that an employee’s statement that he was “sick” and needed to see a doctor was adequate notice under the FMLA. 568 F.3d at 978. Similarly, Burnett told Polo that he was “sick” during his December absence and that he had seen a doctor and learned he had high PSA and cholesterol levels. He also informed Habitat that he had upcoming doctor’s appointments and needed to see a urologist. These statements were sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett required leave for a serious health condition. *See also Arban v. West Publishing Corp.*, 345 F.3d 390, 400 (6th Cir. 2003) (employee’s statement that he was “sick” and needed to see a doctor was sufficient notice).

Habitat argues that Burnett’s notice was untimely because he did not expressly invoke the FMLA. However, the FMLA does not require magic words. *Davis*, 568 F.3d at 978. The notice requirement is satisfied if the employer is aware that the employee’s absences may be for a serious health condition. *Id.* (citing *Cobb*, 452 F.3d at 549). Here, Burnett’s statements to Polo and Hehr apprised Habitat of his medical issues and need for leave. The district court erred in concluding otherwise.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON BURNETT’S ADA CLAIM BECAUSE HE PROVIDED SUFFICIENT NOTICE OF HIS DISABILITY AND REQUEST FOR ACCOMMODATION.**

The ADA requires an employee to provide notice of his disability and request for accommodation. *EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co.*, 417 F.3d 783, 790 (7th Cir. 2005). Notice may be given to a supervisor, and the employee need not use the term “ADA” or “disability.” *Id.* at 791. The notice requirement is satisfied if the employer is aware of the employee’s medical condition and the need for accommodation. *Id.*

Burnett’s communications with Habitat in December 2003 and January 2004 were sufficient to apprise Habitat of his disability and need for accommodation. Burnett told Polo that he had a “weak bladder” and that he was seeing a doctor to determine the cause. He also provided Habitat with a doctor’s note explaining his upcoming prostate biopsy and the need for leave. These communications were sufficient to put Habitat on notice that Burnett had a disability and required accommodation. *See also EEOC v. Yellow Freight System, Inc.*, 253 F.3d 943, 951 (7th Cir. 2001) (employee’s statement that he was “sick” and needed to see a doctor was sufficient notice of disability).

Habitat argues that Burnett’s notice was untimely because he did not expressly request accommodation. However, the ADA does not require an employee to use specific language to request accommodation. *Sears*, 417 F.3d at 791. The notice requirement is satisfied if the employer is aware of the employee’s medical condition and the need for accommodation. *Id.* Here, Burnett’s statements to Polo and Hehr apprised Habitat of his medical issues and need for accommodation. The district court erred in concluding otherwise.
