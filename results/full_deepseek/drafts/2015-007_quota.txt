**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida’s Capital Sentencing Scheme Violates the Sixth Amendment Because It Does Not Require a Jury to Find Each Aggravating Factor Unanimously and Beyond a Reasonable Doubt.**

In *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the existence of any aggravating factor that makes a defendant eligible for the death penalty. *Id.* at 609. The Court reasoned that such aggravating factors function as “the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense.” *Id.* at 609 (quoting *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)). Because the Sixth Amendment guarantees a jury trial on every element of a criminal offense, the jury must determine whether the prosecution has proved each aggravating factor beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 610.

Florida’s sentencing scheme, however, permits a judge to impose a death sentence based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation. The jury in this case recommended death by a vote of seven to five. The Florida Supreme Court affirmed, holding that the Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury finding of aggravating factors. That holding cannot survive *Ring*.

Under Florida law, a death sentence may be imposed only if the judge finds at least one statutory aggravating factor. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3). But the jury’s role is merely advisory; the judge makes the ultimate factual findings. This scheme directly contravenes *Ring*, which requires the jury to find every fact that makes a defendant death-eligible. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 604. As this Court explained, “the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee” applies to “any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in the maximum punishment.” *Id.* at 589. Because Florida conditions the death penalty on the existence of aggravating factors, those factors must be found by a jury—and, as this Court has repeatedly held, jury findings in criminal cases must be unanimous. *See, e.g., Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948) (“In criminal cases, the requirement of unanimity is a safeguard of the highest importance.”).

Florida is the only state in the nation that permits a death sentence based on a non-unanimous jury finding of aggravating factors. *See Hurst v. Florida*, 202 So. 3d 40, 70 (Fla. 2016) (Pariente, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part) (noting that “every state that imposes the death penalty, as well as the federal system, requires a unanimous jury verdict as to the finding of an aggravating circumstance”). This outlier status underscores the constitutional infirmity of Florida’s scheme. *See Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 316 (2002) (looking to “the consistency of the direction of change” among states in evaluating Eighth Amendment claims).

**II. Florida’s Refusal to Require a Jury to Determine Mental Retardation Violates the Eighth Amendment.**

In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Id.* at 321. The Court left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” *Id.* at 317. But the Court made clear that the determination of mental retardation must be made with “the heightened reliability demanded by the Eighth Amendment in the determination whether the death penalty is appropriate in a particular case.” *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987).

Florida’s scheme fails to provide that reliability. Under Florida law, the question of mental retardation is decided by the judge, not the jury. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137. The jury is permitted to consider evidence of mental retardation only as mitigation, not as an absolute bar to execution. This approach undermines the Eighth Amendment’s requirement of “heightened reliability” in capital sentencing. *See Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976) (plurality opinion) (holding that the Eighth Amendment requires “reliability in the determination that death is the appropriate punishment in a specific case”).

The jury is the “conscience of the community” and plays a critical role in ensuring that the death penalty is imposed only in the most deserving cases. *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510, 519 (1968). By excluding the jury from the mental retardation determination, Florida deprives the defendant of a “meaningful basis for distinguishing the few cases in which [death] is imposed from the many cases in which it is not.” *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 313 (1972) (White, J., concurring). This is particularly troubling given that mental retardation is a “constitutionally significant” fact that “diminishes [a defendant’s] personal culpability.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318.

**III. Florida’s Non-Unanimous Jury Recommendation Violates the Eighth Amendment’s Prohibition Against Cruel and Unusual Punishment.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that capital sentencing procedures “channel the sentencer’s discretion by ‘clear and objective standards’ that produce ‘specific and detailed guidance.’” *Godfrey v. Georgia*, 446 U.S. 420, 428 (1980) (quoting *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 198 (1976) (joint opinion)). Florida’s scheme, which permits a death sentence based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation, fails to provide such guidance. A seven-to-five vote does not reflect the “community’s judgment” that death is the appropriate punishment. *See Witherspoon*, 391 U.S. at 519.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment’s “evolving standards of decency” require that capital sentencing procedures reflect a national consensus. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). As noted, Florida is the only state that permits a death sentence without a unanimous jury finding of aggravating factors. This isolation demonstrates that Florida’s scheme is “unusual” within the meaning of the Eighth Amendment. *See Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 312 (looking to “objective indicia of consensus” such as state legislation).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be reversed.
