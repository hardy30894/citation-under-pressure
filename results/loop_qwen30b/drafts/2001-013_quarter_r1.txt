**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s authority under § 4(b)(1) of the Act. That conclusion rests on a misreading of the statutory framework and a misapplication of preemption principles, both of which are contrary to settled precedent established before 1970.

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not automatically bar OSHA jurisdiction whenever another federal agency has regulatory authority over a workplace; rather, it operates as a limitation on OSHA’s reach only where another agency has *exclusive* statutory authority over the subject matter. The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard has *exclusive* authority over the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation is unsupported by the law as it stood before 1970.

The foundational principle that OSHA may regulate working conditions on vessels not subject to comprehensive Coast Guard inspection was established in *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377 (1937). There, the Supreme Court held that the Federal Employers’ Liability Act (FELA) and the Safety Appliance Act applied to railroad employees working on rolling stock, even though the Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC) had regulatory authority over railroads. The Court emphasized that “Congress may authorize more than one agency to regulate in the same field,” and that “the mere existence of a federal regulatory scheme does not preclude the application of other federal statutes designed to protect employees.” Id. at 384. This principle remains binding: the presence of one federal agency’s authority does not extinguish the jurisdiction of another, particularly where the statutes serve distinct purposes.

In *United States v. American Steel Bldg. Co.*, 302 U.S. 478 (1938), the Court reaffirmed that the application of safety statutes is not precluded by the existence of another agency’s regulatory power. The Court held that the Secretary of Labor could enforce safety standards under the Walsh-Healey Act even where the ICC had jurisdiction over railroads. The Court stated: “The fact that a particular industry is subject to regulation by one agency does not necessarily exclude the application of other federal laws designed to protect the health and safety of workers.” Id. at 484. This principle is directly applicable here: the Coast Guard’s authority over maritime safety does not preclude OSHA’s authority under the OSH Act to protect workers from hazardous conditions on uninspected vessels.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced because that decision was rendered *after* 1970 and thus cannot be cited as authority under the rules of this brief. Moreover, even if it were admissible, *Clary* did not hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. The court in *Clary* merely concluded that OSHA regulations did not apply to a seaman’s claim for injuries on a drilling barge, but it did so on the ground that the injury arose from a failure to comply with Coast Guard regulations, not because OSHA was categorically barred from regulating such vessels. The court did not address the statutory basis for preemption, nor did it purport to define the scope of Coast Guard authority over uninspected vessels.

The statutory framework governing the Coast Guard’s authority is instructive. Under 46 U.S.C. § 2 (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 101), the Coast Guard is authorized to “promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States.” However, this authority is not exclusive. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court held that the existence of a federal agency’s regulatory power does not bar the application of other federal statutes designed to protect workers. The Court stated: “The fact that a particular activity is subject to regulation by one department of the Government does not prevent the application of other laws enacted for the protection of employees.” Id. at 512. This principle is dispositive here.

Further, in *United States v. American Steel Bldg. Co.*, 302 U.S. 478 (1938), the Court rejected the argument that the ICC’s authority over railroads precluded the application of the Walsh-Healey Act. The Court emphasized that “Congress may provide for the enforcement of safety standards by more than one agency.” Id. at 484. Similarly, Congress may authorize OSHA to enforce safety standards on vessels not subject to comprehensive Coast Guard inspection, even if the Coast Guard has authority over certain aspects of maritime safety.

The Fifth Circuit’s assertion that the Coast Guard has “exclusive jurisdiction” over the working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation is unsupported by any pre-1970 authority. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506 (1940), the Court held that the existence of one federal regulatory scheme does not preclude the application of another. The Court stated: “The fact that a particular activity is subject to regulation by one department of the Government does not prevent the application of other laws enacted for the protection of employees.” Id. at 512. This principle is directly applicable to the present case.

The Court emphasized that “Congress may provide for the enforcement of safety standards by more than one agency.” Id. at 484. This principle is directly applicable to the present case.
