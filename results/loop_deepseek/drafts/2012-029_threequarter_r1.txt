**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Possess Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims Merely Because They Require Application of Patent Law.**

The Supreme Court of Texas erred in holding that federal courts have exclusive subject-matter jurisdiction over Minton’s state-law legal malpractice claim. The “arising under” jurisdiction of the federal courts, codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), extends only to cases where federal patent law creates the cause of action or where the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). This Court has long held that a case does not “arise under” federal law simply because a federal issue is present. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). Rather, the federal issue must be “substantial” and its resolution must be necessary to the state-law claim. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

Here, Minton’s legal malpractice claim is a creature of state law. The claim requires proof that Gunn’s negligence proximately caused Minton’s injuries. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). To establish causation, Minton must show that, but for Gunn’s failure to plead the experimental use exception, he would have prevailed in his patent infringement suit. This is a “case within a case” analysis, not a freestanding patent claim. *Schaeffer v. O’Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied). The patent issue is merely an element of the state-law claim, not the claim itself.

**II. The Patent Issue in This Case Is Not “Substantial” Under Governing Precedent.**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that federal jurisdiction does not lie where the federal issue is incidental to the state-law claim. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) (“A suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.”). In *Grable*, the Court emphasized that federal jurisdiction is proper only when the federal issue is actually disputed and substantial and when exercising jurisdiction would not disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

The patent issue here—whether the experimental use exception applies—is not substantial in the context of this malpractice suit. The resolution of that issue will not affect the validity of Minton’s patent or create binding precedent for future patent cases. It will only determine whether Gunn’s negligence caused Minton’s loss. This is precisely the type of state-law dispute that federal courts have long declined to hear. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

**III. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

Congress has carefully delineated the boundaries of federal patent jurisdiction. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). State courts have traditionally adjudicated legal malpractice claims, even those requiring reference to federal law. *See, e.g., New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005) (finding no federal jurisdiction over malpractice claim involving patent law). Allowing federal courts to assert exclusive jurisdiction over every state malpractice claim that touches on patent law would flood the federal docket and undermine the role of state courts in resolving professional negligence disputes. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

**IV. The Federal Circuit’s Contrary Precedent Is Not Binding and Is Inconsistent with Supreme Court Authority.**

The Supreme Court of Texas relied on *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007). But those decisions are not binding on this Court and are inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Grable*. The Federal Circuit’s approach—asserting jurisdiction over any malpractice claim that requires resolution of a patent issue—ignores the federalism analysis mandated by *Grable*. This Court should not adopt a rule that expands federal jurisdiction beyond the limits set by Congress and the Supreme Court.

**V. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings in state court.
