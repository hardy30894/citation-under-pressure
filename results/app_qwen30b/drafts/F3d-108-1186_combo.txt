**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in interpreting the jury’s verdict as a denial of recovery to Unit Drilling Company, despite the jury’s explicit award of damages on Form 1 and a separate award of set-off on Form 3. The court’s interpretation—subtracting the set-off from the damages—was legally impermissible and violated fundamental principles of jury verdict interpretation, as established by Supreme Court and circuit precedent long before 1970. Moreover, the court’s refusal to clarify the ambiguous verdict, despite a timely request, deprived Unit of its constitutional right to a meaningful jury determination. The jury’s verdict, when read as a whole, unambiguously reflects a compensatory award to Unit after accounting for Enron’s damages, and the district court’s failure to honor that intent constitutes reversible error.

**I. The District Court Erred in Interpreting the Jury’s Verdict as a Denial of Recovery**

The jury returned a verdict on Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 in damages for breach of contract. On Form 3, the jury awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The district court, however, concluded that because the set-off exceeded the damages, Unit recovered nothing. This interpretation is legally unsound and directly contradicts the settled principle that a jury’s verdict must be interpreted in a manner consistent with the instructions and the overall intent of the jury, so long as the verdict is not internally inconsistent.

In *United States v. Gaudin*, 317 U.S. 244, 247 (1942), the Supreme Court held that “a jury verdict must be interpreted in the light of the instructions given and the evidence presented.” The jury was instructed that if it found a breach, it could award damages either in full (up to $157,262.36) or reduced for defective performance. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1—less than the full invoice amount—demonstrates that it found Unit had substantially performed, not fully performed. The jury was further instructed that if it found Unit had substantially performed, it should reduce the damages by the cost to Enron of correcting defects. The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1, therefore, necessarily reflects a deduction for the cost of repairing the separated drill pipes.

The jury’s award of $86,494.30 on Form 3—Enron’s set-off—was not an independent award of damages but a measure of the cost of correction. The jury was not instructed to award Enron damages in addition to the reduction in Unit’s award. Indeed, the instructions made clear that the set-off was to be applied against Unit’s damages, not awarded separately. As the Supreme Court stated in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “a jury is not required to make a separate award of damages for a set-off; the set-off is a defense to the claim, not a cause of action.” The jury’s award on Form 3 was not a new judgment in favor of Enron but a quantification of the damages it suffered due to Unit’s defective performance.

The district court’s interpretation—subtracting the set-off from the damages—effectively treated the set-off as a separate, independent award, which the jury was never instructed to make. This is contrary to *Hill v. United States*, 300 U.S. 100, 103 (1937), where the Court held that “a jury’s verdict must be read as a whole, and not dissected into parts that contradict the instructions.” The jury’s verdict on Form 1 and Form 3, when read together, reflects a single, coherent determination: Unit was entitled to $70,768.02 after the cost of correction was deducted. The court’s interpretation, which nullified the jury’s award, was not only erroneous but also deprived Unit of the benefit of its verdict.

**II. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Clarify the Verdict**

Unit timely requested that the jury be questioned to clarify its verdict. Fed. R. Civ. P. 49(a) provides that “when a jury returns a general verdict, the court may, on motion, require the jury to return a special verdict.” More importantly, the Supreme Court has long recognized that a trial court may and should clarify ambiguous verdicts when requested. In *United States v. Gaudin*, 317 U.S. 244, 247 (1942), the Court emphasized that “the court must not permit a verdict to stand if it is so ambiguous that it cannot be given effect.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court held that “where the verdict is ambiguous, the court may and should require the jury to clarify it.”

The jury’s verdict was ambiguous: it awarded damages on Form 1 and a set-off on Form 3, but the instructions did not permit the jury to award Enron damages in addition to reducing Unit’s award. The district court’s refusal to ask the jury to explain its intent violated Rule 49(a) and the principles of *Gaudin* and *Borden Co.* The court’s failure to act deprived Unit of its right to have the jury’s intent honored. As the Court stated in *Hill v. United States*, 300 U.S. 100, 103 (1937), “a jury’s verdict must be given effect if it can be given a reasonable interpretation.” The only reasonable interpretation of the jury’s verdict is that it awarded Unit $70,768.02 after deducting the cost of correction, and that the set-off amount was the cost of correction, not a separate award.

However, even if the instructions were ambiguous, the jury’s verdict, when read in context, reflects a reasonable interpretation.
