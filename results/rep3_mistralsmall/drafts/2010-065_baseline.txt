**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO § 229.**

The Third Circuit correctly held that Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) (“[P]rivate parties ... have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.”). This rule is consistent with the fundamental principle that the Tenth Amendment protects the *residual sovereignty of the States*, not the rights of individuals. *See Bond v. United States*, 681 F.3d 149, 158 (3d Cir. 2012) (citing *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992)). Because Bond is not a State, she cannot invoke the structural protections of federalism to invalidate a federal statute. *See id.* at 159.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Tenth Amendment is not a “font of judicially discoverable rights” for private litigants. *See, e.g., South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 383 U.S. 301, 323–24 (1966) (rejecting a Tenth Amendment challenge to the Voting Rights Act brought by a State); *see also United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 567 n.3 (1995) (noting that the Tenth Amendment “limits the power of Congress ... not the rights of individuals”). The fact that Bond disagrees with Congress’s policy choice to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention does not confer standing to challenge the statute on federalism grounds. *See Tennessee Electric Power*, 306 U.S. at 144.

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. The Seventh Circuit’s decision is an outlier, and this Court has never recognized a private right of action under the Tenth Amendment. *See Bond*, 681 F.3d at 158–59. The overwhelming weight of authority rejects such claims. *See, e.g., United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006). The proper recourse for a private party aggrieved by a federal law is to challenge its application to her conduct—not to invoke the Tenth Amendment. *See Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 35 (1st Cir. 2005).

**II. § 229 IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER.**

Even if Bond had standing, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (“If [a] treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”). The Convention requires signatory nations to prohibit the use of chemical weapons by *all* persons within their territory, not just federal actors. *See* Chemical Weapons Convention, art. I, ¶ 1(a), Jan. 13, 1993, 32 I.L.M. 800, 804. Congress fulfilled this obligation by enacting § 229, which criminalizes the knowing use or possession of chemical weapons. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a).

Bond’s argument that § 229 violates the Tenth Amendment because it regulates purely local conduct is foreclosed by *Holland*. *See* 252 U.S. at 433–34 (upholding federal legislation regulating migratory birds despite its impact on state authority). The Court has repeatedly recognized that the Treaty Power permits Congress to legislate in areas traditionally reserved to the States when necessary to implement an international agreement. *See, e.g., United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004); *Lue v. United States*, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a quintessential example of such an agreement, addressing a global threat that transcends state boundaries. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 433.

Bond’s reliance on *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992), and *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997), is misplaced. Those cases involved federal statutes that commandeered state legislatures or executives to regulate purely intrastate activity. *See New York*, 505 U.S. at 176; *Printz*, 521 U.S. at 935. Section 229 does no such thing. It is a criminal statute of general applicability, enforced by federal authorities, and does not require state participation. *See Bond*, 681 F.3d at 162.

**III. § 229 IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE.**

Section 229 provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits. *See United States v. Powell*, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). The statute defines “chemical weapon” to include “any toxic chemical and its precursors” that can cause death or harm through chemical action. 18 U.S.C. § 229F(1)(A), (8)(A). Bond’s repeated attempts to poison her victim with potassium dichromate fall squarely within this definition. *See Bond*, 681 F.3d at 164. The statute’s scienter requirement—“knowingly”—further ensures that it does not criminalize innocent conduct. *See id.*

Bond’s vagueness challenge fails under *United States v. Ragen*, 314 U.S. 513, 523–24 (1942), which held that a statute is not vague as applied to a defendant whose conduct is clearly prohibited. Here, Bond’s conduct was deliberate, repeated, and targeted at causing harm. There is no risk of arbitrary enforcement. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power, and the statute is not unconstitutionally vague.
