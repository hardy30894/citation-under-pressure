**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE INDICTMENT FAILED TO STATE AN OFFENSE BECAUSE § 201(b)(2)(C) REQUIRES THE GOVERNMENT TO PROVE THAT APPELLANTS POSSESSED THE AUTHORITY TO PERFORM AN “OFFICIAL ACT.”**

The Fifth Circuit has long held that § 201(b)(2)(C) criminalizes the bribery of public officials only where the alleged bribe is tied to an “official act” as defined in § 201(a)(3). *United States v. McDonald*, 620 F.3d 434, 441 (5th Cir. 2010). An “official act” must be one that the defendant was authorized to perform. *Id.* (citing *United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers*, 526 U.S. 398, 404-05 (1999)). Here, the government failed to allege—and the evidence failed to show—that Mrs. Parker had any authority to approve or deny SSI benefits or to sign ALJ Aronson’s name. Her duties were clerical: assisting the ALJ, recording hearings, and typing decisions. She lacked the power to render a binding decision on any claim. Thus, the indictment was fatally defective for failing to state an offense under § 201(b)(2)(C).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223 (1914), is misplaced. In *Birdsall*, the Supreme Court upheld a conviction where the defendant, a government attorney, accepted a bribe to dismiss a case. *Id.* at 231. The Court reasoned that the defendant’s official duty included the discretion to decide whether to prosecute, even if that discretion was not explicitly codified. *Id.* Here, by contrast, Mrs. Parker had no discretion to approve benefits; she could not, and did not, render a final decision on any claim. Her actions—typing letters and forging signatures—were not “official acts” under § 201(a)(3) because they were outside her authority and did not constitute a decision or action on a pending matter. *See McDonald*, 620 F.3d at 441.

The Sixth Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968 (6th Cir. 1983), is similarly inapposite. In *Gjieli*, the defendant, a state trooper, accepted a bribe to refrain from issuing a traffic citation. *Id.* at 972. The court held that the trooper’s decision to refrain from writing a ticket was an “official act” because it was within the scope of his duties. *Id.* Here, Mrs. Parker’s forgery and extortion were not within the scope of her duties; they were criminal acts committed in derogation of her official responsibilities. *See United States v. Coughlin*, 898 F.2d 642, 646 (7th Cir. 1990) (holding that a court clerk’s forgery of a judge’s signature was not an “official act” under § 201(a)(3)).

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT APPELLANTS’ CONVICTIONS BECAUSE THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PROVE THAT THEY POSSESSED THE AUTHORITY TO PERFORM AN “OFFICIAL ACT.”**

Even assuming the indictment was sufficient, the evidence was insufficient to convict. The government’s theory rested on the premise that Mrs. Parker’s forgery of ALJ Aronson’s signature constituted an “official act.” This premise is legally erroneous. Forgery is not an “official act” under § 201(a)(3) because it is not a decision or action taken in an official capacity. *See Coughlin*, 898 F.2d at 646. The government’s reliance on *Birdsall* is again misplaced, as *Birdsall* involved a lawful exercise of prosecutorial discretion, not a criminal forgery. 233 U.S. at 231.

Moreover, the government failed to prove that Mr. Parker performed any “official act.” The evidence showed only that he accompanied Mrs. Parker to a meeting where she extorted money. There was no evidence that he had any authority to approve benefits or that he took any action in his official capacity. Thus, his conviction must be reversed for lack of evidence. *See Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (standard for sufficiency of evidence review).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the convictions of Joann A. Parker and Ralph Parker must be reversed. The indictment failed to state an offense under § 201(b)(2)(C), and the evidence was insufficient to support their convictions.
