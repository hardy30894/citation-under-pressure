**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL.**

The Controlled Substances Act’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Attorney General does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may delegate authority to executive officials so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide their discretion. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), satisfies this standard.

Section 811(h) directs the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only if it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and meets three enumerated criteria: (1) the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse; (2) the scope, duration, and significance of such abuse; and (3) the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). These factors are sufficiently specific to cabin the Attorney General’s discretion. *See Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 1419, 1423 (3d Cir. 1990) (upholding the delegation as constitutional). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress provided similarly broad but discernible standards. *See, e.g., Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424-26 (1944) (upholding delegation to fix price ceilings based on “fair and equitable” standards); *FCC v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co.*, 309 U.S. 134, 138 (1940) (upholding delegation to regulate broadcasting in the “public interest, convenience, or necessity”).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations that lacked any meaningful standards, whereas § 811(h) provides clear criteria. *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1423. The Court has since clarified that the non-delegation doctrine is not a “roving judicial power to strike down legislation” but a limited check on congressional abdication. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372. Section 811(h) easily clears this threshold.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S SUBDELEGATION TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is lawful. The Supreme Court has recognized that executive officials may subdelegate authority where the original delegation permits it. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding subdelegation of rulemaking authority under the Forest Reserve Act). The CSA’s text does not prohibit subdelegation, and the Attorney General’s regulation explicitly assigns the DEA Administrator the functions vested in him by the Act. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This is a permissible exercise of executive discretion. *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1424.

The Toubys argue that subdelegation is improper because § 811(h)(6) bars judicial review of temporary scheduling orders. But this provision does not limit constitutional challenges to the delegation itself. *See Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (holding that statutory preclusion of review does not bar constitutional claims). The Third Circuit correctly concluded that § 811(h)(6) does not foreclose review of the delegation’s validity. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1422.

**III. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING OF “EUPHORIA” WAS LAWFUL.**

The DEA’s temporary scheduling of 4-methylaminorex (Euphoria) complied with § 811(h). The Administrator found that Euphoria posed an “imminent hazard to the public safety” and met the statutory criteria. 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174 (1987). The Toubys do not dispute these findings. The Third Circuit properly deferred to the agency’s factual determinations under the deferential standard of *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-45 (1984). *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1425.

**CONCLUSION**

The delegation and subdelegation of temporary scheduling authority under the CSA are constitutional. The judgment below should be affirmed.
