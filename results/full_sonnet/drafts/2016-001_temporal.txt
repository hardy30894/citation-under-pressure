**ARGUMENT**

**The plain text and history of 35 U.S.C. § 289 establish that "the article of manufacture" to which a design is applied may be a component of a larger product, and that total-profit damages must be measured accordingly.**

Section 289 makes an infringer "liable to the extent of his total profit" from "the manufacture or sale of the article of manufacture to which such design or colorable imitation has been applied." The statute does not say "the product sold" or "the end product"; it says "article of manufacture," a term that Congress and the courts have long understood to describe the specific thing embodying the patented ornamental design, whatever the scope of that thing may be. Where a design patent claims only a component of a multicomponent product—here, a shape of a phone's front face or a grid of icons—the "article of manufacture" is that component, and the "total profit" recoverable is the profit derived from that article, not from the entire commercial product in which it is housed.

This reading follows the historical purpose of the statute. Design-patent damages developed in reaction to *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), and *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), in which this Court held that a design-patent owner suing for lost profits or damages under the general patent-damages provisions could recover only nominal damages absent proof of the profit specifically attributable to the design, separate from the value of the article's utilitarian features. Congress responded with what became § 289's predecessor, permitting recovery of "total profit" so that design patentees would not be relegated to nominal recovery merely because the infringer's profit could not be neatly apportioned between ornamental and functional value. But relieving patentees of that apportionment burden does not answer—and was never understood to answer—the antecedent question of what "article" produced the profit in the first place. That antecedent question remains governed by the ordinary meaning of "article of manufacture," which looks to the thing to which the design has actually been applied.

This Court's apportionment jurisprudence confirms that patent damages must be tied to the specific item or feature the patent covers, not to an entire commercial product merely because the patented feature is incorporated in it. In *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), this Court explained that a patentee seeking an infringer's profits "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion" the profits attributable to the patented feature from those attributable to unpatented features, and that the patentee's proof "must be reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative." *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121. Although *Garretson* addressed utility-patent accounting rather than § 289's predecessor, its underlying premise—that a defendant's profit recoverable by a patentee corresponds to the specific patented article or feature, not to the whole commercial product—illuminates what Congress meant when it directed that a design-patent infringer disgorge profit from "the article of manufacture to which such design . . . has been applied." The "article of manufacture" language was chosen precisely to identify the relevant profit-generating unit; it was not chosen to sweep in every unrelated component sold together with it.

Courts applying the design-patent damages statute in the decades following its enactment confirmed this component-specific approach. In *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916), the Second Circuit considered a design patent covering the ornamental case of a piano, not the piano's musical mechanism. The court limited the profit award to the profit attributable to the case—the "article of manufacture" bearing the patented design—rather than awarding the defendant's total profit from selling complete pianos. The court's approach reflected the understanding that where "the design patent is for a distinct and separable part of a more complex product," the profit recoverable is the profit "upon that part," not upon the whole. *Id.* at 80–81. That result cannot be reconciled with a rule mandating recovery of an infringer's entire enterprise-wide profit whenever a patented design happens to adorn one component of a multicomponent device.

By contrast, in cases where the patented design was applied to the entire visible article offered for sale—with no distinct, severable component apart from the design itself—courts properly awarded profits on the whole article. *Untermeyer v. Freund*, 58 F. 205 (2d Cir. 1893), so held where the design patent covered the ornamentation of an entire spoon handle inseparable from the utensil itself. *Untermeyer* does not undermine the component-based rule; it illustrates it; when the "article of manufacture" is coextensive with the product sold, total profit on the product is total profit on the article. But when, as here, the patented design covers only a discrete component of a much larger multifunction device, *Bush & Lane Piano* controls, and the profit recoverable must likewise be limited to that component.

Applying that settled understanding, the courts below erred by awarding Apple Samsung's entire profit on complete smartphones, when the design patents at issue claimed only discrete features—such as a rounded rectangular front face and colored icon grid—rather than the smartphones' overall configuration or their myriad unclaimed functional components. Because the "article of manufacture" under § 289 is the component to which the patented design was applied, damages should be limited accordingly.
