**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S ADA AND MHRA CLAIMS BECAUSE A REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT PINE LAWN REGARDED EPPS AS DISABLED WITHIN THE MEANING OF THE ADA AND MHRA.

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12102(2)(A), a person is “regarded as” disabled if an employer “perceives [the individual] as having a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities.” *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The inquiry is not whether the individual actually has a disability, but whether the employer “regarded” the individual as having one. *Id.* at 67. The Eighth Circuit has held that “regard as” status may be established by evidence that the employer “treated the employee as if he were disabled,” even if the employer did not formally label the employee as such. *Henderson v. City of St. Louis*, 344 F.3d 733, 738 (8th Cir. 2003).

Here, the record establishes that Pine Lawn treated Epps as disabled in a manner that substantially limited his major life activities—specifically, his ability to work. Chief Hardy’s memoranda, written in September 1999 and March 2000, repeatedly referenced Dr. Mirkin’s conclusion that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation” and that he “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” These statements, made by the Chief of Police and relied upon by the Mayor and Board of Aldermen in terminating Epps, demonstrate that Pine Lawn perceived Epps as permanently unable to perform the essential functions of a police officer—a major life activity. *See* *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68 (regard as disability includes “perception that an individual is substantially limited in performing a major life activity”).

Moreover, Pine Lawn’s decision to terminate Epps was based on the belief that he could not perform the physical demands of the job—bending, squatting, running—despite the absence of any formal job reassignment or accommodation process. The fact that Pine Lawn refused to consider light-duty assignments, despite the availability of such positions in other departments, further supports the conclusion that it regarded Epps as disabled. *See* *Henderson*, 344 F.3d at 739 (employer’s failure to consider accommodation, despite known limitations, supports “regarded as” claim).

Pine Lawn’s reliance on Dr. Mirkin’s private medical opinion—obtained at Epps’s own expense and not deemed work-related by the workers’ compensation carrier—does not negate the employer’s perception. The ADA does not require that the employer’s perception be objectively correct; it only requires that the employer “regard” the employee as disabled. *See* *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68. The record shows that Pine Lawn acted on the belief that Epps was permanently disabled, and that belief was the basis for his termination. This is sufficient to establish a genuine issue of material fact as to whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled under the ADA.

The Missouri Human Rights Act, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010(7), defines “disability” similarly to the ADA, including individuals “regarded as” having a disability. *See* *Henderson*, 344 F.3d at 738. Thus, the same evidence supports Epps’s MHRA claim. The district court erred in concluding that Epps could not establish a “regarded as” disability, as the record contains ample evidence that Pine Lawn perceived Epps as unable to perform his job due to a physical impairment.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S RETALIATION CLAIM UNDER MISSOURI REVISED STATUTES § 287.780.

Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780(1) prohibits employers from retaliating against employees who file workers’ compensation claims. *See* *Henderson v. City of St. Louis*, 344 F.3d 733, 740 (8th Cir. 2003) (holding that § 287.780 creates a private right of action). The statute protects employees from adverse employment actions taken “because of” a workers’ compensation claim. *Id.* at 741.

Here, Epps filed two workers’ compensation claims—on August 1, 1998, and April 13, 1999. Chief Hardy, upon reviewing Epps’s personnel file, noted that Epps “had filed quite a few workers’ compensation claims, and that he missed a lot of work.” This observation directly preceded Hardy’s recommendation to terminate Epps. The timing, combined with the explicit reference to Epps’s claims, creates a triable issue of fact as to whether the termination was motivated by retaliation.

Pine Lawn argues that its decision was based on Epps’s inability to perform job duties, not his claims. But the record shows that the decision was based on Dr. Mirkin’s private medical opinion—obtained after Epps paid for the visit and after he filed his second claim. The fact that Pine Lawn relied on a medical opinion that was not work-related and that was obtained at Epps’s own expense does not insulate it from liability under § 287.780. *See* *Henderson*, 344 F.3d at 741 (employer cannot use “pretextual” justification to avoid liability for retaliation).
