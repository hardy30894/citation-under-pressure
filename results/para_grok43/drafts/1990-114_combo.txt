The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not forbid a State from classifying its officers by age when the classification rests upon a rational basis. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911); *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27, 31-32 (1885). A classification “which has some reasonable basis does not offend” the Clause “merely because it is not made with mathematical nicety or because in practice it results in some inequality.” *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949). Missouri’s requirement that its judges retire at seventy satisfies this standard. The provision prevents the continued service of officers whose faculties may diminish with advanced years, *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm’rs*, 330 U.S. 552, 563 (1947), and permits the orderly infusion of new judges without individualized removal proceedings. Such a line “avoids the necessity of making difficult and often invidious determinations,” *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955), and therefore lies within the broad latitude the Clause affords the States.

The same federalism principles that protect a State’s authority to structure its own government likewise preclude reading the Age Discrimination in Employment Act to reach appointed state judges. The Constitution “presupposes the continued existence of the states and … the preservation to the states of the powers necessary to their continued existence.” *Collector v. Day*, 78 U.S. (11 Wall.) 113, 126 (1871). “The power to create and to regulate the duties of” state judicial officers “is one of the most important and delicate” functions reserved to the States. *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 431 (1819). Congress does not lightly intrude upon that domain. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 203 (1824). The statutory definition of “employee” excludes “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judges necessarily exercise the “judicial Power” to “say what the law is,” *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803), and therefore occupy precisely the policymaking positions the exception removes from federal regulation.

Because the Act contains no “clear and manifest” statement that Congress intended to displace the States’ traditional control over the tenure of their judges, *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 371 (1886), the Missouri constitutional provision remains operative. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
