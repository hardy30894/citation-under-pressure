**Argument**

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree constitutes a “settlement” that “resolved” Guam’s liability under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B).**

The United States and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) entered into a Consent Decree in 2004, which the District Court of Guam approved. The Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system. Under the terms of the Decree, Guam agreed to “complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). This agreement is a judicially approved settlement.

Under common law principles, a settlement is a compromise of claims. *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, 265 (1947). By agreeing to pay a civil penalty and perform the cleanup obligations, Guam compromised its claims against the United States. The Decree’s requirement that Guam “resolve” its liability for the response action is satisfied by these substantive obligations. The Decree is not merely a procedural agreement; it is a settlement that resolves liability.

**II. The 3-year statute of limitations for contribution claims has expired.**

CERCLA § 113(g)(3) imposes a three-year statute of limitations on contribution claims. The limitations period begins to run from the date of the settlement. *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 30 (1953). The Consent Decree was entered in 2004. Guam did not initiate this action until 2017. More than three years have passed since the Decree was entered. Consequently, Guam’s contribution claim is time-barred.

**III. The § 107 claim is precluded.**

Once a party has resolved its liability under § 113(f)(3)(B), it cannot pursue a § 107 cost-recovery claim against the settling party. The statute is clear. *Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia*, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958). “Where a statute is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe the statute other than according to its terms, or to depart from its clear requirements.” The “clear” language of § 113(f)(3)(B) links the right to contribution to the resolution of liability. Because the Consent Decree resolved Guam’s liability, the right to contribution arose, and the three-year limitations period began. Guam may not now pursue a § 107 claim.
