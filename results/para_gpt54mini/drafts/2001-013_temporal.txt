Mallard’s reading would require this Court to infer a sweeping exclusion from a statute that Congress enacted to assure nationwide workplace safety. But the only precedents available before 1970 point the other way: when Congress creates overlapping federal safety regimes, courts do not lightly presume that one regime silently displaces another absent a clear statutory command. See Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. Public Service Commission of Indiana, 250 U.S. 566 (1919); Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1 (1937).

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act withdraws OSHA authority only where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The critical term is “exercise.” Mallard has not shown that the Coast Guard had exercised comprehensive authority over the specific working conditions cited here. Before 1970, the Supreme Court repeatedly treated preclusion as dependent on actual regulatory action, not on the mere existence of a possible federal power. In Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1 (1937), the Court upheld state safety regulation where Congress had not occupied the field by affirmative federal regulation, explaining that federal legislation does not displace local authority absent a clear and comprehensive regulatory scheme. Id. at 9-10. Likewise, in Todd Shipyards Corp. v. Muñoz, 309 U.S. 90 (1940), the Court recognized that overlapping authority is not enough; the question is whether Congress or the responsible federal agency has actually regulated the subject matter in a way that leaves no room for supplementation. Id. at 95-96.

That principle controls here. Mallard points to the Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels, but limited regulation is not the same as exclusive regulation of occupational safety. The Court has long distinguished between a federal agency’s general supervisory powers and an actual regime that displaces other law. See Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. Public Service Commission of Indiana, 250 U.S. 566, 569-70 (1919) (“the mere silence of Congress” does not authorize an inference of preemption where the federal scheme does not address the precise safety matter at issue). The Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over navigation and marine safety does not, by itself, demonstrate that it has exercised statutory authority over the full range of occupational hazards presented by an offshore drilling barge. See Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co., 302 U.S. 1, 9-10 (1937).

Nor does Mallard’s vessel-based argument answer the statutory question. The OSH Act applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace,” and nothing in pre-1970 precedent suggests that a floating work platform is categorically exempt from federal workplace regulation merely because it operates on water. To the contrary, maritime cases before 1970 recognize that vessels can be workplaces and that workers aboard them remain entitled to the protections of applicable safety law. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205, 214-15 (1917) (recognizing the distinct but not exclusive character of maritime regulation); Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co., 264 U.S. 219, 227-28 (1924) (allowing regulation where it does not conflict with governing maritime law). Those decisions do not establish Coast Guard exclusivity; they show only that maritime activity may implicate specialized rules. That is not enough to defeat a later-enacted workplace statute unless Congress or the responsible agency has clearly occupied the field. See Kelly, 302 U.S. at 9-10.

Mallard’s reliance on minimal Coast Guard regulation also misunderstands the structure of the OSH Act. Congress used Section 4(b)(1) to avoid duplicative regulation only where another agency is actually regulating occupational safety and health. A narrow set of Coast Guard rules on equipment or vessel operations does not amount to a comprehensive code governing the explosion hazards, housekeeping, maintenance, or emergency procedures cited by OSHA. Pre-1970 authority rejects the notion that partial regulation creates total immunity from otherwise valid federal safety law. See Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. Public Service Commission of Indiana, 250 U.S. 566, 569-70 (1919); Todd Shipyards Corp. v. Muñoz, 309 U.S. 90, 95-96 (1940).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary premise—that the Coast Guard’s general maritime authority necessarily forecloses OSHA—finds no support in pre-1970 precedent. This Court should hold instead that Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA jurisdiction only when the Coast Guard has actually exercised statutory authority over the cited working conditions, not where regulation is limited and incomplete. On that reading, OSHA properly issued the citations.
