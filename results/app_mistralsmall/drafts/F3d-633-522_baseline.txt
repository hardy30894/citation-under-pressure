**ARGUMENT**

**I. DHL VIOLATED THE WARN ACT BY FAILING TO PROVIDE 60 DAYS’ NOTICE BEFORE CLOSING ITS CHICAGOLAND FACILITIES AND TERMINATING EMPLOYEES.**

The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act requires employers to provide 60 days’ written notice before a “plant closing” or “mass layoff.” 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). A “plant closing” is defined as “the permanent or temporary shutdown of a single site of employment, or one or more facilities or operating units within a single site of employment, if the shutdown results in an employment loss at the single site of employment during any 30-day period for 50 or more employees.” *Id.* § 2101(a)(2). Here, DHL’s decision to cease domestic shipping operations and close five Chicagoland facilities—each of which employed more than 50 workers—plainly triggered the WARN Act’s notice requirement. *See, e.g., Int’l Union, United Auto., Aerospace & Agric. Implement Workers of Am. v. Dana Corp.,* 620 F.3d 631, 635 (6th Cir. 2010) (holding that a facility shutdown resulting in 50+ layoffs constitutes a “plant closing” under WARN).

DHL concedes that it provided no WARN notice before announcing its decision on November 10, 2008, or implementing it on January 30, 2009. Instead, it argues that the severance agreements negotiated with Local 705 mooted the notice requirement. This argument fails. The WARN Act’s notice requirement is mandatory and cannot be waived by private agreement. *See, e.g., United Steelworkers of Am. v. Royster-Clark, Inc.,* 564 F.3d 594, 600 (4th Cir. 2009) (“WARN’s notice provisions are not subject to waiver by collective bargaining agreements.”). Courts have consistently rejected the notion that post-termination severance agreements cure a WARN violation. *See, e.g., Collins v. Gee West Seattle, Inc.,* 140 F.3d 1258, 1263 (9th Cir. 1998) (holding that severance payments do not retroactively satisfy WARN’s notice requirement).

DHL’s reliance on *In re IBP, Inc. WARN Act Litigation,* 535 F. Supp. 2d 1196 (D. Kan. 2008), is misplaced. That case involved a plant closing where employees were given notice *and* severance, and the court held that the severance did not negate the notice already provided. *Id.* at 1204. Here, by contrast, DHL provided *no* notice at all. The severance agreements thus cannot retroactively cure the violation.

**II. THE WARN ACT’S “UNFORESEEABLE BUSINESS CIRCUMSTANCES” EXCEPTION DOES NOT APPLY.**

DHL alternatively argues that its decision to close the facilities was an “unforeseeable business circumstance” under 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(2)(A), relieving it of the notice requirement. This exception is “narrowly construed” and applies only where the business circumstance was “not reasonably foreseeable” at the time notice would otherwise have been required. *See, e.g., Watson v. Michigan Indus., Inc.,* 499 F.3d 540, 546 (6th Cir. 2007). DHL’s decision to exit the domestic shipping market was not unforeseeable; it had been losing market share for years and had been shedding jobs since August 2008. *See, e.g., Int’l Union, United Auto.,* 620 F.3d at 637 (finding that a decline in business over several years was foreseeable). Moreover, the exception applies only where the circumstance “was caused by some sudden, dramatic, and unexpected action or condition outside the employer’s control.” *Id.* (quoting 20 C.F.R. § 639.9(a)). DHL’s strategic business decision to discontinue domestic operations was entirely within its control and thus does not qualify.

**III. THE WAIVERS SIGNED BY APPELLANTS ARE INVALID AS AGAINST PUBLIC POLICY.**

Appellants’ waivers of WARN claims are unenforceable because they contravene the Act’s remedial purpose. The WARN Act was enacted to “protect workers, their families, and communities” by ensuring advance notice of layoffs. 29 U.S.C. § 2101(b). Courts have refused to enforce waivers that undermine this purpose. *See, e.g., Royster-Clark,* 564 F.3d at 601 (“WARN’s notice provisions are mandatory and cannot be waived.”); *Collins,* 140 F.3d at 1263 (holding that waivers of WARN claims are unenforceable). Here, the waivers were presented to employees under extreme time pressure—drivers had just two days to decide whether to accept severance and forfeit their WARN claims—further underscoring their invalidity. *See, e.g., Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc.,* 522 U.S. 422, 427 (1998) (holding that waivers must be “knowing and voluntary” and not the result of “overreaching”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Appellants’ WARN claims. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
