**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts only funds that are set aside for the debtor’s own retirement. An inherited IRA does not qualify because it is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle that must be distributed within a few years of the original owner’s death. The Seventh Circuit correctly held that such an account is not “retirement funds” within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12).

**I. The Plain Meaning of “Retirement Funds” Excludes Inherited IRAs**

The term “retirement funds” is not defined in the Bankruptcy Code, so it must be given its ordinary meaning: funds held for use after the owner stops working. *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. 482, 486 (1868) (statutory words are understood in their ordinary sense). An inherited IRA fails that ordinary meaning because the debtor cannot hold the funds until retirement. Mandatory distributions begin within a year of the original owner’s death. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). The account is a current-consumption vehicle, not a retirement savings device. The debtor may withdraw the entire balance at any time without penalty and use it for any purpose. That is the antithesis of a retirement fund.

**II. The Character of Property Is Determined by Current Use, Not Origin**

The Clarks argue that the inherited IRA is exempt because it once belonged to the decedent’s retirement account. That conflates a property’s origin with its current character. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the tax treatment of property depends on its use and enjoyment in the hands of the current owner. *Burnet v. Harmel*, 287 U.S. 103, 110 (1932) (the character of income for tax purposes is determined by the nature of the transaction in the taxpayer’s hands). An asset that was once a retirement fund loses that character when transferred to a non-spouse beneficiary who cannot use it for retirement. *Helvering v. Horst*, 311 U.S. 112, 118 (1940) (the economic benefit of property is taxed to the person who realizes it, not the person who created it). The inherited IRA gives Heidi Heffron-Clark the right to withdraw funds immediately, free of penalty, and to use them for any purpose—precisely the opposite of a retirement fund. *Lucas v. Earl*, 281 U.S. 111, 114–15 (1930) (income is taxed to the earner, not the assignee; the character of income follows the earner’s activity).

**III. The Internal Revenue Code’s Treatment Confirms That Inherited IRAs Are Not Retirement Funds**

The Bankruptcy Code incorporates the Internal Revenue Code’s taxonomy of retirement accounts. An inherited IRA cannot receive new contributions, cannot be rolled over into the debtor’s own retirement account, and must be fully distributed within a fixed period. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). These features distinguish it from a traditional IRA, which is designed to accumulate wealth until the owner’s retirement. *Hecht v. Malley*, 265 U.S. 144, 157 (1924) (the legal definition of a trust depends on the settlor’s intent and the beneficiary’s rights). The debtor’s rights in an inherited IRA are those of a beneficiary receiving a time-limited payout, not of an owner saving for retirement. *Blair v. Commissioner*, 300 U.S. 5, 12–13 (1937) (a beneficiary’s interest in a trust is not the same as the settlor’s; the character of the interest is determined by the terms of the trust). Here, the terms of the inherited IRA compel immediate distribution, precluding the debtor from using it as retirement savings.

**IV. The Exemption’s Purpose Supports the Seventh Circuit’s Holding**

The purpose of the retirement-funds exemption is to protect funds the debtor will need after leaving the workforce. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (exemption statutes are designed to preserve the debtor’s ability to support himself and his family). An inherited IRA, by contrast, is a source of immediate cash that the debtor can spend at any time. Allowing an exemption for such an account would permit debtors to shelter assets freely available for current consumption, defeating the Bankruptcy Code’s goal of equitable distribution to creditors. The homestead exemption provides a parallel: a debtor cannot claim a house as a homestead if it is not her residence. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A) (requiring the debtor’s domicile). The same principle applies here—the retirement-funds exemption requires that the funds be for the debtor’s retirement.

**V. Strict Construction Requires Rejecting the Debtor’s Expansive Reading**

Bankruptcy exemptions are matters of legislative grace, not entitlement. *Stellwagen v. Clum*, 245 U.S. 605, 618 (1918). The debtor claiming an exemption must prove that the property falls squarely within the statutory language. *Wetherell v. United States*, 218 U.S. 346, 356 (1910) (tax exemptions are strictly construed). The debtor’s expansive reading—that any funds that *once* were retirement funds remain exempt forever—has no limiting principle. It would exempt funds that were expressly withdrawn from a retirement account and given to a child, as the Seventh Circuit observed. The Supreme Court has long held that tax-deferred status alone does not confer exemption; the property must also serve a retirement purpose. *Stratton’s Independence, Ltd. v. Howbert*, 231 U.S. 399, 414 (1913) (tax exemption for mining corporations required that the income be used for mining, not merely that it came from mining). An inherited IRA provides tax deferral but no retirement purpose.

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
