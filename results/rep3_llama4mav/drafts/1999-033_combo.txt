The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Court of Appeals' holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective" is an unwarranted extension of equal protection jurisprudence.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See Strauder v. West Virginia, 100 U.S. 303 (1880); Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886). In Strauder, this Court held that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits state action that discriminates against individuals based on their race. 100 U.S. at 306. Similarly, in Yick Wo, the Court struck down a law that was administered in a discriminatory manner against Chinese laundry owners. 118 U.S. at 373-74.

The concept of equal protection has been consistently applied to protect against class-based discrimination. See Truax v. Raich, 239 U.S. 33 (1915); Buchanan v. Warley, 245 U.S. 60 (1917). In Truax, the Court held that a state law that discriminated against aliens was unconstitutional. 239 U.S. at 43. In Buchanan, the Court struck down a municipal ordinance that prohibited African Americans from owning property in certain areas. 245 U.S. at 82.

The Court has also recognized that equal protection claims require a showing of discriminatory intent against a particular class or group. See Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944); Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield, 247 U.S. 350 (1918). In Snowden, the Court held that a plaintiff must show that the state's action was taken with the intent to discriminate against a particular class. 321 U.S. at 8. In Sunday Lake Iron Co., the Court held that a tax assessment that was intentionally discriminatory against a particular taxpayer was unconstitutional. 247 U.S. at 353.

Allowing a "class of one" to bring an equal protection claim would be a significant departure from the traditional understanding of equal protection. See Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs, 330 U.S. 552 (1947); Tigner v. Texas, 310 U.S. 141 (1940). In Kotch, the Court held that a state's pilotage laws did not violate equal protection, even though they may have benefited certain individuals over others. 330 U.S. at 563. In Tigner, the Court held that a state law that exempted certain individuals from antitrust laws did not violate equal protection. 310 U.S. at 149.

Furthermore, the Court has consistently held that the Equal Protection Clause is not a source of substantive rights, but rather a guarantee that similar individuals will be treated similarly. See Barbier v. Connolly, 113 U.S. 27 (1885); Missouri Pac. Ry. Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885). In Barbier, the Court held that a state law that prohibited certain activities on Sundays did not violate equal protection. 113 U.S. at 31. In Missouri Pac. Ry. Co., the Court held that a state law that imposed certain penalties on railroads did not violate equal protection. 115 U.S. at 523.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a cause of action for a "class of one." Respondent Olech did not allege membership in a class or group, and therefore, her equal protection claim is not cognizable.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
