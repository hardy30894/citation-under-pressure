The District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. Under Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a class may be certified only if "the court finds that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating the controversy." The predominance requirement is not met here because the plaintiffs' damages model fails to measure damages attributable to their theory of liability.

In Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), this Court held that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence and arguments in making the class certification decision. The Court emphasized that the analysis requires "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations" and "may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits." Id. at 317. The plaintiffs' damages expert, Dr. James McClave, presented a model that purported to measure damages on a class-wide basis. However, the model failed to isolate damages attributable to the alleged anticompetitive conduct, specifically the clustering scheme.

The District Court's acceptance of Dr. McClave's model was an abuse of discretion. The model assumed that all four theories of antitrust impact identified by Dr. McClave were valid, despite the Court's subsequent narrowing of the class's claims to a single theory: that Comcast's clustering deterred overbuilder competition. As a result, the model did not provide a reliable measure of damages attributable to the clustering scheme. This failure is fatal to the plaintiffs' claim, as the Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff must prove that their damages are attributable to the alleged anticompetitive conduct. See Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562-566 (1931).

Furthermore, the District Court's decision to certify the class despite the flaws in Dr. McClave's model was an improper application of law to fact. The Court should have required the plaintiffs to provide a damages model that was consistent with their theory of liability. By failing to do so, the Court effectively shifted the burden of proof from the plaintiffs to the defendant, requiring Comcast to disprove the plaintiffs' damages claims.

The Court of Appeals' affirmance of the District Court's decision was also erroneous. The appellate court failed to properly review the District Court's findings of fact, which were clearly erroneous. The Court of Appeals should have applied the "clearly erroneous" standard set forth in United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), and reversed the District Court's decision.

In conclusion, the District Court erred in certifying the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The plaintiffs' damages model failed to measure damages attributable to their theory of liability, and the District Court's acceptance of the model was an abuse of discretion. The Court of Appeals should have reversed the District Court's decision, and this Court should now reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals.
