**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment demands that a defendant who seeks to waive the right to counsel and plead guilty possess the mental capacity for a reasoned choice among the available alternatives. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399 (1993) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987)). This standard is higher than the minimal competency required to stand trial, which merely requires a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960). The trial court erred by failing to apply this heightened standard when Moran sought to waive his constitutional rights.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that due process requires a trial court to initiate a competency hearing whenever it has or should have a good-faith doubt about a defendant’s ability to make a reasoned choice. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *Seiling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981); *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479 (9th Cir. 1983)). This duty is rooted in the principle that waivers of fundamental constitutional rights must be "voluntary, knowing, and intelligent." *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969). The trial court’s failure to conduct such an inquiry violated due process.

The trial court’s reliance on psychiatric evaluations assessing Moran’s competency to stand trial was misplaced. Competency to stand trial requires only a rational understanding of the proceedings and the ability to consult with counsel. *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. Competency to waive constitutional rights, by contrast, demands a higher level of mental functioning—the capacity for a reasoned choice. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. The psychiatric reports in this case addressed only Moran’s ability to stand trial and did not evaluate his capacity for reasoned choice. Thus, they were insufficient to support a finding of competency to waive counsel and plead guilty.

The record contained substantial evidence of Moran’s incompetence. Moran had recently attempted suicide, was heavily medicated, and responded to the court in monosyllables. These factors should have triggered a good-faith doubt about his competency. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1982)). The trial court’s failure to inquire further violated due process.

The state’s post-conviction proceedings did not cure this error. The state court applied the wrong legal standard—competency to stand trial rather than the heightened "reasoned choice" standard. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. Moreover, the psychiatric evaluations relied upon were not designed to assess Moran’s capacity for reasoned choice. Thus, the state court’s conclusion that Moran was competent to waive his rights was unsupported by the record.

This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s judgment. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights. The trial court’s failure to do so here violated due process, and the state’s post-conviction proceedings did not remedy the error.
