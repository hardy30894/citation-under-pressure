**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing enhancements—contradicts settled precedent and undermines the fundamental protection against multiple prosecutions for the same offense. This Court should reverse.

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The Clause is not limited to trials on the underlying substantive offense; it applies whenever a defendant is subjected to a second prosecution for the same offense, including when the prosecution seeks to enhance punishment based on prior convictions. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969) (holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause is “fundamental to the American scheme of justice” and enforceable against the states via the Fourteenth Amendment). The Clause’s protection is not contingent on the severity of the punishment; it applies equally to capital and noncapital cases. *United States v. Halper*, 490 U.S. 435, 447 (1989) (“The Double Jeopardy Clause protects the individual from being subjected to multiple punishments for the same offense, regardless of the severity of the punishment.”).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that sentencing proceedings may constitute “trials” for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause when they involve the same essential characteristics as trials on guilt or innocence. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a sentencing enhancement after a state appellate court reversed a prior finding of a prior felony conviction. The Court emphasized that the sentencing hearing in that case “involved the same essential characteristics as a trial on guilt or innocence,” including the presentation of evidence, the burden of proof, and the risk of a more severe penalty. *Id.* at 439. The Court concluded that “the State’s interest in obtaining a more severe sentence does not justify a second prosecution for the same offense.” *Id.* at 440.

The Court reaffirmed this principle in *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), where it held that a defendant could not be retried on a prior conviction allegation after a federal appellate court reversed a sentencing enhancement based on insufficient evidence. The Court stated that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense,” and that “the sentencing proceeding involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 209–210. The Court rejected the argument that the sentencing proceeding was merely “a determination of the appropriate sentence,” noting that “the defendant is entitled to the same protections against double jeopardy as in a trial on the merits.” *Id.* at 210.

The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* and *Rumsey* on the ground that they involved capital cases is legally unsound. The Court in *Bullington* did not limit its analysis to capital cases. The opinion stated that the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” were present regardless of the penalty at stake. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. The Court in *Rumsey* similarly applied the Clause to a noncapital sentencing enhancement. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209. The Supreme Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies only in capital cases. To the contrary, *United States v. Halper*, 490 U.S. 435 (1989), held that the Clause applies to civil penalties that are “punitive in nature,” regardless of the form of the penalty. The same logic applies to sentencing enhancements that increase punishment based on prior convictions.

The California Supreme Court also erred in relying on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection in noncapital sentencing. *Williams* held that due process does not require a jury trial or formal evidentiary procedures at sentencing. *Id.* at 245–246. But *Williams* did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court in *Williams* explicitly noted that the “due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251. That observation does not justify the absence of double jeopardy protection; it merely recognizes that sentencing procedures may differ from trial procedures. The Double Jeopardy Clause is not a procedural rule; it is a substantive constitutional protection. *United States v. Jenkins*, 420 U.S. 358, 364 (1975) (“The Double Jeopardy Clause is not a mere procedural safeguard; it is a substantive protection against multiple punishments for the same offense.”).

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), is equally misplaced. In *Caspari*, the Court noted that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, but did not hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply in noncapital cases. The Court’s observation was made in the context of determining whether a new rule of law required prospective application under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392. The Court did not decide the constitutional question before it. To treat this passing observation as a holding is to misread the precedent.
