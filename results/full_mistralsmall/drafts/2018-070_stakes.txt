**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A “GAP” IN FEDERAL COVERAGE BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ADOPTED AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with [federal law], the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State . . . are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. This Court has long recognized that the OCSLA is a “gap-filling statute.” *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 359 (1969) (“[T]he OCSLA was designed to fill a ‘gap’ in the coverage of federal law.”); *Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1126, 1135 (9th Cir. 2010) (en banc) (noting that OCSLA “fills a gap in federal law”).

The Fifth Circuit has consistently applied this “gap-filling” requirement. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law applies on the OCS only “to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. This rule was reaffirmed in *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990) (“State law is adopted as surrogate federal law only to the extent it fills a gap in federal coverage.”). The Fifth Circuit’s approach is consistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction that the OCSLA “was designed to fill a ‘gap’ in the coverage of federal law.” *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 359.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law—ignores the OCSLA’s gap-filling purpose. The Ninth Circuit’s approach would allow state law to displace federal law even where federal law is comprehensive, contrary to the statute’s design. *See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988) (noting that the OCSLA “was intended to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the OCS).

II. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE STATUTORY SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO GAP FOR STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS TO FILL.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., is a comprehensive federal statute governing minimum wages, overtime, and related employment standards. The FLSA expressly preserves more protective state wage and hour laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218 (“[N]o provision of [the FLSA] shall excuse noncompliance with any Federal or State law or municipal ordinance establishing a minimum wage higher than the minimum wage established under [the FLSA].”), but this savings clause does not create a gap in federal coverage. *See, e.g., Cipriano v. Disc. Drug Mart, Inc.*, 785 F.3d 1140, 1147 (9th Cir. 2015) (noting that the FLSA’s savings clause allows state law to supplement, not supplant, federal law).

The Supreme Court has recognized that the FLSA is a “comprehensive legislative scheme.” *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 737 (1981). The Court has also held that federal labor laws preempt state law where federal law is comprehensive. *See, e.g., San Diego Bldg. Trades Council v. Garmon*, 359 U.S. 236, 244 (1959) (federal labor law preempts state law where federal law is comprehensive). The FLSA’s savings clause does not alter this principle; it merely allows states to impose stricter standards where federal law sets a floor, not a ceiling. *See, e.g., California Div. of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Constr., N.A., Inc.*, 519 U.S. 316, 335 (1997) (state law may be more protective than federal law but cannot conflict with federal law).

The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow state wage and hour laws to displace the FLSA’s comprehensive scheme, contrary to the OCSLA’s gap-filling purpose. *See Shell Oil*, 488 U.S. at 27 (OCSLA “was intended to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the OCS). The FLSA’s savings clause does not create a gap; it merely allows states to impose stricter standards where federal law sets a floor.

III. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ARE INCONSISTENT WITH THE FLSA AND THEREFORE DO NOT APPLY ON THE OCS.

Even if the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement were satisfied, California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA. The FLSA sets a floor for wages and overtime, while California law imposes additional requirements (e.g., meal periods, rest breaks, and reporting time pay). *See, e.g., Brinker Rest. Corp. v. Superior Court*, 53 Cal. 4th 1004, 1027 (2012) (California law requires meal periods and rest breaks). These additional requirements conflict with the FLSA’s scheme, which does not require meal periods or rest breaks. *See, e.g., 29 C.F.R. § 785.19 (FLSA does not require meal periods or rest breaks).*

The Supreme Court has held that state law is preempted where it conflicts with federal law. *See, e.g., Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 516 (1992) (state law is preempted where it conflicts with federal law). California’s wage and hour laws conflict with the FLSA because they impose requirements that the FLSA does not. *See, e.g., Dillingham Constr.*, 519 U.S. at 335 (state law may not conflict with federal law).

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision requires a gap in federal coverage before state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law. The FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no gap for state wage and hour laws to fill. California’s wage and hour laws are inconsistent with the FLSA and therefore do not apply on the OCS. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule should be reversed.
