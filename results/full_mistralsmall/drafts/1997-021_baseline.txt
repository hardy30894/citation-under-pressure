**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling is Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act and Warrants Deference Only if it is Rational and Consistent with the Act.**

The Board’s standard for polling—requiring an employer to possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status”—is rational and consistent with the Act. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board has long recognized that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling” to labor relations, *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), and thus permissibly limits its use to circumstances where the employer already has substantial evidence casting doubt on the union’s majority. *Id.* at 1062-63. This approach balances the employer’s interest in testing employee support with the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *Id.*

The Board’s standard is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), as it represents the Board’s reasonable interpretation of the Act. *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 42; *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978). The D.C. Circuit erred in rejecting this standard in favor of a lower threshold, as the Board’s judgment reflects a permissible policy choice. *Id.*

**II. The Board’s Factual Determination that Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Board improperly discounted employee statements and misapplied its own precedent.

First, the Board erred in excluding statements from employees hired after January 25, 1991—the date Allentown Mack announced its poll. *Id.* at 1064. These employees were part of the bargaining unit at the time of the poll and their views were probative of majority support. *Cf. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (employer may rely on contemporaneous evidence).

Second, the Board improperly discounted statements from employees who expressed dissatisfaction with the union during job interviews. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978). Such statements are unreliable because they may reflect strategic responses to the employer’s non-union stance rather than genuine anti-union sentiment. *Id.*

Third, the Board disregarded hearsay statements from employees like Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr about the views of coworkers. *Westbrook Bowl*, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989). The Board has consistently questioned the reliability of such secondhand reports. *Id.*

Finally, the Board ignored the poll’s results—19 employees voting against the union and only 13 in favor—which provided objective evidence of the union’s loss of majority support. *Cf. NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 610 (1969) (employer may rely on poll results to rebut presumption of majority support).

**Conclusion**

The Board’s polling standard is rational and consistent with the Act, and its factual determination is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and deny enforcement of the Board’s order.
