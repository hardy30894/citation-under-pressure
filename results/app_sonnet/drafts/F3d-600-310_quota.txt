ARGUMENT

I. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT LACKED SUBJECT-MATTER JURISDICTION TO ADJUDICATE POST-PETITION INTEREST AND COLLECTION COSTS THAT ACCRUED AFTER THE ESTATE WAS CLOSED.

Federal courts, including bankruptcy courts, "possess only that power authorized by Constitution and statute." Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am., 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994). Jurisdiction does not survive the conclusion of the underlying proceeding merely because a dispute is loosely connected to it; enforcement of obligations arising after a case ends "require[s] its own basis for jurisdiction." Id. at 378. Kirkland's Chapter 13 case was closed in February 2006, her discharge entered, and the estate fully administered. The default and resulting interest and collection-cost dispute arose thereafter, in the ordinary creditor-debtor relationship, not as an incident of estate administration.

Bankruptcy jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b) extends to proceedings that "arise under," "arise in," or are "related to" a case under title 11. The touchstone for "related to" jurisdiction is "whether the outcome of that proceeding could conceivably have any effect on the estate being administered in bankruptcy." Pacor, Inc. v. Higgins, 743 F.2d 984, 994 (3d Cir. 1984); accord Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 308 n.6 (1995) (adopting the Pacor formulation). Once the estate is closed and the case administered, however, that broad pre-confirmation test no longer applies. This Court has recognized that post-confirmation matters require "a close nexus to the bankruptcy plan or proceeding" — one that affects "the interpretation, implementation, consummation, execution, or administration of the confirmed plan." In re Valley Historic Ltd. P'ship, 486 F.3d 831, 836 (4th Cir. 2007). Other circuits agree that "related to" jurisdiction contracts sharply after confirmation because "the case is no longer pending" in the same sense that governed the broader pre-confirmation test. Brandt v. Wand Partners, 242 F.3d 6, 10 (1st Cir. 2000); see also In re Craig's Stores of Tex., Inc., 266 F.3d 388, 391 (5th Cir. 2001) (post-confirmation jurisdiction requires the same "close nexus").

Here there is no such nexus. Kirkland's plan was fully performed and the case closed years before the default at issue. ECMC's post-petition interest and collection-cost claims arise from Kirkland's post-discharge default on a nondischargeable student loan — a matter governed by the underlying loan contract and the Higher Education Act, not by the confirmed plan or any residual estate administration. Adjudicating those amounts does not implement, interpret, or enforce the plan; it resolves a fresh collection dispute that happens to involve a former debtor. That is precisely the kind of attenuated connection Pacor and Valley Historic hold insufficient.

Nor can jurisdiction rest on the bankruptcy court's authority to interpret and enforce its own orders. In Travelers Indem. Co. v. Bailey, 557 U.S. 137 (2009), the Supreme Court confirmed that bankruptcy courts retain jurisdiction to construe and enforce their own injunctions and orders. But the bankruptcy court here was not enforcing any prior order fixing post-petition interest or collection costs; it was determining, for the first time, obligations that arose from conduct occurring entirely after the case closed. Travelers does not extend jurisdiction to new post-closing disputes simply because they are tethered to a debt once listed in a closed case.

II. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT EXCEEDED THE PROPER SCOPE OF THE PROCEEDING BEFORE IT.

Even assuming some residual jurisdiction existed, the court should not have exercised it here, because neither party asked it to determine post-petition interest or collection costs. Kirkland's Complaint sought only a determination that the principal had "been paid in full and discharged except to the extent of any interest that may have" accrued during the bankruptcy. J.A. 5. She expressly conceded that any interest accruing during the case "would be non-dischargeable." ECMC's response likewise addressed only whether the loan had been paid and discharged, and argued that no discharge could occur absent an undue-hardship finding under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8). Congress's insistence on an undue-hardship determination before any student-loan obligation is disturbed reflects a considered judgment that such debts are to be treated differently from ordinary claims, and that procedural protections attend any adjudication touching them. See United Student Aid Funds, Inc. v. Espinosa, 559 U.S. 260, 275 (2010) (emphasizing that § 523(a)(8) requires "a undue hardship" determination and associated procedural safeguards before a student loan is discharged). By reaching beyond the discharge question actually litigated to fix a specific dollar amount of post-petition interest — and by purporting to resolve collection costs neither party briefed — the bankruptcy court adjudicated matters outside the four corners of the adversary proceeding, without notice or an opportunity to be heard on the merits of those figures.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's judgment affirming the bankruptcy court's jurisdiction to determine post-petition interest and collection costs should be reversed.
