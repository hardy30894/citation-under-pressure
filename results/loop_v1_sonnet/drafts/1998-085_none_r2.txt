ARGUMENT

The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind, before punitive damages may be awarded under Title VII. The answer is yes. That answer follows ineluctably from more than a century of this Court's precedent establishing that punitive damages are a penal remedy reserved for conduct of an aggravated character—not a windfall available whenever a plaintiff proves intent or recklessness. Congress legislated against that backdrop when it enacted 42 U.S.C. § 1981a, and nothing in the statute's text suggests it meant to dispense with the safeguard that has always separated ordinary wrongdoing from punishable wrongdoing.

I. The common law has always required conduct of an aggravated character before punitive damages may be awarded; proof of a culpable state of mind alone has never sufficed.

This Court's earliest punitive-damages decisions focused on the character of the wrong itself, not merely on the mental state that accompanied it. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court explained that a jury awarding exemplary damages looks to "the enormity of [the defendant's] offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." Id. at 371. That formulation is significant: it is the enormity of the offense—its objective gravity—that justifies punishment, not simply the presence of some culpable mental state that might accompany conduct falling well short of that threshold.

The decisions that followed confirmed the point. In Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202 (1858), and Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), this Court treated exemplary damages as a penal remedy available only where the defendant's conduct carried the aggravated quality that has traditionally distinguished punishable wrongs from ordinary civil violations. See Humes, 115 U.S. at 521. In Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), the Court likewise described exemplary damages as compensation-plus-punishment, available only where circumstances of aggravation attended the wrong—not wherever a plaintiff proved intent or disregard of another's rights standing alone.

The distinction between mental state and the independent requirement of egregious conduct is clearest in Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893). There, a railroad conductor wrongfully ejected a passenger, and this Court held that the corporation could not be held liable for punitive damages absent proof that the corporation itself had authorized, participated in, or ratified conduct of a sufficiently aggravated character. See id. at 108–09. The conductor's own motive, whatever it may have been, was not enough; something more was required—an aggravated wrong properly attributable to the defendant against whom punishment was sought. Prentice thus establishes, in terms directly applicable here, that punitive liability cannot rest on state of mind divorced from the objective seriousness of the underlying conduct.

Huntington v. Attrill, 146 U.S. 657 (1892), reinforces this understanding by distinguishing remedial awards, which merely compensate, from penal awards, which punish and deter. See id. at 667–68. That penal label has traditionally attached only to conduct the community regards as warranting punishment—not to every instance of intentional or even reckless wrongdoing. Taken together, Day, Quigley, Humes, Barry, Prentice, and Huntington establish a single, consistent rule: exemplary damages require proof of aggravated, egregious conduct, evaluated independently of the defendant's state of mind.

II. Congress legislated against this settled backdrop, and its choice of the terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" must be read accordingly.

Congress did not write § 1981a on a clean slate. It selected words—"malice" and "reckless indifference"—that carry more than a century of accumulated common-law meaning. This Court has long instructed that "where Congress borrows terms of art in which are accumulated the legal tradition and meaning of centuries of practice, it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word in the body of learning from which it was taken." Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952).

The common-law tradition surrounding those very terms—reflected in Day, Quigley, Humes, Barry, Prentice, and Huntington—consistently demanded conduct of an aggravated, egregious character before punishment, rather than mere compensation, was permitted. This Court should presume Congress intended the same limitation here. To hold otherwise—permitting punitive damages upon nothing more than proof of intentional discrimination—would sever the words Congress chose from the accumulated legal tradition that gives them content, and would transform every ordinary intentional-discrimination verdict into a potential punitive-damages case. Nothing in the statute's text or history suggests Congress intended so significant a departure from settled practice.

III. Imputing punitive liability to an employer requires independent proof of aggravated corporate conduct, not merely an individual agent's state of mind.

Even if an individual supervisor's state of mind might satisfy § 1981a's malice or recklessness standard, imputing that state of mind to the employer for purposes of punishment demands more. This Court's decision in New York Central & Hudson River Railroad Co. v. United States, 212 U.S. 481 (1909), recognized that corporate responsibility for an agent's wrongful acts turns on whether those acts were committed within the scope of the agent's authority and in furtherance of the corporation's business. See id. at 493–95. Read together with Prentice, that principle confirms that an employer's exposure to the penal sanction of punitive damages cannot rest on an isolated employee's state of mind alone; it must be tied to conduct—and to the employer's own connection to that conduct—sufficiently aggravated to justify visiting punishment on the organization itself.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, an award of punitive damages under Title VII requires proof that the employer's conduct was egregious or outrageous, independent of the actor's state of mind. The judgment below should be affirmed.
