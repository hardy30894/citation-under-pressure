**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court has long held that a case becomes moot when the controversy between the parties has ceased to exist, and the plaintiff no longer has a personal stake in the outcome. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). This principle applies with equal force to claims under § 1983, where the plaintiff seeks declaratory and injunctive relief, as well as nominal damages, for a past violation of constitutional rights.

Here, the challenged policies were revised during litigation, eliminating the very basis of the plaintiffs’ claims. The prior “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct” were replaced with new policies that permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. The district court correctly concluded that this change rendered the plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot. *See Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (citing *Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. at 39). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer live or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953). That is precisely the situation here.

The plaintiffs argue that their claim for nominal damages preserves jurisdiction. But the Supreme Court has made clear that nominal damages alone cannot sustain a case that would otherwise be moot. *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632–33. In *W.T. Grant*, the Court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages did not prevent dismissal where the challenged conduct had ceased and the plaintiff had no further interest in the outcome. The Court explained: “The mere fact that a plaintiff has suffered a legal wrong does not mean that a court must provide a remedy if the wrong is no longer capable of being redressed.” *Id.* at 633. The same principle applies here. The plaintiffs’ constitutional rights were not violated in the present; the policy that allegedly violated them has been eliminated. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages, which, as the Court has held, “does not create a live controversy where none otherwise exists.” *Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. at 39.

The plaintiffs’ argument that their claim is “as-applied” and thus not moot is without merit. The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that an as-applied challenge survives when the challenged conduct has ceased and the plaintiff has no intention of repeating it. *United States v. St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co.*, 340 U.S. 170, 173 (1950). In *St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co.*, the Court dismissed a challenge to a railroad’s conduct after the conduct had ended and the plaintiff had no intention of engaging in similar activity. The Court stated: “The controversy is not one which is still pending, but one which has become academic.” *Id.* at 173. The same is true here. The plaintiffs have not attempted to speak on campus since the incident in 2016, and they have no intention of doing so under the new policy. Their claim is therefore not “live” and cannot be redressed by any judicial action.

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ request for nominal damages does not transform a moot case into a justiciable one. The Supreme Court has held that nominal damages are “not a substitute for a real controversy.” *United States v. Carver*, 322 U.S. 604, 607 (1944). In *Carver*, the Court dismissed a claim for nominal damages where the underlying conduct had ceased and the plaintiff had no further interest in the outcome. The Court emphasized that “the purpose of a suit is to obtain relief from a present wrong, not to vindicate a past injury.” *Id.* at 607. The plaintiffs here seek no relief from a present wrong. They seek only to vindicate a past violation of their rights, which is not sufficient to maintain jurisdiction.

Finally, the plaintiffs’ claim that they should be allowed to amend their complaint to seek compensatory damages is procedurally improper and legally unavailing. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend, as the request was made in a response to a motion to dismiss, not in a proper motion under Rule 15. *See Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (citing *Rosenberg v. Gould*, 554 F.3d 962, 967 (11th Cir. 2009)). Even if the request had been properly made, the plaintiffs failed to specify the substance of the proposed amendment or attach a proposed pleading. *See Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018). Without such specificity, the district court could not properly evaluate the amendment. Moreover, the plaintiffs had ample opportunity to amend before the motion to dismiss was granted, and they failed to do so.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be affirmed. The plaintiffs’ claims are moot, and nominal damages cannot save a case that has become academic. The Supreme Court’s precedent in *Munsingwear*, *W.T. Grant*, *St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co.*, and *Carver* is clear: when the challenged conduct has ceased and the plaintiff has no intention of repeating it, the case is no longer justiciable. The judgment below is correct and should be affirmed.
