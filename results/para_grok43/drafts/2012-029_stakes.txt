**ARGUMENT**

Federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) extends only to cases “in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). Minton’s malpractice claim is created by Texas law. The sole question is therefore whether the embedded patent issue satisfies the four-part test of *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005): the federal issue must be (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution in federal court “without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” *Id.* at 314.

The first two elements are satisfied. Causation in a malpractice action requires Minton to prove that timely assertion of the experimental-use exception would have defeated the on-sale bar. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). That question turns on the proper application of 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) and is genuinely contested.

The third and fourth elements are not. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that “a suit does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of [federal] law, upon the determination of which the result depends.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912); *see also Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936). The patent question here is backward-looking and case-specific: it asks only whether, on the facts known in 2002, the TEXCEN lease would have qualified as experimental use. Resolution of that question “will not change the real-world result for the patent holder or the alleged infringer,” nor will it “control numerous other cases.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313 (internal quotation omitted). It therefore lacks the “importance … to the federal system as a whole” required for substantiality. *See Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh*, 547 U.S. 677, 700 (2006).

Even if substantiality were present, federal jurisdiction would upset the congressionally struck balance. Legal malpractice has long been the domain of state courts. *See, e.g., Krouse v. Palmer*, 131 S.W.2d 258, 260 (Tex. Civ. App. 1939). Congress has never indicated an intent to federalize the entire field of attorney negligence merely because the underlying litigation involved a patent. *Cf. Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 811-12 (1986). Allowing federal jurisdiction here would open the federal courts to “a tremendous number of cases” that Congress reserved to the states. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 319.

Because the *Grable* test is not satisfied, exclusive federal jurisdiction does not lie. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
