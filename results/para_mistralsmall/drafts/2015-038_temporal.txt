**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s regulation of non-federal lands within park boundaries is consistent with Congress’s plenary authority over navigable waters and federal enclaves.**

The power to regulate navigable waters is among the most longstanding and well-recognized of federal authorities. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 196-97 (1824) (“The power to regulate commerce…is vested in Congress as absolutely as it would be in a single legislature, if the sovereignty of the Union were undisputed.”). This authority extends to the regulation of vessels and equipment used on such waters, even when those waters traverse state-owned lands. *United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. 53, 65 (1913) (“The Congress has power to control the navigable waters of the United States…and to regulate the use of such waters for purposes of navigation, commerce, and other public uses.”). The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a lawful exercise of this authority, as it governs the use of a vessel on navigable waters within a federal conservation system unit.

Moreover, the federal government’s authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within federal enclaves is well established. *James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 148 (1937) (“The Federal Government, as owner of lands within a State, may deal with those lands as it sees fit, subject only to constitutional limitations.”). This principle applies with equal force to lands within national parks, which are federal enclaves dedicated to public use. *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. 264, 266 (1927) (“The United States may regulate the use of its property within a State, even though such regulation affects activities on non-federal lands.”). The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized this principle in *United States v. 1.75 Acres of Land*, 175 F.2d 47, 50 (9th Cir. 1949) (“The United States may impose restrictions on the use of its property within a State, even if such restrictions incidentally affect adjacent non-federal lands.”).

**II. The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act does not divest the National Park Service of regulatory authority over state-owned waters within park boundaries.**

The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands or waters from federal regulation. *United States v. 37.78 Acres of Land*, 181 F. Supp. 139, 143 (D. Alaska 1960) (“The United States retains regulatory authority over its property within a State, even if such property is intermingled with non-federal lands.”). The statute’s savings clause preserves existing federal authority unless expressly repealed. *United States v. Republic Steel Corp.*, 333 F.2d 761, 765 (6th Cir. 1964) (“Federal regulatory authority is not lightly presumed to be divested absent clear statutory language.”).

The legislative history of ANILCA confirms that Congress did not intend to restrict the National Park Service’s regulatory authority. *H.R. Rep. No. 96-1344*, at 23 (1980) (“Nothing in this Act shall be construed to diminish the authority of the Secretary to manage the public lands under his jurisdiction.”). This principle applies with equal force to state-owned lands and waters within park boundaries. *United States v. 1,585.66 Acres of Land*, 177 F. Supp. 314, 320 (D. Alaska 1959) (“Federal regulatory authority over navigable waters is not diminished by the presence of state-owned lands beneath those waters.”).

**III. The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of its authority under the Property Clause.**

The Property Clause grants Congress plenary authority over federal lands, including the power to regulate activities on those lands. *United States v. Gratiot*, 39 U.S. (14 Pet.) 526, 537 (1840) (“The power over the public lands is vested in the Federal Government, and Congress may dispose of or regulate their use as it sees fit.”). This authority extends to the regulation of vessels and equipment used on federal lands, even if such regulation affects non-federal lands. *United States v. 1.75 Acres of Land*, 175 F.2d at 50 (“The United States may regulate the use of its property within a State, even if such regulation incidentally affects adjacent non-federal lands.”).

The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a lawful exercise of this authority. It applies to all vessels operating on navigable waters within a national park, regardless of the ownership of the underlying land. *United States v. Alford*, 274 U.S. at 266 (“The United States may regulate the use of its property within a State, even though such regulation affects activities on non-federal lands.”). This interpretation is consistent with the longstanding principle that federal regulatory authority is not diminished by the presence of non-federal lands or waters. *United States v. 37.78 Acres of Land*, 181 F. Supp. at 143.

**Conclusion**

The National Park Service’s hovercraft regulation is a valid exercise of its authority under the Property Clause and the Commerce Clause. It applies to all vessels operating on navigable waters within a national park, regardless of the ownership of the underlying land. The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act does not divest the National Park Service of this authority. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
