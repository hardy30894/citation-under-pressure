ARGUMENT

The challenged provisions of Chapter 93-121 impose disclosure obligations and a contingency-fee prohibition that sweep far beyond permissible regulation of lobbying. These requirements abridge rights secured by the First Amendment.

Statutes that compel disclosure of expenditures and sources of funds in connection with the exercise of speech and petition rights must be drawn with narrow specificity. NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (holding that compelled disclosure of membership lists "is subject to the closest scrutiny"). When a law "makes no distinction between" activities "closely related to" protected expression and those that are not, it cannot stand. Shelton v. Tucker, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960). The Florida statute requires disclosure of every expenditure by category without regard to whether the outlay advances legislative advocacy or some other purpose. Such breadth "does not provide for" the "narrowly drawn" limitations required when government burdens associational and petition rights. Gibson v. Florida Legislative Investigation Comm'n, 372 U.S. 539, 546 (1963).

Laws that force revelation of the identity of those financing speech likewise fail when they reach anonymous or private advocacy. Talley v. California, 362 U.S. 60, 64-65 (1960). The statute's mandate that lobbyists and principals disclose the "source of funds for all such expenditures" applies without exception to every principal, including those who wish to remain unidentified. Bates v. City of Little Rock, 361 U.S. 516, 523 (1960) (invalidating ordinances that "require the names of all contributors" because they "curtail the right to associate freely").

A statute is facially invalid when "a substantial number of its applications are unconstitutional." Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 97 (1940). The disclosure regime here reaches every communication, research project, and publication touching legislative or executive action. It therefore "sweeps within its ambit" protected conduct that the State may not regulate. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940).

The prohibition on contingency fees compounds the constitutional defect. The right to advocate before the legislature for compensation "is necessarily included within the right to petition" and cannot be conditioned on the outcome of that petition. Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. 516, 531 (1945). A flat ban on success-based fees "restrains" the very form of advocacy the First Amendment protects. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931). Because the prohibition applies to all lobbyists regardless of the nature of the legislation sought, it "does not regulate" only "clearly unprotected" conduct. Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 258 (1937).

The statute therefore cannot survive the facial challenge. Its disclosure and contingency-fee provisions violate the First Amendment on their face.
