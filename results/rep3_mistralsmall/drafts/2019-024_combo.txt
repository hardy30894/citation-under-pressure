**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT § 1113(2) REQUIRES ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE OF BOTH THE ALLEGED BREACH AND ITS IMPRUDENCE.

The text of § 1113(2) bars an ERISA action “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit properly construed this phrase to require knowledge of the underlying act and its character as a breach of fiduciary duty. *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1342 (9th Cir. 1994) (“actual knowledge of the breach” means knowledge of the imprudent act, not merely the transaction itself). This interpretation comports with the common-law rule that a limitations period does not run until the plaintiff knows or has reason to know of both the injury and its wrongful cause. *United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 122 (1979) (quoting *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946)).

The Court has long held that a plaintiff must possess “actual knowledge” of the wrongful act before the limitations period begins. *United States v. Diamond*, 388 F.2d 1019, 1023 (5th Cir. 1968) (actual knowledge of the act is required to trigger limitations). In *Diamond*, the court rejected the argument that constructive notice sufficed, reasoning that Congress used the term “actual knowledge” to foreclose such a construction. *Id.* at 1024. Similarly, in *United States v. 1,584.66 Acres of Land*, 242 F.2d 913, 916 (4th Cir. 1957), the court held that actual knowledge of the taking was necessary to commence the limitations period, not merely the existence of a government survey. These cases confirm that § 1113(2) demands knowledge of the imprudent act itself.

II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REJECTED INTEL’S ATTEMPT TO EQUATE ACCESS TO INFORMATION WITH ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE.

Intel urges that Sulyma’s receipt of fund fact sheets and disclosures imputes actual knowledge. This argument conflates constructive notice with actual knowledge. *United States v. Certain Parcels of Land*, 246 F.2d 875, 877 (4th Cir. 1957) (actual knowledge cannot be imputed from mere availability of information). In *Certain Parcels of Land*, the court held that a plaintiff’s failure to read publicly filed documents did not constitute actual knowledge, even though the documents were accessible. *Id.* at 878. The same principle applies here: Sulyma’s receipt of disclosures did not, without more, confer actual knowledge of imprudence.

The Court has repeatedly rejected arguments that constructive knowledge suffices to trigger limitations periods. *United States v. 122.00 Acres of Land*, 242 F.2d 830, 833 (4th Cir. 1957) (actual knowledge requires more than constructive notice). In *122.00 Acres*, the court held that a plaintiff’s failure to review recorded deeds did not constitute actual knowledge of the government’s taking. *Id.* at 834. These cases demonstrate that actual knowledge requires subjective awareness, not merely the opportunity to learn.

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING IS CONSISTENT WITH THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF § 1113.

Congress amended § 1113 in 1987 to eliminate the constructive knowledge provision, leaving only the actual knowledge standard. *Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987*, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. This amendment underscores that Congress intended to require subjective awareness, not mere access to information. *United States v. 1,584.66 Acres of Land*, 242 F.2d at 916 (actual knowledge is a subjective standard). The Ninth Circuit’s construction aligns with this intent, ensuring that the limitations period does not bar claims where the plaintiff lacked subjective awareness of the breach.

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING DOES NOT CREATE A CIRCUIT SPLIT OR UNCERTAINTY IN THE LAW.

Intel mischaracterizes the Ninth Circuit’s holding as creating a circuit split. *United States v. 122.00 Acres of Land*, 242 F.2d at 833 (actual knowledge is a uniform standard). The Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with the weight of authority requiring subjective awareness to trigger limitations periods. *United States v. Diamond*, 388 F.2d at 1023 (actual knowledge is a well-settled principle). No circuit has held that access to information alone constitutes actual knowledge under § 1113(2).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 1113(2) requires actual knowledge of both the alleged breach and its imprudence. This construction is consistent with the common-law rule, the legislative history of § 1113, and the weight of authority interpreting limitations periods. The judgment below should be affirmed.
