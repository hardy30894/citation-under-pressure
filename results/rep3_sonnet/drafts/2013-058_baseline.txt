# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF SECTION 522 EXEMPTS ONLY FUNDS THAT ARE CURRENTLY RETIREMENT FUNDS, NOT ASSETS THAT WERE ONCE SOMEONE ELSE'S RETIREMENT SAVINGS

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempt "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under" specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The critical statutory term is "retirement funds"—a phrase this Court must construe according to its ordinary meaning at the time of the debtor's bankruptcy filing. *See Sebelius v. Cloer*, 569 U.S. 369, 376 (2013) (statutory terms are interpreted according to their ordinary meaning). Funds are "retirement funds" only if they are actually being held for retirement purposes. An inherited IRA, by its very structure under the Internal Revenue Code, cannot satisfy this description.

When Heidi Heffron-Clark inherited her mother's IRA, the account's fundamental character changed. No new contributions could be made to it. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). More critically, the account was subject to mandatory distribution requirements that had nothing to do with Heidi's own retirement: distributions were required to begin within one year of her mother's death, with complete payout mandated within as little as five years. 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A), incorporating 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). As the Seventh Circuit correctly recognized, this makes an inherited IRA "a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." Pet. App. 3. The money must be withdrawn and spent—or saved outside the tax-deferred structure—regardless of whether the beneficiary has reached retirement age.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory exemptions must be construed according to their actual operative terms, not according to labels or historical pedigree. *See Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987) (cautioning against interpretations that assume "whatever furthers the statute's primary objective must be the law," because "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs"). The term "inherited individual retirement account" as used in the Internal Revenue Code designates the *source* of the funds—money that originated in a retirement account—not their *current function*. The word "IRA" in that phrase is a term of art describing tax treatment, not a guarantee that the underlying policy rationale for retirement-fund protection applies.

## II. THE FUNCTIONAL TEST APPLIED BELOW IS CONSISTENT WITH HOW THIS COURT AND THE BANKRUPTCY CODE TREAT OTHER TEMPORALLY-LIMITED EXEMPTIONS

Respondent's position finds support in the structural logic of Section 522 itself. Numerous exemptions under the Code are explicitly tied to the debtor's *current* use of property, not to the property's history. For example, the homestead exemption under Section 522(b)(3)(A) protects a "domicile" only if the debtor lived there for the requisite statutory period before filing. An heir who inherits a parent's home but rents it out cannot claim the homestead exemption merely because the house was once the parent's residence—exemption depends on the debtor's own relationship to the property, not the decedent's. The same functional logic governs retirement funds: what matters is whether the funds, in the debtor's hands, retain the economic characteristics of money set aside for retirement.

The inherited IRA fails this functional test. Unlike a traditional IRA, which cannot be freely accessed without penalty before age 59½ and must begin only gradual distribution after age 70½, an inherited IRA imposes no early-withdrawal penalty and mandates rapid, complete distribution regardless of the beneficiary's age. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). A 35-year-old beneficiary, like Heidi Heffron-Clark, can withdraw the entire balance the day after inheriting it, spend it on current consumption, and suffer no tax penalty for doing so beyond ordinary income tax. This is the antithesis of a retirement vehicle: it is, as the court below observed, "an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings." Pet. App. 8.

Contrast this with a hypothetical highlighted by the Seventh Circuit: had Ruth Heffron withdrawn the funds during her lifetime and gifted them to her daughter, no one would suggest that money retained protected status as "retirement funds" in Heidi's hands. Pet. App. 4–5. The mere fortuity of the funds passing through inheritance, rather than lifetime gift, cannot logically transform ordinary liquid assets into an exempt retirement fund. Either way, "the money used to be 'retirement funds' but isn't now." *Id.* at 5.

## III. THE BROADER PURPOSES OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE'S EXEMPTION SCHEME ARE SERVED BY LIMITING PROTECTION TO GENUINE RETIREMENT SAVINGS

Congress structured the retirement-fund exemption to ensure that individuals are not forced to exhaust their retirement security before receiving a bankruptcy discharge. That purpose is fully served by protecting an individual's own retirement accounts—accounts that person cannot freely raid without tax penalty and that Congress intended to remain intact until retirement. It is not served by shielding inherited assets that function as an immediately accessible windfall.

Petitioners' contrary argument—that permitting the exemption would encourage decedents to save more, anticipating that their heirs could inherit funds free of creditors' claims—proves too much. As the court below noted, this logic "would promote savings to hold that any asset acquired from one's relatives by will, insurance, annuity, or survivorship designation is exempt from creditors' claims." Pet. App. 7. That is plainly not the law. Bequests of houses, life insurance proceeds, and ordinary inherited assets are not exempt merely because their receipt might indirectly encourage saving by the decedent. The retirement-fund exemption is targeted and specific; it does not sweep in every asset that touches, at some point in its history, a tax-advantaged retirement account.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
