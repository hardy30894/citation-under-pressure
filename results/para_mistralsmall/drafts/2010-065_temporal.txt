**ARGUMENT**

**I. Petitioner Lacks Standing to Raise a Tenth Amendment Challenge to the Chemical Weapons Statute**

The Third Circuit correctly held that petitioner Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to assert a Tenth Amendment challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies sought to enjoin the federally chartered TVA from selling electricity in competition with them, arguing that the federal government’s entry into the local electricity market violated the Tenth Amendment. The Supreme Court rejected the claim, explaining that the utilities “have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment,” because the Tenth Amendment “is an expression of the relationship between the National Government and the States, not between the National Government and individuals.” *Id.* at 143–44. The Court emphasized that the utilities’ injury was not “a violation of the Tenth Amendment,” but rather a consequence of the federal government’s lawful exercise of its enumerated powers. *Id.* at 144. Just as the utilities could not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge the federal government’s entry into the electricity market, Bond cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge the federal government’s implementation of a valid treaty. *See also United States v. Butler*, 297 U.S. 1, 63 (1936) (holding that the Tenth Amendment “states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered” and does not confer any substantive rights on individuals).

The Court’s reasoning in *Tennessee Electric Power* controls here. Bond’s claim that § 229 violates the Tenth Amendment is not a cognizable injury; it is merely an objection to the federal government’s exercise of its treaty power. The Tenth Amendment “adds nothing to the [Constitution] as a limitation upon the authority of the federal government to exercise its delegated powers.” *Id.* at 63. Thus, Bond’s attempt to invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge the federal government’s implementation of the Chemical Weapons Convention is foreclosed by *Tennessee Electric Power*.

**II. Congress Validly Enacted § 229 Pursuant to Its Treaty Power**

Even if Bond had standing, her challenge to § 229 would fail because the statute is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), the Supreme Court upheld the Migratory Bird Treaty Act, which implemented a treaty between the United States and Great Britain for the protection of migratory birds. The Court rejected the argument that the statute exceeded Congress’s enumerated powers, holding that “[i]f the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” *Id.* at 432. The Court explained that the treaty power, combined with the Necessary and Proper Clause, permits Congress to legislate in areas that would otherwise fall within state police powers, so long as the treaty itself is valid. *Id.* at 433–34.

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing it. The Convention was negotiated under the authority of the President, with the advice and consent of two-thirds of the Senate, as required by Article II, Section 2 of the Constitution. *See* 22 U.S.C. § 6701 (finding that the Convention “is approved and ratified”). Section 229 implements the Convention’s prohibition on the development, production, acquisition, stockpiling, retention, transfer, and use of chemical weapons. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a). As in *Holland*, Congress’s exercise of its treaty power to enact § 229 is valid, regardless of whether the statute would otherwise fall within state police powers. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–34.

**III. The Necessary and Proper Clause Authorizes § 229**

Congress’s authority to enact § 229 is further supported by the Necessary and Proper Clause, which empowers Congress to “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States, or in any Department or Officer thereof.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), the Supreme Court upheld the creation of the Second Bank of the United States as a necessary and proper means of carrying into execution Congress’s enumerated powers, including its power to regulate commerce. The Court explained that the Necessary and Proper Clause permits Congress to use “means ... which are really calculated to produce the end” of its enumerated powers, even if those means are not explicitly enumerated. *Id.* at 421.

Here, § 229 is a necessary and proper means of carrying into execution Congress’s treaty power. The Convention requires the United States to prohibit the use of chemical weapons by its citizens, and § 229 criminalizes such conduct. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a). This is a classic example of a law that is “really calculated to produce the end” of implementing a valid treaty. *See McCulloch*, 17 U.S. at 421. Thus, § 229 is a valid exercise of Congress’s authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. Petitioner lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to § 229, and even if she had standing, the statute is a valid exercise of Congress’s treaty power and the Necessary and Proper Clause.
