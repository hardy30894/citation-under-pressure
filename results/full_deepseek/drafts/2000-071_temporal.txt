## ARGUMENT

Milford Central School’s exclusion of the Good News Club from its limited-use facilities did not violate the First Amendment. The school’s community-use policy lawfully reserved its property for specified secular purposes, and the Club’s activities—quintessentially religious worship and instruction—fell outside those purposes. Even if the Club’s speech were protected, the school’s action was justified by its compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.

### I. The School’s Limited Public Forum Excludes Religious Worship, and the Club’s Activities Constitute Worship.

The State has “power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated.” *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966). Milford’s policy opened its facilities only for “social, civic and recreational meetings” and other secular uses, expressly “not be used by any individual or organization for religious purposes.” This is a classic limited public forum. The school may permissibly restrict speech based on subject matter, so long as the restriction is reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *See, e.g., Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965) (state may regulate time, place, and manner of expression on public property). The exclusion of religious worship is a reasonable subject-matter limitation, not viewpoint discrimination.

The Good News Club’s meetings were not merely a religious viewpoint on a secular subject. They included prayer, Scripture memorization, Bible lessons, and an invitation to accept Jesus Christ as Savior. The lesson plan explicitly distinguished between “saved” and “unsaved” children and urged the latter to “believe on the Lord Jesus.” Such activities are “quintessentially religious” and fall outside the bounds of moral and character development from a secular perspective. The school’s policy was not aimed at suppressing any particular viewpoint; it excluded all religious worship, regardless of denomination. That is viewpoint neutrality. *Cf. Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303–04 (1940) (First Amendment protects religious beliefs but does not require government to open its property for religious worship).

### II. Even If the Club’s Speech Were Protected, the School’s Exclusion Is Justified by the Establishment Clause.

The school’s concern that permitting the Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause is well founded. The First Amendment commands that “Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion.” The Establishment Clause means at least that “neither a state nor the Federal Government . . . can pass laws which aid one religion, aid all religions, or prefer one religion over another.” *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 15–16 (1947). This Court has consistently held that public schools must be scrupulously neutral in matters of religion. *See McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 212 (1948) (invalidating religious instruction on school premises during school hours); *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 424–25 (1962) (striking down school prayer); *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963) (holding Bible reading unconstitutional). Although the Club’s meetings occurred after hours, the school’s provision of facilities could be perceived as endorsing the Club’s religious message, especially given the young audience and the school’s captive status. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952) (distinguishing McCollum because released-time program was off school premises). The school’s interest in avoiding even the appearance of official sponsorship of religion is compelling. *See Everson*, 330 U.S. at 16.

This Court has recognized that the government may sometimes restrict speech to avoid constitutional violations. *See, e.g., Sherbert v. Verner*, 374 U.S. 398, 403 (1963) (government may place burdens on free exercise only for compelling interests). Here, the school had a compelling interest in preventing an Establishment Clause violation. The Club’s activities—including explicit proselytizing and religious instruction—would have placed the school in the position of aiding religious propagation. The school was not required to await a lawsuit alleging an establishment before taking preventive action.

### III. The School’s Policy Is Reasonable and Viewpoint Neutral.

The school’s distinction between secular moral instruction and religious worship is reasonable and does not discriminate based on viewpoint. The school allowed organizations like the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts to teach morals and character from a secular perspective. The Good News Club, by contrast, conducted religious worship and instruction. This is not a case of viewpoint discrimination; it is a permissible subject-matter limitation. *Cf. Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 509 (1969) (school may not restrict speech based on viewpoint, but may regulate for other reasons). The school’s policy did not target the Club’s religious perspective; it targeted the subject matter of religious worship. The Club remained free to express its religious views on any secular topic the school had opened to discussion. But the school was not required to open its limited forum to religious worship itself.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
