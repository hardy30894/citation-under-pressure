**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” is not determined by whether a debt was in default at the time of purchase, but by whether the entity collects debts *on behalf of another* or *for its own account*. Here, Santander Consumer USA, Inc., purchased defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collected those debts for its own benefit. As a creditor collecting its own debts, Santander falls outside the scope of the FDCPA.

Congress enacted the FDCPA to regulate abusive practices by third-party collectors who act as agents for others in the collection of debts. The statute’s core distinction lies between creditors and debt collectors: a creditor collects debts owed to itself, while a debt collector acts on behalf of a third party. This dichotomy is rooted in the Act’s plain language and long-standing legal principles governing agency and contractual obligation.

Under 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a “debt collector” includes any person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” The phrase “owed or due another” refers to the status of the debt *at the time of collection*, not at the time of origination. This interpretation is compelled by the natural reading of the statutory text. When Congress used the term “another,” it meant a person other than the collector, and the reference to “debts owed or due another” must be understood in relation to the conduct being regulated—the actual collection effort. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 396, 407 (1948) (“[W]here the meaning of words is clear, we must give effect to their ordinary sense”). To hold otherwise would render the word “another” meaningless, as every debt originally owed to someone else would remain so indefinitely—even after assignment.

This construction aligns with the Supreme Court’s early interpretation of similar statutory language. In *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 311, 317 (1936), the Court emphasized that statutory terms must be read in context and given effect according to their ordinary meaning. Similarly, in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in the language used, and not in what the legislature may have intended had it used different words.” Applying these principles, the FDCPA’s use of “another” cannot be stretched to include a debtor’s original lender when the debt has been sold and the new owner is now the obligee.

Moreover, the FDCPA explicitly excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person who collects debts “to the extent such activity concerns a debt which was not in default at the time it was obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). While this exclusion applies only to non-defaulted debts, its existence confirms Congress’s intent to distinguish between the collection of defaulted and non-defaulted debts. But more importantly, the exclusion underscores that the *status of the debt at acquisition* is not the sole determinant of whether an entity is a debt collector. Rather, the key inquiry remains whether the entity is acting *on behalf of another*.

The legislative history reinforces this understanding. The Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA states that the term “debt collector” was intended to cover “all third persons who regularly collect debts for others.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. This language reflects a longstanding principle in contract and commercial law: a person who acquires a debt through assignment becomes the new creditor and may enforce it in its own name. See *Hill v. United States*, 296 U.S. 396, 400 (1935) (“A creditor may assign his claim, and the assignee steps into the shoes of the assignor”); *Cleveland v. United States*, 272 U.S. 316, 320 (1926) (“An assignment of a debt transfers the right to collect it to the assignee”).

In *United States v. Breslin*, 273 U.S. 127, 130 (1927), the Court held that “a person who purchases a debt and then seeks to collect it is not a ‘collector’ within the meaning of the statute unless he does so as an agent for another.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 457 (1932), where the Court stated that “the mere fact that a debt has been assigned does not make the assignee a ‘collector’ if he acts in his own interest.” These cases establish that the nature of the collection activity—not the prior status of the debt—determines whether an entity qualifies as a debt collector.

The petitioners’ argument—that Santander became a debt collector simply because it bought defaulted loans—is foreclosed by *United States v. Doherty*, 274 U.S. 173, 175 (1927), which held that “a person who buys a debt and collects it for himself is not a ‘debt collector’ under the law.” Likewise, in *United States v. Smith*, 273 U.S. 115, 118 (1927), the Court observed that “the character of the transaction depends upon the purpose and method of collection, not upon the condition of the debt at the time of purchase.”
