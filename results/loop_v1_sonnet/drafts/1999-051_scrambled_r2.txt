**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedural Default That Is Not the Product of the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Bar Reliance on the Underlying Constitutional Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

This Court has long recognized that the writ of habeas corpus is not to be shackled by mechanical procedural rules where a petitioner's own conduct did not produce the default. In *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266 (1948), the Court emphasized that habeas corpus proceedings must remain flexible enough to secure the substance of a fair hearing on a prisoner's constitutional claims, rather than allowing procedural technicalities to override the merits. *Id.* at 269. The touchstone in this Court's decisions has consistently been the petitioner's own intelligent choice to forgo a state remedy—not the tactical or negligent omissions of counsel. Carpenter did not himself choose to abandon his sufficiency claim; his appellate lawyer did. Under the reasoning of *Price*, that distinction is decisive.

This principle rests on the settled understanding that constitutional rights are not lightly deemed abandoned. In *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), this Court made clear that waiver of a fundamental right cannot be presumed from a silent record, and that something more than mere inaction is required before a defendant will be held to have relinquished a constitutional protection. *Id.* at 516. A defendant cannot be said to have relinquished a constitutional claim merely because appointed counsel failed to raise it. Attributing counsel's default to the petitioner as though it were his own considered waiver inverts the very premise of *Carnley*—that fundamental rights are surrendered only by the defendant's own knowing act, not by counsel's oversight.

**The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a mere procedural nicety; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. In *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), the Court explained that "the right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel." *Id.* at 68–69. That same principle animated *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), in which the Court extended the guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that a State may not condition the vindication of federal rights on the fortuity of retained counsel while indigent appellants go unrepresented. *Id.* at 357–58. Carpenter's claim—that his appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the kind of constitutional deprivation *Powell* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A default of that claim, itself born of the same disabling failure of counsel, should not extinguish its capacity to explain and excuse the companion default it caused.

**Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require an Independently Exhausted Cause Claim Where the Default Flows from Counsel's Own Failure**

The exhaustion requirement exists, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), to ensure that state courts have "the first opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of . . . constitutional rights." *Id.* at 118. It is a rule of comity, not a rule of forfeiture. Ohio had every opportunity to correct counsel's failure when Carpenter sought reopening of his appeal; the state court declined only on a timeliness ground unrelated to the merits. Nothing in the comity rationale of *Hawk* requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself first survive a second, independent gauntlet of exhaustion before it may serve its traditional function of explaining a related default.

**This Court's Broader Habeas Corpus Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Rules**

The great writ has always been treated with a flexibility that guards against the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101 (1942), the Court held that habeas relief remains available where a fundamental constitutional defect has infected the proceedings, explaining that the writ reaches "the very foundation of the proceeding" when a prisoner has been deprived of a basic right. *Id.* at 104–05. And in *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), the Court cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake," *id.* at 8, and that abuse-of-writ principles must be applied with equitable discretion rather than rigid formalism, *id.* at 17–18. Likewise, *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), confirmed that federal courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims presented in habeas, rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive. *Id.* at 458.

Taken together, *Price*, *Carnley*, *Powell*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Waley*, *Sanders*, and *Brown* establish a consistent principle: procedural defaults attributable to counsel's own failings, rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice, should not be permitted to compound themselves into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient constitutional substance to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
