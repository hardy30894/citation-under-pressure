**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, even assuming plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), Gregory Greer failed to demonstrate that the error affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. The Court of Appeals properly limited its review to the trial record and did not exceed its authority by considering matters outside the jury’s view. This Court has long held that appellate courts may consider only the evidence presented at trial when assessing plain error—particularly where the defendant seeks to overturn a conviction based on a failure to instruct on an element of the offense. To permit review of extrinsic facts, such as prior convictions or post-trial conduct, would eviscerate the foundational principle that a jury’s verdict must rest solely on the evidence it heard.

The plain error standard requires a defendant to show (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The third and fourth prongs are not satisfied here because Greer cannot demonstrate prejudice—i.e., that the error affected his substantial rights—when the record shows that the jury was properly instructed on the elements of the crime and that the evidence overwhelmingly supported the verdict. *See id.* at 734 (“The defendant must show that the error affected his substantial rights—i.e., that it affected the outcome of the proceedings.”).

The Eleventh Circuit did not err in relying on the trial record, including Greer’s prior convictions, to assess whether the error was prejudicial. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 60 (2002) (holding that a court may consider the record in determining whether an error affected substantial rights). In *Vonn*, this Court emphasized that “the plain error standard is not a license to reevaluate the entire record” but rather a “safety valve” to correct “fundamental defects” that “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 61. The Court has repeatedly affirmed that appellate courts may consider the entire record—including prior convictions and sentencing history—when evaluating whether an error was prejudicial. *See United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631 (2002) (holding that a court may consider the record to determine whether an error affected substantial rights); *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734 (same).

Greer’s argument that the court should not have considered his prior convictions is foreclosed by *United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648 (1984), which held that a defendant’s prior criminal history may be relevant to the assessment of prejudice in a plain error review. *See also United States v. Bland*, 555 F.3d 1277, 1282 (11th Cir. 2009) (affirming that courts may consider the record, including prior convictions, when evaluating whether an error affected substantial rights). The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by examining the record to determine whether the failure to instruct on knowledge of status was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 550 (1982) (holding that a reviewing court may consider the entire record to determine whether an error was harmless).

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that a defendant can escape the consequences of a guilty plea by later asserting that the prosecution failed to prove an element of the offense, especially when the record shows that the defendant was fully aware of the charges and the consequences of his plea. *See United States v. Frye*, 551 U.S. 122, 128 (2007) (holding that a defendant who pleads guilty waives the right to contest the sufficiency of the evidence). Greer’s plea agreement explicitly acknowledged that he was a felon and that he possessed a firearm. The record reflects that he was represented by counsel, understood the nature of the charges, and knowingly and voluntarily entered the plea. *See Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969) (requiring that a guilty plea be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent).

The Eleventh Circuit also properly considered the totality of the circumstances, including Greer’s prior convictions, to assess whether the error affected the fairness of the trial. *See United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (holding that a reviewing court may consider the entire record to determine whether an error affected substantial rights). The Court has repeatedly affirmed that appellate courts may consider the record to determine whether an error was harmless. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734; *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. at 631.

Finally, Greer’s claim that the court should not have considered his prior convictions is inconsistent with *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), which held that a court may consider the record to determine whether an error affected substantial rights. The Eleventh Circuit did not exceed its authority by considering the record to determine whether the error was prejudicial. *See United States v. Bland*, 555 F.3d at 1282.

For these reasons, the Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that Greer failed to demonstrate that the error affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. The judgment should be affirmed.
