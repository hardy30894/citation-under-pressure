**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Procedural Default Attributable to Counsel's Failure, Rather Than the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice, May Serve as Cause to Excuse a Related Default**

The question here reduces to a familiar principle of this Court's habeas jurisprudence: a petitioner forfeits federal review of a constitutional claim only when *he himself* makes the deliberate choice to bypass state procedures—not when his lawyer, without his knowledge or consent, fails to raise it. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that a habeas court may deny relief to a petitioner "who has deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts and in so doing has forfeited his state court remedies." *Id.* at 438. That standard presupposes a personal, considered decision. Carpenter made no such decision. His appellate counsel simply failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—an omission Carpenter neither directed nor discovered until new counsel later uncovered it. Under *Fay*, counsel's unilateral failure cannot be attributed to Carpenter as though it were his own deliberate bypass.

This distinction tracks the settled rule of *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. Treating counsel's oversight as Carpenter's own waiver would erase the line *Zerbst* draws between a client's knowing relinquishment of a right and counsel's unilateral neglect of it. If a claim of constitutional dimension cannot be waived by counsel alone, a default rooted in that same neglect should not categorically foreclose the petitioner from invoking it as cause for a related default.

**II. Because the Underlying Claim Concerns the Fundamental Right to Effective Appellate Counsel, It Retains Force Even Though It Too Was Defaulted**

The right at stake—effective assistance of appellate counsel—is no mere technicality but a fundamental guarantee this Court has vigorously protected. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court explained that "any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him." *Id.* at 344. That same Term, *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), extended this guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that "where the merits of the one and only appeal an indigent has as of right are decided without benefit of counsel, we think an unconstitutional line has been drawn between rich and poor." *Id.* at 357. Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim asserts precisely the deprivation these decisions were designed to remedy. A rule stripping that claim of any excusing force simply because it, too, went unexhausted would punish Carpenter twice for a single constitutional failure—once through loss of his sufficiency claim, and again through loss of the very claim explaining why it was lost.

**III. The Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require Independent Exhaustion of a Cause Claim Where the State Had a Fair Opportunity to Correct the Error**

Exhaustion exists to give the State a fair opportunity to correct its own errors before federal courts intervene—not to erect a second procedural trap layered atop the first. In *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), the Court explained that federal habeas relief ordinarily awaits exhaustion of state remedies so that state courts have "the first opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of . . . constitutional rights." *Id.* at 118. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise grounded exhaustion in "comity between the state and federal sovereignties," not in a desire to bar meritorious claims outright. *Id.* at 204. Ohio had exactly the opportunity these decisions contemplate: Carpenter moved to reopen his direct appeal specifically to raise his ineffective-assistance claim. That the state court rejected the motion as untimely does not diminish the fact that the State was given, and declined, the chance *Hawk* and *Darr* require before federal review may proceed.

**IV. This Court's Habeas Jurisprudence Counsels Against Mechanical Forfeiture Rules That Sacrifice Liberty to Procedural Formalism**

The writ has always demanded flexibility where fundamental rights are jeopardized by default rather than by guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), this Court recognized an independent federal duty to examine whether a state proceeding satisfied the Constitution, without regard to the procedural posture in which the state courts addressed—or failed to address—the underlying claim. *Id.* at 91–92. In *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), the Court cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake." *Id.* at 8. And in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), the Court confirmed that federal habeas courts bear an independent responsibility to evaluate the substance of a petitioner's constitutional claims, rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive of the federal question. *Id.* at 458–63.

Read together, *Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Brown v. Allen* confirm that this Court has never treated procedural default as a rigid, self-compounding bar—least of all where, as here, the default stems from counsel's own constitutional failing rather than the petitioner's deliberate choice. A rule requiring that a cause claim first survive its own independent gauntlet of exhaustion, on pain of losing all excusing force, would visit upon habeas petitioners exactly the kind of "conventional notion[] of finality" that *Sanders* rejected, and would deny them the "first opportunity" for state correction that *Hawk* and *Darr* were designed to secure. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient force to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
