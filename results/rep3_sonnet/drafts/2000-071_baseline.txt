# ARGUMENT

## I. MILFORD'S EXCLUSION OF THE CLUB WAS VIEWPOINT-NEUTRAL SUBJECT MATTER REGULATION, NOT UNCONSTITUTIONAL VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION

This Court has long recognized that when the government creates a limited public forum, it "need not permit all forms of speech" and "may be justified in reserving [its] forum for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics." *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). The critical constitutional line is between content-based subject-matter exclusions, which are permissible if reasonable and viewpoint-neutral, and viewpoint discrimination, which is never permitted. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) ("[T]he government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses on an otherwise includible subject.").

Milford's Community Use Policy draws precisely this permissible line. It excludes "religious purposes" as a subject matter while permitting the "welfare of the community" topics of "social, civic and recreational" activity, including moral and character development from secular perspectives. The Second Circuit correctly recognized that the Club's proposed use—weekly instruction combining "religious instruction and prayer," 202 F.3d at 510—falls outside the "bounds of pure 'moral and character development'" that Milford permits other groups, such as the Boy Scouts and 4-H Club, to address. This is subject-matter regulation, not viewpoint suppression, because Milford does not exclude the Club because it advocates a religious *perspective* on an otherwise permitted secular topic; it excludes the Club because its activities—proselytizing, prayer, and religious conversion—constitute the subject of religious worship itself.

This distinction finds firm support in *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993). There, the Court invalidated a school's refusal to allow a religious group to show a film addressing family values "from a religious standpoint," because the school district had opened its forum to discussion of family values and child-rearing generally, and merely excluded the religious viewpoint on that permitted topic. *Id.* at 393. Milford's policy is different in kind. Milford does not permit "moral development" generally and then exclude only the religious viewpoint on that topic; the record demonstrates that the Good News Club's meetings are substantially devoted to religious conversion, prayer, and proselytization—the "challenge and invitation" segment inviting children to "ask [Jesus] to come into your heart" is not a viewpoint on morality, but an invitation to religious commitment. 202 F.3d at 507. As the district court found, and the Second Circuit affirmed, the Club sought to use school premises for "religious instruction and prayer" rather than "the teaching of values or morals from a religious perspective." *Id.* at 510.

This Court's decision in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), does not compel a different result. *Widmar* addressed a university's exclusion of *all* religious groups from a forum broadly open to all manner of student organizations—an blanket exclusion of religious speech as such. Milford's policy is narrower and more precise: it excludes religious worship and instruction while permitting extensive use by community organizations addressing overlapping secular topics. The forum in *Widmar* was a "forum generally open for the use of student groups," 454 U.S. at 267–68; Milford's forum is expressly limited to enumerated categories that do not include religious instruction.

## II. EVEN IF THIS COURT FINDS VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION, MILFORD'S EXCLUSION WAS JUSTIFIED BY ITS COMPELLING INTEREST IN AVOIDING AN ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE VIOLATION

Even assuming, arguendo, that Milford's policy effected viewpoint discrimination, such discrimination may be justified where necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. See *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271 (recognizing that "the state interest in achieving greater separation of church and State than is already ensured under the Establishment Clause" may, in some circumstances, justify content-based restrictions, though finding it insufficient there on the facts presented).

Milford's facilities are used immediately after the schoolday ends, on school property, by young children—kindergartners through sixth graders—many of whom are still on campus and who may reasonably associate the Club's activities with the school itself. This Court has recognized that the "endorsement" inquiry under the Establishment Clause is particularly sensitive to context involving impressionable schoolchildren. *See Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (recognizing "heightened concerns" regarding "subtle coercive pressure" on students "to participate" in religious exercises). Allowing the Club—whose sponsor is a minister who leads children in prayers for personal salvation and invites them to "ask Jesus to save you" during the schoolday's immediate aftermath—to operate under the imprimatur of the school's own facilities and community-use umbrella raises genuine Establishment Clause concerns that Milford was entitled to consider and avoid.

Milford's judgment merits respect. School officials possess special competence to assess the practical effects of school-based programming on young students within their charge. *See Board of Educ. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 251 (1990) (plurality opinion) (recognizing schools' legitimate concern that students not perceive government endorsement of particular religious views). Where, as here, the activity in question includes overt religious conversion efforts directed at children as young as six, immediately following the school day and on school premises, Milford's concern that permitting such activity would constitute or appear to constitute an establishment of religion was reasonable and sufficient to justify the exclusion.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
