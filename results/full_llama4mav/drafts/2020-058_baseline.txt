ARGUMENT

I. The Conferral of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an "Admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255

The Third Circuit's decision in this case conflicts with the plain meaning of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) and ignores the legislative intent behind the Temporary Protected Status (TPS) program. The INA defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the statute also provides that an alien granted TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4).

The grant of TPS is tantamount to an admission because it involves an inspection and authorization by an immigration officer, albeit after the alien's initial entry. The Ninth Circuit correctly held in Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), that a grant of TPS satisfies the admission requirement under § 1255(a). The court reasoned that the language of § 1254a(f)(4) "equates 'being in ... lawful status as a nonimmigrant' with § 1258(a)'s 'lawfully admitted ... as a nonimmigrant.'" Id. at 961-62.

Furthermore, the purpose of the TPS program is to provide a safe haven for individuals fleeing war, natural disasters, or other extraordinary conditions in their home countries. Denying TPS recipients the opportunity to adjust their status to lawful permanent residents would undermine the humanitarian purpose of the program. As the Ninth Circuit noted, "it would be anomalous to conclude that Congress intended to provide TPS recipients with a temporary safe haven, but not to allow them to adjust their status to lawful permanent residents." Id. at 962.

The Third Circuit's decision, on the other hand, relies on a narrow reading of the statute that ignores the legislative intent behind the TPS program. The court held that a grant of TPS does not constitute an admission because it is not a "lawful entry" into the United States. However, this interpretation fails to account for the fact that TPS recipients are considered to be in lawful status as nonimmigrants, which is a critical factor in determining their eligibility for adjustment of status.

II. The Third Circuit's Decision is Inconsistent with the Statutory Text and Legislative History

The Third Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the statutory text and legislative history of the INA. The court relied on the definition of "admission" in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A), but failed to consider the broader context of the statute. The INA provides that an alien who is considered to be in lawful status as a nonimmigrant is eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4).

Moreover, the legislative history of the TPS program suggests that Congress intended to provide a pathway to lawful permanent residence for TPS recipients. The Senate Report accompanying the bill that created the TPS program stated that the program was designed to "provide temporary relief to nationals of countries experiencing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions." S. Rep. No. 101-249, at 3 (1990). The report also noted that TPS recipients would be eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255. Id. at 11.

In conclusion, the conferral of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary is inconsistent with the plain meaning of the INA, the legislative intent behind the TPS program, and the statutory text and legislative history. This Court should reverse the Third Circuit's decision and hold that TPS recipients are eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255.
