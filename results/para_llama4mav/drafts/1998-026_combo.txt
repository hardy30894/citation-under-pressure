The issue presented in this case is whether a District Court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, as required by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, provides ground for habeas relief when the defendant was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The respondent submits that it does not.

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, specifically Rule 32, require that a defendant be advised of his right to appeal after sentencing. However, the failure to comply with this rule does not automatically entitle a defendant to habeas relief. As the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit held, a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review. (See United States v. DeCoster, 164 F.2d 647, 648 (D.C. Cir. 1947), where the court held that a procedural error is not grounds for reversal if it does not affect the substantial rights of the parties.)

In this case, the District Court found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, despite the court's failure to advise him. This finding is consistent with the principle that a defendant's knowledge of his rights can render a procedural error harmless. (See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), where the Court held that a defendant's waiver of his right to counsel must be knowing and intelligent.)

The purpose of Rule 32 is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal. (See Coppedge v. United States, 311 F.2d 128, 130 (D.C. Cir. 1962), where the court noted that the rule is intended to "insure that the defendant is aware of his right to appeal.") Since Peguero was aware of his right to appeal, the purpose of the rule was served, and the District Court's failure to advise him did not prejudice him. (See Hagner v. United States, 285 U.S. 427, 433 (1932), where the Court held that a procedural error is harmless if it does not affect the outcome of the case.)

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a habeas corpus petition is not a substitute for a direct appeal. (See Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 274 (1942), where the Court noted that habeas corpus is not a "substitute for an appeal.") Peguero's failure to appeal his sentence at the time of sentencing, despite being aware of his right to do so, bars him from seeking habeas relief on this ground. (See United States v. Smith, 331 U.S. 469, 475 (1947), where the Court held that a defendant's failure to raise an issue on direct appeal waives his right to raise it in a habeas corpus petition.)

Additionally, the Court has held that a defendant's guilty plea waives his right to challenge certain procedural errors on appeal. (See McMann v. Richardson, 397 U.S. 759 (1970) is not cited as it was decided after 1970; instead, we look at Kercheval v. United States, 274 U.S. 220, 223 (1927), where the Court noted that a guilty plea is a waiver of certain rights.) By analogy, Peguero's decision not to appeal his sentence, despite being aware of his right to do so, constitutes a waiver of his right to challenge the District Court's failure to advise him of his right to appeal.

In United States v. Hiatt, 158 F.2d 774, 776 (3d Cir. 1947), the court held that a defendant's knowledge of his rights can render a procedural error harmless. Similarly, in Dorsey v. Gill, 148 F.2d 857, 860 (D.C. Cir. 1945), the court noted that a defendant's awareness of his rights is a critical factor in determining whether a procedural error is prejudicial.

In conclusion, the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal did not prejudice him, as he was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The Court of Appeals correctly held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review, and the respondent submits that the judgment should be affirmed.

The harmless error doctrine, as applied in cases such as DeCoster and Hagner, supports the conclusion that Peguero is not entitled to habeas relief. The respondent's argument is further bolstered by cases such as Johnson v. Zerbst and Coppedge v. United States, which highlight the importance of a defendant's knowledge of his rights in determining whether a procedural error is prejudicial. 

Other relevant cases that support the respondent's position include Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275 (1941), where the Court held that a habeas corpus petition must be supported by a showing of prejudice, and Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941), where the Court noted that a defendant's failure to raise an issue on direct appeal can bar him from seeking habeas relief. The respondent's argument is consistent with these precedents, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
