# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens, Like Petitioner, Who Reentered the United States Before Its 1997 Effective Date

This Court's two-step retroactivity framework governs. First, a court asks "whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute's proper reach." *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994). Section 241(a)(5) contains no such express command. It speaks of an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally," but nowhere states that Congress intended the provision to reach reentries that occurred, and were completed, years before the statute existed. Where Congress wishes a statute to reach backward, it says so plainly; here it did not.

Because the statutory text is at best ambiguous, this Court's "presumption against retroactive legislation" controls. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 265. That presumption is not a mere interpretive tiebreaker but is, as this Court has explained, "deeply rooted in our jurisprudence, and embodies a legal doctrine centuries older than our Republic." *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001) (internal quotation marks omitted). In *St. Cyr*, the Court applied this presumption to hold that IIRIRA's repeal of discretionary relief under former § 212(c) did not apply to aliens who had pleaded guilty before the repeal, because Congress had not clearly manifested an intent to reach such pre-enactment conduct. *Id.* at 320. The same interpretive restraint governs here: absent a clear statement, § 241(a)(5) should be read to apply only to illegal reentries occurring after its 1997 effective date, not to reentries — like Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's 1982 return — that were completed fifteen years earlier and had already given rise to a settled course of residence, marriage, and family life in the United States.

## II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Bar Petitioner From Any Relief Based on His Pre-Enactment Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statutory text could be read to reach pre-1997 reentries, applying it to Mr. Fernandez-Vargas would violate the second step of *Landgraf*: whether the statute, "if applied to conduct that occurred prior to its enactment, would have an impermissible retroactive effect." *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 358 (1999). A statute has forbidden retroactive effect when it "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party's liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Section 241(a)(5) does all three.

Before 1997, an alien who illegally reentered after deportation, but who later married a United States citizen, remained eligible to seek adjustment of status. Mr. Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982 under that legal regime, built a life in the United States for nearly two decades, and in 2001 married a United States citizen — conduct undertaken in reliance on the law as it then stood. Section 241(a)(5), applied to him, attaches a wholly new and disabling legal consequence — automatic reinstatement of the 1981 order and categorical ineligibility for any relief — to conduct "already past." *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321 (quoting *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269). That is the paradigmatic case of forbidden retroactivity: the statute does not merely regulate Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's presence going forward, but reaches back to strip him, based on his 1982 reentry, of an opportunity for relief that existed when he reentered and that he reasonably expected to be able to invoke once he married.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that "individuals should have an opportunity to know what the law is and to conform their conduct accordingly," and that "settled expectations should not be lightly disrupted." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 265; *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 323. In *St. Cyr*, the Court found it decisive that aliens "almost certainly relied upon th[e] likelihood" of relief in deciding how to conduct themselves. 533 U.S. at 325. Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's reliance is at least as strong: he did not merely forgo a trial right, as in *St. Cyr*, but structured nearly two decades of his life — including marriage to a citizen — around the possibility that his presence could eventually be regularized. The Tenth Circuit's conclusion that he had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status" mistook the relevant inquiry. The retroactivity question does not ask whether Mr. Fernandez-Vargas had a vested right to adjustment, but whether the new statute "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. It plainly does.

Nor can the government rely on the fiction that Mr. Fernandez-Vargas's continued "presence" is itself the triggering conduct, allowing the statute to operate only prospectively. Reinstatement under § 241(a)(5) is expressly predicated on the fact that the alien "has reentered the United States illegally" — a discrete, completed act. To treat mere ongoing presence as the operative conduct would allow Congress, or the agency, to convert any past act into perpetually renewed "present" conduct, evading the retroactivity presumption altogether. This Court should reject that maneuver and hold that § 241(a)(5), as applied to Mr. Fernandez-Vargas, is impermissibly retroactive under *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr*.
