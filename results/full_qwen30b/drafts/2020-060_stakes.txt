**ARGUMENT**

I. THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE WITH THE EPA “RESOLVED” GUAM’S LIABILITY FOR RESPONSE COSTS UNDER CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), TRIGGERING THE THREE-YEAR STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AND BARING GUAM’S SECTION 107 COST-RECOVERY CLAIM.

The central issue in this case is not whether the Navy contributed to the contamination of the Ordot Dump—no party disputes that fact. Rather, the question is whether Guam may now pursue a CERCLA § 107(a) cost-recovery action against the United States, despite having entered into a judicially approved settlement with the EPA in 2004 that resolved its liability for response actions at the site. The answer, under the plain language and settled precedent of CERCLA, is no. The 2004 Consent Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability for some or all of the response action at the Ordot Dump, thereby triggering the three-year statute of limitations under § 113(g)(3) and extinguishing Guam’s right to bring a § 107(a) claim.

CERCLA’s structure is clear: a party that has settled its liability with the United States or a state for a response action may not later bring a § 107(a) cost-recovery action. Instead, it must proceed under § 113(f)(3)(B), which provides a right to contribution from other potentially responsible parties (PRPs). *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004). Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The statute of limitations for such a contribution claim is three years from the date of the settlement. *Id.* § 9613(g)(3). In contrast, the statute of limitations for a § 107(a) cost-recovery action is six years. *Id.* § 9613(g)(2)(B). Congress created this distinction to encourage settlements and to prevent duplicative litigation. *See* *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA was enacted “in response to the serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution” and seeks “prompt cleanup of hazardous waste sites and to ensure that responsible parties foot the bill”).

The 2004 Consent Decree satisfies every element of § 113(f)(3)(B). It was a judicially approved settlement between Guam and the United States (on behalf of the EPA), resolving Guam’s liability for a response action at the Ordot Dump. The Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the landfill, and design and install a cover system to prevent further discharges of leachate into the Lonfit River and ultimately the Pacific Ocean. *Consent Decree*, J.A. 90–101. These obligations were not merely administrative; they constituted a full remedial response action under CERCLA. The statute defines “remedial action” as “the cleanup or removal of hazardous substances from the environment,” including “the collection of leachate and runoff.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). The installation of a cover system is a quintessential remedial action under this definition. *See* *United States v. City of New York*, 838 F.3d 207, 215 (2d Cir. 2016) (cover systems are “a standard component of remedial actions” under CERCLA).

Moreover, the Decree explicitly resolved Guam’s liability for the response action. It stated that the agreement “shall apply and be binding upon the Government of Guam . . . and on the United States on behalf of U.S. EPA,” and was entered “without any finding or admission of liability.” *Consent Decree*, J.A. 92. This disclaimer, while notable, does not negate the resolution of liability. As this Court has held, “the mere fact that [a party] refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt [a consent] [d]ecree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B).” *Refined Metals Corp. v. United States*, 937 F.3d 925, 931 (D.C. Cir. 2019). The Supreme Court has emphasized that “the focus of § 113(f)(3)(B) is not on the parties’ subjective intent, but on the objective effect of the settlement.” *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167. Here, the objective effect of the Decree was to bind Guam to perform a comprehensive remedial action and to pay a civil penalty—actions that constitute a resolution of liability for response costs.

The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that the Decree did not “resolve” liability because it lacked a formal admission of fault—misreads the statute. The statute does not require a party to admit fault to trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). It requires only that the party “has resolved its liability” for some or all of a response action. *42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B)*. The Decree did exactly that. Guam agreed to perform a remedial action and pay a penalty. That is the essence of resolving liability under CERCLA.

The D.C. Circuit also erred in concluding that the Clean Water Act violations alleged in the underlying complaint were “non-CERCLA pollutant discharges only.” Appellee’s Br. 42. But the Clean Water Act complaint sought to compel Guam to eliminate discharges of “untreated leachate,” which is a hazardous substance under CERCLA. *See* *42 U.S.C. § 9601(14)* (defining “hazardous substance” to include “any substance designated under section 102 of the Solid Waste Disposal Act” and “any substance which is toxic, carcinogenic, or otherwise harmful to human health or the environment”).
