**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School District’s facilities constitute a limited public forum open to all community groups, and the District may not exclude Lamb’s Chapel based on the religious content of its speech.**

The Second Circuit erred in treating the School District’s facilities as a nonpublic forum merely because the District had not previously permitted religious uses. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983), explains that a forum’s classification turns on the government’s policy and practice, not on hypothetical or speculative exclusions. Here, the District permitted numerous secular community groups to use its facilities, including the Southern Harmonizers (a musical group), a parapsychology lecture series, and various civic organizations. *See* App. 46–48. This pattern of open access created a limited public forum under *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985), which held that when the government opens property for expressive activity, it may not discriminate based on viewpoint.

The District’s blanket exclusion of religious speech violates the First Amendment. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 (1981), established that once a university creates a forum for student expression, it may not exclude religious groups based on the content of their speech. The same principle applies here: the School District’s facilities are open to community groups, and the exclusion of religious speech is a content-based restriction subject to strict scrutiny. *See also* *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (applying *Widmar* to a public high school).

**II. The District’s policy violates the Free Speech Clause by suppressing religious speech while permitting secular speech on similar topics.**

The District permitted films and lectures on family values, child-rearing, and social issues—topics identical to those in Lamb’s Chapel’s proposed film series. Yet it denied access solely because the Chapel’s perspective was religious. This is viewpoint discrimination, which is “presumptively unconstitutional.” *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (citing *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46). Even under pre-*Rosenberger* precedent, the Court condemned such selective exclusions. *Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement*, 505 U.S. 123, 134 (1992), held that a content-based permit fee violated the First Amendment because it allowed officials to “suppress disfavored speech.” The same danger exists here, where the District’s policy empowers officials to exclude religious speech while permitting secular alternatives.

**III. The District’s policy does not survive strict scrutiny.**

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations is insufficient to justify its exclusion of religious speech. *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421, 430 (1962), held that the Establishment Clause prohibits government from favoring or endorsing religion, but it does not permit the suppression of religious speech in a public forum. *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), recognized that accommodation of religion is permissible, but only if it does not coerce or endorse. The District’s policy does neither; it simply excludes religious speech entirely, which is far more restrictive than the accommodations upheld in *Zorach*.

**IV. The District’s policy violates the Free Exercise Clause by targeting religious speech.**

The District’s policy singles out religious speech for exclusion, which burdens the free exercise of religion. *Minersville School Dist. v. Gobitis*, 310 U.S. 586, 594 (1940), held that the Free Exercise Clause protects religious practices from discriminatory burdens. While *Gobitis* was later overruled in *West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624 (1943), the principle that religious speech cannot be singled out for disfavored treatment remains intact. The District’s policy does precisely that, and thus violates the Free Exercise Clause.

**V. The District’s policy is not saved by the Establishment Clause.**

The District argues that its policy avoids Establishment Clause violations, but the opposite is true. The policy discriminates against religious speech, which risks fostering hostility toward religion—a harm the Establishment Clause was designed to prevent. *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Educ.*, 333 U.S. 203, 210 (1948), held that the Establishment Clause prohibits government from “aid[ing] one religion, aid[ing] all religions, or prefer[ring] one religion over another.” The District’s policy prefers secular speech over religious speech, which is equally unconstitutional.

**CONCLUSION**

The Second Circuit’s decision upholding the District’s exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel’s religious speech is contrary to settled First Amendment principles. The School District’s facilities are a limited public forum, and the District may not exclude religious speech based on its content. The policy violates the Free Speech, Free Exercise, and Establishment Clauses. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for entry of judgment in favor of Lamb’s Chapel.
