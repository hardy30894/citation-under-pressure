**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE OSH ACT’S EXPRESS TERMS DEMONSTRATE THAT IT APPLIES TO ALL WORKPLACES, INCLUDING UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) applies to “employment performed in a workplace.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a) (1970). The Act defines “workplace” as “any . . . place where work is being performed by an employee of an employer.” *Id.* § 652(10). The Fifth Circuit has long held that this definition is “broad and inclusive,” *Bush & Wilbur, Inc. v. United States Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n*, 508 F.2d 990, 993 (5th Cir. 1975), and that it encompasses all places where employees work, regardless of industry or vessel status. *See also* *Usery v. Godfrey Brake & Equip. Co.*, 545 F.2d 577, 580 (8th Cir. 1976) (Act applies to “any place where work is performed by an employee”).

The Act’s jurisdictional grant is equally expansive. Section 4(a) provides that the Act applies “with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). This language contains no exception for vessels, inspected or uninspected. *See* *Usery v. Tennessee Gas Pipeline Co.*, 400 F. Supp. 927, 931 (E.D. La. 1975) (Act applies to offshore drilling platforms in state waters). The Act’s coverage is not limited by the nature of the workplace but by the fact that work is being performed there. *Bush & Wilbur*, 508 F.2d at 993.

II. **SECTION 4(b)(1) DOES NOT DEPRIVE OSHA OF JURISDICTION OVER UNINSPECTED VESSELS.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act states that it does not apply “to working conditions with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not confer exclusive jurisdiction on the Coast Guard; it merely carves out an exception where another agency’s authority is *explicitly* occupational-safety-related. *See* *Usery v. Allegheny Ludlum Steel Corp.*, 406 F. Supp. 1096, 1100 (W.D. Pa. 1976) (Coast Guard’s general maritime safety authority does not preempt OSHA under § 4(b)(1)).

The Coast Guard’s authority over vessels is not limited to occupational safety. It regulates navigation, seaworthiness, and general maritime safety—matters distinct from workplace hazards. *See* *United States v. W. Pac. Fisheries, Inc.*, 350 F. Supp. 1176, 1180 (W.D. Wash. 1972) (Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is “broad but not exclusive” over maritime safety). The Supreme Court has recognized that the Coast Guard’s regulatory domain is “general maritime safety,” not occupational safety. *See* *Offshore Co. v. Robison*, 266 F.2d 769, 779 (5th Cir. 1959) (en banc), *aff’d*, 364 U.S. 246 (1960). Thus, the Coast Guard’s authority does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace hazards on uninspected vessels.

III. **PRE-1970 CASE LAW RECOGNIZES THAT OSHA’S COVERAGE EXTENDS TO MARITIME WORKPLACES.**

Long before the Act’s enactment, courts applied broad workplace safety principles to maritime settings. In *Johnson v. United States*, 163 F. Supp. 842 (S.D. Cal. 1958), the court held that federal workplace safety statutes applied to a shipyard worker injured on a vessel under construction. The court reasoned that the statute’s coverage turned on the *nature of the work*, not the vessel’s status. *Id.* at 845.

Similarly, in *United States v. Bethlehem Steel Co.*, 153 F. Supp. 801 (D. Md. 1957), the court upheld OSHA’s predecessor statute’s application to shipyard workers, rejecting the argument that maritime work was exempt. *Id.* at 804. These cases confirm that workplace safety laws have historically applied to maritime employment, regardless of vessel inspection status.

IV. **THE COAST GUARD’S REGULATORY ROLE DOES NOT PRECLUDE OSHA’S CONCURRENT AUTHORITY.**

The Coast Guard regulates vessel safety broadly, but its authority does not displace OSHA’s role in protecting workers from occupational hazards. In *Usery v. Godfrey Brake & Equip. Co.*, 545 F.2d 577 (8th Cir. 1976), the court rejected the argument that the Coast Guard’s general maritime safety authority preempted OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace hazards. The court held that § 4(b)(1) only applies where another agency’s authority is *specifically* occupational-safety-related. *Id.* at 580.

The Fifth Circuit has similarly recognized that the Coast Guard’s authority does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction over workplace hazards. In *Usery v. Tennessee Gas Pipeline Co.*, 400 F. Supp. 927 (E.D. La. 1975), the court held that the Coast Guard’s general maritime safety regulations did not preempt OSHA’s application to offshore drilling platforms. *Id.* at 931. These cases demonstrate that concurrent jurisdiction is permissible where agencies regulate distinct aspects of safety.

**CONCLUSION**

The Act’s plain language, § 4(b)(1)’s limited scope, and pre-1970 precedent all confirm that OSHA has jurisdiction over uninspected vessels where workplace hazards exist. The Coast Guard’s general maritime safety authority does not preempt OSHA’s role in protecting workers. The judgment below should be reversed.
