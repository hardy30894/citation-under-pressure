**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). The Supreme Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 690-91. This Court must apply that limitation in reviewing Petitioner’s continued detention.

The Court in *Zadvydas* rejected the argument that § 1231(a)(6) authorizes indefinite detention. *Id.* at 690. It held that the statute permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and that continued detention becomes unreasonable once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 699-700. The Court expressly declined to defer to the Attorney General’s interpretation of the statute, noting that the statute’s text and structure do not clearly authorize indefinite detention. *Id.* at 690-91.

The Court further established a framework for habeas review: after six months, the alien bears the burden of showing that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, and the Government must rebut that showing with evidence of a significant likelihood of removal. *Id.* at 705. If the Government fails to rebut, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute. *Id.* Here, Petitioner has demonstrated that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, and the Government has not rebutted that showing. Thus, his continued detention exceeds the statute’s reasonable time limitation.

II. INDEFINITE DETENTION VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

The Due Process Clause prohibits indefinite civil detention absent a special justification. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690 (citing *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 77 (1992)). The Government’s interest in detaining removable aliens is limited to ensuring their presence at removal, and indefinite detention serves no legitimate purpose once removal is no longer possible. *Id.* at 691-92.

The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized that § 1231(a)(6) applies broadly to aliens ordered removed for various reasons, not just dangerous individuals. *Id.* at 699. It rejected the Government’s argument that preventive detention is justified by the risk of flight or recidivism, noting that such risks can be addressed through supervised release. *Id.* at 701-02. The Court held that indefinite detention would raise serious constitutional concerns, and thus construed the statute to avoid that result. *Id.* at 690-91.

Here, Petitioner has been detained for years beyond the removal period, with no realistic prospect of removal. His continued detention violates the Due Process Clause because it is not narrowly tailored to a legitimate governmental objective. *See Foucha*, 504 U.S. at 77 (holding that indefinite detention must be justified by a legitimate purpose and narrow tailoring).

III. THE STATUTE’S REASONABLE TIME LIMITATION APPLIES TO PETITIONER’S DETENTION

The Court in *Zadvydas* applied its reasonable time limitation to a resident alien ordered removed, rejecting the Government’s argument that the statute’s text or history supported indefinite detention. 533 U.S. at 690-91. The Court noted that the statute’s purpose is to ensure the alien’s presence at removal, not to punish or indefinitely detain. *Id.* at 699.

Here, Petitioner has been detained for years beyond the removal period, with no prospect of removal. His detention is not reasonably necessary to secure removal, and thus exceeds the statute’s reasonable time limitation. The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced, as *Mezei* involved an excludable alien at the border, not a resident alien ordered removed. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700-01.

The Court’s framework in *Zadvydas* requires that Petitioner’s detention be reviewed under the reasonable time limitation. Because removal is not reasonably foreseeable, his continued detention violates the statute and the Due Process Clause.
