**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or are connected with” the defendant’s activities in the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945). The “connection” requirement does not demand that the defendant’s in-state contacts *cause* the plaintiff’s injury. Rather, it is satisfied when the defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958), and the claims bear a “substantial connection” to those forum contacts, *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957).

Ford Motor Company has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana by advertising its vehicles, maintaining a network of authorized dealers, and selling replacement parts and services throughout the State. *Cf. Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643, 648–49 (1950) (solicitation of business in forum establishes purposeful availment). These activities are not random or fortuitous; they reflect a deliberate effort to cultivate a market for Ford products in Montana. *See McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223 (single contract with forum resident sufficient for jurisdiction where defendant “deliberately” reached into forum). Accordingly, Ford cannot claim that it lacked notice of the possibility of being haled into Montana courts. *See Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940) (due process requires that defendant have “certain minimum contacts” such that “maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice’”).

The claims in this case—arising from an allegedly defective Ford Explorer that was driven in Montana—are “connected with” Ford’s Montana activities. The vehicle’s presence in Montana is the direct result of Ford’s nationwide marketing and distribution system, which includes extensive advertising and parts sales within the State. Ford’s in-state activities create a link between the forum and the litigation that is far more than “attenuated.” *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 251. Indeed, this Court has long recognized that jurisdiction may be proper when the defendant’s forum contacts, though not the immediate cause of the injury, are “related to” the claim. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 320 (activities “connected with” the cause of action satisfy due process); *see also Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356–57 (1927) (nonresident motorist subject to jurisdiction for claims arising from use of roads in forum, even though the accident was not caused by defendant’s in-state business activities).

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction requires a causal link between its Montana contacts and the plaintiff’s injury finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 precedents. In *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), the Court upheld jurisdiction over a foreign corporation that conducted continuous and systematic activities in the forum, even though the plaintiff’s claim did not arise from those activities. *Id.* at 447–48. While *Perkins* involved general jurisdiction, its reasoning underscores that the “arise out of or relate to” standard is not a strict causation test. *See also Old Wayne Mut. Life Ass’n v. McDonough*, 204 U.S. 8, 22–23 (1907) (jurisdiction over nonresident insurer based on business conducted in forum, even though policy was issued elsewhere). Here, Ford’s Montana-specific activities—advertising, parts sales, and dealer networks—are purposefully directed at the forum and are “related to” the claims because they made it foreseeable that Ford vehicles, including the Explorer at issue, would be used in Montana. *Cf. Henry L. Doherty & Co. v. Goodman*, 294 U.S. 623, 627–28 (1935) (nonresident securities dealer subject to jurisdiction based on in-state solicitation, even though the specific transaction occurred elsewhere).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s contacts with the State—including the “sale of parts, service, and advertising” in Montana—establish a “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” That conclusion is consistent with the Due Process Clause as interpreted in *International Shoe*, *McGee*, and *Hanson*. Ford’s insistence on a causation requirement would improperly narrow the “arise out of or relate to” standard and would immunize manufacturers from jurisdiction in any state where they market products but do not sell the specific item that caused injury. Such a result would contravene the “liberal trend” in personal jurisdiction recognized by this Court. *McGee*, 355 U.S. at 222 (citing *International Shoe*). The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
