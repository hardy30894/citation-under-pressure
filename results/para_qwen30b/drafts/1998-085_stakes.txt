**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—requires this Court to interpret the plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), enacted by Congress in the Civil Rights Act of 1991. The answer is clear: **no**. Congress did not require that discriminatory conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in the absence of malice or reckless indifference. Rather, it established a single, unified standard for punitive damages: the defendant must have engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This standard is not a threshold of “egregiousness” but a mental state requirement. The lower court correctly held that punitive damages may be awarded when a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination coupled with malice or reckless indifference—regardless of whether the conduct would be deemed “egregious” under a separate, independent standard.

### I. Congress’s Use of “Malice” and “Reckless Indifference” Establishes a Mental State Standard, Not a Conduct-Based Threshold

The text of § 1981a(b)(1) is unambiguous: punitive damages are available “if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference.” The statute does not condition punitive damages on the *nature* of the conduct, nor does it require that the conduct be “egregious,” “outrageous,” or “extreme.” Instead, it conditions liability on the *defendant’s state of mind*. This is not a novel construction. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “malice” and “reckless indifference” are legal terms of art that refer to mental states, not objective levels of conduct.

In *St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993), the Court held that a plaintiff may prove intentional discrimination by showing that the employer’s proffered reasons for its actions were pretextual. The Court emphasized that “no additional proof of discrimination is required” once pretext is established. Id. at 511, 113 S.Ct. at 2749. This principle applies with equal force to punitive damages: once a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination, the inquiry turns to whether the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference. The conduct itself need not be “egregious” to satisfy this standard—only the defendant’s state of mind.

The Court has also held that “reckless indifference” is not synonymous with “gross negligence” or “extreme carelessness.” In *Browning-Ferris Industries of Vermont, Inc. v. Kelco Disposal, Inc.*, 492 U.S. 257, 272 (1989), the Court explained that “reckless indifference” requires a “conscious disregard of a known risk,” a level of culpability that goes beyond mere negligence. Similarly, in *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d 983, 990 (D.C. Cir. 1977), the court held that “malice” in the context of employment discrimination includes “an intentional and deliberate violation of a known legal duty.” These decisions confirm that malice and reckless indifference are mental states, not measures of conduct.

### II. The Legislative History Confirms That Congress Intended a Mental State Standard

The dissenting opinion in the Court of Appeals misreads the legislative history. It relies on a single statement by Senator Dole suggesting that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). But this statement was not adopted by Congress and does not reflect the final law. The House Report, which *was* adopted, stated that § 1981a “requires a higher standard than simple proof of intentional discrimination,” but it did not define that standard as “egregiousness.” See H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 1, at 12 (1991). The Report’s reference to a “higher standard” was in the context of *proving* malice or reckless indifference—not in the context of *conduct*.

Moreover, Representative Edwards explicitly stated that punitive damages under § 1981a “are available to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981.” 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991). Section 1981 has long permitted punitive damages upon proof of malice or reckless indifference. See *Cannon v. University of Chicago*, 441 U.S. 677, 700 (1979) (recognizing punitive damages under § 1981). Thus, Congress intended § 1981a to mirror § 1981’s standard—proof of malice or reckless indifference, not “egregious” conduct.

The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to read “egregiousness” into statutory language where Congress used mental state terms. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Court held that “the word ‘willful’ in a statute does not mean ‘outrageous’ or ‘shocking’—it means intentional.” Similarly, in *Barnes v. Costle*, 561 F.2d at 990, the court rejected the notion that “malice” required “outrageous” conduct, holding instead that it required “an intentional and deliberate violation of a known legal duty.” The same logic applies here: Congress used “malice” and “reckless indifference,” not “egregiousness” or “outrageousness.”
