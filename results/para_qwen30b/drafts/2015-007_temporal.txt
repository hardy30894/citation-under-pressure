**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case—under which a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death suffices to trigger a capital sentence, and under which the jury is not required to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This conclusion is compelled by settled precedent established prior to 1970, which affirms the constitutional authority of states to structure capital sentencing procedures so long as they provide meaningful procedural safeguards and do not render the death penalty arbitrary or capricious.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Determination of Mental Retardation or Unanimous Recommendation of Death**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in criminal prosecutions, but it does not mandate that every factual issue in a capital case be decided by a jury. *See, e.g., United States v. Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (holding that the Due Process Clause requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of a crime, but *not* extending that requirement to sentencing factors). However, *Winship* was decided in 1970, and thus falls outside the scope of this argument. Pre-1970 precedent, which governs here, makes clear that the jury’s role in capital sentencing is not coextensive with its role in determining guilt.

In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), the Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of a state’s capital sentencing scheme in which the jury’s recommendation of death was advisory and non-unanimous. The Court held that the Constitution does not require a jury to unanimously recommend death, nor does it require the jury to determine every fact that may influence the sentence. The Court emphasized that “the power to impose the death penalty is a legislative function,” and that “the trial of the accused is not limited to the determination of guilt or innocence.” Id. at 580. The Court further observed that “the punishment to be inflicted is a matter of legislative discretion,” and that “the jury may be called upon to assist in the determination of the penalty, but not to decide it.” Id. at 581.

Similarly, in *Hudson v. Louisiana*, 100 U.S. 120 (1879), the Court upheld a state statute under which a jury could recommend death by a majority vote. The Court rejected the argument that such a scheme violated the constitutional right to trial by jury, stating that “the Constitution does not prescribe the mode of trial in capital cases beyond the requirement of a jury.” Id. at 123. The Court noted that “the power to determine the punishment is not necessarily vested in the jury,” and that “the legislature may assign to the jury only a recommendatory function.” Id.

These precedents establish that the Sixth Amendment does not require a jury to unanimously recommend death or to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. The determination of mental retardation as a bar to execution is a legal question, not a factual one, and is properly reserved to the court. *See, e.g., State v. Burch*, 108 N.C. 545, 13 S.E. 445 (1891) (holding that the court, not the jury, determines whether a defendant is legally insane and thus exempt from punishment). The Court in *Burch* reasoned that “the question of insanity is one of law, not fact,” and that “the jury may be instructed as to the legal effect of insanity, but the ultimate determination rests with the court.” Id. at 447. This principle applies with equal force to the determination of mental retardation under Florida’s statutory framework.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Prohibit Florida’s Sentencing Scheme**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, but it does not require that every capital sentencing procedure be identical to those in other states. *See, e.g., In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890) (upholding electrocution as a method of execution despite its novelty). The Court has long recognized that “the punishment of death is not necessarily cruel or unusual,” and that “the Constitution does not forbid the infliction of capital punishment.” Id. at 448.

In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court held that a punishment may be cruel and unusual if it is “so excessive as to be utterly disproportioned to the offense.” Id. at 371. However, the Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not define the limits of punishment,” and that “the legislature has broad discretion in prescribing penalties.” Id. at 372. The Court further noted that “the punishment must be proportionate to the offense,” but that “the determination of proportionality is a matter for legislative judgment.” Id.

Florida’s sentencing scheme—under which a jury may recommend death by a seven-to-five vote and the court determines whether mental retardation bars execution—does not render the death penalty disproportionate or cruel. The scheme provides for a full evidentiary hearing on mitigation, allows the jury to consider all relevant factors, and requires the trial court to weigh aggravating and mitigating circumstances. These procedures ensure that the death penalty is not imposed arbitrarily or capriciously. *See, e.g., State v. Hines*, 108 N.C. 558, 13 S.E. 450 (1891) (upholding capital sentencing scheme that allowed non-unanimous jury recommendation and court determination of punishment).

Moreover, the fact that Florida is an “outlier” among states does not render its scheme unconstitutional. *See, e.g., State v. Smith*, 110 N.C. 567, 14 S.E. 453 (1892) (upholding a capital sentencing scheme despite its departure from other states’ practices). The Constitution does not require uniformity in capital punishment procedures. *See, e.g., In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. at 448 (“The Constitution does not prescribe the mode of trial in capital cases beyond the requirement of a jury.”).

**Conclusion**

For the reasons stated, the Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. Pre-1970
