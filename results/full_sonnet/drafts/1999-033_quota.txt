# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE REDRESSES DISCRIMINATION AGAINST IDENTIFIABLE CLASSES, NOT AD HOC GRIEVANCES OF A SINGLE INDIVIDUAL.

The Equal Protection Clause commands that no State shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has consistently construed that guarantee as a protection against discriminatory treatment of persons because of their membership in a class. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), the Court explained that a violation requires "an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination," *id.* at 8, directed at a defined group—there, candidates for public office treated differently from others similarly situated by virtue of an identifiable characteristic. The doctrine has never been understood to convert every instance of allegedly unequal government action toward a single citizen into a constitutional claim.

That class-based understanding pervades this Court's equal protection jurisprudence. In *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), the Court held that a plaintiff must show that government action "was motivated by a discriminatory purpose" toward a cognizable class, *id.* at 240, and in *Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), the Court reiterated that the Clause guards against action taken "because of," not merely "in spite of," its effect on an identifiable group, *id.* at 279. Likewise, in *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1 (1973), the Court's tiered scrutiny framework presupposes that the plaintiff has identified a class subject to disparate treatment, whether suspect, quasi-suspect, or otherwise. And in *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center*, 473 U.S. 432 (1985), even under rational-basis review the Court analyzed the claim by reference to the treatment of "the retarded" as a group, *id.* at 446, not by reference to a single permit applicant's subjective sense of unfairness. Respondent's suit, which alleges no membership in any race, class, or group whatsoever, falls entirely outside this framework.

## II. THE NARROW TAX-VALUATION PRECEDENTS INVOKED BELOW DO NOT SUPPORT A FREESTANDING "CLASS OF ONE" CAUSE OF ACTION.

The Seventh Circuit's holding traces to a pair of tax-assessment decisions that are readily distinguishable and should not be extended to ordinary municipal permitting decisions. In *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441 (1923), the Court found an equal protection violation where a single taxpayer's property was assessed at full value while all other property in the county was systematically assessed at a fraction of value—a context in which the "single" plaintiff was in truth the *only* member of a discrete, identifiable comparison group created by the assessor's uniform practice. Similarly, in *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission of Webster County*, 488 U.S. 336 (1989), the Court condemned a assessment scheme that, "over time," *id.* at 345, produced "gross disparities" in valuation attributable to a systematic administrative practice rather than an individualized, one-time discretionary decision. Both cases involved mechanical, recurring valuation systems susceptible to objective, mathematical comparison—not a single discretionary easement negotiation between a municipality and a landowner. Extending those narrow tax-valuation holdings to cover every discretionary land-use or permitting decision would strip them of their limiting principle and convert isolated administrative disputes into constitutional litigation.

## III. RECOGNIZING A "CLASS OF ONE" THEORY WOULD FEDERALIZE ROUTINE MUNICIPAL DECISION-MAKING, CONTRARY TO THIS COURT'S REPEATED ADMONITIONS AGAINST CONSTITUTIONALIZING ORDINARY GOVERNMENT DISPUTES.

This Court has repeatedly cautioned against transforming the Fourteenth Amendment into a general font of tort law for supervising the day-to-day operations of state and local government. In *DeShaney v. Winnebago County Department of Social Services*, 489 U.S. 189 (1989), the Court warned against reading the Fourteenth Amendment to impose "an affirmative obligation" on the State that would effectively federalize decisions properly left to state tort and administrative law, *id.* at 196. And in *Collins v. City of Harker Heights*, 503 U.S. 115 (1992), the Court emphasized that the Due Process Clause "is not a guarantee against incorrect or ill-advised" governmental decisions and that federal courts must resist the temptation to constitutionalize claims that "are, in essence, tort claims," *id.* at 128-29—admonitions equally applicable to a theory that would convert the Equal Protection Clause into a vehicle for federal review of every discretionary permitting or licensing decision a municipal employee makes.

If a single citizen's subjective belief that a municipality demanded a larger easement than it requested from a neighbor states a federal constitutional claim, then virtually every zoning variance, permit condition, and licensing decision becomes fodder for federal litigation whenever the aggrieved party can allege that some other applicant received more favorable treatment. Nothing in *Snowden*, *Davis*, *Feeney*, *Rodriguez*, or *Cleburne* countenances so sweeping an expansion of the Equal Protection Clause, and the narrow tax-assessment precedents in *Sioux City Bridge* and *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal* provide no warrant for it. This Court should hold that the Equal Protection Clause does not recognize a cause of action brought by a plaintiff who alleges no class membership at all, and should reverse the judgment below.
