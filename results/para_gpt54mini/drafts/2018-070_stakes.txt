**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), imports adjacent state law whenever that law is “applicable” and merely not inconsistent with federal law. That reading ignores the statute’s structure, history, and controlling precedent. OCSLA does not federalize every adjacent state rule touching offshore employment; it adopts state law only as surrogate federal law when federal law leaves a gap. See *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355-66 (1969); *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97, 101-04 (1971); *Suncor Energy (U.S.A.) Inc. v. BP Am. Prod. Co.*, 817 F.3d 1287, 1296-1300 (5th Cir. 2016).

1. **OCSLA’s text incorporates state law only “to the extent” it functions as surrogate federal law.**  
Section 1333(a)(2)(A) provides that adjacent state law is adopted only “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Those words do not authorize state law wherever it touches the subject matter of the dispute. They limit adoption to the extent state law can operate as federal law on the Shelf. The Supreme Court has repeatedly described OCSLA’s purpose as creating a body of law for the seabed and fixed structures on the Shelf, not preserving a parallel regime of state regulation. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355-66; *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 221-24 (1986). In *Rodrigue*, the Court explained that fixed platforms are to be treated like “artificial islands,” and that state law applies only because Congress made it surrogate federal law. 395 U.S. at 355, 366.

2. **The question is whether federal law is already “applicable” to the dispute; if so, there is no room for state law.**  
The statute’s “applicable” requirement is not satisfied merely because a state rule addresses the same topic. Rather, state law is incorporated only where federal law is absent or deficient as the rule of decision. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365-66; *Chevron Oil*, 404 U.S. at 104-05. This Court’s precedents likewise apply adjacent state law only to the extent it fills a gap in federal law. See *Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1126, 1132-34 (9th Cir. 2010). The Fifth Circuit’s earlier formulation in *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990), cannot be read to displace the statute’s gap-filling function; later Fifth Circuit decisions confirm that state law is incorporated only when federal law does not already provide the governing rule. *Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc.*, 713 F.3d 208, 214-15 (5th Cir. 2013); *Suncor Energy*, 817 F.3d at 1296-1300.

3. **The FLSA provides the governing federal law here, and its savings clause does not invite state law onto the Shelf.**  
Congress enacted the Fair Labor Standards Act as a comprehensive wage-and-hour scheme. *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 739-41 (1981). Its savings clause preserves more protective state minimum-wage and overtime laws in their own sphere; it does not expand OCSLA’s adoption rule or authorize adjacent state law as a surrogate federal remedy on the Shelf. See 29 U.S.C. § 218(a); *Williamson v. Gen. Dynamics Corp.*, 208 F.3d 1144, 1149-50 (9th Cir. 2000). Where Congress has supplied a federal standard, OCSLA does not transform state wage-and-hour law into additional federal law merely because it is not inconsistent with the FLSA. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365-66; *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 223-24.

4. **Respondent’s theory would collapse OCSLA into ordinary preemption analysis and defeat Congress’s offshore choice-of-law scheme.**  
OCSLA is not a general conflict-preemption statute. Congress directed courts first to determine whether federal law applies, and only then—if a gap remains—to borrow adjacent state law as federal law. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365-66; *Chevron Oil*, 404 U.S. at 104-05. The Ninth Circuit’s rule would instead allow state law to govern whenever it concerns the same subject matter and is not expressly inconsistent with federal law. That approach would produce patchwork offshore regulation, contrary to Congress’s objective of creating a uniform federal regime for Shelf development. *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 222-24; *Suncor Energy*, 817 F.3d at 1298-1300.

Because the FLSA governs the wage-and-hour issues raised by Newton’s complaint, there is no statutory gap for California law to fill. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
