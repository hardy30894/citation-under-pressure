ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS may not collect a partnership’s unpaid employment taxes from individual general partners unless it has assessed those partners separately within the three-year period prescribed by 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Because no such assessments occurred, the ten-year collection period of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) never commenced as to respondents.

Section 6203 requires that an assessment “be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The Code defines “taxpayer” to include both a “partnership” and an “individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The implementing regulation therefore mandates that every assessment “provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. An assessment against one taxpayer does not automatically constitute an assessment against another. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (lien invalid where assessment named only the partnership and “an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers”).

This Court has long recognized that “the taxpayer against whom a deficiency has been assessed” is the same taxpayer whose limitations period is extended. See, e.g., United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561, 562-63 (7th Cir. 1995) (tolling analysis presupposed separate assessments against both partnership and partners). The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion follows directly: the timely assessments against Marina Cabrillo Partners extended only the partnership’s limitations period. Respondents, as distinct taxpayers, remained subject to the ordinary three-year bar. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

California partnership law supplies no alternative route to collection. Under Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c), “[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” A creditor must obtain a separate judgment against the partner before reaching personal assets. The IRS obtained no such judgment, and the three-year period for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a).

Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is not to the contrary. There the partner had already paid the tax; the court merely refused a refund. The opinion expressly noted that collection without an individual assessment or judgment would require “resort[] to court action.” Id. at 910. No such action was timely commenced here.

Because the IRS failed to assess respondents within three years and failed to obtain judgments against them, its proofs of claim are “unenforceable against the debtor.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
