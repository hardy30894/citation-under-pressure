**ARGUMENT**

I. **The district court erred in holding that Selam was not denied compulsory process.**

The right to compulsory process is fundamental, and when a defendant is deprived of the meaningful ability to present defense witnesses, the conviction cannot stand. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), held that the Sixth Amendment protects an accused’s right to obtain witnesses in his favor and that arbitrary restrictions on defense testimony violate due process. The Court explained that “the right to present the defendant’s version of the facts” is essential to a fair adversary trial. *Id.* at 19. Likewise, *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400 (1965), recognized that the confrontation and compulsory-process guarantees are among the basic protections applicable to criminal trials because they secure the reliability of adjudication.

Selam did not knowingly and intelligently waive this right. The record shows that he was advised of the ability to subpoena witnesses, but the court-appointed spokesperson had already located potential witnesses, two of whom were affirmatively harmful, and Selam was indigent, unrepresented by counsel, and tried in a tribal forum without the ordinary safeguards of a modern criminal proceeding. Waiver of a fundamental trial right is never presumed. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), held that courts must “indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver” of constitutional rights. The same principle applies here: silence or imperfect understanding cannot be treated as waiver of the right to compulsory process. *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1 (1966), held that a defendant may not be deemed to have waived a fundamental right absent an intentional relinquishment made with understanding. See also *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962) (“Presuming waiver from a silent record is impermissible.”). On this record, there is no affirmative, informed relinquishment.

The error was prejudicial. Selam was convicted in two bench trials in which the prosecution relied heavily on hearsay testimony and child statements, while the defense presented no witnesses other than Selam himself. In a case turning on credibility, the ability to call character or rebuttal witnesses is critical. *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), confirms that arbitrary exclusion of defense testimony undermines the truth-seeking function of trial. Because Selam was effectively denied the practical use of compulsory process, the judgment on the first claim should be reversed.

II. **Selam did not waive his confrontation claim, and the district court erred in refusing to reach it.**

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. That ruling conflicts with settled waiver principles. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), requires an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), makes clear that waiver cannot be inferred from silence. And *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), explained that habeas relief is not barred absent a deliberate bypass of available procedures. Nothing in this record shows that Selam intentionally surrendered his confrontation objection; at most, he failed to anticipate every legal theory in the tribal appeal.

That failure should not defeat federal review. *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241 (1886), and *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), recognize that habeas is an exceptional remedy to test unlawful custody, and technical forfeiture rules should not be stretched to insulate convictions obtained in violation of fundamental rights. Selam consistently challenged the fairness of the trial process, including compulsory process, and his confrontation objection arises from the same evidentiary practices. The district court’s rigid waiver ruling is inconsistent with the liberal protection of habeas rights reflected in those decisions.

III. **The admission of the victims’ out-of-court statements violated the confrontation right.**

The Confrontation Clause protects the accused against conviction by ex parte accusation. *Mattox v. United States*, 156 U.S. 237 (1895), recognized that face-to-face confrontation is a core trial guarantee, subject only to narrow exceptions. *Motes v. United States*, 178 U.S. 458 (1900), held that the government may not rely on testimony from absent witnesses where the defendant lacked a meaningful opportunity to confront. *Kirby v. United States*, 174 U.S. 47 (1899), likewise rejected convictions resting on evidence from declarants whom the accused could not cross-examine. In *Douglas v. Alabama*, 380 U.S. 415 (1965), the Court reaffirmed that where the prosecution introduces a witness’s statements without cross-examination, the accused’s confrontation rights are violated.

Those principles apply with special force here. In the second trial, the five-year-old victim never testified. Instead, the prosecution introduced her accusations through a physician, her parents, and an investigator. Selam had no opportunity to test the child’s memory, perception, or sincerity through cross-examination. *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400 (1965), held that confrontation is not a mere formality; it is an essential means of exposing testimonial weakness. Because the conviction rested on repeated out-of-court accusations, the trial court denied Selam the confrontation guaranteed by law.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgments should be reversed, and the petition for habeas relief granted.
