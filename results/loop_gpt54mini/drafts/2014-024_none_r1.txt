**Argument**

Section 875(c) does not impose strict liability. When Congress enacts a criminal statute silent as to mens rea, courts presume it incorporates a guilty mind requirement. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-63 (1952); *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-52 (1922); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). That presumption is strongest where the statute reaches speech, because the First Amendment forbids punishment of expression absent a constitutionally sufficient culpable mental state. *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard cannot be reconciled with that background rule. Under its approach, a speaker may be convicted if a “reasonable person” would foresee the words as threatening, even if the defendant meant something else entirely. But this Court has repeatedly insisted that criminal liability for speech turns on the speaker’s own unlawful purpose. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that advocacy may be punished only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action” and likely to do so. In *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 320-31 (1957), the Court limited liability for political advocacy by requiring proof of purposeful promotion of unlawful action. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502-10 (1951), the Court sustained liability only after closely examining the defendants’ knowing conduct. Those cases reflect a consistent principle: where speech is the conduct charged, the Constitution demands proof of the defendant’s subjective culpability.

The same principle governs threats. This Court has never held that a threat conviction may rest on the speaker’s mere negligence. To the contrary, the Court’s decisions distinguish true threats from protected speech by looking to context, purpose, and meaning. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the Court reversed a conviction because the statement at issue, viewed in context, was “political hyperbole” rather than a genuine threat. The Court’s analysis makes sense only if what matters is whether the defendant actually meant to threaten, not whether some listener could later say the words sounded frightening.

The historical understanding of threats confirms the same point. At common law, criminal threats were wrongful because they were intended to intimidate. See *Rex v. Ireland*, 98 Eng. Rep. 189 (K.B. 1761). American law inherited that approach. *Baender v. Barnett*, 255 U.S. 224, 226-27 (1921). And this Court has explained that, absent clear statutory language, Congress is presumed to legislate against that backdrop of intent-based criminal liability. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260-61. Nothing in §875(c) displaces that rule.

The government will likely argue that an objective standard is necessary to protect recipients from fear and disruption. But this Court has rejected the notion that speech may be punished simply because it causes alarm. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court held that speech cannot be suppressed because it “stirs people to anger” or invites dispute. In *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 237 (1963), the Court reversed convictions where expression provoked hostility from onlookers. And in *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04, the Court held that the State may not punish speech solely because it offends or disturbs listeners. These cases demonstrate that listener reaction cannot supply the missing element of subjective intent.

The First Amendment concern is especially acute here because Elonis’s postings were not direct face-to-face threats, but ambiguous online messages, many in rap-lyric form and some self-referentially discussing what is “illegal” to say. The law does not permit a jury to infer guilt merely from offensiveness or from the fact that the communications were crude and alarming. Criminal punishment must rest on proof that the defendant meant his statements as threats. Otherwise, protected speech will be swept into the statute’s reach whenever a jury later finds it menacing.

That is why this Court’s speech precedents have required purpose in analogous settings. In *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 229-30 (1961), the Court required active participation and specific intent before imposing criminal liability for association. In *Noto v. United States*, 367 U.S. 290, 297-98 (1961), the Court rejected guilt by association absent proof of purposeful furtherance. In *Ashton v. Kentucky*, 384 U.S. 195, 200-01 (1966), the Court invalidated a vague criminal conviction touching expression because the State had not cabined liability with adequate precision. These cases reinforce the same command: speech-related crimes require careful proof of culpable intent.

Elonis’s posts may have been disturbing, vulgar, and deeply offensive. But the Constitution does not permit conviction for threatening another person under §875(c) unless the government proves the defendant subjectively intended the communication as a threat. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250-63; *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708; *Brandenburg*, 395 U.S. at 447. The judgment should be reversed.
