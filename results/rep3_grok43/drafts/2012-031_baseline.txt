ARGUMENT

I.

A district court may certify a class under Rule 23(b)(3) only after conducting a “rigorous analysis” that demonstrates, by a preponderance of the evidence, that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate.” In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig., 552 F.3d 305, 311, 318 (3d Cir. 2008). That analysis “may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits,” id. at 317, and frequently will entail “overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim,” Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011). When the element of damages is at issue, predominance therefore requires admissible evidence that damages “may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis.” Behrend v. Comcast Corp., 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010).

The District Court’s finding that plaintiffs satisfied this requirement rested on a clearly erroneous assessment of their expert’s model. The model purported to isolate damages attributable to the sole remaining theory of antitrust impact—deterrence of overbuilders—yet it incorporated variables reflecting conduct that the court had already excluded from the case. Because the model could not isolate the effect of the certified theory from the effects of theories no longer in the case, it supplied no common proof of class-wide damages. The District Court’s acceptance of the model therefore constituted an abuse of discretion. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 312.

II.

The same defect defeats predominance under Wal-Mart. There the Court held that a class may not be certified when the plaintiffs’ proof “provides no . . . common method of proving” an essential element. 564 U.S. at 357. Plaintiffs’ damages methodology likewise fails to furnish a common method: it measures damages only by assuming the very clustering conduct that the District Court removed from the case. The resulting figure therefore cannot be attributed to the sole remaining theory of liability. Without evidence that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement tied to that theory, common questions cannot predominate.

The Third Circuit’s affirmance excused this mismatch by deferring to the District Court’s “plausible” view of the evidence. That deference misapplies the clearly erroneous standard. Anderson v. Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573-74 (1985). When expert evidence is offered to satisfy a Rule 23 requirement, the court must determine whether the evidence actually supports the finding; it may not certify simply because the expert’s conclusion is internally consistent. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 323. The District Court performed no such examination, and the court of appeals compounded the error by treating the model’s facial plausibility as sufficient.

III.

Because the record contains no admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis under the single theory of impact the District Court permitted, the certification order cannot stand. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
