**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), does not extend Longshore Act benefits to an injury sustained at an onshore processing facility merely because the employee also worked offshore. Congress limited coverage to disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” That text requires a real offshore nexus; it does not create universal compensation coverage for all injuries traceable in some way to offshore employment.

The analysis begins and ends with the statute’s words. “Where the words of a law are plain, and the meaning clear, we have no right to speculate upon the intentions of the legislature.” **Lake County v. Rollins, 130 U.S. 662, 670 (1889)**. Courts may not “add to a statute what Congress has omitted.” **Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)**. Nor may they “stretch” statutory language beyond its “plain and obvious import.” **United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820)**. Section 1333(b) does not say “any injury related to offshore work.” It says injury “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). That geographic and operational limitation must be enforced.

Older Supreme Court authority confirms the point. In **Brown v. Duchesne, 60 U.S. (19 How.) 183, 194 (1856)**, the Court refused to extend a statute beyond the territorial scope Congress had specified. In **Ex parte McNiel, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 236, 242 (1871)**, the Court treated statutory conditions as binding. In **Morrill v. Jones, 106 U.S. 466, 467 (1883)**, the Court stated that a statutory grant “cannot be enlarged by implication.” Those cases forbid respondent’s effort to transform § 1333(b) into a generalized injury provision untethered to the Outer Continental Shelf itself.

The compensation cases are to the same effect. In **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54-55 (1932)**, the Court recognized that compensation jurisdiction turns on facts and limits fixed by Congress. In **South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 260-61 (1940)**, the Court again honored the statutory boundary Congress drew. And in **Baltimore & Philadelphia Steamboat Co. v. Norton, 284 U.S. 408, 416 (1932)**, the Court made clear that remedial legislation still must be applied according to its terms. Those decisions foreclose the notion that a court may expand coverage because a claimant’s job bears some relationship to offshore production.

Respondent’s contrary theory would effectively excise the phrase “conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” from the statute. That cannot be right. The injury here occurred at La Conchita, an onshore oil-processing facility. Whatever the facility’s importance to Pacific Operations’s offshore business, it was not on the Shelf. Congress knew how to frame a broader standard if it wanted one. It did not do so. Courts may not supply what Congress omitted. **Hebert v. Louisiana, 272 U.S. 312, 316-17 (1926)**; **Baltimore Mail S.S. Co. v. Fawcett, 269 U.S. 247, 251-52 (1925)**.

Nor does OCSLA’s remedial purpose authorize an expansion beyond the statutory text. The Court has long held that even beneficent statutes must be enforced as written. **United States v. Merriam, 263 U.S. 179, 187-88 (1923)**. And the Court has recognized that compensation statutes, though protective, remain bounded by the lines Congress drew. **Ocean Accident & Guarantee Corp. v. Washington, 281 U.S. 574, 580-81 (1930)**. Respondent’s reading would replace Congress’s chosen offshore limitation with a far broader “but for” test. That is not interpretation; it is amendment.

The better reading of § 1333(b) is straightforward: coverage extends to injuries that are sufficiently tied to operations conducted on the Outer Continental Shelf, not to every downstream accident at a land-based facility. A forklift death at an onshore processing site does not fall within that text. The Court should therefore affirm the denial of OCSLA benefits.
