**ARGUMENT**

**I. The barge is a “workplace” under the Occupational Safety and Health Act.**

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the barge was not a “workplace” under the Act. The OSH Act defines a “workplace” as “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not a term of art with a fixed meaning. *NLRB v. Denver Building & Construction Trades Council*, 341 U.S. 675, 681 (1951). A barge where employees perform their duties is a place of employment. Therefore, the barge is a “workplace” under the Act.

**II. OSHA has jurisdiction because the Coast Guard does not have “exclusive” authority over the working conditions at issue.**

The Fifth Circuit erred in finding that the Coast Guard’s authority preempts OSHA jurisdiction. The OSH Act preempts OSHA only if “other Federal agencies... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s authority is limited to the “safety of life and property on and under the high seas.” 14 U.S.C. § 2. This is distinct from the occupational safety and health standards of the OSH Act. In *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Court upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act, recognizing Congress’s broad power to regulate working conditions to protect health and safety. This precedent supports the broad jurisdiction of the OSH Act. The Coast Guard’s “minimal regulation” of uninspected vessels does not constitute the “comprehensive regulation” required to trigger preemption. The Coast Guard has not “prescribed or enforced” OSHA standards. Therefore, OSHA has jurisdiction.
