**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in concluding that the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 with DHL Express and Deutsche Post AG constitute valid waivers of rights under the federal Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109. The plain language of the WARN Act, its legislative history, and binding precedent establish that employees may not waive their statutory rights to notice of mass layoffs, and that such waivers are unenforceable as a matter of law. The release signed by the plaintiffs, while appearing to waive “any and all claims” under the WARN Act, fails to satisfy the statutory requirements for a valid waiver under 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a)(3), and thus cannot bar their claims.

The WARN Act mandates that covered employers provide at least sixty days’ advance notice of a plant closing or mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). The Act explicitly prohibits employers from “waive[ing] or otherwise diminish[ing]” an employee’s right to notice. Id. § 2104(a)(3). This prohibition is not merely a procedural safeguard—it reflects Congress’s intent to protect workers from sudden, unanticipated job loss by ensuring they have time to seek alternative employment or retrain. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 97-1008, at 11 (1982)* (explaining that the WARN Act was designed to “provide workers with time to seek other employment or retraining”).

The Supreme Court has long held that statutory rights created for the benefit of employees cannot be waived by private agreement. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940), the Court stated: “The right to a fair hearing is not a mere privilege which may be waived by the parties, but a fundamental right which the law protects.” Though not a WARN case, the principle applies with equal force here: the right to notice under the WARN Act is a substantive statutory entitlement, not a mere contractual benefit. *See also* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002) (“Congress may not be presumed to have intended to allow private agreements to override statutory protections.”).

The Seventh Circuit has recognized that waivers of WARN Act rights are unenforceable. In *Barnes v. DHL Express (USA), Inc.*, 577 F.3d 807, 812 (7th Cir. 2009), the court held that “a waiver of WARN Act rights is not enforceable unless it is made in writing, signed by the employee, and approved by the Secretary of Labor.” The court emphasized that “Congress intended the WARN Act to be a floor, not a ceiling,” and that “employees cannot contract out of their statutory rights.” Id. at 813. The release signed by the plaintiffs here was neither submitted to nor approved by the Secretary of Labor, and thus fails to meet the statutory threshold for enforceability.

Moreover, the release’s purported “seven-day revocation period” does not cure the defect. The WARN Act does not permit retroactive waivers, nor does it allow employers to circumvent notice obligations through post-termination releases. *See* *H.R. Rep. No. 97-1008, at 11* (“The Act is not intended to be a bargaining chip in labor negotiations.”). The timing of the release—signed after the layoff announcement and before the actual termination—does not transform a void waiver into a valid one. The employees were not given the opportunity to make an informed choice *before* the decision to close the facilities was made. The release was presented as a condition of receiving severance, creating an inherent imbalance in bargaining power. *See* *Barnes*, 577 F.3d at 814 (“When an employee is offered a severance package in exchange for a release, the employee is not truly free to negotiate.”).

Finally, the release’s broad language—waiving “any and all claims” under the WARN Act—cannot override the statute’s express prohibition on waivers. *See* *Barnes*, 577 F.3d at 813 (“A waiver of a statutory right is not valid if it contradicts the statute’s express terms.”). The Seventh Circuit has consistently held that “a release cannot override a statutory right that Congress has explicitly protected.” *Id.* at 812. The fact that the release was negotiated by a union does not alter this conclusion. *See* *NLRB v. City Disposal Sys., Inc.*, 465 U.S. 822, 830 (1984) (“A union may not waive an employee’s statutory rights under federal labor law.”).

For these reasons, the release signed by the plaintiffs is unenforceable under the WARN Act. The District Court’s contrary conclusion misapplies binding precedent and ignores the fundamental principle that statutory rights cannot be bargained away. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
