ARGUMENT

The district court erred in admitting the expert testimony of Agent Jones, which impermissibly invaded the province of the jury and prejudiced Stanley Hill's right to a fair trial.

The admission of Agent Jones' testimony regarding Stanley's credibility and guilt was plain error. As an expert witness, Jones was not permitted to offer an opinion on Stanley's guilt or innocence. See United States v. Spaulding, 293 U.S. 498, 506 (1935) (holding that expert testimony that invades the province of the jury is inadmissible). Jones' statements that Stanley's answers "did not make sense" and that his behavior was "common among the criminal element" were tantamount to an opinion on Stanley's guilt. See Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946) (noting that the admission of improper evidence can be prejudicial if it bears on the defendant's guilt).

Moreover, Jones' testimony regarding Stanley's invocation of God and his statement that he would rather die than face charges was particularly prejudicial. Jones' claim that "when people start bringing faith into validating their statements, they're deceptive" is a sweeping generalization that has no basis in empirical evidence. See Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923) (holding that expert testimony must be based on a scientific principle or discovery that is sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field). Furthermore, Jones' statement that he had "never in his career" seen an innocent person make a statement like Stanley's is an impermissible vouching for the government's theory of the case. See Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (noting that a prosecutor's expression of personal opinion on a defendant's guilt can be prejudicial).

The admission of Jones' testimony was not harmless error. See Weiler v. United States, 323 U.S. 606, 611 (1945) (holding that the admission of improper evidence can be harmless if it does not affect the outcome of the trial). The government's case against Stanley relied heavily on circumstantial evidence, and Jones' testimony may have tipped the scales in favor of conviction. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 67 (1942) (noting that the admission of improper evidence can be prejudicial in a close case).

The plain error standard requires that the error be "plain" and "affect substantial rights." See United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (noting that plain error is error that is "so plain that the trial judge...should have taken action sua sponte"). The admission of Jones' testimony meets this standard, as it was a clear error that affected Stanley's right to a fair trial. See Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) (noting that the plain error standard is met when the error is "so fundamental as to amount to a miscarriage of justice").

In light of the foregoing, this Court should reverse Stanley Hill's conviction and remand for a new trial. See Brasfield v. United States, 272 U.S. 448, 450 (1926) (reversing a conviction due to the admission of improper evidence). The district court's error in admitting Jones' testimony was plain and prejudicial, and Stanley is entitled to a new trial free from the taint of this improper evidence.
