The Ninth Circuit's decision below misinterprets the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act's (OCSLA) choice of law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), by holding that state law applies to the outer Continental Shelf (OCS) whenever it is "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law, without requiring a gap in federal law. This interpretation contravenes the plain meaning of the statute and the Fifth Circuit's well-reasoned precedent.

The OCSLA's choice of law provision declares that state law is to be the law of the United States on the OCS "to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). While the Ninth Circuit correctly notes that the statute does not explicitly require a gap in federal law, its absence is implicit in the term "applicable." As the Fifth Circuit has held, state law is only "applicable" when there is a significant void or gap in federal law that needs to be filled. Cont'l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

The Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), does not support the Ninth Circuit's broad interpretation of the OCSLA's choice of law provision. In Rodrigue, the Court held that state law applied to accidents on fixed drilling platforms on the OCS because there was no federal admiralty remedy available. Id. at 366. The Court's decision was based on the absence of federal law governing the subject matter, not simply because state law was "applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law.

Furthermore, the Fifth Circuit has consistently applied the "significant void or gap" test in determining whether state law applies on the OCS. See, e.g., PLT Eng'g, Inc. v. Union Tex. Petroleum Corp., 895 F.2d 1043, 1048 (5th Cir. 1990); Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc., 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014). This test is consistent with the OCSLA's purpose of providing a comprehensive body of law for the OCS.

In this case, the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) provides a comprehensive statutory scheme governing wage and hour claims. The FLSA's savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), allows for more protective state laws, but it does not create a gap in federal law that state law can fill. Rather, it preserves state laws that are more protective than the FLSA. The Ninth Circuit's decision would allow state law to supplement federal law even when there is no gap or void, contravening the OCSLA's purpose.

The Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that state law applies on the OCS only when there is a significant void or gap in federal law. This interpretation is consistent with the plain meaning of the OCSLA, the Fifth Circuit's precedent, and the Supreme Court's decision in Rodrigue.
