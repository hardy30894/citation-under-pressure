**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE GOVERNMENT MUST PROVE THE PREDICATE CONVICTIONS BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT.**

The government must prove every element of the offense charged, including the predicate conviction requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). In *United States v. Rodriguez*, the Supreme Court explained that while a recidivist statute “penalizes only the last offense committed by the defendant,” the prior convictions are nonetheless “elements of the offense of conviction.” 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). Consequently, the government must prove the existence of the prior convictions beyond a reasonable doubt.

In *United States v. Nichols*, the Supreme Court held that a prior uncouneled misdemeanor conviction cannot be used to establish an element of a crime. 511 U.S. 738, 746 (1994). The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is “intended to guarantee reliability in the determination of guilt,” and an uncouneled conviction is inherently unreliable for that purpose. *Id.* at 746–47. Because § 117(a) requires the government to prove the existence of two prior convictions, the government cannot rely on an uncouneled conviction to satisfy that element. *Id.* at 746. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to invalidate Bryant’s tribal court convictions for this purpose is therefore correct.

**II. THE GOVERNMENT CAN PROVE THE PREDICATE CONVICTIONS WITHOUT VIOLATING THE SIXTH AMENDMENT.**

Although the government cannot use an uncouneled conviction to establish an element of the crime, it may prove the existence of a valid conviction through reliable evidence. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 748. The Court noted that the government may use a prior conviction to establish an element of the crime if the defendant had a “full and fair opportunity to litigate the issue” in the prior proceeding. *Id.* at 748. Here, the government can prove the validity of Bryant’s tribal court convictions through certified records, such as tribal court docket sheets and judgment entries. This satisfies the reliability requirement of *Nichols* without requiring the government to re-litigate the underlying tribal court proceedings.

**III. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT APPLY TO TRIBAL COURTS.**

The government is not required to prove that tribal court proceedings complied with the Sixth Amendment because the Sixth Amendment does not apply to Indian tribes. In *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, the Supreme Court explained that it is “beyond dispute that the ‘Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.’” 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Instead, tribal court proceedings are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA). *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978). As long as the tribal court provided the process required by ICRA, the convictions are “valid” for purposes of § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to apply Sixth Amendment standards to tribal courts is an impermissible intrusion on tribal sovereignty.
