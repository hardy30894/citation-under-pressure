**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds” from a debtor’s bankruptcy estate. An inherited individual retirement account (“inherited IRA”) does not qualify for this exemption because such an account, by its very nature, is not composed of “retirement funds” in the hands of the debtor-beneficiary. The funds in an inherited IRA are held for current consumption, not for the debtor’s post-employment years.

**I. The Term “Retirement Funds” Requires That the Funds Be Held for the Debtor’s Retirement**

The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but the term’s ordinary meaning dictates that funds qualify only if they are set aside for use after the owner stops working. An inherited IRA lacks the essential attributes of a retirement vehicle. Under the Internal Revenue Code, an inherited IRA prohibits new contributions, cannot be rolled over, and—critically—requires mandatory distributions to begin within one year of the original owner’s death, often long before the beneficiary’s own retirement. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). These features transform the inherited IRA into a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, not a repository for long-term retirement savings. *See Boteler v. Ingels*, 308 U.S. 57, 61 (1939) (holding that where a statute uses ordinary terms, courts must give them their ordinary meaning). Because the debtor in this case cannot hold the funds for her own retirement, the account does not contain “retirement funds.”

**II. The Exemption’s Focus on the Debtor’s Use, Not the Decedent’s, Precludes Exemption**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds” without specifying *whose* retirement funds they must be. But context and structure confirm that the exemption turns on the debtor’s intended use, not the original owner’s. For example, § 522(b)(3)(A) exempts a debtor’s “domicile,” and this Court has held that such an exemption depends on how the debtor uses the property, not how a predecessor used it. *See Myers v. Matley*, 318 U.S. 622, 627 (1943) (holding that a homestead exemption is determined by the debtor’s use and intent at the time of the bankruptcy filing). Similarly, a debtor cannot claim an exemption for farm tools that she is not using as a farmer, even if the tools were once used by a family member for farming. *See Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States*, 391 U.S. 224, 228 (1968) (holding that a priority provision’s purpose—providing immediate support to displaced workers—governs its application, not the source of the funds). Here, the inherited IRA funds are not held for the debtor’s retirement; they are available for immediate consumption. That the funds were once someone else’s retirement savings does not change their character in the debtor’s hands.

**III. The Absence of the Possessive “Debtor’s” in the Statute Does Not Expand Exemptions to Former Retirement Funds**

Respondents argue that because § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) omits the word “debtor’s,” the exemption covers any funds that were ever anyone’s retirement funds. This argument conflates temporal scope with subject-matter scope. The omission of “debtor’s” in this provision simply allows debtors to exempt contributions made to retirement accounts *during* the bankruptcy case—new contributions that a debtor could not have made before filing. *See Schrader v. Manufacturers’ Nat. Bank of Chicago*, 133 U.S. 67, 70 (1890) (explaining that exemptions are to be construed in light of their purpose). It does not transform the term “retirement funds” into a permanent label that follows assets regardless of their current function. *Kaufman v. Tredway*, 195 U.S. 271, 273 (1904) (holding that property exempt by its nature must retain that character at the time of bankruptcy to be exempt). Here, the inherited IRA’s mandatory distribution schedule confirms that the funds are not serving a retirement function for the debtor.

**IV. Exemptions Must Be Construed Narrowly to Protect Creditors’ Rights**

Bankruptcy exemptions are designed to balance the debtor’s need for a fresh start with the creditors’ legitimate claims. This Court has consistently held that exemptions should not be expanded beyond their plain meaning. *See Bruning v. United States*, 376 U.S. 358, 360 (1964) (declining to expand bankruptcy discharge to reach obligations the statute did not cover). *Cf. Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294, 301 (1903) (recognizing that exempt property remains subject to creditors’ claims under state law unless Congress clearly intends otherwise). Allowing debtors to shield funds that are currently available for consumption—and that Section 522’s exemption of “retirement funds” cannot plausibly cover—would undermine the fundamental principle that exemptions are a privilege, not a windfall. *Bronx Brass Foundry, Inc. v. Irving Trust Co.*, 297 U.S. 230, 235 (1936) (holding that statutory exemptions must be strictly followed to avoid injustice to creditors).

**V. The Inherited IRA’s Tax Attributes Do Not Transform It into Retirement Funds**

It is true that an inherited IRA retains some tax-deferral benefits. But tax deferral alone does not make funds “retirement funds.” The Bankruptcy Code requires that funds be *both* tax-exempt *and* retirement-oriented. *See McKee v. Paradise*, 299 U.S. 119, 123 (1936) (holding that merely tracing funds to a special account does not make them preferential or exempt; the purpose of the funds must be shown). Here, the inherited IRA’s mandatory distribution schedule well before the debtor’s retirement demonstrates that the funds are not held for retirement. Tax deferral is merely a timing mechanism; it does not alter the funds’ current purpose as a source of immediate, taxable income.

**CONCLUSION**

An inherited IRA does not contain “retirement funds” within the meaning of § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
