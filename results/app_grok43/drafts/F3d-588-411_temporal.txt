A. Supplemental Jury Instruction

The district court erred when it overrode Ye’s objection and supplied the jury with a definition of “shielding.” In Sparf v. United States, 156 U.S. 51, 102 (1895), the Court held that “it is the duty of the court to instruct the jury on the law.” When the statute itself supplies no definition, however, the ordinary meaning of the word controls. United States v. Wiltberger, 5 Wheat. 76, 95 (1820) (“The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself.”). The supplemental instruction departed from that rule by adopting a non-statutory formulation that neither tracked the text nor limited the term to acts performed with the purpose of evading detection.

The error was compounded by the breadth of the definition given. Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 612 (1946), cautioned that “the influence of the trial judge on the jury is necessarily and properly of great weight.” An instruction that sweeps in routine employment acts without requiring specific intent to prevent governmental detection therefore misstates the offense and invades the jury’s fact-finding province. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (“mere knowledge” does not establish the “specific intent” the statute demands).

Because the instruction was both vague in its scope and overbroad in its application, the conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) cannot stand.
