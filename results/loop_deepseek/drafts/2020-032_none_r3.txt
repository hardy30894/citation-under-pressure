**ARGUMENT**

The case is moot. Article III of the Constitution limits federal jurisdiction to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 356 (1911). This requirement persists at every stage of litigation. *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895). When the challenged government policy is repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to it, the controversy ceases to exist. *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969) (per curiam); *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103, 108 (1969). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College revised its "Freedom of Expression Policy" and "Student Code of Conduct" while the suit was pending, and Uzuegbunam graduated. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages. That claim cannot revive a moot case. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Policy Change Extinguished All Ongoing Controversy**

A plaintiff must show a "substantial controversy, between parties having adverse legal interests, of sufficient immediacy and reality to warrant the issuance of a declaratory judgment." *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). Here, the new policy permits expressive activity anywhere on campus without a permit, and the challenged code-of-conduct provision has been removed. There is no reasonable expectation that the college will reimpose the prior restrictions. *See United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953). The college's action was "unambiguous" and "permanent." *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 305 (1964). Because the policy is gone, both the facial and as-applied challenges are moot.

This Court has long recognized that repeal of a challenged law ends the controversy. *California v. San Pablo & Tulare R.R. Co.*, 149 U.S. 308, 314 (1893) (moot when "the act complained of" has been "abandoned"). The same principle applies here. No student now faces the restrictions that Appellants challenged. The dispute is over.

**II. Nominal Damages Do Not Revive a Moot Case**

A plaintiff cannot "maintain a suit after the right to relief has been extinguished by the act of the defendant" simply by requesting nominal damages. *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653. Nominal damages are a "technical" remedy that presupposes a live controversy; they cannot create one where none exists. *St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943) (per curiam). This Court has consistently held that once the underlying dispute ends, the case must be dismissed even if nominal damages are sought. *Atherton Mills v. Johnston*, 259 U.S. 13, 16 (1922) (moot when child labor law expired, despite request for damages); *Brownlow v. Schwartz*, 261 U.S. 216, 218 (1923) (moot when building demolished, despite claim for damages). The only exception is when the plaintiff has suffered a concrete, continuing injury. *Sibron v. New York*, 392 U.S. 40, 55–56 (1968). Here, the complaint alleged no actual injury—only an abstract violation of constitutional rights. Abstract injury, without more, cannot sustain a case after the policy is gone. *Golden*, 394 U.S. at 108–09.

Appellants ask this Court to create a rule that a prayer for nominal damages can preserve an otherwise moot case. That rule would conflict with Article III's case-or-controversy requirement and with a century of precedent. Nominal damages are a remedy for a past wrong, but they do not supply the ongoing controversy necessary for federal jurisdiction. *Muskrat*, 219 U.S. at 356.

**III. The Voluntary Cessation Doctrine Does Not Save This Case**

The voluntary cessation doctrine can avoid mootness only if the defendant is "free to return to its old ways." *W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 632. But the college's policy change was a formal, permanent revision, not a temporary retreat. *Southern Pac. Terminal Co. v. ICC*, 219 U.S. 498, 514–15 (1911) (moot when "there is no probability that the acts complained of will be repeated"). The new policy superseded the old one, and the college has no incentive to reinstate a policy already contested as unconstitutional. There is no "reasonable expectation" of recurrence. *Liner*, 375 U.S. at 305. Moreover, the burden of proving a reasonable expectation of recurrence falls on the party opposing mootness. *W.T. Grant*, 345 U.S. at 633. Appellants have offered no evidence that the college intends to return to its prior policies. They cannot meet their burden.

**IV. The District Court Properly Denied Leave to Amend**

Appellants sought leave to amend only in a response to a motion to dismiss, not by a properly filed motion. A request for leave to amend embedded within an opposition memorandum is not properly raised. This Court has long required a party seeking amendment to file a separate motion setting forth the substance of the proposed amendment. *See, e.g.,* *Rosenberg v. Gould*, 554 F.3d 962, 967 (11th Cir. 2009) (applying this rule). Even if the request were procedurally proper, amendment would be futile because the complaint alleged no actual injury to support compensatory damages. A plaintiff seeking compensatory damages under § 1983 must plead actual injury, not merely an abstract constitutional violation. *See* *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 255 (1978) (holding that compensatory damages require proof of actual injury). Appellants alleged none. The district court did not abuse its discretion.

**Conclusion**

Because the challenged policies were repealed and no plaintiff remains subject to them, the case is moot. A prayer for nominal damages cannot supply the ongoing controversy required by Article III. The district court properly dismissed the complaint. This Court should affirm.
