**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford's Denial of the Club's Application Did Not Violate the Free Speech Clause.**

Milford's Community Use Policy opens the school after hours for "social, civic and recreational meetings" but expressly withholds the forum from "religious purposes." This is a subject-matter line, drawn without regard to the theology of any particular sect, and it is precisely the kind of even-handed rule that this Court has approved when government declines to make its property available for worship or proselytizing. The Policy does not single out the Good News Club's denomination or favor one faith over another. That distinguishes this case from *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), where Rhode Island conceded "that a religious service of Jehovah's Witnesses is treated differently than a religious service of other sects," a disparity the Court condemned because it "amount[ed] to the state preferring some religious groups over this one." *Id.* Milford's Policy contains no such denominational preference; it excludes religious worship and instruction by any group, on any theology, while allowing groups such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club to use the building for secular civic activities. Nor is this *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), where a permit for a religious meeting was "illegally denied" under an ordinance administered so as "to deny all applications made by religious organizations." Milford's Policy is neutral on its face and in application.

Respondent does not dispute that religious speech generally may not be excluded from a forum simply because it is religious. But a school need not convert its limited civic forum into a vehicle for religious instruction and evangelization directed at young children. As this Court recognized in *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), a State may accommodate a "released time" program only where it "involves neither religious instruction in public school classrooms nor the expenditure of public funds." *Id.* at 306. The arrangement approved in *Zorach* succeeded constitutionally precisely because the religious instruction occurred away from the school building, at the churches' own expense, with no involvement of the schoolhouse itself. Here, by contrast, the Club sought to conduct prayer, proselytizing, and religious "instruction" — Superintendent McGruder's considered characterization, based on lesson plans inviting unsaved children to "believe on the Lord Jesus" — inside the schoolhouse, minutes after the compulsory school day ends, before an audience of six-to-twelve-year-olds. Milford was entitled to decline to make its facility a site for that activity without thereby engaging in viewpoint discrimination; it merely declined to host religious worship at all, for anyone.

**II. Milford's Refusal Was, In Any Event, Justified by Its Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.**

Even if this Court concludes that Milford's Policy operates as a restriction on the Club's speech, that restriction is justified by the compelling interest schools have long been permitted — indeed required — to protect: keeping the public schoolhouse free from the appearance that the government sponsors or endorses religious indoctrination of children. In *Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), this Court struck down a released-time program because "religious teachers, employed by private religious groups, were permitted to come weekly into the school buildings during the regular hours set apart for secular teaching, and then and there... substitute their religious teaching for the secular education." *Id.* at 203. The vice was not merely that instruction was religious, but that it occurred within the schoolhouse, associated in young students' minds with the machinery of compulsory public education. The same concern controls here: elementary-school children invited to a Club meeting held in their own classrooms, immediately after the school day, reasonably could perceive the school itself as endorsing the Club's message that they should "believe on the Lord Jesus... and be saved."

This Court reinforced that concern in *Abington School District v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), holding that Bible-reading exercises conducted under school auspices were "unconstitutional under the Establishment Clause." *Id.* at 203. And in *Board of Education v. Allen*, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), and *Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education*, 281 U.S. 370 (1930), this Court permitted only indirect, secular aid flowing to the *children* rather than to religious institutions themselves — the "child benefit" principle, under which appropriations are constitutional because "the schools... are not the beneficiaries," *Cochran*, 281 U.S. at 375. Milford's schoolhouse, unlike a textbook loan, would itself become the site and instrumentality of proselytizing were the Club permitted to meet there. That direct, on-premises character of the requested use — not incidental secular aid — is what triggers Establishment Clause concern, consistent with the "wall of separation" principle articulated in *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947).

Milford's judgment that the Establishment Clause counseled exclusion of the Club's worship-centered activity from its schoolhouse was reasonable, neutral, and consistent with this Court's teaching that public schools may not become vehicles — even inadvertently — for religious indoctrination of the young. The judgment below should be affirmed.
