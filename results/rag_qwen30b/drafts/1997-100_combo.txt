**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *U.S. Const., amend. V*. This constitutional guarantee is not limited to the trial on guilt or innocence; it extends to any proceeding that subjects a defendant to a second prosecution for the same offense, including those that determine the factual basis for sentencing enhancements. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings—contradicts settled precedent and must be reversed.

The foundational principle that a defendant may not be retried after a conviction is reversed on appeal is rooted in *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662 (1896). There, the Court held that “a defendant may be retried after a conviction has been reversed on appeal, even though the reversal was on the ground of error in the admission of evidence.” Id. at 671. The Court emphasized that “the interest of the public in the punishment of crime” outweighs the defendant’s interest in avoiding retrial, and that “the public has a right to have its laws enforced.” Id. at 672. This principle, however, applies only when the prior conviction is reversed on appeal, not when the defendant is acquitted or when the conviction is set aside due to insufficient evidence. In *Sapir v. United States*, 348 U.S. 373 (1955), the Court made clear that “the guarantee against double jeopardy would prevent a new trial of the old offense” when an appellate court reverses a conviction on the ground of insufficient evidence. Id. at 377. The Court stated: “If the jury had acquitted, there plainly would be double jeopardy to give the Government another go at this citizen.” Id. The same logic applies when a trial court, after a full hearing, finds that the evidence fails to prove a prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. To allow retrial in such circumstances is to permit the State to “have another go” at a charge that was effectively dismissed.

The Court has consistently held that a defendant is placed in jeopardy when the trial court determines the truth of a prior conviction allegation, particularly when that determination is made after a formal hearing. In *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), the Court held that a defendant was placed in double jeopardy when he was retried for first-degree murder after his conviction for second-degree murder was reversed. The Court reasoned that “Green was in direct peril of being convicted and punished” for the same offense—first-degree murder—on the second trial, even though the original jury had rejected that charge. Id. at 197. The Court emphasized that “jeopardy attaches when the jury is empaneled and sworn,” and that “the trial court’s authority to impose a sentence is not a mere formality.” Id. at 196. The same reasoning applies here: when a jury determines the truth of a prior conviction allegation, the defendant is placed in jeopardy for the enhanced sentence, and a reversal of that finding on insufficient evidence bars retrial.

The Court has also recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing determinations when those determinations involve factual findings that are integral to the punishment. In *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711 (1969), the Court held that a defendant may not be sentenced to a harsher term after a retrial following a reversal of his conviction, unless the trial judge provides “adequate reasons” for the increase. Id. at 725. The Court stated that “the possibility of a greater punishment on retrial may deter a defendant from exercising his constitutional right to appeal.” Id. at 725. This concern is equally applicable when the enhanced sentence depends on a prior conviction. If the State may retry a defendant on a prior conviction allegation after a finding of insufficient evidence, it creates a powerful incentive to litigate the issue to the point of conviction, knowing that a reversal on appeal will not bar retrial.

The Court has further held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to proceedings that are “in essence a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence.” In *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause is “fundamental to the American scheme of justice” and enforceable against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. Id. at 796. The Court emphasized that “the right to be free from double jeopardy is not a mere technicality, but a vital protection of individual liberty.” Id. This protection extends to any proceeding that determines the factual basis for punishment, including those involving prior convictions.

The Court has also recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to proceedings that are “in the nature of a trial.” In *Cichos v. Indiana*, 385 U.S. 76 (1966), the Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a charge of involuntary manslaughter after the first jury had returned a verdict of guilty only on reckless homicide. The Court stated that “the first jury’s silence with respect to the manslaughter charge amounted to an acquittal under Indiana law,” and that “his retrial on that charge placed him twice in jeopardy.” Id. at 78. The Court emphasized that “the Fifth Amendment’s prohibition against placing an accused in double jeopardy is applicable to state court prosecutions under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.” Id. at 79. The same principle applies here: when a jury determines the truth of a prior conviction allegation, that determination is not a mere administrative formality, but a factual finding that is essential to the punishment.

Finally, the Court has held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to proceedings that are “in the nature of a trial” and that involve the same kind of factual inquiry as a trial on guilt. In *McDonald v. Massachusetts*, 180 U.S. 311 (1901), the Court upheld a conviction under a habitual criminal statute, noting that the prior convictions were “alleged” and “proved” in a trial-like proceeding. Id. at 315.
