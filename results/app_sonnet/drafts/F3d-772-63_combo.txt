**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Complaint States a Claim for Breach of Contract Because Foley Is an Intended Beneficiary of the Settlement Agreement and Wells Fargo Breached Its Implied Duty of Good Faith.**

Massachusetts has long permitted a person who is not a signatory to a contract to sue upon it when the contracting parties intended to confer a direct benefit on him. In *Mellen v. Whipple*, 67 Mass. (1 Gray) 317 (1854), the Supreme Judicial Court allowed a third party to enforce a promise made for his benefit, rejecting the notion that only the immediate parties to a bargain may sue on it. The rule was refined in *Forbes v. Thorpe*, 209 Mass. 570, 95 N.E. 955 (1911), which held that a stranger to a contract may recover on it where the language and circumstances show that the promisor undertook to render performance directly to him. New York’s seminal decision, *Lawrence v. Fox*, 20 N.Y. 268 (1859), likewise established that a promise made to one party for the benefit of another may be enforced by that other, even absent privity.

Foley’s complaint alleges precisely this kind of arrangement. The Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement was negotiated for the direct benefit of Settlement Class B members like Foley: it required Wells Fargo to consider each such borrower for a HAMP modification, then for MAP2R, before pursuing foreclosure. That obligation ran expressly to the individual borrower, not merely to the class representatives or to the California court. Under *Forbes* and *Mellen*, that is sufficient to make Foley an intended, not merely incidental, beneficiary entitled to enforce the servicing commitments Wells Fargo promised to perform.

Even apart from third-party-beneficiary doctrine, Foley’s complaint alleges a breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing that attaches to every contract. In *Kirke La Shelle Co. v. Paul Armstrong Co.*, 263 N.Y. 79, 87, 188 N.E. 163 (1933), the court explained that “[i]n every contract there is an implied covenant that neither party shall do anything which will have the effect of destroying or injuring the right of the other party to receive the fruits of the contract.” This Court, applying Massachusetts law, recognized the same principle in *Uproar Co. v. National Broadcasting Co.*, 81 F.2d 373 (1st Cir. 1936), holding that a party may not exercise even literal contractual rights in a manner that frustrates the very purpose for which the contract was made. Foley alleges that Wells Fargo strung him along for over a year — promising a HAMP application, delivering it seven months late, then claiming never to have received his completed submission, ignoring his follow-up calls, and steering him toward short sale or deed-in-lieu options while scheduling foreclosure — all while the settlement obligated the bank to give him genuine, sequential consideration for a modification. Those allegations, taken as true, plainly state a breach of the obligation identified in *Kirke La Shelle* and *Uproar Co.*: the destruction of Foley’s contractual right to have his eligibility evaluated before the bank moved against his home.

**II. Foley’s State-Law Claims Are Not Preempted by the Home Owners’ Loan Act.**

Wells Fargo’s preemption defense cannot overcome the settled presumption against preemption of state law in areas of traditional state regulation. As the Supreme Court held in *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947), “we start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress.” Contract enforcement and the regulation of foreclosure procedure — the subjects of Foley’s state common-law and Chapter 93A and Chapter 244 claims — are quintessential exercises of state police power. Nothing in the settlement agreement or in Foley’s claims asks a court to second-guess federal underwriting standards; Foley seeks only to hold Wells Fargo to the bargain it voluntarily struck. Preemption analysis under *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), asks whether state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment of the full purposes of the federal scheme. Enforcing a contractual promise that Wells Fargo itself made to resolve federal Truth-in-Lending litigation does not obstruct federal objectives; it vindicates them.

**III. Foley’s Complaint Satisfies the Governing Pleading Standard.**

The district court held Foley to a standard far more exacting than the law permits. A complaint should not be dismissed “unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts in support of his claim which would entitle him to relief.” *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 45–46 (1957). Foley’s complaint details specific dates, specific representations, and a specific contractual provision Wells Fargo failed to honor. That is more than sufficient to survive dismissal.

Finally, Foley’s statutory claims allege the kind of half-truths that Massachusetts courts have long condemned. As the Supreme Judicial Court observed in *Kannavos v. Annino*, 356 Mass. 42, 48, 247 N.E.2d 708 (1969), “[o]ne half of the truth may obviously be as misleading as a statement wholly false.” Wells Fargo told Foley for seven months that HAMP was his only option, concealed the existence of MAP2R and the settlement’s servicing commitments, and then offered him short sale and deed-in-lieu alternatives instead of the modification review to which he was entitled. Those allegations state a claim under Chapter 93A and Chapter 244 fully consistent with *Kannavos*.

For these reasons, the judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
