**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Relief Because Riddle's Conviction Was Obtained in Violation of Due Process When the Elected Prosecutor Testified as a Witness Against Him While Simultaneously Directing His Capital Prosecution.**

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees that "a fair trial in a fair tribunal is a basic requirement of due process." *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955). That guarantee extends not merely to an impartial judge or jury, but to the structural fairness of the proceeding itself. Here, District Attorney Danny Hill—the chief architect of the State's case—took the witness stand to rebut Riddle's testimony that Hill had promised to seek his execution, and then resumed his role as prosecutor, ultimately permitting his assistant to argue to the jury that Riddle was a liar because "Hill had shown" it to be so. This dual role transformed the prosecutor into both accuser and advocate, a role incompatible with the disinterested pursuit of justice that due process demands.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a prosecutor "is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty whose obligation to govern impartially is as compelling as its obligation to govern at all." *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). The prosecutor's "interest, therefore, in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done." *Id.* When Hill inserted himself as a witness whose credibility became central to the jury's assessment of Riddle's guilt, and then continued to control the prosecution's presentation and argument, the "twofold aim" identified in *Berger* was abandoned in favor of institutional self-vindication.

This conflict mirrors the structural due process concern the Court identified in *Young v. United States ex rel. Vuitton et Fils S.A.*, 481 U.S. 787 (1987), where the appointment of an interested party's counsel to prosecute contempt charges was condemned because a prosecutor with a personal stake in the controversy "cannot be expected to prosecute the contempt with the same vigor and detachment as would an appointed prosecutor unconnected to the case." *Id.* at 804. Hill's personal stake in defending his own credibility—and in convicting the man who had publicly accused him of prosecutorial vindictiveness—created precisely the same danger of partisan zeal displacing prosecutorial neutrality.

The analogy to *Turner v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 466 (1965), is equally instructive. There, the Court found a due process violation where the two key prosecution witnesses served in continuous, intimate association with the jury throughout trial, because "it would be blinking reality not to recognize the extreme prejudice inherent" in such dual functioning. *Id.* at 473. So too here: Hill's dual functioning as sworn witness and unsworn advocate blurred the line between testimony and argument, allowing the jury to credit the prosecution's closing remarks as though they carried the imprimatur of Hill's own sworn assurances.

Finally, under *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637 (1974), the relevant due process inquiry asks "whether the prosecutors' comments so infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process." *Id.* at 643. The assistant prosecutor's closing argument—built expressly on Hill's own testimony to brand Riddle a liar—did precisely that. It converted the prosecutor's institutional authority into a vehicle for personal vindication, a use of governmental power condemned since *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264 (1959), and *Giglio v. United States*, 405 U.S. 150 (1972), both of which recognize that a prosecutor's personal credibility and interest in a witness's testimony demand the strictest safeguards against unfairness.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle an Evidentiary Hearing on His Related Claim That Trial Counsel Was Constitutionally Ineffective for Failing to Seek Hill's Disqualification.**

Riddle's trial counsel never moved to disqualify Hill despite his obvious conflict. Under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), counsel's performance is deficient where it "fell below an objective standard of reasonableness," *id.* at 688, and prejudice exists where "there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel's unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different." *Id.* at 694. No competent capital defense counsel could reasonably permit the case's chief accuser to also testify as a rebuttal witness and thereafter argue credibility to a capital jury.

Because the state courts never developed a factual record on this claim, Riddle was entitled to an evidentiary hearing. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963), holds that a federal habeas court must hold a hearing when "the material facts were not adequately developed at the state-court hearing." Riddle diligently sought such development below; the denial of a hearing therefore cannot be excused under the diligence requirement recognized in *Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 420, 437 (2000).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment below, vacate Riddle's conviction and sentence, and remand for an evidentiary hearing or, alternatively, for issuance of the writ.
