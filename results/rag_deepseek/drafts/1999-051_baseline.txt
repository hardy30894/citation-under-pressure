**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel can serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of a separate constitutional claim. Under this Court’s settled precedent, an ineffective-assistance claim may establish cause for a procedural default only if the petitioner has properly presented that claim to the state courts and it is not itself procedurally defaulted. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 489 (1986). Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed as untimely by the Ohio courts—a procedural default that he has not sought to excuse—that claim cannot provide cause for his default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.

**I. Cause Requires a Factor External to the Defense, and Attorney Error Constitutes Cause Only if the Error Is Itself Not Procedurally Defaulted.**

A habeas petitioner may overcome a procedural default by showing “cause” for the default and “actual prejudice.” *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 485. Cause must be “some objective factor external to the defense” that impeded compliance with the state procedural rule. *Id.* at 488. Attorney error ordinarily is attributable to the petitioner and thus cannot serve as cause unless the error amounts to constitutionally ineffective assistance of counsel. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 753-754 (1991). But even then, the ineffective-assistance claim itself must be “presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489. If the ineffective-assistance claim is itself procedurally defaulted, it cannot serve as cause unless the petitioner can show cause and prejudice for that second default. *Id.*; *see also Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-753 (attorney error that is not constitutionally ineffective does not constitute cause; if it is ineffective, the claim must be preserved).

**II. Carpenter’s Ineffective-Assistance Claim Is Procedurally Defaulted and Therefore Cannot Excuse His Default of the Sufficiency Claim.**

Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed by the Ohio Court of Appeals as untimely. That dismissal rests on an independent and adequate state procedural bar. *See O’Sullivan v. Boerckel*, 526 U.S. 838, 848 (1999) (failure to comply with state filing deadlines constitutes procedural default). Carpenter has never argued—and the courts below did not find—any cause or prejudice to excuse *that* default. Accordingly, the ineffective-assistance claim is procedurally defaulted and cannot be used to establish cause for the default of the sufficiency claim. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489.

The Court of Appeals nevertheless held that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause regardless of its own procedural default. That holding conflicts with *Murray* and *Coleman*. In *Murray*, this Court made clear that the rule requiring presentation of the ineffective-assistance claim as an independent claim “is not a novel one.” *Id.* at 489. It follows that if the petitioner fails to preserve that independent claim in state court, it is “no longer open to him” to use it as cause. *Id.* The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule would allow petitioners to circumvent state procedural bars by simply asserting an ineffective-assistance claim that the state courts have already rejected as untimely. That would undermine the principles of federalism and comity that underlie the procedural default doctrine. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 730-731.

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s Approach Also Conflicts with this Court’s Decision in *Edwards v. Carpenter* (the Instant Case).**

The question presented in this case is precisely whether a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim can excuse the default of another claim. The answer is no. *Murray* and *Coleman* require that the ineffective-assistance claim itself be properly preserved. Allowing a defaulted claim to serve as cause would effectively permit a petitioner to bootstrap his way around two separate procedural defaults without ever showing cause for either. That result is inconsistent with the “cause and prejudice” standard, which is designed to ensure that federal habeas review respects state procedural rules. *See Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255, 262 (1989) (procedural default doctrine prevents federal courts from “unduly interfering with the state’s legitimate interest in enforcing its own procedural rules”).

**CONCLUSION**

Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim is procedurally defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
