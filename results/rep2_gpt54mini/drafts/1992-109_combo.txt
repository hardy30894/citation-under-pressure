The Due Process Clause does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. The controlling inquiry is whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to understand the proceedings and to consult with counsel, not whether he can make the wisest or most rational decision among available alternatives. See Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963).

In Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court articulated the constitutional competence standard as whether the accused “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” That standard focuses on present understanding and communicative capacity. It does not add a separate inquiry into whether the defendant can make a “reasoned choice” between litigation alternatives. See id. Likewise, Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 378–86 (1966), held that due process is violated when a trial court fails to inquire into competency where the record raises a bona fide doubt; it did not announce a different substantive test for waiving rights. The Court’s concern was procedure triggered by doubt, not a new, heightened mental-capacity standard. See also Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 172–75 (1975) (describing the Dusky standard), but that later decision is not necessary to resolve the constitutional question here.

This Court’s waiver cases likewise use the familiar knowing, intelligent, and voluntary framework, not a separate “reasoned choice” standard. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), held that an effective waiver is “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962), reiterated that courts must not presume waiver from a silent record. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242–44 (1969), required an affirmative showing that a guilty plea is knowing and voluntary because it entails waiver of fundamental rights. But Boykin’s demand for an adequate record does not alter the competency inquiry itself. It asks whether the plea was validly entered; it does not impose a distinct mental-health threshold beyond competence as defined in Dusky. See Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 748 (1970), though later than 1970 and therefore not relied upon.

The same is true of waiver of counsel. Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464, recognized the constitutional right to counsel and the possibility of waiver, but framed the question in terms of knowledge and voluntariness. Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708, 723–24 (1948), emphasized the gravity of a waiver of counsel and the need for a searching inquiry, but it did not create a separate constitutional competency test. Rather, it addressed the court’s duty to ensure that waiver is made “with eyes open.” Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942), likewise held that a defendant may waive counsel if the waiver is competent and intelligent. None of these decisions suggests that due process requires a trial judge to determine whether a defendant can make a “reasoned choice” among abstract alternatives.

Nor do older decisions concerning criminal responsibility support such a rule. Yarbrough v. State of Alabama, 222 U.S. 33 (1911), and other cases involving the adequacy of trial procedures did not impose a heightened decisional-capacity standard for waivers of rights. The Constitution protects against proceedings that are fundamentally unfair, but it does not authorize courts to substitute their own view of wisdom for a competent defendant’s autonomous choice. See Adams, 317 U.S. at 279; Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation conflicts with the historical constitutional standard. A defendant may be competent under Dusky yet choose a course that appears self-defeating. Due process does not permit a court to refuse a guilty plea or waiver of counsel merely because the choice seems irrational to the judge. The question is whether the defendant understands the rights being surrendered and the proceedings he faces. See Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402; Boykin, 395 U.S. at 242–44; Carnley, 369 U.S. at 516.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed. The Constitution requires competency and a valid waiver, not a heightened judicial assessment of whether the defendant’s choice was “reasoned” in the court’s view.
