**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires a faithful reading of the OCSLA’s text, its legislative history, and the settled principles of federal preemption and statutory construction as established by this Court before 1970. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that California’s wage and hour laws may be applied on the Outer Continental Shelf as “surrogate federal law” simply because they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. That interpretation ignores the foundational principle that federal law governs the OCS in the absence of a clear congressional directive to the contrary, and that state law may only be assimilated where federal law is silent or inadequate. The correct rule, grounded in pre-1970 precedent, is that state law applies on the OCS only when federal law leaves a “significant void or gap” that state law is intended to fill. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding undermines the supremacy of federal law and misreads the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State ... are declared to be the law of the United States” for purposes of the OCS “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” This language does not create a presumption in favor of state law; rather, it reflects a limited and conditional assimilation of state law. The Supreme Court has long held that “where a federal statute is comprehensive and leaves no room for state legislation, the state law cannot be applied.” *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947). The Court emphasized that “the federal law must be so complete in its coverage that it leaves no room for state regulation.” *Id.* This principle applies with equal force to the OCSLA, which was enacted to establish a uniform federal framework for the development of offshore resources.

The legislative history of the OCSLA confirms that Congress did not intend to permit the automatic application of state law where federal law already governs. In the 1953 debates, Congress explicitly rejected the idea that state law should be applied “in the absence of federal law.” *99 Cong. Rec. 6963* (1953). Senator Morse, a principal sponsor of the bill, stated: “We are not going to allow the States to take over the Outer Continental Shelf. We are going to have a Federal law.” *Id.* The Senate Report explained that the OCSLA was designed to “avoid the confusion and conflict that would result from the application of conflicting state laws” and to “provide a single, uniform system of law.” *S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6* (1953). Congress was concerned that “the application of state law might be inconsistent with the national interest in the development of offshore resources.” *Id.* Thus, the assimilation of state law was not intended to expand state authority but to fill gaps where federal law was insufficient.

This understanding is reinforced by *United States v. Locke*, 321 U.S. 399 (1944), where the Court held that state law could not be applied to regulate activities on federal enclaves unless federal law was “inadequate or silent.” *Id.* at 405. The Court emphasized that “the federal government has exclusive jurisdiction over its own property,” and that “state laws are not to be applied to federal enclaves unless they are necessary to fill a gap in federal law.” *Id.* The OCS, like a federal enclave, is not a state territory but a federally administered zone. The same principle applies: state law may only be applied where federal law is absent or incomplete.

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), correctly interpreted this principle. The court held that “state law may not be applied to the OCS unless there is a significant void or gap in federal law.” *Id.* at 1036. The court reasoned that “the federal law must be so comprehensive that it leaves no room for state legislation.” *Id.* This test was not created in a vacuum; it was derived from the settled doctrine that “where federal law is complete and exclusive, state law cannot be applied.” *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. at 305.

The Court has also long recognized that the mere existence of a federal statute does not preclude the application of state law if the federal law is “inadequate or incomplete.” *United States v. Locke*, 321 U.S. at 405. But where federal law is “comprehensive and leaves no room for state legislation,” state law is excluded. *Id.* The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., is precisely such a comprehensive statute. It establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements that are uniformly applicable across the nation. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218, permits states to enact more protective laws, but only “to the extent that they are not inconsistent with this chapter.” This provision does not authorize the application of state law where federal law already governs. Rather, it allows states to supplement federal law where federal law is less protective. *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. at 305.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision as a general invitation to apply state law whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law is inconsistent with *United States v. Locke*, *Continental Oil*, and *Standard Oil*. It would allow state law to displace federal law in areas where Congress has already established a complete regulatory scheme. This would violate the principle that “federal law is supreme and controls in the absence of a clear congressional intent to the contrary.” *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. at 305.
