**ARGUMENT**

**THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT GIVE RISE TO A CAUSE OF ACTION FOR A “CLASS OF ONE” WHERE THE PLAINTIFF FAILS TO ALLEGE MEMBERSHIP IN A CLASS OR GROUP.**

This Court has consistently held that the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment is designed to protect individuals from invidious discrimination that targets them based on their membership in a particular class. *See, e.g., United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 n.4 (1938) (suggesting that heightened scrutiny applies to legislation directed at “discrete and insular minorities”); *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 112–13 (1949) (Jackson, J., concurring) (noting that the Equal Protection Clause prevents “the singling out” of persons “for burdens that are not imposed on others” only where the classification is arbitrary). The text of the Clause itself—which prohibits denying “to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws”—presupposes that the individual is being treated unequally *because* of some characteristic that defines a group. To read the Clause otherwise would transform it into a general guarantee of fair treatment, divorced from its historic purpose.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision below expansively held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated “solely by a spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386, 387 (7th Cir. 1998). That holding cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents. In *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000) (per curiam), this Court recognized that the “class of one” theory may be viable in limited circumstances. However, the Court expressly declined to decide whether a purely subjective, malicious motive—untethered to any objective classification—can support such a claim. *Id.* at 565. The Court acknowledged that the plaintiff in *Olech* had alleged that she was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated and that there was “no rational basis for the difference in treatment.” *Id.* at 564–65. Critically, the Court did not endorse the idea that bare allegations of ill will or spite, without more, are sufficient.

This Court’s decision in *Engquist v. Oregon Department of Agriculture*, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), further clarifies the limits of the “class of one” theory. In *Engquist*, the Court held that the theory does not apply in the context of public employment because “the class-of-one theory of equal protection—which presupposes that like individuals should be treated alike, and that to treat them differently is to classify them in a way that must be justified at some level of scrutiny—is simply a poor fit in the public employment context.” *Id.* at 605. The reasoning of *Engquist* underscores that the “class of one” theory is not a free-floating cause of action for any person who feels aggrieved by a government decision. Rather, it is a narrow avenue for relief where the plaintiff can demonstrate that the government has acted without any conceivable rational basis—not merely with an improper motive.

To the extent the Seventh Circuit held that a spiteful motive alone can establish an equal protection violation, that holding conflicts with the requirement that a plaintiff show discriminatory treatment based on an impermissible classification. *See Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979) (“‘Discriminatory purpose’ ... implies more than intent as volition or intent as awareness of consequences. It implies that the decisionmaker ... selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part ‘because of,’ not merely ‘in spite of,’ its adverse effects upon an identifiable group.”). Here, respondent Olech did not allege that she was a member of any protected class or that the Village’s demand for a 33-foot easement was based on any group characteristic. Instead, she relied solely on the Village’s alleged animus toward her personally. Such a claim, if allowed, would subject virtually every routine government decision to federal constitutional litigation whenever a disgruntled citizen can allege ill will. That result finds no support in the text, history, or jurisprudence of the Equal Protection Clause.

Accordingly, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed.
