**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Disclosure Provisions of Chapter 93-121 Are Facially Overbroad and Violate the First Amendment.**

A statute regulating expressive and associational activity may be struck down on its face when "a substantial number of its applications are unconstitutional, judged in relation to the statute's plainly legitimate sweep." *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). Lobbying is not a disfavored activity standing outside constitutional protection; it is the direct exercise of "the right of the people . . . to petition the Government for a redress of grievances," U.S. Const. amend. I, exercised through professional intermediaries whose associational and expressive interests merit full protection. *See NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 460-61 (1958) (compelled disclosure of associational activity "may induce members to withdraw . . . and dissuade others from joining").

Compelled disclosure of political activity is subject to "exacting scrutiny," even where, as here, the government asserts an interest in transparency of the legislative process. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 64 (1976). In *Buckley*, the Court sustained federal disclosure requirements only because they were narrowly confined to expenditures made "for the purpose of . . . influencing" federal elections. *Id.* at 79-80. Chapter 93-121 contains no comparable limitation. Section 11.045(3)(a) requires disclosure of "all lobbying expenditures," including a non-exclusive list of categories — "food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging" — that sweeps in innocuous, non-corrupting associational conduct alongside whatever narrow category of expenditures might legitimately concern the State. This is precisely the kind of "shotgun approach" the Court has condemned: where "[l]ess drastic means" would serve the State's interest, the government may not choose a broader remedy that burdens protected activity. *Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960).

This Court should not save the statute by presuming a narrow construction where none has been supplied. In *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), the Court upheld the federal Regulation of Lobbying Act only by construing it narrowly to reach solely those who solicit, collect, or receive funds for the "principal purpose" of influencing legislation through *direct* communication with legislators. *Id.* at 620, 623. Chapter 93-121 admits no such narrowing gloss; its disclosure obligations attach to virtually any expenditure connected to lobbying activity, without regard to whether the expenditure bears any relation to the corrupting influence the State purports to target. Because "a substantial number" of the statute's applications sweep beyond any legitimate governmental interest, *Broadrick*, 413 U.S. at 615, and because the availability of an advisory-opinion procedure does not cure a statute's overbreadth, *see Board of Airport Comm'rs of Los Angeles v. Jews for Jesus, Inc.*, 482 U.S. 569, 574-76 (1987) (invalidating resolution on its face despite its narrow application to appellee, because of its sweeping potential reach), the disclosure provisions cannot stand.

**II. The Contingency-Fee Ban Cannot Be Reconciled with Modern First Amendment Doctrine Governing Compensated Advocacy.**

The district court, and this Court's own opinion below, rested the contingency-fee ban on nineteenth-century contract-law precedents — decisions never framed in First Amendment terms — holding contingent lobbying fees void as against public policy. *See Trist v. Child*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 441, 450-51 (1874) (contracts for contingent compensation to lobby Congress are "void" because they "tend to corrupt or contaminate . . . the legislature"). Those cases predate the modern doctrine recognizing that compensation for advocacy is itself constitutionally protected activity, and they cannot bear the weight this Court places on them.

The Supreme Court has since made clear that laws restricting the compensation of those who engage in petitioning or advocacy activity are subject to searching First Amendment review, not mere rational-basis deference. In *Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414 (1988), the Court struck down a Colorado law banning payment to petition circulators, holding that such a restriction "restricts access to the most fundamental of all liberties" and directly burdens core political speech. *Id.* at 424. And in *Riley v. National Federation of the Blind of North Carolina, Inc.*, 487 U.S. 781 (1988), the Court invalidated a statute capping the fees professional fundraisers could charge, explaining that "the First Amendment protects the right to receive compensation for advocacy activity," and that restrictions on such compensation must satisfy strict, not deferential, scrutiny. *Id.* at 795, 801.

A lobbyist's contingent fee is functionally indistinguishable from the compensation arrangements protected in *Meyer* and *Riley*: it is the mechanism by which advocates are induced to engage in petitioning activity on behalf of citizens who lack the resources or expertise to petition government effectively themselves. *Trist* was decided nearly a century before the Court articulated any First Amendment theory of compensated advocacy, and this Court should not treat it as controlling simply because it has not been formally overruled. The rule that lower courts must follow directly applicable Supreme Court precedent until the Court itself overrules it, *see Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express, Inc.*, 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989), presupposes that the earlier precedent is in fact "directly applicable" to the question presented. *Trist* addressed the common-law enforceability of a private contract; it did not purport to adjudicate — and could not have adjudicated — a First Amendment challenge under a doctrine that did not yet exist. Because no directly controlling precedent forecloses appellant's claim, this Court is free — and obligated — to apply *Meyer* and *Riley* and to hold the contingency-fee ban unconstitutional.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
