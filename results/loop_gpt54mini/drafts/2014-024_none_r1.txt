**Argument**

Section 875(c) does not authorize conviction without proof of mens rea. When Congress codifies a crime and omits any express mental-state requirement, this Court ordinarily presumes that criminal liability still depends on a guilty mind. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-63 (1952). The Court has refused to infer strict liability from statutory silence where the offense carries the stigma and punishment of a serious crime. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-52 (1922); *Baender v. Barnett*, 255 U.S. 224, 226-27 (1921); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). That presumption is especially strong here, because §875(c) criminalizes speech.

This Court’s First Amendment precedents confirm that speech may be punished only when the speaker is shown to have the requisite wrongful purpose. In *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the Court explained that words may be punished when they are used in a manner producing a forbidden result with their “natural and intended effect.” In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that advocacy may be sanctioned only when it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action” and likely to produce such action. And in *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940), the Court recognized that even offensive speech cannot be punished absent a constitutionally sufficient basis. Those cases are inconsistent with an objective-only threat standard that dispenses with proof of what the defendant himself intended.

The Third Circuit’s approach also conflicts with this Court’s treatment of purported threats in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). There, the Court reversed a conviction because the statement, viewed in context, was political hyperbole rather than a true threat. *Id.* *Watts* teaches that context matters, but it does not hold that listener reaction alone is enough. To the contrary, the Court looked to surrounding circumstances to determine whether the speaker meant what he said. An objective standard collapses that inquiry and permits punishment of speech that is joking, exaggerated, theatrical, or venting.

The government argues that an objective test is necessary because true threats are excluded from First Amendment protection. But the cases the government relies on do not support discarding subjective intent. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court rejected punishment of speech merely because it “stirs people to anger” or “invites dispute.” In *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 237 (1963), the Court similarly held that the Constitution does not permit conviction simply because expression provokes hostility. The Constitution demands more than proof that listeners were frightened; it requires proof that the defendant culpably meant to threaten.

The common-law background points the same way. Criminal threat and intimidation offenses historically turned on the speaker’s deliberate communication of menace. See *Rex v. Ireland*, 98 Eng. Rep. 189 (K.B. 1761). American criminal law inherited that principle, and this Court has repeatedly explained that “wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal.” *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 252. A federal statute that reaches speech should not be read to abandon that settled rule absent clear congressional command. *Spurr v. United States*, 174 U.S. 728, 735 (1899).

The need for a subjective standard is particularly acute in a case like this one, where the government seeks to transform rap-style, internet-posted rhetoric into a felony. This Court has warned that constitutional rights cannot depend on arbitrary line-drawing by listeners or prosecutors. See *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 229-30 (1961). As in *Scales*, *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502-10 (1951), and *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 320-31 (1957), the law requires proof of the defendant’s own culpable state of mind before punishment may be imposed for expressive conduct.

Elonis’s posts may have been crude, ugly, and deeply disturbing. But criminal law does not punish bad taste or offensive expression; it punishes culpable intent. Under *Morissette*, *Schenck*, *Brandenburg*, and *Watts*, §875(c) must be construed to require proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. The judgment below should be reversed.
