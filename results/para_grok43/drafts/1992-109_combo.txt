The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that a defendant be competent to stand trial, to plead guilty, or to waive counsel. This Court has long held that the standard of competency is whether the accused "has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). The same formulation governs the validity of a guilty plea. See Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969); Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962).

A waiver of counsel is likewise measured by whether it is made "with an apprehension of the nature of the charges, the statutory offenses included within them, the range of allowable punishments thereunder, possible defenses to the charges and circumstances in mitigation thereof, and all other facts essential to a broad understanding of the whole matter." Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708, 724 (1948); see also Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464-465 (1938); Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942). These inquiries are not distinct from the Dusky test; each demands only that the defendant possess the capacity to understand the proceedings and to make knowing choices among available alternatives.

No authority imposes a more demanding "reasoned choice" threshold. To the contrary, this Court has uniformly applied the Dusky formulation across contexts without differentiation. See Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966) (competency hearing required when evidence raises doubt under the Dusky standard); Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (due process protects the fundamental right to assistance of counsel through a fair hearing). The presumption against waiver of constitutional rights, Johnson, 304 U.S. at 464, is satisfied by ensuring the defendant meets the Dusky criteria; it does not require proof of superior mental functioning.

Because the record demonstrates that Moran satisfied the Dusky standard at the time of his pleas and waiver, the state courts' acceptance of those acts comported with due process. The Ninth Circuit's imposition of a heightened standard finds no support in the decisions of this Court.
