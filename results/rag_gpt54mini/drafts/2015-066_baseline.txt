**ARGUMENT**

Section 117(a) permits the government to turn prior tribal-court convictions into an element of a new federal felony. That use of uncounseled convictions violates the Sixth Amendment because it does not merely increase punishment; it is part of the government’s proof of guilt. The Constitution requires more. “Prosecutors must prove each element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt,” In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), and a conviction that was obtained without counsel cannot be treated as reliable enough to establish an element of a felony charge. See Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963); Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967).

1. **Nichols does not control because this case concerns guilt, not sentencing.**  
The government’s reliance on Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced. Nichols approved use of a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction at sentencing, where the Court understood the proceeding to be governed by a different burden and different procedural protections. 511 U.S. at 748. Nichols expressly rested on Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), which held that the Sixth Amendment does not require counsel when no prison term is imposed. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-47. But this case is not about a sentencing factor. Section 117(a) makes the prior convictions an express element of a separate federal offense. Congress did not authorize a mere enhancement; it defined a felony only if the defendant “has a final conviction on at least 2 separate prior occasions.” 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). Winship, not Nichols, governs. See Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196, 201 (1948).

2. **Burgett forbids using uncounseled convictions as substantive proof in a later criminal case.**  
The Constitution has long prohibited the government from using a constitutionally infirm conviction to prove guilt in a later prosecution. In Burgett, the Court held that an uncounseled felony conviction “could not be used” to enhance punishment under a recidivist statute. 389 U.S. at 115. That principle rests on the unreliability of the prior conviction and the impossibility of allowing a conviction obtained in violation of the right to counsel to “be used to support guilt or enhance punishment.” Id. Nichols did not overrule Burgett; it distinguished sentencing use of a valid uncounseled misdemeanor conviction where no prison term had been imposed. Nichols, 511 U.S. at 746-47. Section 117(a) goes further than the statutes in Nichols and Burgett by using the prior conviction as a predicate element of guilt itself. That is a constitutional difference, not a formal one.

3. **Alabama v. Shelton and Baldasar confirm the Sixth Amendment problem.**  
In Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment bars a suspended sentence that may result in imprisonment when the defendant was not afforded counsel. 535 U.S. at 662. The Court explained that a conviction cannot be converted into a prison-eligible judgment without counsel at the critical stage. Id. Shelton underscores that the Sixth Amendment protects against the use of uncounseled proceedings to generate imprisonment. Likewise, Baldasar v. Illinois, 446 U.S. 222 (1980), rejected the use of an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction to transform a later misdemeanor into a felony with a prison term. Id. at 224. Although Nichols later rejected Baldasar’s reasoning for sentencing purposes, Nichols did not authorize using such convictions as elements of a felony prosecution. See Nichols, 511 U.S. at 748.

4. **Tribal-court validity does not answer the federal constitutional question.**  
It is true that the Sixth Amendment does not directly bind tribal courts. See United States v. Wheeler, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978); Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). But the issue here is not whether the tribal convictions were valid in tribal court; it is whether Congress may use those convictions, uncounseled and imprisonment-eligible if tried in federal court, as the predicate facts establishing a new federal felony. The answer is no when the conviction is used to prove guilt. Federal courts have long insisted on reliability when prior convictions are used in criminal adjudication. See Parke v. Raley, 506 U.S. 20, 29-34 (1993); Johnson v. Mississippi, 486 U.S. 578, 590 (1988).

5. **Section 117(a) should be construed, if possible, to avoid the constitutional problem.**  
Because the statute can be read to require constitutionally valid predicates, the Court should adopt that construction. See Cole, 333 U.S. at 201. Otherwise, § 117(a) would permit the government to obtain a felony conviction without the safeguard that the Sixth Amendment treats as fundamental. That result cannot be squared with Gideon, Burgett, Winship, or Shelton.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
