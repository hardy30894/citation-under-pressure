**ARGUMENT**

I. **JOHNSON’S RULE ANNOUNCING THE UNCONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE ACCA’S RESIDUAL CLAUSE APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure ordinarily applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. See, e.g., Griffith v. Kentucky, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987) (“a new rule for the conduct of criminal prosecutions is to be applied retroactively to all cases ... pending on direct or collateral review”); Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989) (plurality opinion) (“a new rule of criminal procedure ... will not be applied retroactively to cases on collateral review unless ...”). Johnson v. United States, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), announced such a new rule: the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague in violation of the Due Process Clause. Id. at 2556–57. Because Johnson’s rule is substantive in nature—it alters the legal framework governing the imposition of punishment—it applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. See Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004) (a rule is substantive when it “narrows the scope of a criminal statute’s ... penalty provisions”); see also Welch v. United States, 136 S. Ct. 1257, 1265 (2016) (holding that Johnson applies retroactively to cases on collateral review).

The Eleventh Circuit erred by failing to apply Johnson retroactively. The court below reasoned that Johnson did not announce a new rule because the Supreme Court had previously expressed “serious misgivings” about the residual clause in James v. United States, 550 U.S. 192, 210 n.6 (2007), and Begay v. United States, 553 U.S. 137, 144 (2008). See United States v. Welch, 805 F.3d 1258, 1262 (11th Cir. 2015). But the Supreme Court has made clear that a rule is new unless “dictated by precedent existing at the time the defendant’s conviction became final.” Welch, 136 S. Ct. at 1265 (quoting Teague, 489 U.S. at 301). The Court’s “misgivings” in James and Begay did not compel the conclusion that the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague; they merely signaled the Court’s discomfort with the clause’s application. See id. (“[T]he fact that a court previously expressed concern about a rule’s constitutionality does not mean the rule was previously held unconstitutional.”). Johnson’s holding that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague was therefore a new rule.

II. **WELCH’S 1996 FLORIDA ROBBERY CONVICTION DOES NOT QUALIFY AS A “VIOLENT FELONY” UNDER THE ACCA’S ELEMENTS CLAUSE**

Even under the categorical approach, Welch’s 1996 Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s elements clause. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as one that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). The Supreme Court has interpreted this clause to require “force that is violent in nature,” i.e., force capable of causing physical pain or injury. Johnson, 135 S. Ct. at 2557 (citing Leocal v. Ashcroft, 543 U.S. 1, 11 (2004)). Florida’s robbery statute at the time of Welch’s conviction criminalized “force, violence, assault, or putting in fear,” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996), but Florida courts had repeatedly held that mere snatching—such as grabbing cash from a victim’s hand—could suffice for a robbery conviction. See, e.g., Andre v. State, 431 So. 2d 1042, 1043 (Fla. 5th Dist. Ct. App. 1983) (“the act of ‘snatching’ ... money from another’s hands is force and that force will support a robbery conviction”); Larkins v. State, 476 So. 2d 1383, 1385 (Fla. 1st Dist. Ct. App. 1985) (same). This “slight force” was insufficient to satisfy the ACCA’s elements clause. See Johnson, 135 S. Ct. at 2557 (holding that “slight” force is not enough).

The Eleventh Circuit erred by relying on post-conviction Florida precedent that narrowed the scope of the robbery statute. See Welch, 805 F.3d at 1263–64. The ACCA’s elements clause looks to the “elements of the state offense as construed by the state courts at the time of the defendant’s conviction.” United States v. Archer, 531 F.3d 1347, 1350 (11th Cir. 2008) (emphasis added). At the time of Welch’s 1996 conviction, Florida courts had not limited robbery to conduct involving violent force. See, e.g., Andre, 431 So. 2d at 1043; Larkins, 476 So. 2d at 1385. The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion conflicts with this Court’s instruction to apply the categorical approach as of the time of the defendant’s conviction. See Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 260 (2013) (“[T]he question is what the state conviction necessarily involved, not what it happened to involve in a particular case.”).

III. **WELCH’S SENTENCE VIOLATES THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE BECAUSE THE ACCA’S RESIDUAL CLAUSE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

The residual clause of the ACCA, which criminalizes conduct that “otherwise involves ... a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” 18 U.S.C.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague. Johnson, 135 S. Ct. at 2556–57. The clause fails to provide fair warning of the conduct it punishes and invites arbitrary enforcement. Id. at 2557. The Eleventh Circuit erred by failing to recognize that Johnson’s holding invalidates Welch’s sentence. See Welch, 805 F.3d at 1262 (holding that Johnson did not apply retroactively). Because Johnson’s rule applies retroactively, Welch’s sentence—based in part on the residual clause—must be vacated.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment and remand with instructions to vacate Welch’s sentence.
