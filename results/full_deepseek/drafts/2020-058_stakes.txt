## ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an “admission” for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. The text, context, and purpose of the statute all compel the opposite conclusion.

### I. The Plain Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Establishes that TPS Recipients Are “Admitted”

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” The INA defines “admission” and “admitted” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When the government grants TPS, it necessarily inspects and authorizes the alien’s presence. The process of obtaining TPS—including application, biometrics, and adjudication—constitutes inspection and authorization. *See Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (“By the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.”). The Third Circuit’s insistence that “lawful status” and “admission” are distinct concepts ignores that the statute treats them as intertwined for TPS recipients. *See Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 553 (6th Cir. 2013) (holding that § 1254a(f)(4) “plainly indicates that TPS recipients are considered to have been admitted as nonimmigrants”).

### II. The Statutory Context Confirms that TPS Satisfies § 1255(a)’s Threshold Requirement

The Third Circuit relied on the fact that Congress created express exceptions to the admission requirement for special immigrants and visa applicants—but not for TPS recipients. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i). That argument cuts the wrong way. Congress did not need to create a separate exception for TPS recipients because § 1254a(f)(4) already equates TPS with admission. The canon *expressio unius* does not apply where the statute already provides coverage. *See I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (canon applies only when “Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act”). Here, Congress addressed TPS separately in § 1254a, and that provision supplies the necessary admission.

Moreover, § 1255(k) refers to “lawful admission” and “lawful status” as distinct concepts, but TPS satisfies both. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(2)(A) (requiring “lawful admission” and no failure to maintain “lawful status”). The Third Circuit’s interpretation would render § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous: if TPS recipients are not “admitted,” then the provision’s explicit reference to “lawful status” for adjustment purposes has no effect. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (statute should be construed so that no part is superfluous).

### III. The Purpose of TPS Supports Petitioners’ Interpretation

TPS was designed to shield aliens from removal during temporary crises in their home countries. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s rule forces TPS recipients who entered without inspection to either remain in TPS limbo indefinitely or leave the United States and re-enter lawfully—often to dangerous conditions. That outcome undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964 (noting that requiring TPS recipients to depart and re-enter “would undermine the purpose of TPS”). The Third Circuit’s reliance on § 1254a(h)—which requires a supermajority for Congress to adjust TPS recipients’ status directly—is misplaced. That provision addresses congressional action, not the general adjustment-of-status process. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h)(2). Nothing in § 1254a(h) prohibits an individual TPS recipient from qualifying for adjustment under § 1255.

### IV. The Third Circuit’s Distinction Between Admission and Status Is Artificially Narrow

The Third Circuit distinguished between “admission” as a physical event and “status” as a legal classification. *See Sanchez v. Mayorkas*, 987 F.3d 295, 304 (3d Cir. 2020). But the INA does not rigidly separate the two. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) (definition of “admission” includes “authorization”). The Ninth and Sixth Circuits have correctly recognized that a grant of TPS, like a grant of a visa, involves both inspection and authorization. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960; *Flores*, 718 F.3d at 553. The Third Circuit’s approach would also create an anomaly: TPS recipients who entered lawfully can adjust status, but those who entered illegally—despite being in identical lawful status—cannot. Congress could not have intended such a distinction without clear statutory language.

### Conclusion

The Third Circuit’s judgment should be reversed. A grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255, entitling petitioners to seek adjustment of status.
