**ARGUMENT**

The Texas courts possessed subject-matter jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. Federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) extends only to cases in which federal patent law creates the cause of action or the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913). A suit does not arise under the patent laws merely because it involves a patent or requires construction of patent statutes as an incident to decision of a state-created right. *Wilson v. Sandford*, 51 U.S. (10 How.) 99, 101 (1850); *Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 282, 287 (1902).

The well-pleaded complaint rule confines the inquiry to the face of the plaintiff's pleading. *Tennessee v. Union & Planters' Bank*, 152 U.S. 454, 460 (1894); *Gold-Washing & Water Co. v. Keyes*, 96 U.S. 199, 203 (1877). Minton's malpractice petition asserts only Texas negligence and causation elements; it does not plead a patent claim created by federal law. Any patent-law question arises solely as a defense or as part of the "case within a case" proof of causation, matters the Court has long held insufficient to confer arising-under jurisdiction. *The Mayor v. Cooper*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 247, 254 (1868); *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

Federal jurisdiction likewise requires that the federal issue be substantial and essential to the outcome. *Gully v. First Nat'l Bank*, 299 U.S. 109, 112-13 (1936); *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569. Here the experimental-use exception appears only as one possible theory of causation in a common-law tort action. Its resolution would neither create nor destroy any patent right and would bind no future litigant in a patent infringement suit. Such an incidental federal question does not satisfy the substantiality requirement. *Osborn v. Bank of the United States*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 822 (1824) (federal ingredient must be "an original ingredient" in the cause of action).

Congress has not displaced the historic presumption that state courts are competent to adjudicate state-law claims that merely touch upon federal patent law. *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 396 (1821). Allowing federal jurisdiction whenever a malpractice plaintiff must prove what would have happened in a hypothetical patent case would expand § 1338(a) far beyond its text and upset the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities long recognized by this Court. The judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should therefore be reversed.
