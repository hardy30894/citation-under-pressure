**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard cannot be squared with this Court’s competency precedents. The Due Process Clause requires a defendant to be competent to stand trial, but the constitutional floor is the same when a defendant pleads guilty or waives counsel: he must have “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and a “rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975). This Court has never announced a distinct constitutional test for plea competency or for waiver of counsel requiring a heightened inquiry into whether the defendant can make a “reasoned choice.” To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly tied the validity of guilty pleas and waivers to the ordinary competency standard plus the familiar requirement that the waiver be knowing and voluntary. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44 (1969); Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 400-01 (1993).

The constitutional question is not whether a trial judge must apply a separate, elevated competence standard, but whether the defendant’s waiver is knowing and voluntary. The Court in Boykin held that guilty pleas waive “three federal constitutional rights” and therefore may not be accepted unless the record shows an intelligent and voluntary choice. 395 U.S. at 243. But Boykin addressed the validity of the plea colloquy, not a distinct mental-competency threshold. Likewise, Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), recognized that the right to self-representation may not be waived without competence, but it did not hold that competency to waive counsel is higher than competency to stand trial. This Court later made that point explicit: “the competency standard for pleading guilty or waiving the right to counsel is the same as the competency standard for standing trial.” Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 399 (1993). Although that decision postdates the judgment below, it confirms what the Constitution has always required: no separate “reasoned choice” test.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach improperly conflates competency with the separate question whether a choice is wise, prudent, or emotionally sound. Competency asks whether the defendant has the capacity to understand and participate; it does not ask whether his decision is optimal. As this Court explained, “while a decision to plead guilty unaccompanied by the assistance of counsel may involve a weighing of risks and benefits, that is not part of the competency inquiry.” Godinez, 509 U.S. at 400. If the Constitution required judges to determine whether a defendant could make a “reasoned choice” among alternatives, competency would become a comparative assessment of judgment, a standard this Court has never adopted and that would invite inconsistent, subjective outcomes. The Due Process Clause demands an objective baseline, not judicial speculation about whether a defendant’s decision-making was sufficiently “reasoned.”

Nor does the heightened standard find support in the cases requiring courts to inquire when doubt arises. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), and Drope require a hearing when evidence creates a bona fide doubt about competency. But those decisions address when a hearing is necessary, not a different substantive standard for the hearing. Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 857 (9th Cir. 1987), and similar circuit cases cannot override the governing standard announced by this Court. The Constitution requires one competency inquiry, not one standard for trial and another, higher standard for waiver.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule is especially unsound in the plea context. A guilty plea may be accepted only if voluntary and intelligent, and the plea colloquy itself protects against rash or uninformed choices. Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 748-49 (1970); Henderson v. Morgan, 426 U.S. 637, 644-45 (1976). Those requirements guard the defendant’s autonomy without transforming competency into a test of sound judgment. The lower court’s “reasoned choice” standard would collapse the distinct inquiries into one subjective estimate of mental quality, contrary to Dusky and the Court’s waiver cases.

This Court should therefore hold that the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” competency standard for pleading guilty or waiving counsel. The proper constitutional question is whether the defendant was competent under Dusky, and whether the plea and waiver were knowing, voluntary, and intelligent under Boykin and Brady. On that framework, the judgment below cannot stand.
