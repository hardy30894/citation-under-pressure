The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has construed this language to extend federal jurisdiction to cases where a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988).

The Grable test, as articulated by the Supreme Court, provides a framework for determining whether a state-law claim arises under federal jurisdiction. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). To satisfy this test, four conditions must be met: (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir.2008) (interpreting Grable).

Minton's legal malpractice claim meets all four conditions. First, resolving the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) is necessary to determine whether Gunn's failure to plead this exception proximately caused Minton's losses. See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex.2004) (requiring proof of causation in a legal malpractice claim). The experimental use exception is a critical issue in Minton's malpractice claim, as it directly affects the validity of the '643 patent and, consequently, the outcome of the underlying patent infringement litigation.

Second, the applicability of the experimental use exception is actually disputed between the parties. Minton argues that the TEXCEN lease was primarily for experimental purposes, while Gunn contends that it was commercial.

Third, the federal issue is substantial because it involves the interpretation of federal patent law, specifically the on-sale bar and the experimental use exception. The Supreme Court has recognized that patent law is a complex and technical area that requires uniform interpretation. See, e.g., Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc., 525 U.S. 55 (1998) (interpreting the on-sale bar).

Lastly, federal jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. The Federal Circuit has recognized that patent law issues are best resolved in federal court, where expertise and uniformity can be ensured. See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed.Cir.2007).

In conclusion, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim arises under exclusive federal patent law jurisdiction because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. The court of appeals erred in affirming the trial court's judgment, and this Court should reverse and dismiss the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
