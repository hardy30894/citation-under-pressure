The plain language of 28 USC section 1407(a) mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's clear directive is reinforced by the principle that courts must adhere to the plain meaning of a statute, as established in Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). 

The District of Arizona's invocation of section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial contravenes the statutory scheme. Section 1407(a) governs the transfer of cases for pretrial proceedings, and its remand provision is not subject to override by a transferee court's section 1404(a) transfer order. The Supreme Court has long recognized that specific statutes control over more general ones, as seen in United States v. Chase, 135 U.S. 255, 260 (1890). Here, section 1407(a) is the specific statute governing multidistrict litigation, and its provisions should take precedence over the more general transfer provision in section 1404(a).

Furthermore, the legislative history and purpose of section 1407(a) support a strict interpretation of its remand requirement. As the court noted in In re Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 489 (J.P.M.L. 1968), the statute aims to facilitate efficient pretrial proceedings while preserving the parties' rights to trial in their chosen forum. Allowing a transferee court to retain a case for trial under section 1404(a) would undermine this purpose, as observed in Foster v. United States, 80 U.S. 506, 507 (1871), where the Court emphasized the importance of adhering to statutory requirements.

The Ninth Circuit's decision below, as well as the Second Circuit's earlier holding in Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and the Third Circuit's decision in In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation, 685 F.2d 810 (3rd Cir. 1982), are not controlling here. Instead, we rely on the reasoning in cases such as United States v. Dickson, 40 U.S. 141, 161-162 (1841), which emphasizes the need to follow the plain language of a statute, and United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. 76, 95-96 (1820), which cautions against judicially creating exceptions to statutory mandates.

Additionally, the principle that a specific statute governs over a more general one, as seen in Rodgers v. United States, 185 U.S. 83, 87-89 (1902), and the rule that courts should not infer exceptions to a statutory rule without clear legislative intent, as stated in Commissioner v. Acker, 361 U.S. 87, 91-92 (1959), and United States v. Oregon, 366 U.S. 643, 648 (1961), further support the conclusion that the District of Arizona erred in assigning the case to itself for trial.

The statutory text, legislative history, and relevant case law, including Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949), and Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955), all confirm that the District of Arizona's actions were contrary to law. We urge the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's judgment and direct the District of Arizona to remand the case to the Northern District of Illinois, as mandated by section 1407(a).
