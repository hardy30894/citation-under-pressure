ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court's ultimate determination that reasonable suspicion existed de novo, while accepting subsidiary findings of fact unless clearly erroneous. Ornelas v. United States, 517 U.S. 690, 699 (1996) (holding that "determinations of reasonable suspicion and probable cause should be reviewed de novo on appeal"); United States v. Kimball, 25 F.3d 1, 3 (1st Cir. 1994).

II. The Pat-Frisk of Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment Because the Officers Lacked Reasonable, Particularized Suspicion that He Was Armed and Dangerous.

A. Terry requires articulable, particularized suspicion of danger.

An officer conducting a lawful investigatory stop may frisk a suspect for weapons only "where he has reason to believe that he is dealing with an armed and dangerous individual, regardless of whether he has probable cause to arrest the individual for a crime." Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). The officer must point to "specific and articulable facts" that, together with rational inferences, justify the intrusion. Id. at 21. Terry's companion case confirms that suspicion cannot rest on generalized or associational grounds; it must be particular to the person searched. Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 62-64 (1968) (invalidating a frisk where the officer articulated no facts suggesting the defendant, who merely spoke with known addicts, was armed).

B. Authority to order a passenger out of the car does not establish authority to frisk him.

Police "may order passengers to get out of the car pending completion of a stop." Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408, 415 (1997); see also Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106, 111 n.6 (1977) (same, for drivers). But that authority rests on the generalized officer-safety concerns inherent in every traffic stop, not on any case-specific showing that a particular occupant is armed. Wilson, 519 U.S. at 413-14. The exit order and the frisk remain analytically distinct: Terry still demands an independent, particularized basis for believing the removed passenger is armed and dangerous before officers may lay hands on him. Terry, 392 U.S. at 27. The district court's ruling collapsed this distinction, treating Soares's removal from the car as if it answered the frisk question. It did not.

C. Presence in a high-crime area and ambiguous pre-stop movements do not supply particularized suspicion.

Officer Reid called out "furtive movements" because Soares and the driver moved inside the car before the stop had even begun - conduct equally consistent with reaching for a seatbelt or a wallet. The Supreme Court has cautioned that a high-crime location, by itself, does not transform innocuous conduct into a basis for suspicion; something more, tied to the individual, is required. Illinois v. Wardlow, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000) (finding suspicion only where unprovoked flight was combined with presence in a high-crime area). Soares did not flee, reach for a weapon, or make any movement officers connected to a weapon. Reasonable suspicion to frisk must be particularized to the individual searched, not borrowed wholesale from the neighborhood or the vehicle's other occupants. Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266, 271-72 (2000) (requiring that suspicion be reliable "in its assertion of illegality," not merely in identifying a person).

D. Soares's agitation and profanity after being ordered still are not evidence of a weapon.

By the time Reid summoned the back-up officers, the only new facts were that Soares argued with police, accused them of racial profiling, and rocked back and forth while moving his hands. Reid did not testify to seeing a weapon, a bulge, or a reach toward the waistband; he testified only that he "got a little uneasy" and "felt really uneasy about ... the way [Soares] was behaving." Generalized unease about demeanor - as opposed to specific, articulable facts pointing to a weapon - is exactly what Terry and Sibron forbid as a basis for a frisk. Terry, 392 U.S. at 27; Sibron, 392 U.S. at 64. A passenger's belligerence or protest of the stop's legitimacy is not evidence of danger; to hold otherwise would let officers frisk any citizen who argues with them, resurrecting the "inarticulate hunch[]" Terry rejected as a constitutional predicate for a search. Terry, 392 U.S. at 22.

E. The cumulative record does not sustain the frisk.

Taken together, the ambiguous pre-stop hand movements, the high-crime location, and Soares's verbal noncompliance amount to nothing more than the discomfort any officer might feel when a citizen protests a seizure. None of these facts, singly or cumulatively, gave rise to reasonable, individualized suspicion that Soares - as distinct from either of his companions - was armed. Because the frisk exceeded what Terry permits, the handgun seized as its direct product must be suppressed as the fruit of an unconstitutional search. See Kimball, 25 F.3d at 3; United States v. Brown, 510 F.3d 57, 61 n.1 (1st Cir. 2007).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress and remand with instructions to vacate his conviction.
