ARGUMENT

I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE LAW IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME, PLACE, AND MANNER REGULATION THAT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIRST AMENDMENT.

The Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. As the First Circuit correctly held, the law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication. See McCullen v. Coakley, 708 F.3d 1, 6-7 (1st Cir. 2013).

The buffer zone law is content-neutral because it does not regulate speech based on its content. The law applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint or message. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719-20 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute). The law's exemptions for certain individuals, such as clinic employees and law enforcement officers, are based on their status or function, not their viewpoint. See McCullen, 708 F.3d at 10-11.

The law is also narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests. The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring public safety and protecting patients' access to healthcare facilities. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 715. The buffer zone law achieves these interests by preventing congestion and obstruction around clinic entrances. See McCullen, 708 F.3d at 8-9.

Furthermore, the law leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication. The petitioners can still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signs, and pray outside the buffer zones. See McCullen, 708 F.3d at 12-13. While the buffer zones may limit the petitioners' ability to engage in "sidewalk counseling" within a few feet of clinic entrances, they can still communicate their message effectively from outside the buffer zones. See id.

II. THE FIRST CIRCUIT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE SUPREME COURT'S RULING IN HILL V. COLORADO.

The petitioners argue that the First Circuit erred in relying on Hill v. Colorado, which upheld a similar buffer zone law in Colorado. However, the First Circuit correctly applied Hill to the Massachusetts law. See McCullen, 708 F.3d at 6-7.

In Hill, the Supreme Court held that a content-neutral buffer zone law around healthcare facilities did not violate the First Amendment. See 530 U.S. at 719-20. The Court emphasized that the law was narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests and left open adequate alternative channels of communication. See id. at 725-26.

The Massachusetts law is similar to the Colorado law in Hill. Both laws create buffer zones around healthcare facilities to protect patients' access and public safety. See McCullen, 708 F.3d at 8-9. Both laws are content-neutral and leave open alternative channels of communication. See id. at 10-13.

The petitioners argue that Hill should be limited or overruled, but there is no compelling reason to do so. Hill remains good law, and the First Circuit correctly applied its principles to the Massachusetts law. See McCullen, 708 F.3d at 6-7.

CONCLUSION

The Massachusetts buffer zone law is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that does not violate the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law under Hill v. Colorado, and there is no reason to disturb that decision. The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
