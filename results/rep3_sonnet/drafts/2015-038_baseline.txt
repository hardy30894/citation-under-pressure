# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP NPS OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that "[o]nly those lands within the boundaries of a conservation system unit which are public lands (as such term is defined in this Act) shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." This provision addresses the *composition* of conservation system units (CSUs)—it determines which lands count as "public lands" within a unit for purposes of ANILCA's land-conveyance and management provisions. It does not, by its terms, purport to withdraw NPS's independent regulatory authority over activities occurring within CSU boundaries, regardless of the ownership of the underlying land or water.

This Court has long recognized that statutory interpretation begins and ends with the plain text where that text is unambiguous. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). Section 103(c) says nothing about NPS's regulatory jurisdiction; it speaks only to land classification. Sturgeon's reading would require this Court to insert words into the statute that Congress did not write—transforming a definitional provision about "public lands" into a broad ouster of federal regulatory authority over navigable waters, non-federal inholdings, and Native corporation lands. This Court "must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there." *Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992).

The hovercraft regulation Sturgeon challenges, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not a regulation of "public lands" as such. It is a generally applicable regulation governing conduct within National Park System units, promulgated pursuant to NPS's organic authority under 54 U.S.C. § 100751(a) (formerly 16 U.S.C. § 3) to "prescribe such regulations as [the Secretary] considers necessary or proper for the use and management of System units." Nothing in § 103(c)'s narrow definitional language displaces this independent grant of regulatory authority.

## II. THE STATUTORY STRUCTURE AND SAVINGS CLAUSES CONFIRM THAT ANILCA PRESERVED, RATHER THAN DIMINISHED, PRE-EXISTING FEDERAL REGULATORY AUTHORITY

ANILCA's own savings provisions foreclose Sturgeon's interpretation. Section 1319 of ANILCA states that "[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto." 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (emphasis added). This is precisely the kind of "specifically set forth" repeal Congress knows how to draft when it intends one—and it did not do so with respect to NPS's general regulatory authority over conduct within CSUs.

Moreover, as a matter of legislative history, when Congress considered provisions that would have altered agency regulatory jurisdiction under ANILCA, it did so explicitly and was met with direct objection when overreach was perceived. The Secretary of Transportation formally objected to an early draft that would have authorized the Secretary of the Interior to "promulgate and enforce boating regulations which relate to construction, performance, and equipment standards," noting that this function had been delegated to the Coast Guard. H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 24 (1976), *reprinted in* 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4290, 4310. That Congress engaged in this granular, agency-by-agency calibration of regulatory authority elsewhere in the legislative process—while leaving § 103(c) as a simple land-classification provision—confirms that Congress did not intend § 103(c) to accomplish the sweeping jurisdictional divestiture Sturgeon proposes.

## III. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT'S NAVIGATIONAL SERVITUDE AND OTHER SOURCES OF AUTHORITY SURVIVE ANY STATE OWNERSHIP OF SUBMERGED LANDS

Even assuming, as the Ninth Circuit did for purposes of decision, that title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River passed to Alaska under the Submerged Lands Act, that conveyance of title does not extinguish independent federal regulatory authority over the waters above them. As Justice Scalia observed in the related context of Glacier Bay, "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters . . . by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75, 130 (2005) (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). Ownership of the riverbed is not coextensive with regulatory supremacy over activity in the watercourse.

This principle is not novel. The navigational servitude has long permitted the federal government to regulate activity on navigable waters notwithstanding state ownership of the submerged lands beneath them. *See, e.g., United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967) ("[T]he Government's right to control navigation for the promotion of commerce is a dominant one which can be asserted to the exclusion of any competing or conflicting one."). Thus, § 103(c)'s classification of submerged lands as non-"public lands" for boundary purposes cannot, standing alone, be read to divest NPS of authority that would otherwise independently exist by virtue of the federal navigational servitude and the Property Clause.

## IV. THIS COURT SHOULD DEFER TO NPS'S REASONABLE, LONGSTANDING INTERPRETATION OF ITS OWN REGULATORY AUTHORITY

To the extent any ambiguity remains, deference to the agency's interpretation is warranted under *Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 842-43. NPS's position—that its general regulations, including the hovercraft ban, apply uniformly throughout CSUs regardless of subsurface ownership—represents a reasonable construction of an ambiguous statutory scheme addressing complex questions of land status in Alaska. Any inconsistency in agency position over time does not defeat deference. *Nat'l Cable & Telecomms. Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005) ("Agency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the *Chevron* framework.").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
