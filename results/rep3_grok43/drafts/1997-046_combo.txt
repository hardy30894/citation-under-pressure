The petitioner contends that the decision below erred in suggesting that any due process scrutiny attaches to clemency and in remanding for further proceedings on the Fifth Amendment claim.

Clemency is an executive function resting in the unfettered discretion of the governor. Ex parte Garland, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866) (pardon power “is unlimited”). It is “an act of grace” that “is rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). The Constitution confers no right to receive clemency, and the Due Process Clause therefore supplies no basis for judicial superintendence of the procedures by which the executive chooses to exercise or withhold that power. Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 311 (1855); see also Murray’s Lessee v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 272, 284 (1856) (due process protects only interests “recognized and protected by the law”).

The same principle precludes recognition of any protected life or liberty interest arising from the clemency process itself. Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516, 535 (1884) (due process does not require “any particular form” of proceeding outside the judicial sphere). Because clemency lies wholly outside the adjudicative process, the state’s decision to afford an inmate an opportunity to be heard cannot convert an act of grace into a constitutionally protected entitlement. Barron v. Mayor of Baltimore, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 243, 250-51 (1833).

The Fifth Amendment claim likewise fails. The interview is entirely optional; an inmate who elects to participate remains free to decline any question. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 91 (1908) (“The prohibition of compulsion . . . is not a prohibition of comment”). No penalty attaches to silence, and therefore no compulsion exists. Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 596 (1896); Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 U.S. 547, 562 (1892). Voluntary statements given without governmental coercion have never been held to violate the privilege. Bram v. United States, 168 U.S. 532, 542 (1897).

Because the regulation imposes no requirement that an inmate waive the privilege or answer any question, the Fifth Amendment is not implicated. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed insofar as it vacated the district court’s dismissal of the complaint.
