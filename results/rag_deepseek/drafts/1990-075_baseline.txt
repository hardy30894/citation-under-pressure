**ARGUMENT**

**I. Congress’s Delegation of Temporary Scheduling Authority to the Attorney General Satisfies the Intelligible Principle Test**

The nondelegation doctrine requires Congress to supply an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989). Section 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), provides that principle. The Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only upon finding that doing so “is necessary to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” § 811(h)(1). The statute further directs him to consider three specific factors: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. § 811(h)(3). He must publish notice in the Federal Register and transmit his proposal to the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose comments must be taken into account. § 811(h)(4). Temporary scheduling lasts at most one year, with a possible six-month extension. § 811(h)(2).

These constraints are far more detailed than those upheld in *Mistretta*, where Congress merely listed seven broad factors for the Sentencing Commission. 488 U.S. at 374–78. They are also narrower than the “public interest” standard sustained in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426 (1944). The Attorney General’s power is circumscribed by objective criteria, mandatory procedural safeguards, and a strict temporal limit. This combination of standards, procedure, and duration satisfies the “intelligible principle” test. *See also Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation where “well-known and generally acceptable standards” exist in the regulated field).

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In those cases, Congress gave the President “virtually unfettered” discretion, providing no policy guidance. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373. Here, by contrast, Congress specified the precise conditions under which the Attorney General may act, the factors he must consider, and the duration of any temporary order. The delegation is a far cry from the “roving commission” condemned in *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541–42.

Moreover, the emergency nature of the temporary scheduling power underscores its constitutionality. Congress added § 811(h) in 1984 precisely because the permanent scheduling process was too slow to combat the rapid emergence of “designer drugs” like Euphoria. The Court has recognized that “in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372. The temporary scheduling provision is a textbook example of such a permissible delegation.

**II. The Attorney General Lawfully Subdelegated Temporary Scheduling Authority to the DEA**

The Attorney General may delegate his functions under the Controlled Substances Act unless Congress expressly or impliedly forbids subdelegation. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that the Attorney General may “authorize” any officer or employee of the Department of Justice to perform any function vested in him by the Act. This broad grant of authority plainly encompasses subdelegation of the temporary scheduling power. *See Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363–64 (1942) (subdelegation is permissible unless Congress indicates a contrary intent). Nothing in the text or history of § 811(h) suggests that Congress intended the Attorney General to act personally. To the contrary, the temporary scheduling provision was enacted to expedite law enforcement, and subdelegation to the DEA—the agency with the requisite expertise—furthers that purpose.

The Attorney General’s regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), explicitly assigns to the DEA Administrator “functions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended.” This subdelegation is consistent with the statute and with the practical need for administrative efficiency. *See* *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372 (delegation is a practical necessity in modern government). The DEA Administrator’s temporary scheduling of Euphoria was therefore valid.

**CONCLUSION**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision supplies an intelligible principle, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA was authorized by statute. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
