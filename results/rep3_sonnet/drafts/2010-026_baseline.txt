# ARGUMENT

## THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED BY ADOPTING A "PREDOMINANTLY INTERNAL" TEST THAT HAS NO BASIS IN THE TEXT OF EXEMPTION 2

Exemption 2 permits withholding only of matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2) (emphasis added). The Ninth Circuit's adoption of the D.C. Circuit's "predominantly internal" test, *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1073-74 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), cannot be reconciled with this statutory text and should be rejected.

### A. FOIA's Text and Structure Foreclose the "High 2" Interpretation

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA "exemptions are 'explicitly exclusive'" and "are to be interpreted narrowly." *Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *FAA Adm'r v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). FOIA reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976).

The word "solely" in Exemption 2 is not surplusage. Congress limited the exemption to internal personnel matters—items like parking, sick leave, and lunch hours that carry no "genuine and significant public interest." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. The ESQD arc data at issue here—information showing the blast radius of explosions and the potential impact on surrounding communities—bears no relationship whatsoever to "personnel rules and practices." It is not a rule *about* Navy employees; it is technical information about explosive effects that happens to be used, among other purposes, for internal planning.

The Ninth Circuit's "High 2" doctrine, borrowed wholesale from *Crooker*, effectively rewrites the statute by substituting "predominantly" for "solely" and by expanding "internal personnel rules and practices" to cover virtually any agency document that is used internally and whose disclosure might complicate enforcement of some agency policy. This Court has never sanctioned such a rewriting. Indeed, in *Rose* itself, this Court held that Air Force Academy honor and ethics case summaries were not exempt under the "Low 2" category because the public had a "genuine and significant" interest in them, 425 U.S. at 369-70—confirming that Exemption 2's scope is tied to the *personnel* character of the material, not merely its internal use.

### B. This Court's Precedent Confirms Exemption 2 Is Limited to Employee-Relations Matters

In *Rose*, this Court examined the legislative history of Exemption 2 and found it targeted "matters in a specific category, clearly less sensitive than those enumerated in the other exemptions." 425 U.S. at 362 (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965)). The Court further explained that Congress intended the exemption to reach material like "regulating lunch hours, parking, and the like," id. at 363, not substantive agency policy affecting the public.

Nothing in *Rose* supports the "risk of circumvention" gloss that lower courts have layered onto Exemption 2 for so-called "High 2" materials. That gloss originated not from this Court's reasoning but from a footnote observation about a different exemption structure, which the D.C. Circuit in *Crooker* expanded into a freestanding doctrine untethered from the statutory text. 670 F.2d at 1073-74. The Ninth Circuit's opinion below compounds this error by extending *Crooker*'s "predominantly internal" standard beyond even the law-enforcement-manual context in which it arose to cover general-purpose technical data such as blast-effect calculations. Pet. App. at 12a (relying on *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980)); *see also Maricopa Audubon Soc'y v. U.S. Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085-86 (9th Cir. 1997).

### C. The Circumvention Rationale Cannot Justify Withholding Information of Direct Public Concern

Even accepting arguendo that some "circumvention" concern animates Exemption 2, that concern is properly addressed—if at all—through Exemptions 1 and 7, which Congress specifically crafted to protect national security and law-enforcement information subject to defined substantive and procedural safeguards. *See* 5 U.S.C. §§ 552(b)(1), (b)(7). Exemption 1 requires formal classification determinations; Exemption 7(F) requires a showing that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual." Allowing Exemption 2 to serve as a catch-all for any information an agency prefers to keep confidential—without the classification rigor of Exemption 1 or the individualized safety showing of Exemption 7(F)—eviscerates Congress's "careful balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure." *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989).

As Judge Fletcher recognized in dissent below, the Navy's own conduct undermines its circumvention rationale: the Navy voluntarily disclosed comparable ESQD arc maps for its Bangor facility while withholding materially identical maps for Indian Island—a disparity that tracks political sensitivity, not genuine security risk. Pet. App. at 34a-35a. If the arc maps truly implicated national security to the degree the Navy claims, the proper vehicle was classification under Exemption 1, not an atextual expansion of Exemption 2. *See id.* at 36a.

### D. Blast-Effect Data Is Not "Internal Personnel" Material Under Any Standard

Whether framed as "law enforcement" material under *Hardy*, 631 F.2d at 656, or as "predominantly internal" under *Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1056-57, the ESQD data at issue is neither. Commander Whitbred's own affidavit states the Navy uses the arcs "to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety"—a purpose bearing no relationship to personnel administration and no "adjudicative or enforcement purpose[ ]." Pet. App. at 33a (quoting *Church of Scientology v. U.S. Dep't of the Army*, 611 F.2d 738, 748 (9th Cir. 1979)). Information about the potential blast effects of explosives near residential communities is precisely the sort of matter FOIA was designed to expose to public scrutiny, not shield under a judicially invented "High 2" exemption.

For these reasons, the Ninth Circuit's judgment should be reversed.
