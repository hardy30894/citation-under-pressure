**ARGUMENT**

The equal-footing doctrine vests a State with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of statehood. *Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845). The constitutional test therefore requires a court to determine the navigability of the relevant stretch at that historic moment. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870) (a river is navigable in fact when "it is used, or is susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce").

This Court has consistently applied the same temporal standard. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931), the Court examined "the physical character of the streams at the time Utah was admitted into the Union." Likewise, *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894), confirmed that title passes only to those waters then navigable. The Montana Supreme Court followed this settled rule, relying on contemporaneous historical evidence of commercial use on the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers in 1889. That evidence satisfied the *Daniel Ball* standard for the segments at issue.

PPL's contrary contention—that post-statehood improvements or modern use may substitute for the historic inquiry—finds no support in the governing decisions. The navigability test remains fixed at statehood; later changes neither enlarge nor diminish the title then acquired. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75. Because the trial court and the Montana Supreme Court applied precisely this constitutional requirement, the judgment should be affirmed.
