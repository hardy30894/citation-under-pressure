The Court should reject California’s PLRA challenge for two independent reasons.

First, the order at issue is not the kind of final, merits-reaching command that the PLRA forbids. The PLRA limits prospective relief to what is “narrowly drawn,” extends “no further than necessary,” and is the “least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). But Congress enacted those limits against the background rule that federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations. *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971); *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978). The relevant inquiry, therefore, is not whether a remedial order affects prison population levels, but whether the challenged relief is necessary to cure the constitutional violation and is appropriately tailored. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12 (2011). Where overcrowding is the “primary cause” of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations, a population-reduction order falls within the PLRA’s remedial scheme because it directly targets the source of the constitutional harm. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 532-33 (2011).

Second, California’s attack on the order mischaracterizes both the district court’s authority and the remedy itself. The PLRA expressly contemplates prisoner-release orders when “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and no other relief will remedy the problem. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). Before entering such an order, a three-judge court must find by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the primary cause of the violation and that no other relief will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). That is exactly what happened here. The record shows longstanding Eighth Amendment violations, repeated failures of less intrusive remedies, and a finding that overcrowding was the principal cause of the unconstitutional conditions. A court does not violate the PLRA by doing what the statute authorizes—indeed, requires—when those findings are made. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12, 531-33 (2011).

California’s contrary position also conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that deference to prison administrators, while important, does not permit a State to maintain unconstitutional conditions indefinitely. *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 84-85 (1987); *Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520, 547-48 (1979). The PLRA was designed to curb unnecessary judicial intrusion, not to disable federal courts from ordering effective relief when the Constitution has been violated and lesser measures have failed. *Gilmore v. California*, 220 F.3d 987, 998-99 (9th Cir. 2000); *Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367, 391 (1992).

Nor does the order exceed the PLRA because it requires California to make population reductions through its own choices. The statute does not require courts to dictate the precise mechanism of compliance. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 538-39 (2011). It requires only that the remedy be tailored to the violation and leave the State with discretion to comply. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978); *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 538 (2011). That is what occurred here.

California’s reliance on the severity of the remedy likewise fails. The Supreme Court has already held that a population cap is permissible under the PLRA when imposed to correct systemic Eighth Amendment violations caused by overcrowding. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 502, 531-34 (2011). The remedy is demanding because the violation was severe. “Courts may not allow constitutional violations to continue simply because a remedy would require difficult or expensive measures.” *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978). The PLRA does not insulate a State from compliance where its own prison system has reached unconstitutional dimensions. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 511-12 (2011).

Accordingly, the district court’s population-reduction order is consistent with the PLRA and should be upheld.
