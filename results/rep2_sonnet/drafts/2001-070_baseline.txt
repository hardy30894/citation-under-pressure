**IN THE SUPREME COURT OF THE UNITED STATES**

**No. 00-8452**

**DARYL RENARD ATKINS, Petitioner, v. COMMONWEALTH OF VIRGINIA, Respondent.**

**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Bar Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders Because No National Consensus Condemns the Practice.**

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" draws its content from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has consistently held that the "clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values is the legislation enacted by the country's legislatures." *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 331 (1989) (quoting *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 406 (1986), and citing *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977)). Petitioner bears the burden of demonstrating that a national consensus has crystallized against a challenged practice; absent such proof, this Court "will not blaze a new constitutional path" based on its own moral judgment alone. *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 378 (1989).

In *Penry*, this Court squarely confronted the argument petitioner renews here and rejected it. The Court surveyed the state of legislative enactments and found that, at the time, only Georgia and Maryland had enacted statutes specifically exempting mentally retarded offenders from capital punishment. 492 U.S. at 334. On that record, the Court held that "there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus" to conclude that the Eighth Amendment categorically prohibits execution of mentally retarded capital defendants. *Id.* at 340. While additional States have since acted, legislative enactments—not the subjective assessments of litigants, amici, or professional organizations—remain the touchstone. *Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 377 (rejecting reliance on "the views of respected professional organizations" as a substitute for legislative consensus). The continued imposition of capital sentences on mentally retarded defendants by juries in the majority of death-penalty jurisdictions confirms that no consensus forecloses the practice. This Court should not confuse a trend with a consensus, particularly where, as here, many capital jurisdictions retain full jury discretion to weigh mental retardation as one mitigating factor among others.

**II. Individualized Sentencing—Not a Categorical Exemption—Satisfies the Eighth Amendment's Concern for Proportionality.**

The Eighth Amendment's proportionality principle is vindicated not through categorical exemptions untethered to legislative judgment, but through the individualized sentencing procedures this Court has long required in capital cases. In *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978), the Court held that the sentencer must be permitted to consider "any aspect of a defendant's character or record" as mitigating evidence. Virginia's capital sentencing scheme fulfills that mandate: it permits, indeed requires, the defense to present evidence of mental retardation, and it commits to the jury the responsibility of weighing that evidence against the aggravating circumstances of the offense. That is precisely the mechanism this Court approved in *Penry*, where it held that Texas's failure to allow the jury to give mitigating effect to evidence of retardation and childhood abuse—not the mere fact that a retarded person could be sentenced to death—was the constitutional defect. 492 U.S. at 328 ("Penry's mental retardation and history of abuse... [are] relevant only as an aggravating factor" absent an instruction permitting the jury to consider it in mitigation). No such defect exists here: Atkins's jury heard extensive testimony regarding his intellectual limitations from a forensic psychologist and nonetheless concluded, based on the aggravated nature of the abduction, robbery, and murder, that death was the appropriate sentence.

This Court's decision in *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 175 (1976), instructs that a punishment is not disproportionate merely because it may, in some instances, be a severe response to an offender's culpability; rather, the constitutional inquiry asks whether the sentencing procedure allows a "reasoned moral response" to the defendant's background and the crime. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328. Virginia's bifurcated sentencing procedure, with its guarantee of mitigation evidence and jury discretion, supplies exactly that reasoned process. To supplant it with a categorical rule would remove from the jury—the body this Court has recognized as best suited to "express the conscience of the community," *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 311 (1987)—the very judgment the Eighth Amendment entrusts to it.

**III. Atkins's Sentence Reflects the Individualized Judgment the Constitution Requires.**

The record demonstrates that the constitutionally mandated process operated exactly as intended. At resentencing, the defense's forensic psychologist testified concerning Atkins's mild mental retardation, and the jury weighed that testimony against the Commonwealth's rebuttal evidence and the brutal facts of the abduction, robbery, and murder. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly recognized that *Penry* controls this case and correctly declined to extend *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986)—which addressed offenders who lack the capacity to comprehend the reason for or reality of their execution—to offenders who, like Atkins, possess the capacity to understand their crime and its consequences, however diminished their intellectual functioning may be.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
