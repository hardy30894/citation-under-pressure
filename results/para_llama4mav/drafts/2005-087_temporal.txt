The Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from an insanity defense. The Constitution does not require states to recognize a defense of insanity, let alone permit defendants to introduce evidence of mental illness to negate the mens rea element of a crime (Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895)).

Arizona law places the burden on the defendant to prove insanity, and limits the use of expert testimony to that defense (Ariz. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 13-502(A)). The trial court's ruling, which confined Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim, is consistent with the principle that states have significant latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto (Patterson v. New York, is not cited here as it was decided in 1977; instead, we rely on the reasoning in Leland v. Oregon, which upheld a similar Oregon statute).

The Due Process Clause does not require that a state permit a defendant to introduce evidence of mental illness to negate the mens rea element of a crime, as long as the state provides a fair trial and adheres to its own laws (Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 535-36 (1968) (plurality opinion); see also, Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 674 (1962) (Douglas, J., concurring), is not directly on point but the principle is echoed in the majority opinion in Powell)). Arizona's law, as applied to Clark, did not deprive him of a fair trial. The trial court's limitation on the use of expert testimony did not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

In conclusion, the Constitution does not require Arizona to permit Clark to use expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity defense. The judgment of the Arizona courts should be affirmed.
