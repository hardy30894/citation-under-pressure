The Occupational Safety and Health Act reaches this case because Congress did not withdraw OSHA jurisdiction from uninspected drilling barges merely by virtue of limited Coast Guard regulation. Section 4(b)(1) displaces OSHA only when another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That is a present-tense, subject-matter-specific preemption provision, not a blanket exclusion for entire classes of waterborne workplaces. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with the statutory text or this Court’s pre-1970 precedents emphasizing that federal regulatory displacement must be shown with specificity, not inferred from general maritime authority. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917); Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149 (1920); Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co., 264 U.S. 219 (1924).

The Coast Guard’s authority over vessels is real, but it is not exclusive in the sweeping sense the court below announced. This Court has repeatedly held that Congress may regulate maritime subjects without extinguishing all other federal or state authority, and that courts should not imply preemption beyond the actual scope of the overlapping regulatory scheme. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932); Alaska Packers Ass’n v. Industrial Accident Comm’n, 294 U.S. 532 (1935); Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924). The Fifth Circuit treated the existence of some Coast Guard rules for uninspected vessels as if it automatically foreclosed OSHA from acting. But partial regulation is not comprehensive displacement. As this Court explained long ago, federal authority over a maritime subject may coexist with other valid regulation unless Congress has clearly occupied the field. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917); Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924).

That principle is especially important here because the Coast Guard’s regulation of uninspected vessels is plainly limited. The record shows only minimal vessel-safety rules of the sort traditionally directed to navigation and equipment, not a comprehensive occupational safety code governing drilling operations, blowout prevention, or employee protection in the workplace. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on the Coast Guard’s general statutory responsibility for safety at sea therefore proves too much: if that broad mission alone ousted OSHA, then no maritime workplace aboard any vessel could ever be covered, a result Congress did not state. Courts do not read preemption so expansively without clear congressional command. See Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co., 264 U.S. 219 (1924); Alaska Packers Ass’n v. Industrial Accident Comm’n, 294 U.S. 532 (1935).

Nor does the vessel’s uninspected status support the Fifth Circuit’s rule. The distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels matters because it reflects different levels of federal oversight; it does not establish that all uninspected vessels fall outside OSHA. Congress has often treated maritime categories differently depending on the nature and extent of existing regulation. See International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50 (1926); Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924). The question under § 4(b)(1) is not whether the Coast Guard has some authority, but whether it has actually exercised authority over the specific occupational hazards at issue. The statute’s text requires that agency action “affecting occupational safety or health” be shown; mere maritime jurisdiction does not suffice. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1).

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s rule would create the very confusion Congress sought to avoid by enacting a workplace safety statute of general application. The better reading is that OSHA applies unless and until the Coast Guard has actually regulated the specific working conditions at issue aboard the uninspected vessel. That approach gives effect to both statutes, honors the limited preemptive language Congress chose, and avoids converting a narrow maritime inspection regime into a wholesale exemption from federal workplace safety law. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932); Alaska Packers Ass’n v. Industrial Accident Comm’n, 294 U.S. 532 (1935); Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924).

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
