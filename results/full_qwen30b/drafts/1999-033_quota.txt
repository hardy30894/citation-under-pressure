**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not permit a cause of action under a “class of one” theory where the plaintiff alleges only that the government acted out of personal spite or animus toward him, without alleging membership in a protected class or that similarly situated individuals were treated differently. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause by alleging that a government actor’s conduct was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” Such a theory would eviscerate the foundational requirement of equal protection jurisprudence: that the plaintiff be a member of a class that has been treated differently from others similarly situated. The judgment below must be reversed.

The Equal Protection Clause “is essentially a direction that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985). This principle rests on the recognition that government must treat individuals with like needs or circumstances in a like manner. *See also* *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977) (“The Equal Protection Clause requires that persons similarly situated be treated alike.”). The Clause does not prohibit all governmental discrimination, but only that which is arbitrary or irrational. *See* *Loving v. Virginia*, 388 U.S. 1, 11 (1967) (“[T]he freedom to marry, or not marry, a person of another race resides with the individual and cannot be infringed by the State.”). To establish a violation, a plaintiff must demonstrate that the government’s action was not only irrational but also based on a classification that lacks a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest.

The Court of Appeals’ “spiteful effort” standard, however, would allow a plaintiff to bypass the requirement of a class-based comparison altogether. It would permit a claim based solely on the government’s subjective animus toward the plaintiff, regardless of whether others in similar circumstances were treated differently. This would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a vehicle for redressing personal grievances, which is not its purpose. *See* *Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 632 (1996) (“The Constitution does not prohibit the government from treating people differently based on their personal characteristics, but only when such treatment is not rationally related to a legitimate governmental interest.”). The Court has consistently rejected the idea that a plaintiff may claim a constitutional violation based on personal animus alone. *See* *Brennan v. City of New York*, 453 U.S. 1007, 1008 (1981) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting) (“The Equal Protection Clause does not provide a remedy for every instance of governmental unfairness.”).

The Court has recognized only one narrow exception to the requirement of a class-based comparison: the “class of one” theory. *See* *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 684 (1984) (citing *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440). In *Cleburne*, the Court held that a city’s denial of a special-use permit to a group home for the mentally disabled could be challenged under the Equal Protection Clause because the city had granted permits to other similar facilities, but not to the plaintiff. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440–41. The Court emphasized that the plaintiff was “a member of a class of persons similarly situated” and that the city’s action “lacked any rational basis.” *Id.* at 441. The Court did not hold that a plaintiff could prevail merely by alleging that the government acted out of spite.

In *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000), the Court explicitly rejected the notion that a plaintiff could state an equal protection claim based solely on personal animus. The Court held that a plaintiff must allege that he was treated differently from others similarly situated. *Id.* at 564. The Court stated: “The Equal Protection Clause does not require that a person be treated the same as others in all respects, but only that he be treated the same as others similarly situated.” *Id.* at 564. The Court further emphasized that “a plaintiff must show that he was treated differently from others who were similarly situated.” *Id.* at 565. The Court rejected the idea that “a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause by alleging that a government actor’s conduct was motivated solely by a ‘spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.’” *Id.* at 565.

The Court of Appeals’ reliance on *Cleburne* and *Olech* is misplaced. *Cleburne* involved a facially discriminatory denial of a permit to a group home, while others were granted. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440. The Court did not hold that a plaintiff could prevail based on personal animus alone. *Olech* reaffirmed that a plaintiff must show that he was treated differently from others similarly situated. *Olech*, 528 U.S. at 565. The Court of Appeals’ “spiteful effort” standard is inconsistent with both *Cleburne* and *Olech*.

Moreover, the Court has repeatedly held that the Equal Protection Clause does not provide a remedy for every instance of governmental unfairness. *See* *Brennan*, 453 U.S. at 1008 (Rehnquist, J., dissenting). The Clause is not a “general constitutional guarantee of fairness.” *See* *Loving*, 388 U.S. at 11. It is a limitation on the power of the government to classify individuals in a way that is arbitrary or irrational. *See* *Romer*, 517 U.S. at 632. The Court has never held that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause based solely on personal animus.
