# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because the Shipowner’s Crew Created and Left Unremedied a Dangerous Condition Within Its Own Control

### A. The shipowner owed Serbin a duty of reasonable care, not a diminished duty owed to a mere licensee.

The shipowner’s duty toward longshoremen lawfully aboard its vessel has long been settled as one of ordinary reasonable care under the circumstances, without regard to the common-law categories of invitee and licensee that once complicated land-based tort law. In *Kermarec v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique*, 358 U.S. 625 (1959), the Supreme Court held that a shipowner owes those lawfully aboard "the duty of exercising reasonable care under the circumstances of each case," rejecting any attempt to import "casual" distinctions between classes of visitors. That duty extends squarely to longshoremen performing cargo operations, who are aboard the vessel not as social guests but to do the ship’s work. *West v. United States*, 361 U.S. 118 (1959), confirms that this duty of reasonable care attaches to conditions of the vessel and its gear of which the shipowner’s crew had, or should have had, knowledge, regardless of whether the vessel is publicly or privately owned.

Here, the ship’s own crew — not the stevedore — was exclusively responsible for operating the block-and-pulley system that raised and lowered the hatch covers, and for returning each snatch block to the vertical position once its work was done. The crew alone had the duty, the practice, and the equipment to move these one-hundred-pound fixtures. When the crew left the No. 3 hatch block resting in the down position atop a cargo unit — a position Serbin correctly recognized as both obstructive and hazardous to anyone working in the wing below — it breached the very duty of reasonable care that *Kermarec* and *West* impose.

### B. The shipowner cannot escape liability for a hazard created by its own crew’s operations merely because an independent stevedore was performing cargo work nearby.

*Pope & Talbot, Inc. v. Hawn*, 346 U.S. 406 (1953), establishes that a shipowner is not automatically relieved of responsibility for dangerous conditions aboard its vessel simply because an independent stevedoring firm is present and performing its own separate work. The presence of Serbin’s longshore gang unloading fruit in the hold did not transfer to the stevedore any responsibility for the crew’s own instrumentality — the snatch block — which remained, as it always had, a matter committed to the ship’s crew alone. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96 (1944), likewise holds that the duty to furnish safe gear and appliances for use in cargo operations belongs to the vessel, and that duty is not discharged by the fortuity that longshoremen, rather than seamen, are the ones exposed to the danger. *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85 (1946), confirms that longshoremen performing what is traditionally the ship’s own work — as Serbin was, in the very hold where the ship’s block-and-tackle system operated — stand in no worse position than the crew with respect to the safety of the vessel’s appliances.

### C. The shipowner cannot invoke Serbin’s voluntary effort to move the block, undertaken under competitive operational pressure, as a complete defense to its own antecedent negligence.

That Serbin, rather than waiting for the crew, chose to move the block himself does not excuse the shipowner’s negligence in creating the hazard in the first place. Under *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424 (1939), a plaintiff’s own conduct in confronting a hazard created by another’s fault operates, at most, to diminish damages; it does not extinguish the underlying liability for the condition itself. Nor can the shipowner defend on the theory that leaving blocks unsecured was standard or tolerable practice in the fruit trade. As Judge Learned Hand explained in *The T.J. Hooper*, 60 F.2d 737 (2d Cir. 1932), "there are precautions so imperative that even their universal disregard will not excuse their omission." The competitive pressures of the East Coast fruit business that led Serbin to act cannot convert the crew’s own dereliction — leaving a heavy, hinged block resting atop cargo in a walkway where the "hookup man would normally stand" — into a risk voluntarily and knowingly assumed as a matter of law.

## II. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment

Summary judgment is inappropriate where the record requires the drawing of inferences concerning knowledge, foreseeability, and reasonableness of conduct. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944), teaches that a court may not resolve on paper questions that turn on the credibility of witnesses and the weight of competing inferences. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), reinforces that summary disposition should be used sparingly where the ultimate facts are in genuine dispute. Here, whether the crew knew or should have known that the block’s position created a foreseeable hazard, and whether Serbin’s response was reasonable under the circumstances, are quintessential jury questions. The district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed.
