**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule cannot be squared with FOIA’s text or this Court’s precedents. FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” are to be “interpreted narrowly,” and apply only when the withheld material falls within the statutory words Congress chose. *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989); *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Exemption 2, by its terms, reaches only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). By exempting documents because they are “predominantly” internal and might allow circumvention, the Ninth Circuit impermissibly broadened that text.

This Court’s decisions do not support the Ninth Circuit’s approach. In *Rose*, the Court held that Exemption 2 covers minor internal matters, but it did not convert the exemption into a free-floating secrecy rule for any operational material an agency prefers to keep confidential. 425 U.S. at 363, 369. To the contrary, *Rose* emphasized that FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Id.* at 360-61. That caution controls here.

The Ninth Circuit relied on a line of cases about circumvention risk, but those cases did not authorize a “predominantly internal” test untethered from the statutory phrase “personnel rules and practices.” In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), the court treated raid-manual instructions as exempt because they were internal and disclosure could aid circumvention. But *Hardy* did not announce that any internal operational document may be withheld whenever an agency asserts risk. Nor did it dispense with the statutory requirement that the material relate to “personnel rules and practices.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision here does both.

The D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” formulation in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1056-57 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), cannot expand the statute either. Whatever force *Crooker* once had in its circuit, it cannot override Congress’s words or this Court’s instruction that exemptions be narrowly construed. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151; *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. Moreover, *Crooker* itself recognized that the exemption turns on materials that are “predominantly internal” and whose disclosure “significantly risks circumvention of the federal statutes or regulations.” 670 F.2d at 1073-75. The Navy’s ESQD documents are not “personnel rules and practices” at all; they are technical safety-planning materials used to design ammunition storage and assess blast effects. A document does not become a personnel rule because an agency uses it internally.

The Ninth Circuit’s own later decision in *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085-87 (9th Cir. 1997), confirms the error. There, the court first asked whether the data “relate[d] ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency,’” and it rejected Exemption 2 because the information did not fit that statutory description. *Id.* at 1085. Only after that did it consider the circumvention theory. *Id.* at 1086-87. The Ninth Circuit inverted that sequence below by treating circumvention risk as enough to make technical safety maps “internal personnel rules and practices.” That is not what *Maricopa* held, and it is not what the statute says.

The government may argue that *Milner v. Department of the Navy*, 562 U.S. 562 (2011), later resolved this precise dispute. It did: this Court reversed and held that Exemption 2 “covers only records relating to employee relations and human resources.” *Id.* at 570. That decision confirms petitioner’s position. The documents at issue here are not “employee relations and human resources” materials, and they therefore fall outside Exemption 2. *Id.* at 570-71. Although *Milner* postdates the Ninth Circuit’s judgment, it demonstrates that the court of appeals’ expansive “predominantly internal” rule was wrong.

In short, the Ninth Circuit relieved the Navy of its burden to fit these records within the actual text of Exemption 2. FOIA does not permit that result. Because the ESQD documents are not “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency,” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2), and because exemptions must be narrowly construed, the judgment should be reversed. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.
