The district court erred in denying habeas relief. The prosecutor’s testimony and continued participation rendered the trial fundamentally unfair.

A prosecutor “is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty . . . whose interest . . . is that justice shall be done.” Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). When the district attorney took the stand to rebut Riddle’s account of a threat and then resumed prosecution, the State placed its own representative in the position of both witness and advocate. This dual role created an inherent conflict that the assistant prosecutor exploited at closing by urging the jury to disbelieve Riddle because the district attorney had shown him to be “a liar.”

Such conduct violates due process when it “so infected the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process.” Darden v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 168, 181 (1986) (quoting Donnelly v. DeChristoforo, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974)). The prosecutor’s testimony supplied the sole rebuttal to Riddle’s explanation for his escape attempts; the subsequent argument converted that testimony into an official endorsement of guilt. See United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 18–19 (1985) (prosecutorial vouching undermines the jury’s impartiality). No curative instruction was given.

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), the state court’s rejection of this claim was contrary to clearly established federal law. Williams v. Taylor, 529 U.S. 362, 405–06 (2000). The error was not harmless. Brecht v. Abrahamson, 507 U.S. 619, 637–38 (1993). Riddle’s credibility was the central issue; the prosecutor’s testimony and closing argument directly attacked it. See Kyles v. Whitley, 514 U.S. 419, 434–35 (1995); Strickler v. Greene, 527 U.S. 263, 289–90 (1999). The resulting prejudice deprived Riddle of a fair trial.

The judgment should be reversed and the writ granted.
