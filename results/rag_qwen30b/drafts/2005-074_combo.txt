**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether Congress, in enacting the reinstatement statute of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), may apply it retroactively to reinstate a prior deportation order issued before the statute’s enactment—thereby stripping an alien of all forms of relief, including adjustment of status—without violating the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The answer is no. The application of Section 241(a)(5) to petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas, whose prior deportation order was issued in 1981 and whose re-entry occurred in 1982, constitutes an impermissible retroactive application of a penal statute that extinguishes a protected legal interest without fair warning. This Court has long held that “a statute which imposes a new disability upon an individual, or which takes away a right previously possessed, is not to be applied retroactively unless the legislature has clearly expressed such an intention.” *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560, 564 (1964). The reinstatement statute, by its very nature, operates as a penal sanction, depriving an alien of the right to seek relief under the INA, including adjustment of status, and thus must be applied prospectively unless Congress has clearly indicated otherwise.

The principle that statutes imposing new disabilities are not retroactive is firmly rooted in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692, 695 (1957), the Court held that “a person who has been convicted of a crime which was not a ground for deportation at the time of the conviction cannot be deported on that ground after the enactment of a statute which makes the offense deportable.” The Court emphasized that “the status of nondeportability which existed at the time of the offense was preserved by the savings clause,” and that “the law cannot be applied retroactively to deprive a person of a status which he had acquired under the law as it existed at the time of the offense.” This principle applies with equal force here: Fernandez-Vargas was not subject to the reinstatement statute when he re-entered the United States in 1982. At that time, no law provided for the automatic reinstatement of a prior deportation order. To apply the statute now, decades later, to strip him of the right to adjust his status—especially after he married a U.S. citizen and lived in the country for nearly two decades—is to impose a new disability on him based on conduct that was lawful at the time.

The Court has repeatedly recognized that “a person has a legitimate expectation of the law as it existed at the time of his conduct.” *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 689 (1957). In *Lehmann*, the Court rejected the government’s argument that a new deportation statute could be applied to an alien whose offense occurred before the statute’s enactment, stating that “the provisions of this section shall be applicable to all aliens belonging to any of the classes enumerated in subsection (a), notwithstanding (1) that any such alien entered the United States prior to the date of enactment of this Act, or (2) that the facts, by reason of which any such alien belongs to any of the classes enumerated in subsection (a), occurred prior to the date of enactment of this Act.” Id. at 688 (emphasis added). The Court held that even though the statute applied to pre-enactment facts, it could not override a “status” that had been established under the prior law. Here, Fernandez-Vargas’s status as a lawful resident—albeit undocumented—was not created by the reinstatement statute, but by the absence of any law that would have allowed his prior deportation order to be reinstated. To now apply the statute retroactively is to retroactively alter that status, which is impermissible.

Moreover, the reinstatement statute operates as a form of punishment, not merely a procedural rule. In *Foti v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 375 U.S. 217, 220 (1963), the Court held that “the denial of discretionary relief, such as suspension of deportation, is not a mere administrative act but a matter of substantial consequence to the alien.” The Court emphasized that “the right to seek such relief is not a mere privilege, but a matter of substantial interest.” Id. The reinstatement statute, by denying all forms of relief—including adjustment of status—effectively imposes a permanent bar on an alien’s ability to remain in the United States, even after a long period of lawful residence and marriage to a U.S. citizen. This is not a mere procedural change; it is a substantive deprivation of a protected interest.

The Court has also recognized that “the right to be free from arbitrary or capricious action by the government is a fundamental component of due process.” *United States Ex Rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72, 75 (1957). In *Hintopoulos*, the Court held that an alien who had lived in the United States for years and had a child born in the country could not be summarily deported without consideration of the hardship to the citizen child. The Court stressed that “the Attorney General may suspend deportation if he finds that deportation would result in serious economic detriment to a citizen or legally resident alien who is the spouse, parent, or minor child of such deportable alien.” Id. at 74. The reinstatement statute, by denying such relief, effectively eliminates the possibility of a discretionary determination based on equitable considerations, thereby violating the principle of due process.

The Court has also held that “a statute which takes away a right previously possessed is not to be applied retroactively unless the legislature has clearly expressed such an intention.” *Low Wah Suey v. Backus*, 225 U.S. 460, 465 (1912). In *Low Wah Suey*, the Court struck down a statute that retroactively deprived an alien of the right to remain in the United States, holding that “the law cannot be applied retroactively to deprive a person of a right previously possessed.” Id. The same principle applies here: Fernandez-Vargas had a reasonable expectation that his prior deportation order would not be reinstated decades later, especially given that no law at the time of his re-entry provided for such a result. To apply the statute now is to retroactively deprive him of a right he had under the law as it existed at the time of his re-entry.
