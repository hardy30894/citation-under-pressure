**Argument**

The Railroad Retirement Act authorizes judicial review of “any final decision of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A refusal to reopen a prior benefits determination is such a final decision because it conclusively determines the claimant’s ability to obtain benefits on the earlier application and is itself the product of a distinct adjudicatory process. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule conflicts with the statute’s text and with decisions recognizing that agency action is reviewable when it “determine[s] rights or obligations” and “from which legal consequences will flow.” *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. 154, 178 (1997).

This Court’s leading case is *Salinas v. United States Railroad Retirement Board*, 141 S. Ct. 691 (2021). There, the Court held that a Board refusal to reopen a prior claim is a “final decision” reviewable under § 355(f). *Id.* at 696–97. The Court explained that “final” means “not any more subject to administrative review,” and that the Board’s denial of reopening “marks the ‘consummation’ of the Board’s decisionmaking process” because “the Board has arrived at its final position” on whether to revisit the earlier claim. *Id.* at 697 (quoting *Bennett*, 520 U.S. at 177–78). The Court further rejected the government’s contention that reopening decisions are unreviewable because they are discretionary, emphasizing that § 355(f) contains no exception for discretionary final decisions. *Id.* at 698–99.

The same result follows from the statutory structure. Section 355(f) does not limit review to initial merits determinations; it authorizes review of “any final decision.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). In *Salinas*, the Court held that this phrase is broad enough to include reopening denials because Congress used inclusive language and did not “carve out” such decisions. 141 S. Ct. at 697–98. That reading accords with the Court’s settled instruction that courts may not “rewrite” a statute by adding limits Congress did not impose. *Lamie v. United States Trustee*, 540 U.S. 526, 538 (2004).

The Board’s own regulations reinforce that reopening decisions are final. The Board treats reopening as a separate procedural step available only within specified time limits and on specified grounds. 20 C.F.R. § 261.2; 20 C.F.R. §§ 261.1–261.9. Once the Board denies reopening, nothing remains for it to do on that request. That is the hallmark of finality. *Bennett*, 520 U.S. at 178. Indeed, the Court in *Salinas* observed that “the denial of reopening ends the Board’s consideration of the matter.” 141 S. Ct. at 697.

The Fifth Circuit’s pre-*Salinas* precedent rested on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), but that case does not control here. *Sanders* construed a different statute, the Social Security Act, and held that the APA does not itself provide jurisdiction to review a refusal to reopen. *Id.* at 107–09. The Court in *Salinas* distinguished *Sanders* on exactly that ground, explaining that § 355(f) expressly authorizes review of “any final decision” and thus supplies the jurisdiction that was absent in *Sanders*. 141 S. Ct. at 699–700.

Nor can the government avoid review by characterizing the Board’s refusal as merely discretionary. Finality under § 355(f) asks whether the agency’s action is terminal, not whether the agency possessed discretion. *Salinas*, 141 S. Ct. at 698. Final agency action is reviewable even when the agency had authority to deny the request in the first place. *Bennett*, 520 U.S. at 178; *Sackett v. Environmental Protection Agency*, 566 U.S. 120, 127 (2012).

Here, the Board’s decision denying reopening was the Board’s last word on Salinas’s request to revisit the 2006 denial. It therefore was a “final decision” within the meaning of § 355(f). The petition for review should be granted, the Fifth Circuit’s jurisdictional dismissal reversed, and the case remanded for consideration of the merits.
