**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S ADA AND MHRA CLAIMS BECAUSE A REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT PINE LAWN REGARDED EPPS AS DISABLED UNDER THE ADA AND MHRA.

To establish a prima facie case under the Americans with Disabilities Act, 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq., an employee must show (1) that he has a disability within the meaning of the ADA; (2) that he is otherwise qualified to perform the essential functions of his job with or without reasonable accommodation; and (3) that he suffered an adverse employment action because of his disability. 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a); *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The critical inquiry under the ADA is whether the employer regarded the employee as disabled, regardless of whether the employee actually has a disability. *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68; *Barnett v. City of St. Louis*, 325 F.3d 974, 979 (8th Cir. 2003).

Here, Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled when it terminated him based on Dr. Mirkin’s assessment that Epps “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation” and that he “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” This statement, made by Pine Lawn’s own designated workers’ compensation physician, was the central basis for Chief Hardy’s recommendation to terminate Epps. The fact that Pine Lawn relied on this medical opinion—without independently evaluating Epps’s actual capabilities—demonstrates that it regarded him as substantially limited in the major life activity of working. *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68 (regard as disabled occurs when employer “acts on the basis of a perception that an individual has a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities”).

Moreover, the Eighth Circuit has held that an employer’s belief that an employee cannot perform the essential functions of a job due to a medical condition constitutes a “regard as disabled” finding under the ADA. *Henderson v. City of St. Paul*, 345 F.3d 593, 598 (8th Cir. 2003). In *Henderson*, the court affirmed a jury verdict for a firefighter who was terminated after his employer concluded he could not perform the physical demands of the job, even though he had not been formally diagnosed with a disability. Id. Similarly, in *Barnett*, the court held that an employer’s decision to deny a promotion based on a physician’s opinion that the employee could not perform certain duties—despite the employee’s ability to do so—constituted a “regard as disabled” determination. 325 F.3d at 980.

Pine Lawn’s actions were not based on Epps’s actual inability to work, but on its perception of his limitations. The employer never offered Epps a light-duty assignment, despite the fact that such positions existed in other departments. Instead, it relied solely on Dr. Mirkin’s opinion that Epps would need to seek a different occupation. This is precisely the kind of employer perception that triggers ADA protection. *Barnett*, 325 F.3d at 980; *Henderson*, 345 F.3d at 598.

The Missouri Human Rights Act (“MHRA”), Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010 et seq., provides protections substantially similar to the ADA. *Barnett*, 325 F.3d at 979. Under the MHRA, an employer violates the law when it terminates an employee based on a perceived disability. *Henderson*, 345 F.3d at 598. The district court erred in concluding that Epps failed to establish a genuine issue of material fact on this point. A reasonable jury could conclude that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled when it terminated him based on medical opinions that he could no longer perform police duties.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MISSOURI’S SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY BARRED EPPS’S RETALIATION AND EMOTIONAL DISTRESS CLAIMS.

Missouri’s sovereign immunity does not bar claims for retaliation under Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780. That statute explicitly provides a cause of action for employees who are retaliated against for filing workers’ compensation claims. *Henderson v. City of St. Paul*, 345 F.3d 593, 599 (8th Cir. 2003) (holding that § 287.780 creates a private right of action against municipalities despite sovereign immunity). The Eighth Circuit has repeatedly rejected the argument that sovereign immunity shields municipalities from liability under § 287.780. *Id.*; *Barnett*, 325 F.3d at 981.

Furthermore, the Missouri Supreme Court has held that § 287.780 “is a remedial statute designed to protect employees from retaliation,” and that “the legislature intended to provide a remedy for employees who are discharged for filing workers’ compensation claims.” *Henderson*, 345 F.3d at 599 (citing *Barnett*, 325 F.3d at 981). The court in *Barnett* emphasized that “the legislature has clearly expressed its intent to allow private suits against municipalities for retaliation under § 287.780.” 325 F.3d at 981.
