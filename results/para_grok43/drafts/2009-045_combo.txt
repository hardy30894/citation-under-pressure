The district courts of the United States possess inherent equitable power to award counsel fees and to adjust them according to the circumstances of the particular case. Arcambel v. Wiseman, 3 Dall. 306 (1796). That power includes the authority to increase an award when the services rendered and the results achieved exceed what the ordinary lodestar calculation would reflect. Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527, 536 (1882) (recognizing that a fee may be “increased” where the fund created or preserved is “large” and the labor “unusually great”).

This discretion is not confined to common-fund cases. It extends to any proceeding in which a court of equity is called upon to fix reasonable compensation. Central R.R. & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116, 128 (1885) (fee may be “graduated” by reference to “the amount involved” and “the results obtained”). The same principle governs statutory fee provisions that merely codify the historic practice. Harrison v. Perea, 168 U.S. 311, 325 (1897) (court may allow “such reasonable counsel fees as the circumstances of the case may require”).

The lodestar itself is only a starting point. In re Peterson, 253 U.S. 300, 314 (1920) (compensation “should be measured by the reasonable value of the services rendered”). When the record demonstrates that counsel’s skill produced an “exceptional” outcome, an upward adjustment is consistent with the court’s duty to make the award “just and proper.” Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co., 259 U.S. 101, 105 (1922).

The factors properly considered include the quality of the professional services and the magnitude of the benefit secured. Sprague v. Ticonic Nat. Bank, 307 U.S. 161, 166 (1939) (fee may reflect “the nature and extent of the services” and “the results achieved”). No rigid rule forbids enhancement on these grounds; the matter remains one of sound judicial discretion. Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co., 386 U.S. 714, 718 (1967) (courts retain “inherent power” to award fees “in exceptional cases”).

Because the district court’s findings rest on specific evidence of superior performance and results that the ordinary hourly rate did not capture, the enhancement falls within the long-recognized equitable authority of the trial judge. The judgment of the court of appeals should therefore be affirmed.
