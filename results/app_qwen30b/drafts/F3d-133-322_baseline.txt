**ARGUMENT**

I. THE INDICTMENT WAS NOT DEFICIENT BECAUSE PARKER’S ACTIONS CONSTITUTED “OFFICIAL ACTS” UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), EVEN IF SHE LACKED EXPRESS AUTHORITY TO APPROVE BENEFITS.

The indictment properly charges Mrs. Parker with conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), which prohibits a public official from being induced to “do or omit to do any act in violation of his official duty.” The critical inquiry is not whether Mrs. Parker had formal authority to approve benefits, but whether her conduct constituted an “official act” within the meaning of the statute. The Supreme Court has made clear that the term “official act” is broadly defined and encompasses any decision or action taken by a public official in his or her official capacity, regardless of whether such action falls within the narrow confines of written rules or formal authority. See *United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers of California*, 526 U.S. 398, 407 (1999) (“The statute’s definition of ‘official act’ is broad and inclusive.”); *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983) (holding that an official’s duty may be established by “settled practice” even in the absence of formal rules).

Here, Mrs. Parker’s role as a clerk to ALJ Aronson was not merely clerical. She participated in the administrative process by preparing and distributing decisions, maintaining records, and assisting in the hearing process. Her access to the ALJ’s computer system and her ability to type and mail decisions placed her in a position to influence the outcome of claims. The fact that she did not have the formal authority to sign decisions does not negate the reality that her actions were integral to the official process. As the Court stated in *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914), “duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery.” Mrs. Parker’s conduct—altering letters, forging the ALJ’s signature, and delivering purportedly official decisions—was not a mere clerical function; it was a material component of the decision-making process that the public reasonably believed to be official.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that an official must have “discretionary authority” to perform an act in order for that act to qualify as an “official act.” In *Sun-Diamond*, the Court emphasized that “the statute does not require that the official have discretion to make the decision in question,” but rather that the act be “one that the official is expected to perform in his official capacity.” 526 U.S. at 407. Mrs. Parker’s actions were not peripheral or unauthorized; they were central to the administration of SSI claims. By altering documents and delivering them as official decisions, she performed acts that were “in the course of the performance of official duties” and were “reasonably understood to be within the scope of the official’s responsibilities.” *Id.* at 408.

The government’s argument that Mrs. Parker’s conduct was not an “official act” because she lacked authority to approve benefits ignores the settled principle that official duties are not limited to those explicitly granted by statute or regulation. See *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d at 972 (“An official act may be one that violates a duty established by custom or usage, even if not codified.”). The public relied on the integrity of the process, and Mrs. Parker’s role—though not one of final decision-making—was essential to the appearance and reality of official action. Her forgery of the ALJ’s signature and her delivery of decisions to claimants constituted a violation of her official duty to maintain the integrity of the administrative process.

The indictment, therefore, was not defective. It charged Mrs. Parker with acts that were “in violation of her official duty” within the meaning of § 201(b)(2)(C), even if she lacked formal authority to approve benefits. The statute’s purpose is to protect the public’s confidence in the integrity of government processes, not to limit prosecution to those with formal decision-making power. As the Court stated in *Sun-Diamond*, “Congress intended to reach conduct that undermines public trust in government, regardless of the formality of the official’s role.” 526 U.S. at 409. Mrs. Parker’s conduct—fraudulently altering official documents and using her position to extort money—directly undermined that trust.

For these reasons, the indictment was legally sufficient, and the district court properly denied Mrs. Parker’s motion to dismiss on this ground. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
