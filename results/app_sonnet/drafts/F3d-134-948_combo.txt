**ARGUMENT**

**I. Selam Was Denied His Right to Compulsory Process Guaranteed by 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6).**

The Indian Civil Rights Act guarantees a tribal defendant the right "to have the assistance of counsel for his defense" and, implicit in that same due-process guarantee, the ability to compel the attendance of favorable witnesses. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This right mirrors the Sixth Amendment right to compulsory process, which the Supreme Court has recognized as fundamental to a fair trial. In *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), the Court held that the right to compulsory process is "the right to present a defense," and that a State (or, by analogy, a tribe) may not erect procedural barriers that leave a defendant unable to secure witnesses in his favor. *Id.* at 19. That is precisely what happened here. Selam gave his appointed lay advocate the names of five potential witnesses; the advocate herself acknowledged she needed Selam's assistance to locate them, yet neither the advocate nor the tribal judge ensured that the machinery of compulsory process—subpoenas—was actually made available to Selam in a meaningful way. A right to compulsory process that exists only on paper, and is never translated into an actual subpoena reaching a witness, is no right at all. *Washington*, 388 U.S. at 19.

Nor can the tribal court's inaction be excused as Selam's own fault. Any relinquishment of so fundamental a trial right must be knowing and voluntary. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Supreme Court defined waiver of a constitutional trial right as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and further instructed that courts must "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942). The record shows no such intentional relinquishment. Selam did not affirmatively refuse to subpoena witnesses; at most he was passive in assisting his advocate in locating them. Passivity by a layman untrained in the mechanics of tribal subpoena practice cannot substitute for the "intentional relinquishment" required by *Zerbst*. 304 U.S. at 464.

**II. Selam's Confrontation Clause Claim Was Not Waived by His Failure to Raise It Before the Tribal Court of Appeals.**

The district court held that Selam forfeited his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to present it to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. That holding applied too rigid a standard. Exhaustion and procedural-default doctrine in habeas corpus does not bar a claim simply because it was not raised below; it bars a claim only where the petitioner engaged in a "deliberate by-passing" of the available procedure. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). The *Fay* Court explained that forfeiture requires that the petitioner, "after consultation with competent counsel or otherwise, understandingly and knowingly" chose to forgo the opportunity to litigate the claim, for strategic or tactical reasons. *Id.* Nothing in this record suggests that Selam's failure to raise a Confrontation Clause theory before the tribal appellate court was a deliberate, strategic choice rather than an oversight by a defendant represented by a lay advocate in a tribal system without formalized appellate briefing practice. Under *Fay*, mere failure to raise a claim—absent a showing of deliberate bypass—does not extinguish federal habeas review. 372 U.S. at 438.

This conclusion is reinforced by the settled principle that waiver of a Confrontation Clause right, like waiver of any other fundamental trial right, must be knowing and intentional. *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1 (1966) (waiver of confrontation right requires an affirmative, knowing choice by the defendant, not inferred from silence). No such showing was made here.

**III. The Admission of the Five-Year-Old Victim's Out-of-Court Statements Through Other Witnesses, Without Any Opportunity for Cross-Examination, Violated Selam's Right of Confrontation.**

Even on the merits, Selam's confrontation claim is substantial. In the second trial, the five-year-old complainant never testified; her accusation reached the tribal court exclusively through the testimony of her physician, her parents, and a police investigator repeating her statements. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the right "to confront the witnesses against him is a fundamental right," *Pointer v. Texas*, 380 U.S. 400, 403 (1965), and that the introduction of an accuser's out-of-court statement, without the accuser being subject to cross-examination, offends that guarantee. *Douglas v. Alabama*, 380 U.S. 415, 419 (1965) (admission of an accomplice's statement implicating the defendant violated confrontation where the accomplice could not be cross-examined); *Bruton v. United States*, 391 U.S. 123, 126 (1968) (same principle barring use of an out-of-court accusatory statement absent opportunity for cross-examination). Nor could the tribe rely on any claim of unavailability, for the Confrontation Clause permits substitution of out-of-court statements only where "the prosecutorial authorities have made a good-faith effort" to produce the witness. *Barber v. Page*, 390 U.S. 719, 725 (1968). No such showing was attempted here; the child was simply never called.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the writ of habeas corpus should issue.
