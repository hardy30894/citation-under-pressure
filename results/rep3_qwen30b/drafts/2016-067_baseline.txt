**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt who collected on its own account, was not a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6) of the FDCPA. The statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion.

### I. THE FDCPA REGULATES DEBT COLLECTORS, NOT CREDITORS COLLECTING ON THEIR OWN ACCOUNTS

The FDCPA’s core purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). It does not regulate creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293, 115 S. Ct. 1489, 131 L. Ed. 2d 395 (1995). This distinction is central to the Act’s structure. As Congress made clear, the term “debt collector” includes only those persons whose principal business is debt collection or who regularly collect debts owed to others. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(1)-(2). A creditor who purchases a debt and then collects it for its own benefit is not a “debt collector” under the Act—regardless of whether the debt was in default at the time of purchase.

The plain language of § 1692a(6) confirms this. The definition begins with two inclusive clauses:  
> (1) any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the **principal purpose** of which is the collection of any debts; or  
> (2) who **regularly collects or attempts to collect**, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.

15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). These definitions are mutually exclusive from the category of “creditor,” which is defined as “any person who owns or claims to own a debt.” Id. § 1692a(4). The Act’s framework thus hinges on *who is collecting* and *for whom*—not on the status of the debt at the time of acquisition.

Here, Santander did not collect on behalf of another. The complaint itself alleges that CitiFinancial Auto sold the defaulted loans to Santander on December 1, 2011, and that thereafter Santander “began communicating with [the plaintiffs] ... in an attempt to collect on the alleged debts.” The complaint further states that Santander acquired the receivables “for a few cents on the dollar,” indicating a commercial investment—not a servicing arrangement. Public SEC filings confirm that Santander purchased $3.55 billion in loan receivables as part of a portfolio transaction. Once the sale closed, Santander became the owner of the debt. When it sought to collect, it did so for its own account, not as an agent for CitiFinancial Auto.

This is precisely the scenario Congress intended to exclude from the FDCPA’s reach. As the Senate Report explains, the Act was not meant to cover “the activities of ... mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3–4 (1977), reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. But even more importantly, the Act’s exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii)—which excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was not in default at the time it was obtained—proves the point: Congress *did* consider default status in crafting the statute, but only as a factor in determining whether a person is *excluded* from being a debt collector. It did not make default status the *threshold* for inclusion.

### II. THE PLAINTIFFS’ INTERPRETATION CONTRADICTS THE STATUTORY TEXT AND STRUCTURE

The petitioners argue that because Santander acquired the debts *after* default, it must be a “debt collector.” This argument fails for three reasons.

First, it misreads the statutory hierarchy. The definition of “debt collector” contains both *inclusions* and *exclusions*. To qualify as a debt collector, a person must first satisfy one of the inclusive definitions in § 1692a(6)(1) or (2). Only then may the exclusions apply. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015). Here, Santander does not meet either inclusive definition: it is not engaged in a business whose principal purpose is debt collection, nor does it regularly collect debts owed to others. Its collection activity was limited to debts it had purchased and now owned.

Second, the petitioners’ interpretation would render the phrase “debts owed or due another” meaningless. If a person collects a debt it owns—even if it was in default when acquired—it is still collecting “on its own account.” The phrase “owed or due another” refers to the *current* relationship between the debtor and the collector. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318 (“Our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection”). The fact that the debt was once owed to CitiFinancial Auto is irrelevant once Santander became the owner.

Third, the petitioners’ theory would create a regulatory loophole in reverse: every entity that ever collected a debt for another would remain a “debt collector” forever—even after purchasing the debt and acting as a creditor. That cannot be Congress’s intent.
