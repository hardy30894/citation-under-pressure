**ARGUMENT**

**I. Genuine Issues of Material Fact Preclude Summary Judgment on Erdman's FMLA Eligibility.**

**A. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews the grant of summary judgment with the same rigor that guided the district courts of an earlier era: affidavits and depositions cannot substitute for the trial to which a plaintiff is entitled when the record yields conflicting inferences. As the Supreme Court cautioned in *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944), "[t]rial by affidavit is no substitute for trial by jury which so long has been the safeguard of British and American justice." *Id.* at 628. That admonition applies with special force where, as here, the ultimate question turns on the employer's own characterization of disputed hours and disputed motive. In *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), the Court held that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" where "motive and intent play leading roles" and "the proof is largely in the hands of" the party seeking judgment. *Id.* at 473. Nationwide's control over Erdman's timekeeping records, and its shifting position on whether her extra hours "counted," place this case squarely within that rule.

**B. Erdman's Authorized Overtime Hours Must Be Counted Toward the Statutory Threshold.**

The District Court treated the 1,250-hour question as a matter of arithmetic resolved entirely by Nationwide's payroll ledger. But an employer's records are not sacrosanct where, as here, the employer's own conduct created ambiguity about which hours were "worked." This Court's predecessors confronted an analogous problem under the Fair Labor Standards Act, the statutory cousin from which the FMLA borrows its concept of compensable hours. In *Tennessee Coal, Iron & R.R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123*, 321 U.S. 590 (1944), the Court refused to let an employer define "work" narrowly so as to exclude time the employee actually spent laboring under the employer's direction, holding that the statutory purpose required a construction faithful to "the widest possible impact" of its remedial design. *Id.* at 597.

That principle was sharpened two years later in *Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680 (1946), where the Court held that when an employer's records are inaccurate or inadequate, the employee need only "produce[] sufficient evidence to show the amount and extent of that work as a matter of just and reasonable inference." *Id.* at 687. The burden then shifts to the employer to negate that inference; it may not sit on incomplete records and demand precision from the employee. *Id.* at 687–88.

Here, Erdman's supervisor, Sarno, expressly authorized her extra hours and either paid for them or credited them as "comp" time. When Getgen succeeded Sarno, she was told of this arrangement in September 2002 and did not disclaim it; indeed, in the January 2003 meeting she accepted Erdman's explanation of "comp" time without objection when reconciling vacation balances. Only on February 10, 2003 — after Erdman had already accrued the disputed hours — did Getgen declare that such hours would no longer be honored. Under *Mt. Clemens Pottery*, Erdman's testimony regarding the hours she actually worked, corroborated by Sarno's own account of her reliability and the payment or crediting of that time, is sufficient to raise a jury question as to whether she reached the statutory threshold. The District Court's contrary conclusion improperly resolved a factual dispute that *Tennessee Coal* and *Mt. Clemens Pottery* commit to the trier of fact.

**C. Nationwide Is Estopped From Disavowing Hours It Previously Authorized and Credited.**

Even apart from the evidentiary force of Erdman's testimony, Nationwide should be estopped from now denying that her extra hours counted. The doctrine of equitable estoppel, articulated by the Supreme Court in *Dickerson v. Colgrove*, 100 U.S. 578 (1879), holds that "he who by his language or conduct leads another to do what he would not otherwise have done shall not subject such person to loss or injury by disappointing the expectations upon which he acted." *Id.* at 580. Sarno's consistent authorization of extra hours, and Getgen's acquiescence in the "comp" time arrangement through January 2003, induced Erdman to continue working those hours in reliance on the understanding that they would be recognized. Nationwide cannot, after the fact, retroactively strip those hours of legal significance to defeat her FMLA eligibility. The same equitable principle that protected the purchaser who relied on a seller's boundary representations in *Dickerson* protects an employee who relied on her employer's representations regarding compensable time.

**II. Conclusion**

Because the record — read in the light most favorable to Erdman as required by *Sartor* and *Poller* — permits a reasonable inference that she worked the requisite 1,250 hours, and because Nationwide is estopped from disclaiming hours it authorized and credited, the District Court's grant of summary judgment on the FMLA claim should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
