**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SCHOOL-ZONE STATUTE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE BECAUSE IT FAILS TO SPECIFY HOW TO MEASURE THE 1,000-FOOT DISTANCE FROM A SCHOOL.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that criminal statutes provide “sufficiently definite warning as to the proscribed conduct when measured by common understanding and practices.” *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 266 (1997) (quoting *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 348 (1971)). A statute is void for vagueness if it “either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning and differ as to its application.” *Connally v. Gen. Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). Section 922(q)(2)(A) fails this standard because it does not specify whether the 1,000-foot distance should be measured “as the crow flies,” along public roads, or by some other method.

This Court has recognized that vagueness challenges may succeed where a statute “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited.” *United States v. Wight*, 17 F.3d 376, 380 (1st Cir. 1994). Here, the statute’s silence on the method of measurement creates precisely that uncertainty. The government’s own expert conceded at trial that the distance could be measured in multiple ways, yet the jury was never instructed on which method to use. This lack of guidance renders the statute impermissibly vague. *See Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 358 (1983) (holding that a statute must “give a person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited”).

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO ESTABLISH MENS REA FOR THE MACHINE-GUN POSSESSION CHARGE.**

To convict Nieves-Castaño under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the government was required to prove she knowingly possessed a machine gun. *See Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994) (holding that § 922(o) requires proof of mens rea). The evidence at trial was insufficient to meet this burden.

Nieves-Castaño testified that she stored the AK-47 at the request of Alexis, who owned the weapon. She claimed she had only briefly looked inside the bag and knew it contained a rifle, but not that it was a machine gun. The government presented no evidence that she knew the rifle’s firing mechanism had been modified to fire automatically. *See United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994) (requiring proof of knowledge of the illegal nature of the conduct).

The jury’s verdict cannot stand because no rational trier of fact could conclude beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was a machine gun. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The evidence showed only that she knew a rifle was present—not its functional characteristics.

**III. THE TRIAL COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MACHINE-GUN CHARGE.**

The district court instructed the jury that “knowingly” meant only that Nieves-Castaño was aware she possessed a firearm, not that she knew it was a machine gun. This was error. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 619 (holding that § 922(o) requires proof of knowledge that the weapon is a machine gun). The instruction diluted the mens rea requirement and permitted conviction without proof of the statutorily mandated mental state. *See United States v. Alarid*, 406 F.3d 1201, 1205 (10th Cir. 2005) (reversing a § 922(o) conviction where the jury was not required to find knowledge of the weapon’s automatic nature).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Nieves-Castaño’s convictions should be reversed. The school-zone statute is unconstitutionally vague, the evidence was insufficient to prove mens rea for the machine-gun charge, and the jury was improperly instructed.
