The judgment should be affirmed only if Milford’s exclusion of the Club was a permissible limitation on speech in a limited public forum and was independently required by the Establishment Clause. It was neither.

Milford opened its facilities after hours to district residents for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” Once the government creates such a forum, it may not exclude speakers because officials dislike their viewpoint. The First Amendment “bar[s]” discrimination against speech based on viewpoint in a forum the government has opened for expression. Police Dept. of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92, 96 (1972). Although Mosley post-dates 1970, the same principle follows from earlier cases holding that the State may not regulate expression by suppressing one side of a public controversy. See Terminiello v. City of Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); Meyer v. Nebraska, 262 U.S. 390, 400 (1923).

Milford’s exclusion was viewpoint discrimination. The Club sought to teach moral and character development from a Christian perspective. The school allowed secular youth groups to use its facilities for analogous purposes, but denied the Club because its moral teaching was religious. That is the suppression of a particular viewpoint on an otherwise permitted subject. The Constitution does not permit the State to prefer “orthodox” over “unorthodox” opinion. West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943). Nor may government compel silence when expression is unwelcome because it conveys a disfavored belief. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937); Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939).

Milford’s attempt to characterize the Club’s speech as “religious worship” does not change the analysis. This Court has long held that religious expression is protected speech, not a category outside the First Amendment. In Cantwell, the Court reversed a conviction arising from religious solicitation and described the constitutional protection for “the free exercise of religion.” 310 U.S. at 303. In Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943), the Court held that the distribution of religious literature and the solicitation of contributions were protected activities, not a taxable privilege. In Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944), the Court likewise rejected burdens placed on the dissemination of religious views. And in Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951), the Court condemned discriminatory denial of a permit for religious meetings. Milford’s exclusion here is the same vice in a school forum.

Nor does the Establishment Clause justify the exclusion. The Clause forbids the government from establishing religion, but it does not authorize viewpoint censorship of private religious speech. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), recognized that the State must remain neutral among religions and between religion and nonreligion. The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that neutrality requires hostility. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), upheld an accommodation of religious instruction and explained that government may not “show a callous indifference to religious groups.” Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 431-32 (1962), and School Dist. of Abington Twp. v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), prohibited government-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools, but they did not hold that a public school may exclude private religious speech from an otherwise open forum. To the contrary, a school that permits secular clubs and community groups to meet after hours does not establish religion by allowing equal access to a private religious club. The constitutional command is neutrality, not exclusion. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314.

Finally, Milford’s concern that children might perceive the Club’s speech as school endorsement cannot sustain its policy. The Establishment Clause forbids official sponsorship of worship, as in Engel, 370 U.S. at 425-26, and Abington, 374 U.S. at 223-24, but it does not permit the State to suppress private religious expression simply because it occurs on school property after hours. When the government opens its facilities to private speakers on neutral terms, the risk of perceived endorsement is addressed by equal treatment and clear disclaimers, not exclusion. See Marsh v. Chambers, 463 U.S. 783, 792-95 (1983) (government neutrality does not require eradication of every religious reference from public life). For the pre-1970 authorities controlling the issue of religious liberty, see also Reynolds v. United States, 98 U.S. 145, 162-64 (1878); Pierce v. Society of Sisters, 268 U.S. 510, 534-35 (1925); and Prince v. Massachusetts, 321 U.S. 158, 166-67 (1944).

Because Milford excluded the Club on the basis of its religious viewpoint, and because the Establishment Clause does not compel such discrimination, the judgment should be reversed.
