# ARGUMENT

## A CONVICTION UNDER 18 U.S.C. §875(c) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENT TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS A THREAT

### I. The Text and Structure of §875(c) Require Proof of Mens Rea as to the Threatening Character of the Communication

Section 875(c) criminalizes transmitting "any communication containing any threat to injure the person of another." This Court has long held that "silence" in a criminal statute as to mental state "does not necessarily suggest that Congress intended to dispense with a conventional mens rea element." *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994). Indeed, this Court has recognized "the background presumption of our criminal law" that "requires knowledge of a critical fact separates innocent from wrongful conduct." *Elonis v. United States* draws on principles this Court articulated in *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952), where the Court explained that mens rea is a doctrine deeply "as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will."

The presumption in favor of scienter applies with particular force where, as here, the absence of an intent requirement would criminalize a "broad range of apparently innocent conduct." *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419, 426 (1985). A purely objective, negligence-based standard—asking merely whether a "reasonable person" would foresee that his words would be understood as threatening—collapses the distinction between the wrongdoer who means to threaten and the speaker who is simply misunderstood. This Court has cautioned against reading criminal statutes to impose liability on "otherwise law-abiding citizens" who engage in "a wide range of apparently innocent conduct." *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 610-12. The mental state requirement must attach to the fact that makes the conduct wrongful—the threatening nature of the communication itself.

### II. The First Amendment's "True Threats" Doctrine Requires Proof That the Speaker Subjectively Intended to Threaten

This Court held in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), that "true threats" encompass "statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). This formulation is inescapably subjective: the Court asked what the *speaker* "means to communicate," not what a reasonable listener might perceive. The Court in *Black* further explained that "the speaker need not actually intend to carry out the threat" in order for a statement to constitute a true threat, *id.*—a qualification that would be entirely superfluous unless the Court presupposed that the speaker must intend to threaten in the first place, even if he has no intention of following through.

The Third Circuit's reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), for an objective "reasonable speaker" standard cannot survive *Black*. The court below reasoned that *Black*'s formulation did not displace *Kosma* because a subjective standard would fail to protect the public from the fear that the "true threat" exception is designed to prevent. But this policy rationale, whatever its merit, cannot override this Court's clear articulation of the doctrine's mens rea requirement. Protecting listeners from fear is the *rationale* for excluding true threats from First Amendment protection; it does not answer the antecedent question of what mental state converts protected expression into an unprotected "true threat."

This Court's speech-protective jurisprudence confirms that an objective, negligence-based standard sweeps too broadly. In *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46, 52 (1988), the Court emphasized that "in public debate we must tolerate insulting, and even outrageous, speech in order to provide adequate breathing space to the freedoms protected by the First Amendment" (internal quotation marks omitted). Similarly, in *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 927-28 (1982), this Court held that "strong and effective extemporaneous rhetoric" containing violent imagery, including threats such as "if we catch any of you going in any of them racist stores, we're gonna break your damn neck," did not lose First Amendment protection absent proof that the speaker intended to incite or produce imminent violence. A reasonable-listener test would criminalize precisely the kind of overheated, hyperbolic rhetoric that *Claiborne Hardware* protects, chilling the "breathing space" that *Falwell* demands.

### III. An Objective Standard Impermissibly Punishes Negligent Speech in Violation of the First Amendment

The Third Circuit's objective standard reduces §875(c) to a negligence offense, punishing speakers for failing to foresee how their words might be received—not for intending to threaten. This Court has never sanctioned criminal punishment of pure speech on a negligence theory. To the contrary, in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam), this Court reversed a conviction for a statement threatening the President, holding that the "true threats" exception must be interpreted "against a background of a profound national commitment to the principle that debate on public issues should be uninhibited, robust, and wide-open," quoting *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 270 (1964). The *Watts* Court held that the defendant's statement—that if drafted, "the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J."—was "political hyperbole" rather than a true threat, notwithstanding that a literal, objective reading might have suggested otherwise. 394 U.S. at 708. This inquiry into the speaker's actual meaning is a subjective inquiry, incompatible with the Third Circuit's reasonable-listener test.

The rap-lyric context of Mr. Elonis's Facebook posts—an art form steeped in violent, hyperbolic imagery—illustrates precisely why an objective standard risks punishing protected expression as criminal threats. Just as the Claiborne Hardware speaker's rhetoric had to be assessed for what the speaker actually intended, so too must Mr. Elonis's statements be assessed according to his own subjective intent, not the reactions of an objectively reasonable listener unfamiliar with the expressive conventions he invoked.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten, reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit, and remand for further proceedings.
