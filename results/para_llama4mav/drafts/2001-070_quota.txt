ARGUMENT

The execution of mentally retarded individuals is not categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that his mental retardation precludes a death sentence. In Penry, this Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded individuals. Id. at 340. Although the Court acknowledged that mental retardation can be a mitigating factor, it declined to establish a per se rule against executing the mentally retarded. Id. at 338-40.

Since Penry, there has been no significant shift in the national consensus regarding the execution of mentally retarded individuals. While some states have enacted legislation prohibiting the practice, see, e.g., Ky. Rev. Stat. Ann. § 532.130 (2001); Tenn. Code Ann. § 39-13-203 (2001), others continue to permit it. See, e.g., Va. Code Ann. § 19.2-264.3:1.1 (2002) (repealed 2003). The lack of a clear national consensus counsels against a categorical prohibition on executing the mentally retarded. See Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989) (rejecting a claim that the Eighth Amendment prohibits capital punishment for 16- and 17-year-old offenders due to a lack of national consensus).

Atkins argues that his mental retardation renders him less culpable and therefore ineligible for the death penalty. However, this Court has consistently held that individualized sentencing determinations are sufficient to account for mitigating factors, including mental retardation. See, e.g., Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262, 272-73 (1976) (plurality opinion); Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 206-07 (1976) (plurality opinion). The jury in Atkins' case was presented with evidence of his mental retardation during the penalty phase, and it was entitled to weigh this factor in determining his sentence. See, e.g., Franklin v. Lynaugh, 487 U.S. 164, 172-73 (1988) (plurality opinion).

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" is not a static concept, but rather one that evolves with changing societal norms. See Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). However, this Court has also cautioned that "the Amendment must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." Id. (emphasis added). In the absence of a clear national consensus against executing the mentally retarded, it cannot be said that such a practice offends contemporary standards of decency. See, e.g., McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279, 300-01 (1987).

The decision to execute mentally retarded individuals is a matter for the states to determine through their legislative processes. See, e.g., Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984). In the absence of a clear national consensus, this Court should not impose a categorical prohibition on the practice. See, e.g., Pulley v. Harris, 465 U.S. 37, 43-44 (1984).

In conclusion, the execution of mentally retarded individuals is not categorically prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied Penry v. Lynaugh, and Atkins' sentence should be affirmed.
