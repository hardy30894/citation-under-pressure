## Argument

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Bora Corp., Ltd. ("the ship") because there is a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether the ship's crew was actively involved in the operations on the vessel at the time of Serbin's accident, thereby breaching its duty to exercise reasonable care under the circumstances. Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), established that a shipowner has a duty to exercise reasonable care to avoid injuries to longshoremen in areas under the ship's active control or where the ship has actual knowledge of a hazard and the longshoremen's ignorance of it. Id. at 167.

The ship's crew was responsible for moving the snatch blocks, and it is undisputed that one of the blocks was left in the down position, creating a hazard. Serbin's decision to move the block was reasonable given the circumstances, as he believed it was obstructing the removal of cargo and creating a safety risk. The fact that the crew had previously left the block in the down position suggests that they may have been actively involved in the operations on the vessel, or at the very least, had actual knowledge of the hazard.

The Third Circuit has held that a shipowner's duty to exercise reasonable care includes a duty to intervene when it has actual knowledge of a hazardous condition and the stevedore's judgment is "obviously improvident." Davis v. Portline Transportes Maritime Internacional, 16 F.3d 532, 536 (3d Cir. 1994) (citing Scindia, 451 U.S. at 175-76). Here, the ship's crew had a responsibility to move the snatch blocks, and their failure to do so may be seen as a breach of their duty to exercise reasonable care.

Furthermore, the fact that Serbin had moved blocks in the past without difficulty does not necessarily mean that he was contributorily negligent. The reasonableness of Serbin's actions is a question for the jury to decide. Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026, 1030 (3d Cir. 1992) ("[T]he question of whether a longshoreman's actions were reasonable is typically a question for the jury.").

In light of the above, the district court's grant of summary judgment was improper. There is a genuine issue of material fact regarding the ship's liability, and the case should be remanded for trial. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
