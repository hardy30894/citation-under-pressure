# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 1113(2)'s "Actual Knowledge" Requirement Is Satisfied Once a Fiduciary Discloses the Facts Underlying the Alleged Breach.

The three-year bar of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) turns on "actual knowledge of the breach or violation," not on knowledge that the breach violated the law. This Court has long distinguished knowledge of a transaction from knowledge of its legal significance, holding that a party's ignorance of the law does not excuse delay once the facts constituting the wrong are known to him. That principle governs here: once Intel disclosed the fact and nature of its alternative-investment strategy, Sulyma possessed the only "knowledge" section 1113(2) requires.

This Court's fraud-discovery jurisprudence confirms that a party cannot avoid a limitations bar by claiming he never absorbed information placed before him. In Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135 (1879), the Court held that a plaintiff who had access to the facts underlying his claim could not toll the statute merely because he failed to appreciate their significance, explaining that "the means of knowledge are the same thing in effect as knowledge itself." Id. at 143. This Court reaffirmed that rule in Exploration Co. v. United States, 247 U.S. 435 (1918), again instructing that where the facts constituting a claim are placed within a plaintiff's reach, the plaintiff is charged with knowledge of them regardless of whether he in fact absorbed the disclosure. Sulyma's position—that a participant may disclaim "actual knowledge" of information Intel unquestionably placed before him—cannot be reconciled with this settled rule.

## II. A Plan Participant Who Receives Plan Disclosures Is Charged With Knowledge of Their Contents as a Matter of Law.

The Ninth Circuit's rule—requiring proof that a participant subjectively absorbed and recalled every disclosed document—inverts the settled common-law principle that a person who receives a document is bound by its contents whether or not he chooses to read it. In Upton v. Tribilcock, 91 U.S. 45 (1875), this Court refused to permit a party to escape the consequences of a written instrument by professing ignorance of its terms, holding that "[i]t will not do for a man to enter into a contract, and, when called upon to respond to its obligations, to say that he did not read it when he signed it, or did not know what it contained." Id. at 50. The same rule of law that binds a contracting party to the terms he was given—regardless of whether he read them—should bind a plan participant to the disclosures Intel gave him. ERISA imposes extensive statutory disclosure obligations precisely so that fiduciaries can discharge their duties by furnishing information; it would defeat that scheme to hold that a participant's failure to read what was furnished preserves his claim indefinitely.

This principle likewise pervades the law of notice. In Rosenthal v. Walker, 111 U.S. 185 (1884), this Court held that a properly directed communication is presumed received, a presumption "not open to be denied by other than affirmative evidence of a satisfactory kind." Id. at 193. The presumption exists because the law cannot function if every recipient of information may deny its receipt through simple denial of subjective recollection. The same logic applies to fund fact sheets, summary plan descriptions, and website disclosures furnished to Sulyma: once the record establishes that Intel gave Sulyma the relevant documents, he cannot indefinitely preserve his claim through post hoc testimony that he does not recall reading them.

Nor is willful inattention a shield. Where a party structures his own conduct to avoid confronting readily available facts, courts have long treated that avoidance as equivalent to knowledge. See Spies v. United States, 317 U.S. 492 (1943) (knowledge and intent may be inferred from a course of conduct designed to avoid confirmation of facts one has reason to know). A participant who has access to fund fact sheets and annual disclosures cannot transform his own failure to read them into a permanent tolling device.

## III. The Purposes of Statutes of Limitations Require a Fixed, Administrable Standard, Not One Dependent on Self-Serving Recollection.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that limitations periods exist to promote repose and prevent the litigation of stale claims based on faded memories and lost evidence. In Order of Railroad Telegraphers v. Railway Express Agency, 321 U.S. 342 (1944), the Court explained that such statutes "are vital to the welfare of society and are favored in the law," representing a judgment that "the right to be free of stale claims in time comes to prevail over the right to prosecute them." Id. at 348–49. Likewise, in Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304 (1945), the Court observed that statutes of limitations "find their justification in necessity and convenience rather than in logic," and represent legislative "expedients" that courts should not dilute through equitable exceptions. Id. at 314. And in Holmberg v. Armbrecht, 327 U.S. 392 (1946), this Court cautioned that tolling doctrines apply only where a plaintiff remains ignorant "without any fault or want of diligence on his part." Id. at 397. A rule permitting a participant to defeat the three-year bar merely by testifying that he does not recall reading disclosures he received would eviscerate the certainty Congress sought when it enacted a firm, fact-based "actual knowledge" trigger. The judgment below should be reversed.
