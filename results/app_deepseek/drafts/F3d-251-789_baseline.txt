## ARGUMENT

The district court clearly erred in finding that Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero consented to the removal and retention of Danelsy within the meaning of the Hague Convention. The court’s factual findings are unsupported by the record and reflect a fundamental misunderstanding of the consent defense.

### I. The District Court Applied an Incorrect Legal Standard for Consent

The Hague Convention’s exceptions to the mandatory return of a wrongfully removed child are to be construed narrowly. *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 78 F.3d 1060, 1067 (6th Cir. 1996). Consent must be “clear and unequivocal” and “not conditional.” *Baxter v. Baxter*, 423 F.3d 363, 371 (3d Cir. 2005). The respondent bears the burden of proving consent by a preponderance of the evidence. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B). However, because the exception undermines the Convention’s core purpose of deterring abduction, many courts require “clear and convincing evidence” of consent. *See, e.g., In re Prevot*, 59 F.3d 556, 560 (6th Cir. 1995) (requiring “clear and convincing” proof for affirmative defenses). The district court applied only a preponderance standard and failed to require that Mena’s evidence be clear and unequivocal.

### II. The Record Does Not Support a Finding of Unconditional Consent

Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero’s testimony—the only direct evidence of her state of mind—consistently described a *conditional* agreement. She stated that she allowed Danelsy to travel to the United States for two weeks until she could obtain the proper paperwork to join her daughter. Alternatively, she agreed to Mena’s removal of Danelsy only on the condition that he help her immigrate to the United States, at which point she would reassume custody. Both accounts describe a temporary arrangement, not a permanent relinquishment of custody.

Mena’s own testimony confirms the conditional nature of the agreement. He told Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero that he would “try to help her legally immigrate to the United States if she made the preliminary arrangements through her sister.” This is not an unconditional consent to permanent removal; it is a promise of future assistance in exchange for temporary custody. Conditional consent is not consent under the Convention. *Mozes v. Mozes*, 239 F.3d 1067, 1077 (9th Cir. 2001) (holding that consent must be “unequivocal” and not “subject to conditions”).

The district court relied heavily on the fact that Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero gave Mena all of Danelsy’s legal documents—birth certificates, passport, and military identification. But providing documents necessary for travel is entirely consistent with a temporary visit. It does not prove that she consented to permanent retention. Indeed, if she had intended to relinquish custody permanently, she would have had no reason to expect to join Danelsy later. Her immediate and persistent efforts to recover Danelsy—contacting Panamanian authorities, the police, the U.S. Judge Advocate General Corps, the Institute for Children, the Panamanian President’s office, and radio stations—demonstrate that she never consented to permanent removal. *See Friedrich*, 78 F.3d at 1068 (noting that a parent’s prompt efforts to recover a child are inconsistent with consent).

### III. The District Court’s Credibility Determinations Are Clearly Erroneous

The district court credited Mena’s account that Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero told him she could no longer care for Danelsy because she was pregnant and abandoned. But this account is contradicted by her immediate and sustained efforts to regain custody. A parent who voluntarily relinquishes a child does not, within days, consult child custody authorities, police, and the President’s office. The district court’s finding that Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero consented is thus “illogical, implausible, or without support in the record.” *Anderson v. City of Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 577 (1985).

### IV. The District Court Improperly Shifted the Burden of Proof

The district court appears to have required Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero to prove that she did *not* consent. But the burden rests squarely on Mena to prove consent by at least a preponderance of the evidence. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B). The court’s analysis focused on the weaknesses in Ms. Gonzalez-Caballero’s testimony rather than on whether Mena met his burden. This is reversible error. *See Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1076 (reversing where district court improperly placed burden on petitioner).

### Conclusion

The district court’s finding of consent is clearly erroneous and unsupported by the record. This Court should reverse and order the return of Danelsy to Panama.
