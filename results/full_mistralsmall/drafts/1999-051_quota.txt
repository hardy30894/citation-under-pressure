**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

The rule is settled: a federal habeas petitioner must show both “cause” and “prejudice” to excuse a procedural default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 485 (1986). “Cause” exists where the procedural default resulted from “some objective factor external to the defense” or “an error by counsel that cannot be fairly attributed to the petitioner.” *Id.* at 488. But when the asserted cause is a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, the petitioner must satisfy the *Strickland* standard—both deficient performance and prejudice—*before* the defaulted claim can be considered on the merits. *Id.* at 488–89; *see also Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 622–23 (1998) (holding that a procedurally defaulted claim cannot be reached unless the petitioner shows cause and prejudice, and that ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause only if the claim satisfies *Strickland*).

The Sixth Circuit erred in excusing Carpenter’s default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim based solely on his assertion of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel. The court reasoned that the ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause regardless of whether it, too, was procedurally defaulted. *Carpenter v. Mohr*, 180 F.3d 774, 781 (6th Cir. 1999). But this approach contravenes *Carrier* and its progeny. In *Carrier*, the Supreme Court explicitly rejected the notion that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could automatically excuse another default. 477 U.S. at 489. The Court cautioned that “[t]he mere fact that counsel may have rendered ineffective assistance does not suffice to justify an exception to the exhaustion requirement.” *Id.* at 488. To hold otherwise would permit petitioners to bypass procedural rules by bootstrapping one default to another. *See also Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991) (rejecting the argument that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could excuse another default, noting that “[t]he rule is well settled that a habeas petitioner may not raise an issue on federal habeas review that was procedurally defaulted in state court”).

The Sixth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with the decisions of other circuits. The Fourth Circuit has held that “a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim.” *Bryant v. United States*, 136 F.3d 761, 764 (4th Cir. 1998). Similarly, the Seventh Circuit has recognized that “[a]n ineffective-assistance claim, even if meritorious, cannot excuse a procedural default unless the petitioner can show that the ineffective assistance prevented him from raising the defaulted claim in the first instance.” *Gray v. Peters*, 102 F.3d 92, 94 (7th Cir. 1996). The Eleventh Circuit has likewise refused to allow a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to serve as cause for another default, emphasizing that “[t]he *Strickland* standard must be met before a defaulted claim can be considered.” *Ford v. Georgia*, 141 F.3d 1417, 1420 (11th Cir. 1998).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule would undermine the finality of state court judgments and encourage piecemeal litigation. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 730–31 (explaining that procedural default rules serve the interests of finality and comity). It would also permit petitioners to circumvent the exhaustion requirement by raising a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim in federal court without first presenting it to the state courts. *See Duncan v. Henry*, 513 U.S. 364, 365–66 (1995) (per curiam) (holding that a claim must be fairly presented to the state courts before it can be considered on federal habeas review).

**II. Even if the ineffective-assistance claim were not defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of the sufficiency claim.**

Assuming arguendo that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was not procedurally defaulted, it still cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. The Supreme Court has made clear that “[a]n ineffective-assistance claim may constitute cause for a procedural default only if the petitioner can show that the ineffective assistance prevented him from raising the defaulted claim in the first instance.” *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623 (quoting *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488). This requires a showing that counsel’s deficient performance “actually caused” the default. *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488.

Here, Carpenter’s appellate counsel did not prevent him from raising the sufficiency claim on direct appeal; indeed, Carpenter did raise a claim on direct appeal—he challenged the length of his sentence. *See Carpenter v. Mohr*, 180 F.3d at 777. The sufficiency claim was not raised because Carpenter chose not to raise it, not because his counsel prevented him from doing so. Thus, even if Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim were meritorious, it would not satisfy the *Carrier* standard, as it did not cause the default of the sufficiency claim. *See Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 623 (holding that the petitioner’s ineffective-assistance claim did not constitute cause because it did not prevent him from raising the defaulted claim earlier).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion is unsupported by the Supreme Court’s decisions. In *Carrier*, the Court rejected the argument that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could automatically excuse another default, emphasizing that the petitioner must show that the ineffective assistance *prevented* the defaulted claim from being raised. 477 U.S. at 488. The Court’s decision in *Bousley* reaffirmed this principle, holding that the petitioner’s ineffective-assistance claim did not constitute cause because it did not prevent him from raising the defaulted claim earlier. 523 U.S. at 623.

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Circuit erred in excusing Carpenter’s procedural default of his sufficiency claim based on his assertion of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another default, and even if it were not defaulted, it would not satisfy the *Carrier* standard. The judgment below should be reversed.
