## Argument

The Bureau of Prisons' ("BOP") denial of Victor Roussos' eligibility for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) was erroneous because it was based on an overly broad interpretation of the term "crime of violence." The BOP's Program Statement 5162.02, which categorizes Roussos' offense as a crime of violence due to the sentencing court's two-level enhancement for possession of a firearm, is inconsistent with the congressional statute and relevant case law.

The statute authorizing early release, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), does not define "nonviolent offense," but the BOP's regulations define it by referencing the term "crime of violence" as used in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). Section 924(c)(3) defines a "crime of violence" as an offense that is a felony and either (A) has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person or property of another, or (B) involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used in the course of committing the offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).

The BOP's Program Statement 5162.02 expands on this definition, stating that a conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 (conspiracy to distribute narcotics) is a crime of violence if the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a dangerous weapon. However, this interpretation is not supported by the case law. In United States v. Canon, 66 F.3d 1073 (9th Cir. 1995), the Ninth Circuit held that a conviction for possession of a firearm during a drug trafficking crime is not necessarily a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3). The court reasoned that the statute requires more than mere possession of a firearm; it requires a nexus between the firearm and the crime. Id. at 1079.

Similarly, in United States v. Cruz, 805 F. Supp. 104 (D. Conn. 1992), the district court held that a conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics is not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3) simply because the defendant possessed a firearm during the conspiracy. The court noted that the government must prove that the defendant used or carried the firearm during and in relation to the crime. Id. at 108.

The Third Circuit has also addressed the issue of what constitutes a "crime of violence" under § 924(c)(3). In United States v. Johnson, 962 F.2d 233 (3d Cir. 1992), the court held that a conviction for bank robbery is a crime of violence because it involves a substantial risk that physical force may be used. However, the court also noted that not all felonies involving firearms are crimes of violence. Id. at 236.

Other circuits have also rejected the BOP's broad interpretation of "crime of violence." In Davis v. Crabtree, 109 F.3d 566 (9th Cir. 1997), the Ninth Circuit held that the BOP's Program Statement 5162.02 is inconsistent with the statute and relevant case law. The court noted that the BOP's interpretation would render many drug convictions crimes of violence, contrary to congressional intent. Id. at 568. See also, Byrd v. Hasty, 142 F.3d 1395 (11th Cir. 1998) (holding that the BOP's interpretation is overly broad); Martin v. Gerlinski, 133 F.3d 1076 (8th Cir. 1998) (same).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of adhering to the plain meaning of statutory text. In Bailey v. United States, 516 U.S. 137 (1995), the Court held that the term "use" in § 924(c)(1) requires active employment of a firearm, rather than mere possession. The Court's reasoning in Bailey is relevant here, as it highlights the need for a nuanced understanding of the statutory language.

In addition, the categorical approach to determining whether an offense is a crime of violence, as set forth in Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), supports Roussos' argument. Under this approach, the court looks to the elements of the offense, rather than the specific circumstances of the case. See also, Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36 (1993) (holding that commentary to the Sentencing Guidelines is binding on courts).

In conclusion, the BOP's denial of Roussos' eligibility for early release was based on an overly broad interpretation of the term "crime of violence." The Program Statement 5162.02 is inconsistent with the congressional statute and relevant case law, including United States v. Canon, 66 F.3d 1073 (9th Cir. 1995), United States v. Cruz, 805 F. Supp. 104 (D. Conn. 1992), Davis v. Crabtree, 109 F.3d 566 (9th Cir. 1997), Byrd v. Hasty, 142 F.3d 1395 (11th Cir. 1998), and Martin v. Gerlinski, 133 F.3d 1076 (8th Cir. 1998). Therefore, the district court's denial of Roussos' habeas petition should be reversed, and he should be granted relief.
