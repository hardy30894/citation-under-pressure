# ARGUMENT

## A Rule 32 Violation Does Not Entitle a Defendant to Habeas Relief Where the Defendant Was Actually Aware of His Right to Appeal

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that a district court's technical failure to advise him of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) automatically entitles him to relief, regardless of whether he actually knew of that right. This position cannot be reconciled with the settled principle that habeas relief redresses actual prejudice flowing from constitutional or fundamental error—not the mere fact of a procedural misstep divorced from any resulting harm.

### 1. Habeas Relief Is Reserved for Violations That Cause Actual Prejudice

This Court has consistently required a showing of prejudice before granting habeas relief for procedural defects at sentencing. In *Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82 (1946), the Court examined a claim that a defendant was not advised of his right to counsel at sentencing, but the inquiry focused on whether the defendant was actually deprived of a fair opportunity to exercise his rights—not on the abstract fact that a procedural formality was skipped.

Similarly, in *Mempa v. Rhay*, 389 U.S. 128 (1967), this Court's discussion of *Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736 (1948), emphasized that a due process violation arose only because sentencing proceeded on "assumptions concerning his criminal record which were materially untrue." *Mempa*, 389 U.S. at 135 (quoting *Townsend*, 334 U.S. at 741). Justice Jackson's opinion in *Townsend* stressed that counsel's absence deprived the defendant of "a requirement of fair play," precisely because it resulted in the conviction being "predicated on misinformation." *Id.* Here, by contrast, the District Court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero already knew of his right to appeal. No misinformation tainted the proceeding; no prejudice resulted from the omission.

*Smith v. O'Grady*, 312 U.S. 329 (1941), likewise grounded habeas relief in actual deprivation—there, the petitioner's "ignorance, confinement and poverty had precluded the possibility of his securing counsel in order to challenge the procedure by regular processes of appeal." *Id.* at 330. The clear implication is that where ignorance does *not* preclude the exercise of a right, no constitutional infirmity exists warranting collateral relief.

### 2. Actual Knowledge of the Right to Appeal Satisfies the Purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)

Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement exists to ensure that defendants know of their appellate rights so that valuable rights are not forfeited through ignorance. Once a defendant possesses that knowledge through any means, the purpose of the Rule is fully served, and the failure to formally recite the advisement is harmless.

This principle finds support in *Woods v. Nierstheimer*, 328 U.S. 211 (1946), where the Court examined whether a defendant had been afforded a genuine opportunity to litigate his claims, rather than mechanically applying a procedural rule without regard to the actual circumstances confronting the defendant. The inquiry was functional, not formalistic.

*Hunter v. Martin*, 334 U.S. 302 (1948), reinforces this functional approach. There, this Court declined to apply a statutory provision in a manner that would produce a result contrary to the provision's evident purpose. As the Court explained, "We do not think such a result is required or intended under the statute, 18 U. S. C. § 709a, or under the terms of the sentence as imposed." *Id.* at 311. So too here: nothing in Rule 32(a)(2) or its purpose requires vacating a sentence when the defendant already possessed the very knowledge the Rule sought to impart.

### 3. Collateral Relief Traditionally Requires More Than a Bare Procedural Irregularity

This Court's habeas jurisprudence has long distinguished between defects that undermine the fundamental fairness of a proceeding and technical irregularities that cause no harm. In *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court rejected a habeas challenge to a sentencing procedure, explaining that although the petitioner raised various procedural objections, the ultimate question was whether the proceeding, viewed as a whole, deprived him of due process. Where, as here, the defendant suffered no actual impairment of his ability to exercise his rights, no such deprivation exists.

*McConnell v. Rhay*, 393 U.S. 2 (1968), is not to the contrary. That case involved the wholesale denial of the right to counsel at deferred sentencing hearings—a structural Sixth Amendment defect. As the Court there explained, "That question was settled by our decision in Mempa." *Id.* at 3. No comparable structural defect is present here, where the sole claim is a technical omission that had no bearing on the defendant's actual knowledge or conduct.

Finally, in *Sigler v. Losieau*, 396 U.S. 988 (1969), the lower courts properly examined whether a sentencing infirmity had any practical effect on the defendant's rights, rather than granting relief automatically upon identification of a procedural lapse. That same prejudice-based inquiry should govern here.

### Conclusion

Because Peguero suffered no prejudice from the District Court's failure to recite the Rule 32(a)(2) advisement—having already known of his right to appeal—the Rule's purpose was fully vindicated. Consistent with this Court's precedents in *Canizio*, *Townsend*, *Smith*, *Woods*, *Hunter*, *Gryger*, *McConnell*, and *Sigler*, habeas relief is unwarranted where no actual harm flows from a technical procedural omission. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
