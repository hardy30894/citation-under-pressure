# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS MERELY BECAUSE THE UNDERLYING LAND OR WATER IS STATE-OWNED

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that "[o]nly those lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are public lands (as such term is defined in this Act) shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). Petitioner reads this sentence to mean that once title to land or water within a conservation system unit ("CSU") passes to the State, the National Park Service ("NPS") loses all regulatory authority over activities occurring there. That reading cannot be squared with the statute's text, which speaks only to which *lands* are deemed part of a unit for purposes of the Act—not to whether NPS may regulate *conduct* occurring within the unit's boundaries irrespective of who holds title to the submerged or adjacent lands.

Congress knows how to draft a jurisdictional carve-out when it intends one. Where Congress has wished to exempt state or private land from federal regulatory reach, it has said so explicitly. See, e.g., 16 U.S.C. § 3101 (statement of purpose distinguishing "public lands" from state, Native, and private lands). Section 103(c)'s function is definitional: it clarifies that inclusion of non-public land within a unit's *boundaries* does not convert that land into "public land" for purposes of ANILCA's land-management provisions, such as those governing subsistence priorities and access rights under Title VIII. It does not purport to disestablish NPS's independent statutory authority—conferred by the Organic Act, 54 U.S.C. § 100101 *et seq.* (formerly 16 U.S.C. § 1), and by ANILCA itself—to regulate activities that occur within the physical boundaries of a national preserve in order to protect the resources and values for which the unit was created.

This Court's guidance on statutory construction compels that conclusion. "It is a fundamental canon of statutory construction that the words of a statute must be read in their context and with a view to their place in the overall statutory scheme." *Davis v. Michigan Dep't of Treasury*, 489 U.S. 803, 809 (1989). Section 103(c) must be read alongside § 1313 of ANILCA, which expressly preserves NPS's regulatory authority over waters within CSUs, and alongside § 1319, which provides that "[n]othing in [the statute] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions." 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Petitioner's reading would require this Court to treat § 103(c) as an implicit repeal of NPS's regulatory authority over navigable waters—precisely the kind of repeal by implication disfavored under settled doctrine. *See Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 549 (1974) ("[R]epeals by implication are not favored.").

## II. THE HOVERCRAFT BAN IS A VALID EXERCISE OF NPS'S AUTHORITY BECAUSE IT DOES NOT REGULATE STATE LAND AS SUCH, BUT REGULATES CONDUCT WITHIN THE PRESERVE'S BOUNDARIES

Even accepting, *arguendo*, that title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River passed to Alaska under the Submerged Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1301 *et seq.*, that fact does not exempt Sturgeon's activity from NPS's hovercraft regulation, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e). The regulation does not purport to regulate the *land* itself—it regulates a category of *conduct* (hovercraft operation) uniformly throughout units of the National Park System, including the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. Nothing in § 103(c)'s text limits this kind of generally applicable conduct regulation to only the "public lands" component of a unit.

This Court has long recognized that the federal government retains substantial authority over navigable waters even where a State holds title to the submerged lands beneath them. In *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75 (2005), Justice Scalia observed that "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters of Glacier Bay by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." *Id.* at 121 (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). The same principle applies with equal force here: the mere fact that Alaska may hold title to the riverbed beneath the Nation River does not divest the federal government of authority to regulate hovercraft use for purposes of preserving the values Congress sought to protect when it designated the Yukon-Charley as a national preserve.

## III. RESPONDENT'S INTERPRETATION IS ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE AND IS CONSISTENT WITH CONGRESSIONAL INTENT

To the extent § 103(c) is ambiguous as to its effect on NPS's regulatory authority over conduct within CSUs, the agency's longstanding interpretation—reflected in 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e) and applied uniformly since 1996—is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837 (1984). Any prior inconsistency in the agency's position is not a bar to such deference: "[a]gency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the Chevron framework." *Nat'l Cable & Telecomms. Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005).

Nor does the legislative history support Petitioner's reading. To the contrary, contemporaneous objections from the Secretary of Transportation confirm that Congress understood ANILCA to authorize the Secretary of the Interior to "promulgate and enforce boating regulations which relate to construction, performance, and equipment standards" within CSUs—authority that would be meaningless if it evaporated whenever the underlying waterway happened to be state-owned. H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 24 (1976), *reprinted in* 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4290, 4310.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals affirming summary judgment in favor of Respondent should be affirmed.
