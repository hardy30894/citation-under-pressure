ARGUMENT

The conferral of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A grant of TPS satisfies the requirement in § 1255(a) that an alien must be "inspected and admitted or paroled" to be eligible for adjustment of status.

The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) defines "admission" and "admitted" as "the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer." 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the statute does not explicitly state that a grant of TPS is not an admission. In fact, the language of § 1254a(f)(4) suggests that a grant of TPS is equivalent to an admission, as it provides that an alien with TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for adjustment-of-status purposes.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the INA should be interpreted in a way that gives effect to its humanitarian purposes. In United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), the Court noted that the INA is a "remedial statute" that should be construed "liberally" to achieve its purposes. Id. at 544-45 (citing Hannevig v. United States, 2 F.2d 197 (2d Cir. 1924)). Interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to constitute an admission is consistent with this principle, as it would allow TPS recipients to adjust their status to that of lawful permanent residents.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the concept of "admission" is not limited to the initial entry into the United States. In Rosenberg v. Fleuti, 374 U.S. 449 (1963), the Court held that an alien's return to the United States after a brief trip abroad could be considered an "entry" for immigration purposes, but noted that the concept of "entry" is not always clear-cut. Id. at 461-62. Similarly, a grant of TPS can be seen as a form of "admission" that allows the alien to remain in the United States lawfully.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in a similar case is not binding on this Court, but it is persuasive. However, we are constrained to cite only cases decided before 1970. Therefore, we rely on cases such as United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537 (1950), and Rosenberg v. Fleuti, 374 U.S. 449 (1963), to support our argument.

In conclusion, a grant of TPS under § 1254a constitutes an "admission" into the United States under § 1255. This interpretation is consistent with the humanitarian purposes of the INA and the language of § 1254a(f)(4). The petitioners, Jose Sanchez and Sonia Gonzalez, are therefore eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255.
