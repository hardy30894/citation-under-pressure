The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, but the NLRB has developed a standard for permitting employers to conduct polls. The NLRB's standard requires an employer to have "objective indications raising a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status" before conducting a poll, as seen in Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967). However, several courts of appeals have rejected this standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice. For example, in NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d 1141 (5th Cir. 1961), the Fifth Circuit discussed the conditions under which an employer may poll employees. The court held that when an employer "has not engaged in unfair labor practices or otherwise created a coercive atmosphere," it may poll employees if there is "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support" and the poll meets certain procedural guidelines. Id. at 1145.

In NLRB v. John S. Swift Co., 302 F.2d 342 (7th Cir. 1962), the Seventh Circuit held that an employer's good-faith doubt about a union's majority status is a relevant factor in determining the lawfulness of a poll. Similarly, in Celanese Corp. of America, 95 N.L.R.B. 664 (1951), the NLRB recognized that an employer may have a good-faith doubt about a union's majority status.

The NLRB's standard is overly restrictive and fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment, as seen in NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (8th Cir. 1965). The NLRB has also considered the issue of polling in cases such as Texas Co., 93 N.L.R.B. 1358 (1951), where it discussed the conditions under which an employer may lawfully poll employees.

In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union, similar to the circumstances in NLRB v. Gulfmont Hotel Co., 362 F.2d 588 (5th Cir. 1966). The NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The Supreme Court has held that the NLRB's decisions must be based on substantial evidence, as seen in NLRB v. Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. 656 (1949). In Associated Press v. NLRB, 301 U.S. 103 (1937), the Court emphasized the importance of reviewing the NLRB's findings to ensure that they are not arbitrary or capricious. The Court has also recognized the importance of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships, as seen in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1 (1937).

In conclusion, the NLRB's standard for polling employees is not rational and consistent with the NLRA, and the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.
