**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER STATE LANDS WITHIN PARK BOUNDARIES IS CONFIRMED BY THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c)**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not withdraw the Secretary’s regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within conservation system units (CSUs). *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 840 F.3d 1041, 1047 (9th Cir. 2016). Section 103(c) provides that “the management of the public lands in Alaska is vested in the Secretary [of the Interior],” and that “[t]he Secretary shall administer [CSUs] through the National Park Service.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added). The statute’s plain text does not distinguish between federal and nonfederal lands; it vests management authority in the Secretary and directs that the NPS administer *all* lands within CSUs. *See also* *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 57 (1997) (rejecting an interpretation of ANILCA that would “sever” federal and state interests in submerged lands).

The petitioner’s contrary reading—that § 103(c) implicitly carves out state lands from NPS jurisdiction—would render the statute internally inconsistent. ANILCA § 1319 explicitly states that nothing in the Act “shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing ... existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. If § 103(c) were read to exempt state lands from NPS regulation, it would conflict with § 1319’s preservation of federal regulatory authority. *See Nat’l Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005) (instructing courts to avoid interpretations that render statutory provisions “inconsistent or superfluous”).

II. **THE NPS’S HOVERCRAFT BAN APPLIES TO ALL LANDS WITHIN CSU BOUNDARIES, INCLUDING STATE-OWNED LANDS**

The NPS’s hovercraft regulation, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), prohibits the operation of hovercraft “within the boundaries of any unit of the National Park System.” The regulation does not distinguish between federal and nonfederal lands. *See also* *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 544 (1976) (holding that Congress may regulate activities on nonfederal lands within federal enclaves if the regulation is “necessary and proper” to effectuate federal purposes). The regulation is a valid exercise of the Secretary’s authority under ANILCA § 103(c) to manage all lands within CSUs.

The petitioner’s reliance on *Katie John I*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Katie John I*, the court held that ANILCA did not abrogate Alaska’s reserved water rights under the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act and the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act. *Id.* at 705. The case did not address the NPS’s regulatory authority over nonfederal lands. *See also* *Katie John II*, 247 F.3d 1032, 1040 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (distinguishing regulatory authority from water rights).

III. **THE PETITIONER LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE NPS’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY**

The petitioner has not suffered an injury-in-fact traceable to the NPS’s regulation. The NPS’s hovercraft ban applies to all lands within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including state-owned lands. The petitioner’s inability to use his hovercraft on the Nation River is a consequence of the regulation’s broad application, not a distinct injury. *See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992) (requiring a “concrete and particularized” injury).

Moreover, the petitioner’s claim that the NPS lacks jurisdiction over state lands is a legal argument, not an injury. *See Allen v. Wright*, 468 U.S. 737, 751 (1984) (holding that a plaintiff may not “rest[] his claim to relief on the legal rights or status of third parties”). The petitioner’s alleged injury—his inability to use his hovercraft—is caused by the regulation itself, not by any purported lack of jurisdiction. *See Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 134 S.Ct. 2334, 2342 (2014) (holding that a plaintiff must allege an “intention to engage in a course of conduct” that is “proscribed by a statute” and faces a “credible threat of prosecution”).

IV. **THE NPS’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY IS CONSISTENT WITH THE SUPREMACY CLAUSE**

The NPS’s regulation does not violate the Supremacy Clause. ANILCA § 103(c) vests the Secretary with management authority over all lands within CSUs, and the hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of that authority. *See* U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2. The regulation applies uniformly to all lands within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, regardless of ownership. *See also* *Hodel v. Virginia Surface Mining & Reclamation Ass’n*, 452 U.S. 264, 290 (1981) (holding that federal regulations may preempt state law if Congress has “expressly or by necessary implication” occupied the field).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA § 103(c) vests the Secretary with regulatory authority over all lands within CSUs, including state-owned lands. The NPS’s hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of that authority. The petitioner lacks standing to challenge the regulation, and the Supremacy Clause does not bar the NPS’s exercise of regulatory authority. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
