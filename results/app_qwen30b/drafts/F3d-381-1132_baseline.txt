**ARGUMENT**

The United States Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government and denying Tuthill Ranch, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment on the ground that the government’s installation and leasing of excess fiber optic capacity across Tuthill’s property did not constitute a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The undisputed facts establish that the government has permanently occupied Tuthill’s land with a physical structure—fiber optic cables—far exceeding the scope of the easement and the government’s legitimate needs, thereby effecting a compensable physical taking. This Court must reverse.

The Fifth Amendment provides that “private property [shall not] be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a “physical invasion” of private property, even if temporary, constitutes a per se taking, regardless of the government’s motive or the public benefit derived. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 435 (1982). In *Loretto*, the Court held that the permanent physical occupation of a property owner’s land by a government-mandated cable installation—no matter how minor—was a taking, even if the government’s purpose was “public” and the burden on the property was slight. Id. at 435. The Court emphasized that “the physical presence of the cable on the property is the taking,” and that “the Constitution does not permit the government to impose such a permanent physical occupation, even if the burden is small.” Id. at 436.

Here, the government installed fiber optic cables—physical structures—on Tuthill’s land pursuant to a perpetual easement. The cables remain permanently affixed to poles and towers across Tuthill’s property. The government does not dispute that the cables are physically present on Tuthill’s land. Nor does it dispute that the cables were installed in 1995 as part of a technological upgrade. The only dispute is whether the government’s use of the cables—specifically, leasing unused fibers to third-party telecommunications companies—constitutes a taking. But *Loretto* makes clear that the nature of the government’s use is irrelevant: “the physical presence of the cable on the property is the taking.” Id. at 436. The government’s decision to lease excess capacity does not transform a permanent physical occupation into a non-takings event. The mere fact that the government may have installed more cables than it currently needs does not negate the permanence of the physical presence.

The government’s argument—that the easement authorized the installation of “appurtenant signal lines necessary in connection therewith”—is legally insufficient to justify a permanent physical occupation beyond the scope of the original agreement. The easement may have authorized the installation of signal lines, but it did not authorize the government to install a system that is many times larger than necessary, especially when the excess capacity is leased to for-profit entities. The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that a property owner’s consent to a limited use can be extended to a vastly expanded, permanent occupation. In *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 175 (1979), the Court held that “a taking may occur even though the government’s action is authorized by statute or contract,” because “the Constitution’s protection against uncompensated takings is not displaced by the existence of a statutory or contractual right.” The government’s right to use the easement does not immunize it from the Fifth Amendment’s requirement of just compensation when the use exceeds the scope of the original agreement and results in a permanent physical occupation.

Moreover, the government’s own projections confirm that the excess capacity is not merely speculative—it is a deliberate overbuild based on anticipated future needs. The BPA designed the system to accommodate projected demand through 2025, but the cables were installed in 1995 and have remained in place. The government has not demonstrated that the excess fibers are necessary for any current or foreseeable public purpose. Instead, the government leases the unused fibers to private telecommunications companies, generating revenue. This commercial use of Tuthill’s land—without compensation—constitutes a physical occupation for private gain, which is precisely the kind of conduct the Fifth Amendment was designed to prevent.

The Court of Federal Claims erred in concluding that “the scope of the easement is not relevant” in determining whether a taking occurred. *Tuthill*, slip op. at 6. That statement misreads *Loretto* and *Kaiser Aetna*. While the existence of an easement may limit the government’s authority, it does not eliminate the constitutional obligation to compensate when the government exceeds the scope of that authority and effects a permanent physical occupation. The government’s actions here go beyond the scope of the easement: it installed a system far larger than needed for its own operations, and now uses the excess for private profit. This is not a mere “burden” on the servient estate; it is a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s land.

The Court of Federal Claims also incorrectly dismissed Tuthill’s claim on the ground that “Tuthill has not identified a property interest that could have been taken.” Id. The property interest at issue is the right to exclusive possession and use of one’s land. The government’s permanent installation of fiber optic cables—over and above what is necessary—constitutes a physical invasion that deprives Tuthill of that right. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “the right to exclude others is a fundamental element of property ownership.” *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 436. By installing and maintaining a permanent physical structure on Tuthill’s land, the government has exercised dominion over that land in a way that excludes Tuthill from full use and control.

For these reasons, the government’s actions constitute a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government. The judgment must be reversed, and the case remanded for determination of just compensation.

**WHEREFORE**, Tuthill Ranch, Inc. respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the United States Court of Federal Claims and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
