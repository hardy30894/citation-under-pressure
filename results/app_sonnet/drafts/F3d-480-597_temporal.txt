ARGUMENT

I. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish That Nieves‑Castaño Knew the Rifle Fired Automatically.

A conviction cannot stand unless the government has offered proof from which a rational jury could find every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) (evidence must be viewed to determine whether it establishes guilt); Curley v. United States, 160 F.2d 229, 232 (D.C. Cir. 1947) (verdict may not rest on evidence that permits only speculation as to an essential element). Where a statute imposes serious felony penalties and is derived from a common-law tradition requiring a guilty mind, courts must presume that Congress intended a mens rea element as to each fact that makes the conduct criminal. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250, 263 (1952) (“mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent” does not dispense with that requirement, for “the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion”). This presumption applies with full force to the machine-gun statute. Unlike the narrow category of public-welfare regulatory offenses recognized in United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922)—where penalties were light and the statute regulated inherently dangerous articles without requiring proof the defendant knew the specific character of the item—the machine-gun offense here carries severe felony consequences, placing it squarely within Morissette’s presumption that guilty knowledge is required.

The government’s proof showed only that Nieves-Castaño knew she was hiding “an AK-47” for a friend; it did not show she knew, or had reason to know, that the weapon fired automatically rather than semi-automatically. Nothing in the record establishes that she examined the weapon’s firing mechanism or was told of its automatic capability. Under Morissette, the absence of any evidence bearing on this critical fact renders the conviction infirm, for a conviction may not rest on inference alone where the inference is unsupported by the evidence. Curley, 160 F.2d at 232.

II. The Jury Instructions Failed to Require a Finding That Nieves‑Castaño Knew the Weapon’s Automatic Character.

Even if the evidence were sufficient, the instructions given to the jury did not require it to find that Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle possessed the characteristics of a machine gun. An erroneous instruction that removes an essential element from the jury’s consideration cannot be excused as harmless. Bollenbach v. United States, 326 U.S. 607, 612 (1946) (“A conviction ought not to rest on an equivocal direction to the jury on a basic issue”). Where a statute might otherwise be read to dispense with scienter, courts have required that instructions be framed to preserve the specific-intent element rather than permit conviction on strict liability. Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101–102 (1945) (plurality opinion) (construing statute to require specific intent to avoid due process concerns). Here, the district court’s charge allowed the jury to convict merely upon finding that Nieves-Castaño knowingly possessed “a rifle,” without any instruction that the government must prove she knew of its automatic firing capacity. That omission requires reversal.

III. Section 922(q)(2)(A) Is Unconstitutionally Vague Because It Fails to Specify How the 1,000‑Foot Zone Is to Be Measured.

The Due Process Clause requires that a criminal statute give ordinary people fair notice of what conduct it prohibits. A statute is void for vagueness when “men of common intelligence must necessarily guess at its meaning.” Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). Similarly, a criminal statute that “fixes no ascertainable standard of guilt” cannot be sustained. Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 458 (1939) (statute void where it provided “no standard of conduct… at all”). Section 922(q)(2)(A) criminalizes possession of a firearm within 1,000 feet of a school, yet nowhere specifies whether that distance is measured as a straight line, along public roadways, or by some other method. In a densely built urban housing project such as the Nemesio Canales complex, the method of measurement can determine guilt or innocence, and ordinary citizens have no way to know, in advance, which method applies. As in United States v. Cardiff, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952), where the Court refused to sustain a conviction under a regulatory statute that left “the句 crucial issue” of coverage “to be resolved… by essentially subjective standards,” Section 922(q)(2)(A)’s silence as to measurement leaves citizens and enforcement officers alike to guess where the zone begins and ends. That ambiguity, applied to impose serious criminal penalties, violates due process.

IV. The Evidence Was Also Insufficient to Sustain the School‑Zone Conviction.

Finally, even accepting the statute’s validity, the government did not prove, under the standard of Glasser, 315 U.S. at 80, and Curley, 160 F.2d at 232, that the distance from Nieves-Castaño’s apartment to any school fell within 1,000 feet measured by a method that a rational jury could adopt without resorting to conjecture. The conviction therefore cannot stand.

For these reasons, both convictions should be reversed.
