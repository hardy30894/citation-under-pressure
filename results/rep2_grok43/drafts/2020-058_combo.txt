The conferral of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an admission. The statutory text equates the grant of such status with lawful nonimmigrant presence, which the Immigration and Nationality Act has long treated as requiring inspection and authorization. See Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86, 101 (1903) (administrative action must conform to the statute's terms). This construction follows the settled rule that every word must be given effect. See Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 462 (1892).

Congress did not create TPS as an exception to the admission requirement; it incorporated the grant into the adjustment framework. Where the legislature includes particular language in one section but omits it in another, the difference is presumed intentional. See Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 719 (1893). The same principle precludes reading the lawful-status provision as surplusage. See United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253, 260 (1905).

The distinction between physical entry and subsequent status does not control here. Administrative grants of protected status have consistently been held to supply the requisite authorization when the statute so provides. See Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896); United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537, 543 (1950). TPS recipients are therefore inspected and admitted within the meaning of the adjustment statute. See Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 214 (1953); Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185, 190 (1958); Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193, 196 (1958).

This reading preserves the statute's structure without rendering any clause inoperative. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Petitioners accordingly satisfy the threshold requirement for adjustment of status.
