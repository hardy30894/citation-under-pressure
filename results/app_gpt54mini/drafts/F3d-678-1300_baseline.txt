**ARGUMENT**

I. **The jury’s § 102(b) verdict cannot stand because Facebook failed to prove, by clear and convincing evidence, that the precritical-date Leader2Leader® product embodied each and every limitation of the asserted claims.**

A patent claim is invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) only if the prior art reference or product contains every element of the claimed invention, arranged as claimed. *Verdegaal Bros., Inc. v. Union Oil Co. of California*, 814 F.2d 628, 631 (Fed. Cir. 1987). Because invalidity must be proven by clear and convincing evidence, Facebook bore a “heavy burden.” *Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. P’ship*, 564 U.S. 91, 95 (2011). The verdict here cannot survive that burden.

The district court permitted the jury to treat Leader’s pre-December 10, 2002 demonstrations and commercialization efforts as if they necessarily disclosed the claimed web-based computing platform with dynamically associated metadata, tracked user movement, and dynamic updating across environments. But the record does not support a finding that the Leader2Leader® product met the full scope of the asserted claims. A verdict of anticipation cannot rest on “probabilities or possibilities”; it requires proof that the prior product was in fact the claimed invention. *Net MoneyIN, Inc. v. VeriSign, Inc.*, 545 F.3d 1359, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2008).

Claim 9, for example, requires “tracking movement of the user from the user environment of the web-based computing platform to a second user environment” and “dynamically updating the stored metadata” with the second environment. Those limitations are not satisfied by evidence that a product supported collaboration, file sharing, or browser-based access in general. Anticipation requires disclosure of the claim as arranged, not merely functional similarity. *Trintec Indus., Inc. v. Top-U.S.A. Corp.*, 295 F.3d 1292, 1295 (Fed. Cir. 2002). Facebook’s proof at most showed that Leader was developing a collaborative software platform and marketing it before the critical date. That is not enough to establish that the product contained all elements of the claimed data-management architecture.

This Court has repeatedly held that a commercial product may anticipate only if it is shown to have included the specific claimed features, not just analogous or aspirational ones. *Finisar Corp. v. DirecTV Grp., Inc.*, 523 F.3d 1323, 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2008); *Elan Pharms., Inc. v. Mayo Found. for Med. Educ. & Research*, 346 F.3d 1051, 1066 (Fed. Cir. 2003). The evidence Facebook relied upon—the January 2002 white paper, the “Big Board” sample, and testimony that the system was “operational now with low user volumes”—described a platform concept and demonstration materials. But anticipation cannot be established by “a mere description of a set of elements” divorced from proof that the system actually worked as claimed. *In re Arkley*, 455 F.2d 586, 587 (C.C.P.A. 1972). 

II. **The district court erred by allowing the jury to infer anticipation from materials that, at most, described an incomplete or experimental system.**

A reference or product anticipates only if it is enabling. *In re Gleave*, 560 F.3d 1331, 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2009); *Rasmusson v. SmithKline Beecham Corp.*, 413 F.3d 1318, 1325 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Although a prior use may qualify as prior art under § 102(b), the accused infringer still must prove that the use placed the claimed invention in the public’s possession. *Lough v. Brunswick Corp.*, 86 F.3d 1113, 1121 (Fed. Cir. 1996). The record here shows only that Leader was “already commercializing” the product and that it was “operational now with low user volumes.” Those statements do not establish public availability of the full claimed invention, much less enablement of the specific limitations found in claim 9 and the other asserted claims.

Indeed, the jury’s verdict depended on a leap from broad similarities to the conclusion that the precritical-date product necessarily practiced the claims. That inference is impermissible where the accused infringer fails to tie each limitation to actual evidence. *Network Commerce, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 422 F.3d 1353, 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The asserted claims are directed to a specific metadata architecture that dynamically follows users across environments. Facebook pointed to no contemporaneous source code, no complete system documentation, and no unrebutted testimony proving that the precritical-date product actually performed the claimed dynamic association and updating functions.

III. **Because the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law, Leader was entitled to judgment notwithstanding the verdict or, at minimum, a new trial.**

Judgment as a matter of law is proper where “a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis” for its verdict. *Fed. R. Civ. P. 50(a); see Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 149 (2000). Here, the only reasonable conclusion from the trial record is that Facebook failed to prove anticipation by clear and convincing evidence. The district court therefore erred in denying Leader’s Rule 50 motion. At minimum, the verdict was against the great weight of the evidence and a new trial should have been granted. *Williamson v. Consolidated Rail Corp.*, 926 F.2d 1344, 1348 (Fed. Cir. 1991).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
