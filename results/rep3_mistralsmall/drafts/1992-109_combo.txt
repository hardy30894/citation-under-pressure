**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to ensure that a defendant who seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty possesses the capacity for a "reasoned choice" among the available alternatives. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399-400 (1993) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), *cert. denied*, 488 U.S. 871 (1988)); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986) (citing *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969)). This standard is more exacting than the test for competency to stand trial, which merely requires a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (citing *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966)); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this heightened standard in reversing the district court’s denial of habeas relief. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 270-71 (9th Cir. 1992).

II. The trial court erred by failing to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte when substantial evidence raised a good-faith doubt as to Moran’s capacity for reasoned choice. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981), *cert. denied*, 456 U.S. 946 (1982)); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981). The record contained ample indicia of incompetence: Moran’s recent suicide attempt, his monosyllabic responses to leading questions, his stated desire to prevent mitigating evidence from being presented, and his ingestion of multiple medications with potential cognitive effects. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 268-69. Under *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973), courts must “indulge in every reasonable presumption against waiver” of constitutional rights and initiate a competency inquiry whenever doubt arises.

III. The state post-conviction court applied the wrong legal standard by relying solely on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s competency to stand trial—not his capacity for reasoned choice. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 272-73. The evaluations by Drs. O’Gorman and Jurasky focused on Moran’s ability to assist counsel and understand the proceedings, not his ability to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of rights. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172; *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 387. The state court’s reliance on these reports was legally erroneous, as the “reasoned choice” standard governs waivers of counsel and guilty pleas. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Evans*, 800 F.2d at 887.

IV. The trial court’s failure to apply the heightened competency standard violated due process. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 384 (holding that due process requires a competency determination when evidence raises a bona fide doubt); *Bishop v. United States*, 350 F.2d 794, 797 (9th Cir. 1965) (holding that a defendant’s mental condition must be evaluated before accepting a guilty plea). The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record did not support a finding that Moran possessed the requisite capacity for reasoned choice. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 274-75.

V. The state’s belated post-conviction competency hearing did not cure the error. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 314-15 (1963) (holding that a state court’s use of an incorrect legal standard precludes deference under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)). The state court’s reliance on the wrong standard deprived Moran of due process, and the federal habeas court properly conducted an independent review. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 116-17 (1985) (applying de novo review to mixed questions of law and fact).

**CONCLUSION**
The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court erred by failing to apply the heightened “reasoned choice” standard and by accepting Moran’s waiver without ensuring his competency. The judgment below should be affirmed.
