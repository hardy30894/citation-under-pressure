**ARGUMENT**

The class certification order should be reversed because the district court certified a Rule 23(b)(3) class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a classwide basis for the only theory of liability the court accepted. Rule 23 does not permit certification on speculation. A class may proceed only when the proponent shows, by competent proof, that common issues will predominate and that the class mechanism will actually advance resolution of the claims. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 551-52 (1949).

That principle is especially important in antitrust cases, where the plaintiff must prove both violation and injury attributable to that violation. Damages may be estimated with some flexibility, but not by a formula that measures harm from theories the court has not certified. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-64 (1931). The law allows approximation of damages only after the plaintiff has shown a causal connection between the wrongful conduct and the injury claimed. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.*, 245 U.S. 531, 533-34 (1918). It does not allow certification first and proof later.

Here, the district court narrowed the case to a single antitrust-impact theory: that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder competition in the Philadelphia DMA. Yet the damages model accepted below was not shown to measure only the injury caused by that theory. That mismatch is fatal. A classwide model must fit the theory of liability actually at issue. If the model includes effects from rejected theories, it does not establish that damages are capable of classwide proof. *Miller v. Mackey Int’l, Inc.*, 452 F.2d 424, 429 (5th Cir. 1969); *Brenham v. Southern Pacific Co.*, 328 F.2d 1, 5 (5th Cir. 1964). Courts have long required a genuine evidentiary basis before allowing a representative action to proceed. *Moore v. New York Cotton Exchange*, 270 U.S. 593, 610 (1926); *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303 (1853).

The court of appeals erred by treating the certification inquiry as if any plausible damages methodology sufficed, even if it was not tied to the accepted liability theory. That approach drains Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement of meaning. Predominance demands that common questions actually drive the litigation, not merely that plaintiffs invoke a common expert model. *Sam Fox Publishing Co. v. United States*, 366 U.S. 683, 689 (1961); *City of Alexandria v. Alexandria Fabric Co.*, 273 F.2d 437, 440 (4th Cir. 1960). Where individualized damages questions depend on proof that varies with the theory of harm, class certification is improper. *Sperry Rand Corp. v. Bell Telephone Laboratories, Inc.*, 272 F.2d 29, 30 (2d Cir. 1959); *Union Pacific Railroad Co. v. Eisenmenger*, 256 F.2d 246, 248 (8th Cir. 1958).

Nor can certification be sustained on the theory that the district court may sort out damages later. A class may not be certified on an assumption that a damages model will eventually be made to work. The trial court must resolve the certification question with evidence, not hope. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Morrison v. Texas Co.*, 289 U.S. 501, 507 (1933). If the record does not show that the proposed methodology isolates damages caused by the certified theory, the class fails Rule 23(b)(3). *West v. Radio-Keith-Orpheum Corp.*, 70 F.2d 621, 626 (2d Cir. 1934); *Brown v. Cole*, 198 F.2d 967, 971 (5th Cir. 1952).

The rule is straightforward: a class cannot be certified unless the plaintiff presents adequate admissible evidence that damages are susceptible to classwide measurement for the liability theory actually certified. Because that showing was not made here, the judgment should be reversed. *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66; *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 563-64.
