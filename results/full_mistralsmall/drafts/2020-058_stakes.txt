**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted Temporary Protected Status (TPS) “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. This statutory language is unambiguous and compels the conclusion that Congress intended TPS to function as an admission for those who entered without inspection. *See Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984) (courts must give effect to unambiguous statutory text).

The Third Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statute by artificially separating “lawful status” from “admission.” However, the text of § 1254a(f)(4) explicitly ties TPS recipients to “lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” a status that, by definition, requires an admission. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A)* (defining “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer”). The statute does not merely confer a temporary shield from removal; it places TPS recipients in a lawful nonimmigrant status, which necessarily presupposes an admission. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (where Congress includes language in one section but omits it in another, courts must presume Congress acted intentionally).

This interpretation is consistent with the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), which held that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255. The Ninth Circuit reasoned that “[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.” *Id.* at 960 (quoting *Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011)). This conclusion is sound because TPS is a statutorily created status that, once granted, places the recipient in a lawful nonimmigrant position—one that cannot exist without an admission.

II. **THE STATUTORY CONTEXT AND PURPOSE SUPPORT THE CONCLUSION THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION**

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the distinction between “status” and “admission” is misplaced. While it is true that these terms are not identical, the text of § 1254a(f)(4) explicitly equates TPS with “lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” a status that, by definition, requires an admission. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A)*. The court’s suggestion that TPS merely confers a shield from removal ignores the plain language of the statute, which places TPS recipients in a lawful nonimmigrant status.

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s interpretation renders § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous. If TPS does not constitute an admission, then TPS recipients who entered without inspection would be permanently barred from adjusting their status under § 1255, despite Congress’s clear intent to place them in lawful nonimmigrant status. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (courts must construe statutes to avoid rendering any provision superfluous).

The purpose of TPS also supports this interpretation. TPS was designed to provide temporary relief to aliens who cannot safely return to their home countries due to natural disasters or other extraordinary conditions. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)*. Denying TPS recipients the ability to adjust their status based on a technical reading of “admission” would frustrate Congress’s intent by forcing them to remain in a state of limbo, unable to regularize their immigration status despite their lawful nonimmigrant status under TPS.

III. **THE GOVERNMENT’S INTERPRETATION IS UNPERSUASIVE AND CONTRARY TO THE TEXT OF THE STATUTE**

The Government argues that TPS does not constitute an admission because it does not involve a physical entry into the United States. However, this argument ignores the plain text of § 1254a(f)(4), which places TPS recipients in “lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” A nonimmigrant status, by definition, requires an admission. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A)*.

The Government also relies on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), for the proposition that “admission” requires a physical entry. However, *Hanif* addressed a different issue—whether an alien who adjusted status in removal proceedings had been “admitted”—and did not address the question presented here. The Government’s reliance on *Hanif* is therefore misplaced.

Finally, the Government’s argument that TPS recipients who entered without inspection must leave the United States and reenter lawfully is impractical and contrary to the purpose of TPS. TPS was designed to provide temporary relief to aliens who cannot safely return to their home countries. Forcing them to depart and reenter would undermine the very purpose of the statute.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that the Third Circuit’s decision should be reversed. A grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255, and Petitioners are therefore eligible to adjust their status.
