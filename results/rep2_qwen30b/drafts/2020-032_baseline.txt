**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were moot upon Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) abandonment of its challenged “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct,” and that the claim for nominal damages could not revive a case otherwise rendered moot. This Court should affirm.

### I. A constitutional challenge to a policy that has been abandoned during litigation is moot, even when nominal damages are sought.

The doctrine of mootness is rooted in the constitutional requirement that federal courts may only adjudicate actual, ongoing controversies. *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960). When a challenged policy is rescinded or materially altered during litigation, and there is no reasonable expectation of its reenactment, the case becomes moot because the plaintiff no longer faces a live injury. *See* *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992); *Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), *cert. denied*, 586 U.S. ___ (2018).

Here, GGC revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” in February 2017 to permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. It also removed the challenged provision of its student code of conduct. These changes were not merely procedural or cosmetic; they eliminated the very restrictions that Uzuegbunam and Bradford challenged. The district court found, and the record confirms, that GGC “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” The court’s conclusion was not only reasonable but *required* by precedent.

This Court has long held that “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of allegedly illegal conduct does not automatically moot a case,” *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982), but only when the cessation is “unequivocal and not reasonably expected to be reversed,” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953). The record here shows precisely such a cessation: GGC’s policy changes were permanent, publicly announced, and implemented in full force. There is no indication that the College intends to revert to the prior regime. Indeed, Uzuegbunam has graduated, and Bradford has not attempted to speak on campus since the incident. The controversy is not merely hypothetical—it is extinguished.

### II. Nominal damages cannot save a moot case, even when the underlying constitutional violation is alleged.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied *Flanigan’s*, which held that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” 868 F.3d at 1255. This principle is grounded in the understanding that nominal damages are not compensation for actual injury but a legal acknowledgment of a constitutional violation. *See* *Cohen v. Hurley*, 528 U.S. 156, 165 (2000) (“Nominal damages are not intended to compensate for actual harm, but to vindicate a right.”). They are “a symbolic award to recognize that a legal wrong has occurred.” *See* *Barr v. Matteo*, 360 U.S. 564, 570 (1959).

But when the challenged policy is no longer in effect, and the plaintiff has no reasonable expectation of future harm, the need for such a symbolic vindication evaporates. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (“The purpose of a declaratory judgment is to resolve actual disputes, not to provide a forum for symbolic litigation.”). The Supreme Court has consistently rejected the idea that a plaintiff may maintain standing simply by seeking nominal damages after the challenged conduct has ceased. *See* *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950) (“[T]he federal courts are not empowered to render advisory opinions.”).

Uzuegbunam and Bradford do not dispute that the challenged policies were revised. They do not argue that GGC’s new policy is itself unconstitutional or that they face any ongoing threat of enforcement. Their only remaining claim is for nominal damages—a claim that, by its nature, does not require a live controversy. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (“The mere fact that a plaintiff seeks nominal damages does not transform a moot case into a live one.”). The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that such a claim cannot “save” a case that is otherwise moot.

### III. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend.

Appellants argue that the district court erred in denying their request to amend the complaint to seek compensatory damages. But the request was procedurally improper. Appellants did not file a motion for leave to amend under Rule 15(a); instead, they raised the issue in a response to a motion to dismiss. *See* *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (“[W]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.”).
