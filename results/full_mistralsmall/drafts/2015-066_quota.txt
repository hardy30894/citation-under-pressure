**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel Applies to Tribal Court Proceedings When the Resulting Conviction Is Used as a Predicate Offense in a Federal Prosecution**

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This Court has long held that the Sixth Amendment applies to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963). The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel attaches at all “critical stages” of a criminal prosecution, including trial. *Id.* at 343; *see also* Rothgery v. Gillespie Cnty., 554 U.S. 191, 200 (2008) (holding that the right to counsel attaches at the initial appearance where a defendant is formally charged).

The Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state prosecutions. In *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), this Court recognized that the Sixth Amendment applies to tribal court proceedings when the resulting conviction is used as a predicate offense in a federal prosecution. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1394-95. The court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is a fundamental right that must be respected to ensure the reliability of convictions used in subsequent federal prosecutions. *Id.* at 1395.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), where the court held that uncounseled tribal court convictions could not be used as predicate offenses under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because they violated the Sixth Amendment. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677. The court explained that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is a “bedrock principle” that ensures the reliability of convictions used in federal prosecutions. *Id.* at 676 (citing *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344).

II. **The Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Violates the Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel**

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could be used to enhance a defendant’s sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 746-47. However, the Court emphasized that its holding was limited to the sentencing context and did not address the use of uncounseled convictions at the guilt phase of a prosecution. *Id.* at 748-49.

The distinction between sentencing and guilt is critical. At sentencing, the government need only prove the existence of a sentence enhancement factor by a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 748. At the guilt phase, however, the government must prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel ensures that a defendant receives a fair trial, which is essential when the government seeks to prove an essential element of an offense. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344.

The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment because it allows the government to rely on unreliable convictions to prove an essential element of the offense. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677. As this Court recognized in *Ant*, the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is necessary to ensure the reliability of convictions used in federal prosecutions. *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1395.

III. **The Indian Civil Rights Act Does Not Displace the Sixth Amendment’s Right to Counsel in This Context**

The dissent argues that the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, governs tribal court proceedings and that the Sixth Amendment does not apply. However, the ICRA does not provide a right to counsel that is coextensive with the Sixth Amendment. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 675. The ICRA only requires that tribal courts provide counsel to indigent defendants in cases where imprisonment is a possible penalty. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)(6).

The Sixth Amendment, by contrast, applies to all criminal prosecutions, regardless of the potential penalty. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344. The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment because it allows the government to rely on unreliable convictions to prove an essential element of the offense. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677.

IV. **The Ninth Circuit’s Decision in *Bryant* Is Consistent with Supreme Court Precedent**

The dissent argues that the Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Bryant* conflicts with Supreme Court precedent. However, the decision is consistent with this Court’s recognition that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is a fundamental right that must be respected to ensure the reliability of convictions used in federal prosecutions. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 344; *Ant*, 882 F.2d at 1395.

The dissent also argues that the decision creates a circuit split. However, the Eighth and Tenth Circuits have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592 (8th Cir. 2011). The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Bryant* is consistent with these decisions and does not create a circuit split.

**Conclusion**

The Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to tribal court proceedings when the resulting conviction is used as a predicate offense in a federal prosecution. The use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment because it allows the government to rely on unreliable convictions to prove an essential element of the offense. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Bryant* is consistent with Supreme Court precedent and does not create a circuit split. This Court should affirm the decision below.
