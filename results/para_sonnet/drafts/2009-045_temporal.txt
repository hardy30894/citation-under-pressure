# ARGUMENT ON BEHALF OF RESPONDENTS

## I. A "Reasonable Fee" Has Never Been a Mechanical Product of Hours and Rates; Equity Has Always Considered the Value of Counsel's Work and the Result Secured.

Long before Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes, courts sitting in equity awarded compensation to counsel who created or preserved a fund for the benefit of others, and in doing so they never confined themselves to a mechanical accounting of hours. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court approved an allowance to a bondholder's representative whose litigation preserved a trust estate, recognizing what later courts have called a "salvage" award — compensation measured not merely by time spent but by the value conferred on the fund through the quality of the work performed. *Id.* at 532-37. The Court reaffirmed the principle four years later in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), holding that a fund created through the diligence and skill of counsel for the common benefit of a class may properly bear a reasonable allowance reflecting that service. *Id.* at 122-27. And in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court explained that "the foundation for the historic practice of granting reimbursement for the costs of litigation other than the conventional taxable costs is part of the original authority of the chancellor to do equity in a particular situation." *Id.* at 166. That authority was never understood as a bare arithmetic exercise; it was, and remains, an equitable judgment about the worth of what was actually accomplished.

This Court's decision in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), confirms the point. There the Court insisted that a fee drawn from a fund be tied to the value of the services actually rendered to the beneficiaries, rejecting any suggestion that a fee could be computed by rote formula divorced from what counsel achieved for those they represented. *Id.* at 105-06. The consistent thread running through these decisions is that "reasonable" compensation for legal services has always been judged, in part, by the quality of representation and the results obtained — precisely the factors the district court considered here.

## II. Congress Legislated Against That Equitable Background When It Authorized Courts to Award "A Reasonable Attorney's Fee."

When Congress directs a federal court to award "a reasonable attorney's fee," it does not write on a blank slate; it imports the pre-existing equitable understanding of what makes a fee reasonable. This Court recognized as much in *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714 (1967), which surveyed the American common-law practice respecting the shifting of counsel fees and traced the exceptions to that practice — including the common-fund doctrine exemplified by *Greenough* and *Pettus* — as part of the settled body of law upon which Congress draws when it creates fee-shifting remedies. *Id.* at 717-18. Because *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, and *Sprague* all treated the quality of counsel's work and the benefit secured for the represented parties as legitimate components of a reasonable fee, that same understanding necessarily informs the statutory command to award "a reasonable attorney's fee" to the prevailing party. A rule categorically barring consideration of performance and result would sever the statutory word "reasonable" from the very body of equitable practice that gave it content.

## III. The Trial Judge Occupies the Superior Vantage Point for Assessing the Value of Counsel's Work, Just as the Chancellor Did in the Common-Fund Cases.

The equitable fee decisions likewise establish that the judge who observed the litigation is uniquely positioned to value counsel's contribution. In *Pettus*, this Court deferred to the trial court's assessment of the "difficulty and importance of the questions" litigated and the "ability and skill" with which counsel presented them, 113 U.S. at 127, because the trial judge, not a reviewing court applying a formula after the fact, watched the case unfold. The same logic applies with full force to a district judge who presided over years of contested litigation culminating in a comprehensive consent decree reforming Georgia's foster-care system. The district court's findings — grounded in its direct observation of counsel's performance and the extraordinary relief achieved for the plaintiff children — are precisely the kind of case-specific, record-based judgment that equity has always entrusted to the court closest to the litigation.

## Conclusion

The traditions reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, *Sprague*, and *Fleischmann* demonstrate that a "reasonable" fee has never been reducible to hours multiplied by rate. Quality of performance and results obtained have always been proper — sometimes decisive — components of that judgment. The judgment below should be affirmed.
