**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, recognizing that reform of the rules governing criminal responsibility implicates policy judgments properly left to the States, and cautioning against constitutionalizing any single approach given the risk that doing so could interfere with a State's legitimate efforts to address, in the Court's words, "serious defects of present-day American criminal justice." *Id.* at 800–01. If a State may constitutionally place the entire burden of proving insanity on the defendant, it follows a fortiori that a State may also determine the channel through which mental-disease evidence enters the case.

This principle was reinforced in *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize any single doctrine of criminal responsibility, observing that "traditional common-law concepts of personal accountability" remain matters "of state law," and that the Court should be "reluctant to take this step in the absence of some indication... that a change in the direction is required." *Id.* at 536. Arizona's rule confining psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry is precisely the sort of substantive line-drawing that *Leland* and *Powell* leave to the States.

This channeling approach is also consistent with history. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent on a fact-by-fact basis. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation.

Moreover, this Court has already sustained the exclusion of similar categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct the jury that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, reasoning that the District of Columbia was entitled to adhere to "the practical difficulties of ingrafting the doctrine of partial responsibility onto our jurisprudence" without violating due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits the evidence to be considered on insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Even apart from the substantive latitude recognized in *Leland* and *Powell*, Arizona's rule survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not offend "some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Nor does the Fourteenth Amendment demand that every State adopt identical procedures for the trial of criminal cases; the States retain broad authority to shape their own criminal justice systems consistent with their local circumstances and traditions. *See Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908).

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt. The Court explained that state rules of evidence governing the presentation of proof to the jury are entitled to substantial deference, and that due process is offended only where the operation of such a rule works a fundamental unfairness in the trial as a whole. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

The touchstone under *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), is whether "the absence of that fairness fatally infected the" trial such that the proceeding was rendered fundamentally unfair, for "denial of due process is the failure to observe that fundamental fairness essential to the very concept of justice." *Id.* at 236. Here, the trial court permitted Clark to introduce the full range of expert psychiatric testimony and to have it evaluated by the factfinder on the issue of insanity—the very evidence he now claims should also negate intent. Nothing in *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), which addressed the burden of proof on insanity under federal law, suggests that the Constitution requires evidence relevant to one defense to be simultaneously admissible for an entirely separate purpose.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend any principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people," *Snyder*, 291 U.S. at 105, and this Court should affirm.
