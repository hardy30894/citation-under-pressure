**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court's Eve-of-Trial Denial of a Defense Expert and a Continuance Violated Winbush's Right to Present a Defense.**

The Sixth Amendment secures to every defendant "the right to present a defense, the right to present the defendant's version of the facts as well as the prosecution's to the jury so it may decide where the truth lies." *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). That right is "a fundamental element of due process of law." *Id.* Winbush was arrested for a struggle in the snow, yet no fingerprints of his were recovered from the jacket, gun, or crack found along his path of flight. The government's own expert, Michael Shay, was prepared to testify to that absence. Winbush's request for an expert of his own to explain the *meaning* of that absence—rather than merely stipulating to the government's characterization of it—went to the heart of his defense that the abandoned contraband belonged to someone else.

This Court reviews denial of a continuance for abuse of discretion, but that discretion is not boundless. "There are no mechanical tests for deciding when a denial of a continuance is so arbitrary as to violate due process. The answer must be found in the circumstances present in every case." *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964). Here the circumstances cut sharply in Winbush's favor: the fingerprint issue was not a surprise conjured five days before trial—it was the centerpiece of the government's own forensic disclosures, and Winbush's need to answer it should have been apparent to the court and counsel alike. A defendant is constitutionally entitled to "a reasonable time to prepare his defense," *Avery v. Alabama*, 308 U.S. 444, 446 (1940), and time for investigation is not a luxury but a necessity when, as here, forensic evidence is the only objective link—or non-link—between the defendant and the contraband. As the Court explained in the analogous context of counsel's preparation time, when "consultation, thoroughgoing investigation and preparation were vitally important," denial of the opportunity to prepare renders the constitutional guarantee illusory. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 57–59 (1932). The magistrate judge's dismissal of the motion as merely "woefully late" did not weigh these competing interests; it treated docket management as dispositive of a constitutional right. That was error.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Sustain Winbush's Convictions for Possessing the Kentucky Crack Cocaine and the Firearm.**

Even crediting the government's proof, no reasonable jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt that Winbush possessed the Kentucky crack and firearm found inside the abandoned jacket. This Court must ask "whether upon the evidence, giving full play to the right of the jury to determine credibility, weigh the evidence, and draw justifiable inferences of fact, a reasonable mind might fairly conclude guilt beyond a reasonable doubt." *Curley v. United States*, 160 F.2d 229, 232 (D.C. Cir. 1947). The verdict "must be sustained if there is substantial evidence, taking the view most favorable to the Government," *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942)—but substantial evidence is not the same as speculation. The jacket was found on a public street, one block from where officers first observed a gun in Winbush's hand, after a foot chase in which Winbush shed clothing to evade capture and other people were in the vicinity, including a fleeing suspect who later cooperated with police. No fingerprint, no eyewitness identification of the jacket's contents, and no admission tied Winbush to the crack or the gun inside it at the moment they were discarded. Proximity and flight establish opportunity, not possession. Absent the fingerprint proof Winbush was denied the chance to develop, the government's case reduces to the inference that because Winbush wore the jacket earlier and fled the area, he must have discarded it with the contraband inside—an inference insufficiently distinguishable from guilt by association to satisfy *Glasser* and *Curley*.

**III. Convictions Under Both § 922(g)(1) and § 924(c) for the Same Firearm Cannot Both Stand.**

Even if the possession convictions are affirmed, punishing Winbush separately under both § 922(g)(1) and § 924(c) for the identical firearm violates the settled rule against multiplicitous punishment for what is, in substance, a single wrong arising from one act of possession. The classic test asks "whether each provision requires proof of a fact which the other does not." *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299, 304 (1932). Where, as here, the two counts rest on the same gun recovered from the same jacket at the same moment, the risk is that Winbush is punished twice for one unlawful act, contrary to the ancient principle, traceable to the common law, "that a person shall be twice punished for the same offence." *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 168 (1873). This Court should examine whether Congress, in enacting § 924(c), intended cumulative punishment for the identical firearm already the subject of a felon-in-possession count, mindful that *Gore v. United States*, 357 U.S. 386, 392 (1958), permits cumulative penalties only where Congress has clearly "decreed multiple penalties for a single act or transaction." No such clear congressional intent supports stacking these two counts on the facts here.

For these reasons, Winbush's convictions and sentence should be vacated.
