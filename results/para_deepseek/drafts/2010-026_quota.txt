**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its statutory text and this Court’s precedents. Exemption 2 protects matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has consistently held that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard contravenes that command.

**I. The Ninth Circuit’s “Predominantly Internal” Standard Lacks Statutory Support**

The plain text of Exemption 2 requires that withheld matters relate “solely” to internal personnel rules and practices. This Court has explained that “solely” means the material must be “limited to” such internal matters. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 363. The ESQD arc maps—which measure explosive blast effects on surrounding communities—do not relate “solely” to internal personnel matters. They concern public safety, environmental impacts, and community planning. As this Court recognized, Exemption 2 covers “minor employment matters” like parking and lunch hours, not documents with significant public interest. *Id.* at 363-64.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test dilutes Congress’s deliberate use of “solely.” This Court has never endorsed such a standard. To the contrary, in *Forsham v. Harris*, 445 U.S. 169, 178 n.11 (1980), this Court described Exemption 2 as covering “rules and practices that affect the internal workings of an agency.” The ESQD maps do not affect internal workings; they govern explosive safety for the surrounding public.

**II. The Ninth Circuit Improperly Expanded Exemption 2 Beyond Law Enforcement Materials**

This Court in *Rose* left open whether Exemption 2 could cover law enforcement materials whose disclosure would risk circumvention of agency regulation. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369. But the Court never suggested Exemption 2 extends to non-law enforcement materials. Every circuit to address the issue before the Ninth Circuit’s decision limited Exemption 2’s “high 2” category to law enforcement or regulatory materials. *See Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051, 1074 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc) (applying test to law enforcement manual); *Caplan v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 587 F.2d 544, 548 (2d Cir. 1978) (same); *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980) (same).

The ESQD maps are not law enforcement materials. They are safety planning documents. The Ninth Circuit’s expansion to cover any “predominantly internal” document with a risk of circumvention has no limiting principle. As the D.C. Circuit recognized, Exemption 2’s circumvention rationale applies only where disclosure would enable individuals to “violate the law or avoid agency regulation.” *Crooker*, 670 F.2d at 1074. The Navy’s concern that terrorists might use the maps does not fit this rationale—terrorism is already illegal, and the maps do not reveal how to circumvent any agency regulation.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with This Court’s FOIA Precedent**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989). Congress carefully crafted nine specific exemptions. If the Navy believes the ESQD maps threaten national security, Exemption 1 (classified information) or Exemption 7(F) (law enforcement records endangering safety) are the proper avenues. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), (b)(7)(F). The Navy chose not to classify these maps, and the district court never reached Exemption 7(F). The Ninth Circuit’s expansion of Exemption 2 allows agencies to bypass Congress’s carefully calibrated exemptions.

Moreover, this Court has held that FOIA’s “basic policy” is “full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 360-61. The Ninth Circuit’s standard is not “clearly delineated.” It invites agencies to withhold any internal document by asserting a risk of circumvention. This Court rejected such open-ended exemptions in *FBI v. Abramson*, 456 U.S. 615, 630 (1982), holding that exemptions must be “given a meaningful reach yet applied with due regard for the Act’s dominant disclosure purpose.”

**IV. The ESQD Maps Are Not “Personnel Rules and Practices”**

Even under a broad reading, the ESQD maps are not “personnel rules and practices.” This Court in *Rose* defined that phrase as “rules and practices that affect the internal workings of an agency.” *Forsham*, 445 U.S. at 178 n.11. The ESQD maps govern explosive storage distances—they do not govern employee conduct, training, or discipline. They are analogous to building codes or safety regulations, not personnel manuals. *See Maricopa Audubon Soc’y v. U.S. Forest Serv.*, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997) (rejecting Exemption 2 for goshawk nesting data because it did not relate to personnel rules). The maps’ purpose is to protect the public, not to guide employee behavior.

**V. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Undermines FOIA’s Core Purpose**

FOIA was enacted to “ensure an informed citizenry, vital to the functioning of a democratic society.” *NLRB v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co.*, 437 U.S. 214, 242 (1978). The ESQD maps are precisely the type of information the public needs to evaluate government safety decisions. Milner seeks to understand whether the Navy’s explosive storage near Puget Sound endangers surrounding communities. This Court has recognized that FOIA’s purpose is “to pierce the veil of administrative secrecy and to open agency action to the light of public scrutiny.” *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Ninth Circuit’s decision shields agency action from that scrutiny.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard has no basis in Exemption 2’s text, this Court’s precedents, or FOIA’s purpose. This Court should reverse and hold that Exemption 2 does not cover the ESQD arc maps.
