**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Procedural Default Not Attributable to the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Disable Reliance on the Underlying Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

Habeas corpus has never been governed by mechanical forfeiture rules divorced from the petitioner's own conduct. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), this Court held that a federal court may deny relief on default grounds only where the petitioner himself has "deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts." *Id.* at 438. The touchstone is the prisoner's own considered choice—not the tactical missteps or negligence of his lawyer. Carpenter did not choose to abandon his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim; his appellate counsel did, by failing to raise it and then by filing an untimely application to reopen. Under *Fay*, that failure cannot be visited upon Carpenter as though it were his own deliberate abandonment.

This distinction follows directly from *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), where the Court defined waiver as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. A defendant relinquishes nothing, within the meaning of *Zerbst*, when counsel—not the client—fails to preserve a claim. To treat counsel's default as the petitioner's own waiver inverts the premise on which *Zerbst* rests: that fundamental rights are lost only through the client's knowing and voluntary act.

**II. The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a procedural formality; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court declared that "the right of one charged with crime to counsel may not be deemed fundamental and essential to fair trials in some countries, but it is in ours." *Id.* at 344. That same Term, in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), the Court extended that guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that a State cannot condition vindication of federal rights on the happenstance of retained counsel while indigent appellants proceed unrepresented. *Id.* at 357–58. Carpenter's claim—that appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence supporting his conviction—asserts precisely the deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A subsequent default of that claim, itself the product of the very disabling failure it describes, should not be permitted to strip it of its capacity to explain and excuse the sufficiency claim's own default.

**III. Comity Principles Do Not Require That an Ineffective-Assistance Claim Be Independently, Successfully Exhausted Before It May Serve as Cause**

The exhaustion doctrine exists to give state courts, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), "the first opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of . . . constitutional rights." *Id.* at 118. It is a rule of comity, not forfeiture. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise grounded exhaustion in "comity between the state and federal sovereignties," *id.* at 204, not in any design to extinguish meritorious federal claims. Ohio had precisely the opportunity *Hawk* and *Darr* contemplate: Carpenter presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio Court of Appeals in an application to reopen his direct appeal. That the state court declined to reach the merits on timeliness grounds does not diminish the fact that the State's courts were given the first opportunity to correct the error. Nothing in the comity rationale underlying exhaustion requires that a claim first survive an independent gauntlet of full state-court adjudication before it may perform its traditional function of explaining a related, intertwined default.

**IV. This Court's Habeas Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Where Liberty Is at Stake**

The great writ has always been administered with the flexibility necessary to prevent the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), Justice Holmes explained that where constitutional guarantees are fundamentally undermined, "it is a duty of the United States . . . to secure" that those guarantees are not defeated by "the machinery of the state." *Id.* at 91. In *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), the Court cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake," *id.* at 8, and required equitable, individualized judgment rather than rigid rule-bound dismissal. *Id.* at 17–18. And in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), the Court confirmed the federal judiciary's independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims in habeas rather than treating state procedural determinations as automatically dispositive. *Id.* at 458.

*Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Brown* together establish that a default attributable to counsel's own failure—rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice—should not compound itself into an absolute bar. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim, though itself defaulted through no fault of his own, retained sufficient constitutional weight to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment below should be affirmed.
