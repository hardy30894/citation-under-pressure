**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because a reasonable jury could find Bora Corp. liable under § 905(b) for breach of the vessel’s “active operations” duty. Under *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), a vessel that actively involves itself in cargo operations owes longshoremen “the duty of exercising reasonable care to protect them from hazards they may encounter in the course of their work.” *Id.* at 167. The Court in *Scindia* emphasized that the vessel may not “actively involve itself in the cargo operations and negligently injure a longshoreman.” *Id.* at 167. This Court has repeatedly held that when the ship’s crew retains control over an instrumentality or operation and the longshoreman is injured by the crew’s negligent conduct in that operation, liability may attach. *Kirsch v. Prekookeanska Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026, 1029–31 (3d Cir. 1992)*; *Hill v. Reederei F. Laeisz G.M.B.H., Rostock, 435 F.3d 404, 411–12 (3d Cir. 2006)*.

Here, the record permits a finding that the ship’s crew, not the stevedore, controlled the snatch blocks. The blocks were part of the vessel’s hatch-cover system; they were fixed to the hatches; and, most importantly, “[m]oving the snatch blocks is the responsibility of the crew.” When Serbin encountered a block left in the down position, the hazard arose from the crew’s failure to restore the ship’s own equipment to a safe configuration. That is not a static condition of the cargo bay left entirely to the stevedore; it is affirmative operational negligence by the vessel in the very equipment it was using to facilitate unloading. Under *Scindia*, such facts present a classic jury question. *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156, 167, 175–76 (1981)*.

The district court appears to have treated Serbin’s decision to move the block himself as a complete bar to recovery. That was error. A longshoreman’s knowledge of a hazard does not automatically relieve the vessel of its negligence, because § 905(b) retains ordinary comparative fault principles. *Edmonds v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique, 443 U.S. 256, 259–63 (1979)*; *Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co., S.A., 512 U.S. 92, 98–100 (1994)*. A vessel may still be liable where its own active negligence contributes to the injury, even if the longshoreman acted imprudently. *Howlett*, 512 U.S. at 98–99; *Johnson v. A/S Ivarans Rederi, 613 F.2d 334, 342–43 (1st Cir. 1980)*. Serbin testified that he moved the block to avoid delay because the cargo operation was time-sensitive; a jury could find that this was a foreseeable response to the crew’s failure to perform its assigned task. Foreseeability is central to maritime negligence. *Kermarec v. Compagnie Generale Transatlantique, 358 U.S. 625, 632 (1959)*; *Kirsch*, 971 F.2d at 1032.

Nor did the vessel’s duty vanish because the stevedore was the independent contractor running the unloading operation. *Scindia* expressly rejects any categorical rule that a vessel may ignore dangerous conduct simply because cargo work is being performed by a stevedore. 451 U.S. at 167–68. Where the vessel’s crew retains active responsibility for a piece of gear and fails to perform that responsibility with reasonable care, the vessel remains subject to liability. *Moore v. M.P. Howlett, Inc., 704 F.2d 39, 41–42 (2d Cir. 1983)*; *Gravatt v. City of New York, 226 F.3d 108, 121–22 (2d Cir. 2000)*. The evidence that the crew alone manipulated, opened, closed, and reset the hatch-cover blocks is enough to distinguish this case from one involving merely a passive, preexisting defect.

Finally, summary judgment was inappropriate because the record contains evidence from which a jury could find both breach and causation. Under Rule 56, the court may not weigh competing inferences where the nonmovant has produced evidence that the vessel’s negligence contributed to the injury. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248–52 (1986)*; *Matsushita Electric Industrial Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 586–88 (1986)*. Serbin was injured when he and a coworker attempted to lower the improperly positioned block from crates seven feet above the deck. A reasonable jury could find that the crew’s failure to reset its own equipment forced Serbin into a dangerous improvisation and that the resulting fall was a foreseeable consequence of that negligence. *Hill*, 435 F.3d at 411–12; *Kirsch*, 971 F.2d at 1031–32.

Because the evidence supports a finding that Bora Corp.’s crew actively negligently failed to perform a vessel operation within its exclusive control, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
