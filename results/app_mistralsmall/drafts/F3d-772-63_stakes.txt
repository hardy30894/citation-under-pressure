**ARGUMENT**

I. **WELLS FARGO BREACHED THE CLASS ACTION SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT BY FAILING TO CONSIDER FOLEY FOR A HAMP OR MAP2R MODIFICATION BEFORE INITIATING FORECLOSURE.**

The First Circuit construes a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) by accepting the plaintiff’s well-pleaded factual allegations as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor. *Medina-Velázquez v. Hernández-Gregorat*, 767 F.3d 103, 108 (1st Cir. 2014). Here, Foley adequately pleaded that Wells Fargo breached the settlement agreement by failing to consider him for a HAMP or MAP2R modification before pursuing foreclosure.

The settlement agreement explicitly required Wells Fargo to “first [] consider[]” Settlement Class B members—including Foley—for a HAMP modification. The agreement further provided that those who did not qualify for or elect HAMP “shall be considered for a MAP2R modification” pursuant to a detailed “waterfall” process. This contractual obligation is enforceable under Massachusetts law. *See generally RESTATEMENT (SECOND) OF CONTRACTS § 317(1) (1981)* (“A promise to perform a duty the promisor owes to a third person is binding on the promisor.”). Wells Fargo does not dispute that Foley is a Settlement Class B member, nor that the bank failed to follow the agreement’s modification process. Instead, Wells Fargo argues that Foley’s claims are preempted by federal law. This argument fails.

II. **FOLEY’S STATE-LAW CLAIMS ARE NOT PREEMPTED BY THE HOME OWNERSHIP AND EQUITY PROTECTION ACT (HOEPA).**

Wells Fargo contends that Foley’s state-law claims are preempted by HOEPA, 15 U.S.C. § 1639 et seq., which regulates certain high-cost mortgage loans. However, HOEPA’s preemptive scope is limited. The statute expressly preempts only state laws that “are inconsistent with” its provisions or that “impose more stringent limitations” on high-cost mortgages. *15 U.S.C. § 1639(l)(1)*. Foley’s claims arise from Wells Fargo’s breach of the settlement agreement, not from any state law regulating the terms of his mortgage. *See Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.*, 505 U.S. 504, 518 (1992) (preemption applies only where state law conflicts with federal law or “stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress”). Because Foley’s claims do not regulate mortgage terms or impose stricter limitations than HOEPA, they are not preempted.

III. **FOLEY ADEQUATELY PLED CLAIMS FOR BREACH OF CONTRACT, VIOLATION OF MASSACHUSETTS GENERAL LAWS CHAPTER 93A, AND VIOLATION OF MASSACHUSETTS GENERAL LAWS CHAPTER 244, § 35B.**

A. **Breach of Contract**
Foley’s complaint sufficiently alleged that Wells Fargo breached the settlement agreement by failing to consider him for a HAMP or MAP2R modification. *See generally RESTATEMENT (SECOND) OF CONTRACTS § 235(2) (1981)* (“Where performance of a duty under a contract is due any non-performance is a breach.”). The agreement’s plain terms imposed a duty on Wells Fargo to evaluate Foley for modification before foreclosure, and the complaint adequately pleaded that Wells Fargo failed to do so.

B. **Chapter 93A**
Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A, § 2(a), prohibits “unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce.” The First Circuit has held that a breach of contract may also violate Chapter 93A if the breach is “accompanied by an element of unfairness or deception.” *Leardi v. Brown*, 394 F.3d 1, 7 (1st Cir. 2005). Here, Foley alleged that Wells Fargo misled him about his modification eligibility while simultaneously pursuing foreclosure, conduct that plausibly satisfies the statute’s requirements.

C. **Chapter 244, § 35B**
Massachusetts General Laws chapter 244, § 35B, requires a mortgagee to “consider” a borrower for a loan modification before foreclosing. *See* Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 35B(1)(i). The statute’s plain language imposes an obligation to evaluate modification requests, and Foley’s complaint adequately pleaded that Wells Fargo failed to do so. *See DiMarzo v. Citibank, N.A.*, 463 Mass. 392, 402 (2012) (construing § 35B to require meaningful consideration of modification requests).

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Foley’s claims. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
