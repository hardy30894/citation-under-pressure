**Argument**

The court-ordered population reduction is a permissible remedial measure under the Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) because the statute authorizes prospective relief that is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct a federal violation, and is the least intrusive means necessary to remedy that violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), (a)(3)(E)(ii). Where, as here, a three-judge district court finds by clear and convincing evidence that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and that no other relief will cure them, the PLRA expressly permits an order directing the State to reduce the prison population. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i), (ii).

The Supreme Court has long held that courts may order population limits as part of structural relief to cure unconstitutional prison conditions. In Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), the Court upheld an injunction limiting punitive isolation because district courts must have “broad power to fashion equitable relief” to correct Eighth Amendment violations. Id. at 687. Likewise, in Brown v. Plata, 563 U.S. 493 (2011), the Court confirmed that “[p]risoners retain the essence of human dignity inherent in all persons,” and that a population-reduction order is authorized when overcrowding is the “primary cause” of constitutional harm and other measures have failed. Id. at 510, 518-19. The Court rejected the argument that the PLRA categorically forbids prisoner-release orders, explaining that Congress “expressly authorized” such relief in 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3). Id. at 526.

That is exactly the situation here. The record shows pervasive, ongoing violations of the Eighth Amendment caused by crowding that has prevented California from providing adequate medical and mental health care. Under Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97 (1976), deliberate indifference to serious medical needs violates the Eighth Amendment. Under Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), prison officials violate the Constitution when they know of and disregard an excessive risk to inmate health or safety. And under Rhodes v. Chapman, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), overcrowding becomes unconstitutional when it leads to the deprivation of basic human needs. The findings below establish precisely that: overcrowding has made constitutional care impossible. When the State has failed to remedy such conditions through less intrusive means, the PLRA permits the court to order the population reduction necessary to end the violation. Brown, 563 U.S. at 526-27; Gilmore v. California, 220 F.3d 987, 1007-08 (9th Cir. 2000).

The State’s contrary position ignores both the statute’s text and the deference owed to the district court’s remedy. The PLRA does not require courts to tolerate unconstitutional conditions until every conceivable alternative has failed in practice; it requires that the court find the relief “extends no further than necessary” and is “the least intrusive means” of correcting the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). That is a judicial finding, not a legislative prohibition. See Brown, 563 U.S. at 531-32. Here, after years of remedial orders, noncompliance, and failed reforms, the district court reasonably concluded that no lesser order would end the constitutional injury. That conclusion falls squarely within the PLRA.

The State also cannot convert federalism concerns into a PLRA defense. Federal courts must respect state sovereignty, but they must also enforce the Constitution. As the Court observed in Brown, “[c]ourts may not allow constitutional violations to continue simply because a remedy would have collateral effects on state policy and budgetary decisions.” 563 U.S. at 511. The PLRA was designed to cabin prison litigation, not to disable equitable relief when Eighth Amendment violations persist. Porter v. Nussle, 534 U.S. 516, 524 (2002); Miller v. French, 530 U.S. 327, 347 (2000). Once the statutory prerequisites are met, the court may order a population reduction even if the State must make difficult administrative choices to comply. Brown, 563 U.S. at 532-33.

In short, the order at issue does not violate the PLRA. It is the very kind of narrowly tailored, necessity-based remedy Congress preserved in § 3626(a)(3). Because the district court found that overcrowding was the primary cause of ongoing constitutional violations and that no lesser remedy would suffice, the population-reduction order should be affirmed.
