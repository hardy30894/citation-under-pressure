**ARGUMENT**

**The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Denying Winbush’s Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and a Continuance, Thereby Violating His Right to Present a Defense.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense.” *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986). This right includes access to expert assistance where the government’s case relies on expert testimony that the defendant cannot adequately challenge without his own expert. *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 83 (1985). Here, the government planned to call two fingerprint experts: Michael Shay, who would testify that no latent prints of value were found on the evidence, and Deputy Commander Michael Reilly, who would explain “the difficulties in obtaining latent fingerprints from different materials and surfaces.” Winbush sought a fingerprint expert to examine the evidence and, if no prints were present, “to testify as to its meaning.” The district court denied this request as “woefully late,” filed only six days before trial in a case pending over two years. That denial was an abuse of discretion and prejudiced Winbush.

First, the timing was not unreasonable. The government filed its expert notice on October 3, 2007—over a month before trial. Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert came on November 8, six days before trial. While late, the motion was not filed on the eve of trial, and the government had ample time to respond. The district court gave no consideration to whether a brief continuance could cure any prejudice. *See United States v. Miller*, 276 F.3d 370, 373 (7th Cir. 2002) (denial of continuance reviewed for abuse of discretion, with factors including “the amount of time available, the likelihood of prejudice, and the reasons for the request”). The court’s summary dismissal—based solely on the passage of two years—ignored that the need for an expert arose only after the government disclosed its experts. Winbush had no obligation to anticipate the government’s expert strategy months earlier.

Second, the denial was prejudicial. The government’s fingerprint evidence was central to linking Winbush to the firearm and the Kentucky crack. Shay would testify that no latent prints were found; Reilly would explain why prints might be absent. Without his own expert, Winbush could not rebut Reilly’s testimony or offer an alternative explanation for the absence of prints—for example, that the gun and baggies had been handled by others or that the surfaces were not conducive to prints. This left the jury with the government’s unchallenged inference that the absence of prints was irrelevant, when in fact it could have supported Winbush’s defense that he never possessed those items. *See United States v. Alden*, 527 F.3d 653, 660 (7th Cir. 2008) (defendant prejudiced when denied expert to rebut government’s expert testimony on critical issue).

Third, the district court should have granted a continuance to allow Winbush to retain an expert. The court had discretion to do so, and the request was not made in bad faith. *See United States v. King*, 627 F.3d 641, 648 (7th Cir. 2010) (continuance appropriate when defendant shows “a reasonable likelihood that the continuance would have benefited the defense”). Winbush demonstrated that an expert could have meaningfully challenged the government’s fingerprint evidence. The government would have suffered no prejudice from a short delay; the case had already been continued multiple times. The district court’s refusal to grant even a brief continuance was arbitrary and effectively denied Winbush the ability to present a defense.

Finally, the error was not harmless. The government’s case against Winbush was strong, but the fingerprint evidence was the only physical link between Winbush and the firearm and the Kentucky crack. Without an expert to explain the absence of prints, the jury was left with the government’s expert testimony that the absence was unremarkable. A defense expert could have cast doubt on that conclusion, potentially creating reasonable doubt. *See Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967) (error harmless only if “beyond a reasonable doubt” it did not contribute to verdict). Here, the error cannot be deemed harmless.

For these reasons, this Court should reverse Winbush’s conviction and remand for a new trial with adequate opportunity to secure expert assistance.
