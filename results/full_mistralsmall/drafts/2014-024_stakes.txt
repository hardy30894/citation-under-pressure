**ARGUMENT**

I. THE TRUE-THREAT EXCEPTION TO FIRST AMENDMENT PROTECTION REQUIRES PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). But this Court has never held that a speaker may be convicted of making a true threat without proof that he *subjectively intended* his words to be taken as a threat. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that the First Amendment requires proof of *mens rea*—a guilty mind—before criminal liability may attach. *See, e.g., Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723, 733 (2015) (plurality opinion) (“Federal criminal liability generally does not turn solely on the results of an act without considering the defendant’s mental state.”); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 252 (1952) (“The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion.”).

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a reasonable speaker would foresee that his words would be interpreted as a threat—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Black*, the Court expressly declined to adopt a purely objective standard. 538 U.S. at 360 (“We need not venture into such uncertain territory.”). The Court instead held that the First Amendment permits states to criminalize cross burnings *done with intent to intimidate*, id. at 365, and that the jury must be instructed that the defendant *intended* the act to be a threat. Id. at 367. The Court’s reasoning applies with equal force to § 875(c): a speaker cannot be convicted of making a true threat unless he *subjectively intended* his words to convey a threat.

This Court’s decision in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), reinforces this principle. In *Watts*, the Court reversed a conviction under a federal statute prohibiting threats against the President, holding that the defendant’s statement—“If they ever make me carry a rifle, the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J.”—was not a true threat because it was made in the context of a political hyperbole. Id. at 708. The Court’s analysis turned on the defendant’s *subjective intent*: the statement was not a true threat because it was not “a serious expression of an intention to inflict bodily harm.” *Id.* (quoting *United States v. Howell*, 418 F.2d 511, 513 (7th Cir. 1969)). *Watts* thus confirms that the First Amendment requires proof of *subjective intent* to threaten.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee that his words would be taken as a threat—is also inconsistent with this Court’s decision in *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011). In *Snyder*, the Court held that the First Amendment protected the speech of protesters at a military funeral, even though their words caused severe emotional distress to the plaintiff. Id. at 461. The Court emphasized that the First Amendment protects speech that is “unpopular” or “outrageous,” so long as it does not constitute a true threat. *Id.* at 457-58. The Court’s reasoning underscores that the First Amendment requires a *subjective* inquiry into the speaker’s intent, not an *objective* inquiry into how a reasonable listener would perceive the speech.

Finally, the Third Circuit’s rule would chill protected speech. As this Court has recognized, the First Amendment protects speech that is “vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp,” *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 270 (1964), including rap lyrics and other forms of artistic expression. See *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 735 (plurality opinion) (“[T]aken in context, [the defendant’s] posts could be read as nothing more than rap lyrics.”). The Third Circuit’s objective standard would permit speakers to be convicted based on how a reasonable listener *perceives* their words, even if the speaker did not *intend* them to be threats. This would chill protected speech and violate the First Amendment.

II. THE GOVERNMENT’S PROPOSED STANDARD—WHETHER A REASONABLE SPEAKER WOULD FORESEE THAT HIS WORDS WOULD BE TAKEN AS A THREAT—IS INCONSISTENT WITH THIS COURT’S PRECEDENTS AND WOULD CHILL PROTECTED SPEECH.

The Third Circuit’s standard—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee that his words would be taken as a threat—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. As discussed above, *Black*, *Watts*, and *Snyder* all require proof of *subjective intent* to threaten. The Third Circuit’s objective standard would permit speakers to be convicted based on how a reasonable listener *perceives* their words, even if the speaker did not *intend* them to be threats. This is inconsistent with the First Amendment and this Court’s precedents.

The Third Circuit’s rule would also chill protected speech. As this Court has recognized, the First Amendment protects speech that is “vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp,” *New York Times*, 376 U.S. at 270, including rap lyrics and other forms of artistic expression. See *Elonis*, 575 U.S. at 735 (plurality opinion). The Third Circuit’s objective standard would permit speakers to be convicted based on how a reasonable listener *perceives* their words, even if the speaker did not *intend* them to be threats. This would chill protected speech and violate the First Amendment.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT’S JURY INSTRUCTION WAS PLAIN ERROR BECAUSE IT FAILED TO REQUIRE PROOF OF SUBJECTIVE INTENT TO THREATEN.

The district court instructed the jury that a “true threat” is a statement that a reasonable speaker would foresee would be interpreted as a threat. This instruction was plain error because it failed to require proof of *subjective intent* to threaten. *See, e.g., Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466-67 (1997) (holding that a jury instruction that omits an element of the offense is plain error). The instruction was inconsistent with this Court’s precedents and the First Amendment, and it permitted the jury to convict Elonis based on how a reasonable listener *perceived* his words, rather than whether he *subjectively intended* them to be threats.

Because the jury instruction was plain error, Elonis is entitled to reversal of his conviction. *See, e.g., United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732-34 (1993) (holding that plain error review applies when a defendant fails to object to a jury instruction at trial).
