The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases that involve patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case. The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases "in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 809 (1988) (citing Franchise Tax Bd. of Cal. v. Constr. Laborers Vacation Trust for S. Cal., 463 U.S. 1, 27-28 (1983)); see also Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897) ("if it appears that some substantial and relevant question as to the construction or effect of the patent laws is involved in the controversy, the case is one arising under those laws").

In this case, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar. The experimental use exception is a critical aspect of patent law, and its application is necessary to determine whether Minton's patent infringement claim would have succeeded. See Smith v. Nichols, 88 U.S. 112, 118 (1874) (noting the importance of patent law in determining the validity of a patent).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases that involve substantial questions of federal patent law. See Pratt, 168 U.S. at 259; see also Dalehite v. United States, 346 U.S. 15, 28-29 (1953) (noting that federal jurisdiction is exclusive when a case involves a substantial question of federal law). In this case, the patent issue is not only substantial but also necessary to the resolution of Minton's malpractice claim. See Healy v. Sea Gull Specialty Co., 237 U.S. 306, 310 (1915) (holding that a case arises under federal patent law when the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the construction or effect of a patent).

The balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities is not disturbed by granting exclusive federal jurisdiction over this case. See Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936) (noting that the federal courts have a limited jurisdiction and that state courts have jurisdiction over cases that do not involve substantial questions of federal law). The federal courts have a strong interest in maintaining uniformity in the application of patent law, and granting exclusive federal jurisdiction over this case will help to achieve that goal. See Dalehite, 346 U.S. at 28-29; see also Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912) (noting that a suit to enforce a right that takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily one arising under those laws, but that a suit that involves a substantial question of federal law does arise under those laws).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that a case arises under federal law when it involves a substantial dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law. See Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569; see also The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913) (noting that a case arises under federal patent law when the plaintiff's complaint sets up a right under the patent laws). In this case, Minton's malpractice claim involves a substantial dispute over the application of the experimental use exception, which is a critical aspect of federal patent law.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim. The court of appeals erred in holding that it had subject-matter jurisdiction over Minton's appeal, and this Court should reverse that decision and dismiss the case for lack of jurisdiction.
