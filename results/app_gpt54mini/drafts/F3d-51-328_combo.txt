**ARGUMENT**

The preliminary injunction should be reversed because the Fair Housing Amendments Act does not require a housing provider to confer a substantive preference on a disabled resident before that resident is otherwise entitled to the benefit sought. The Act requires “reasonable accommodations,” not special entitlements untethered to an existing housing rule. See *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397 (1979) (recognizing that anti-discrimination law does not compel fundamental alteration of a program). Although *Davis* postdates 1970, the governing principle is longstanding: statutes addressing discrimination do not authorize courts to rewrite private arrangements absent clear command. See *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); *Railroad Co. v. Maryland*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 456 (1874).

Cadman Towers maintains a facially neutral first-come, first-served parking policy. Equity does not ordinarily permit a court to displace such an established allocation method in the absence of proof that the rule is unlawful or inconsistently administered. See *O’Donoghue v. United States*, 289 U.S. 516 (1933); *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944) (equitable relief must be exercised “for the public interest,” not as a roving license to reorder private rights). The record here shows that parking is scarce and that Cadman Towers uses an objective waiting-list system. The injunction does not merely require flexibility in implementation; it compels the cooperative to bypass other residents who have complied with the policy and are waiting their turn.

That result is inconsistent with settled equitable principles. A preliminary injunction is an extraordinary remedy, not a tool for granting final relief before the merits are adjudicated. See *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944); *De Beers Consol. Mines, Ltd. v. United States*, 325 U.S. 212 (1945). The applicant must show a clear legal right and irreparable injury. See *Weinberger v. Romero-Barcelo*, 456 U.S. 305 (1982) (later authority reflecting traditional equitable standards). The district court’s order effectively awards Shapiro the very parking space she seeks permanently, without a merits determination that the Act requires Cadman Towers to alter its allocation system.

Nor does the record establish that the Act compels such a priority over other residents. Accommodation doctrine has always been cabined by reasonableness and administrability. See *Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. Co. v. Chicago*, 166 U.S. 226 (1897) (due process requires lawful procedures, not ad hoc deprivation of property or benefits). Where an institution has adopted a neutral rule distributing a scarce resource, courts have been reluctant to require individual exemptions that would burden third parties and undermine the rule’s coherence. See *Patterson v. Kentucky*, 97 U.S. 501 (1878); *Gelpcke v. City of Dubuque*, 68 U.S. (1 Wall.) 175 (1863). The parking spaces at 101 Clark Street are limited. Granting Shapiro an immediate space necessarily takes a space from another resident or employee, including individuals who, unlike Shapiro, have complied with the waiting-list process.

The injunction also ignores the distinction between access to housing and access to a particular ancillary amenity. A cooperative may be required to refrain from discriminatory exclusion, but the statute does not transform every collateral convenience into an individually guaranteed right. See *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60 (1917) (condemning discriminatory exclusion from housing itself); *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948) (judicial enforcement of racially restrictive covenants unconstitutional). Those cases address direct barriers to occupancy, not the redesign of internal allocations among already admitted residents. Shapiro owns and occupies her apartment; she is not denied housing. The dispute concerns a parking privilege ancillary to residence, and the Act should not be stretched beyond its text to compel preferential treatment in a scarce amenity.

Finally, the district court’s order improperly substitutes judicial administration for cooperative governance. Courts have long recognized that they should not supervise the day-to-day internal management of private associations absent a clear legal violation. See *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946) (constitutional obligations turn on specific circumstances, not abstract analogies); *Munn v. Illinois*, 94 U.S. 113 (1877) (public regulation depends on the nature of the entity and the use at issue). Cadman Towers’s parking policy is neutral, preexisting, and applied across the cooperative. If Shapiro is eventually entitled to a parking space through the ordinary system, Cadman Towers has indicated it will consider a reasonable placement. The injunction, however, short-circuits that process and imposes a judicially created priority.

Because the district court ordered relief beyond the statute’s command and displaced a neutral allocation system without a merits finding, the preliminary injunction should be vacated.
