**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Appeals correctly held that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This conclusion flows from the fundamental nature of the right to effective counsel and the flexible character of the writ of habeas corpus.

**I. The Right to Effective Assistance of Counsel Is a Fundamental Constitutional Guarantee Cognizable in Habeas Corpus**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel includes the right to effective assistance. *Nowakowski v. Maroney*, 386 U.S. 542, 543 (1967) (per curiam) (recognizing that ineffective assistance of counsel “denied the aid and assistance of counsel guaranteed by the Constitution”). This right is so essential that a defendant must intelligently waive it. *United States ex rel. McCann v. Adams*, 320 U.S. 220, 221 (1943). When a prisoner alleges that counsel’s ineffectiveness caused the default of a federal claim, that allegation goes to the heart of the constitutional guarantee and must be heard.

**II. Habeas Corpus Is a Flexible Remedy Designed to Vindicate Constitutional Rights**

The writ of habeas corpus is not a rigid, technical remedy. *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266, 269 (1948). Federal courts have broad discretion to consider claims that would otherwise be barred by state procedural rules when fundamental rights are at stake. *Holiday v. Johnston*, 313 U.S. 342, 344 (1941) (procedure must be consistent with due process). The exhaustion doctrine, while important, is a rule of comity, not jurisdiction. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950) (“Ordinarily an application for habeas corpus by one detained under a state court judgment of conviction for crime will be entertained by a federal court only after all state remedies available … have been exhausted.”). Comity does not require that a claim used as cause be itself unexhausted or free from procedural default.

**III. A Procedurally Defaulted Ineffective-Assistance Claim Can Provide Cause Because the Default Itself Does Not Negate the Constitutional Violation**

Cause exists when an objective factor external to the defense impeded compliance with state procedural rules. Ineffective assistance of appellate counsel is precisely such an external factor. The fact that the ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted does not erase the constitutional violation. The federal court may examine the merits of that claim to determine whether it excuses the default of the underlying claim. This approach is consistent with the principle that state procedural bars cannot prevent federal courts from remedying constitutional violations. *Smith v. Bennett*, 365 U.S. 708, 709 (1961) (state may not condition habeas access on payment of a fee); *Long v. District Court of Iowa*, 385 U.S. 192, 193 (1966) (indigent entitled to transcript for habeas appeal).

**IV. Federal Courts Must Not Allow State Procedural Technicalities to Obstruct the Vindication of Federal Rights**

This Court has repeatedly held that federal habeas relief is available even when state procedural rules would otherwise bar review. *Walker v. Wainwright*, 390 U.S. 335, 336 (1968) (per curiam) (reversing district court that thought itself powerless to consider habeas claims because prisoner was serving another sentence). Similarly, a prisoner may challenge a consecutive sentence even before beginning to serve it. *Peyton v. Rowe*, 391 U.S. 54, 55 (1968). These decisions reflect the core purpose of habeas corpus: to provide a swift and effective remedy for unconstitutional detention. Requiring a prisoner to exhaust a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim before using it as cause would elevate form over substance and frustrate that purpose.

**V. Conclusion**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
