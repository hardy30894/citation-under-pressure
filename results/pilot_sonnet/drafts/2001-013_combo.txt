**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 4(b)(1)'s Exemption Applies Only Where Another Agency Has Actually Exercised Authority Over the Specific Working Conditions At Issue, and Must Be Construed Narrowly.**

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA jurisdiction only where another federal agency has exercised statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards affecting the *particular* occupational hazard in question. The provision is an exemption from a remedial safety statute, and this Court has long instructed that such exemptions "are to be narrowly construed against" the party invoking them. *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945); accord *Mitchell v. Kentucky Finance Co.*, 359 U.S. 290, 295 (1959); *Arnold v. Ben Kanowsky, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 388, 392 (1960). The Coast Guard's mere statutory *capacity* to regulate uninspected vessels cannot substitute for the exercise Congress required. See *FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 351–52 (1941) (a grant of regulatory authority does not itself establish that the authority has been exercised as to a given category of conduct; courts must look to what the agency has actually done, not merely to what it might do). Mallard's position collapses the statute's careful distinction between an agency's *jurisdiction* and its *exercise* of that jurisdiction—precisely the distinction §4(b)(1) preserves.

The Secretary's construction of §4(b)(1), moreover, is entitled to substantial weight because it reflects the informed judgment of the agency charged with administering the OSH Act. As this Court explained in *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944), "the rulings, interpretations and opinions" of the administering agency, "while not controlling upon the courts by reason of their authority, do constitute a body of experience and informed judgment to which courts and litigants may properly resort for guidance." The Secretary's longstanding view—that the Coast Guard's exercise of authority over *inspected* vessels does not extend to the comparatively unregulated category of *uninspected* vessels—is entitled to that respect.

**II. The Coast Guard Has Not Exercised Authority Over the Occupational Hazards of Drilling Operations Aboard Uninspected Vessels.**

The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are narrow and equipment-specific—fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and signaling devices—and say nothing about the drilling operations that caused the explosion aboard Rig 52. Where, as here, no comprehensive federal maritime regulatory scheme addresses the specific occupational hazard, general federal regulatory law is not displaced. This Court's admiralty jurisprudence confirms that gaps in a specialized maritime regulatory scheme are properly filled by generally applicable law rather than left unregulated. *Wilburn Boat Co. v. Fireman's Fund Ins. Co.*, 348 U.S. 310, 314–16 (1955) (where no established maritime rule governs a subject, other law may supply the governing standard). The same logic governs here: because the Coast Guard has not established a body of standards governing drilling-related occupational safety on uninspected vessels, no conflict exists between Coast Guard authority and OSHA's regulation of that subject, and no basis exists for treating the Coast Guard's narrow equipment rules as an implicit, field-preemptive exercise of authority over drilling hazards generally.

Nor is it anomalous for a generally applicable federal regulatory statute to reach maritime workers. In *Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB*, 316 U.S. 31 (1942), this Court recognized that Congress did not exempt the maritime industry from statutes of general applicability such as the National Labor Relations Act, notwithstanding the existence of separate bodies of maritime law governing shipboard discipline and safety; the Court examined only whether the *particular* application before it conflicted with an established maritime rule. The same interpretive approach governs §4(b)(1): the question is not whether Rig 52 floats on navigable water, but whether the Coast Guard has actually promulgated standards addressing the hazard at issue. It has not.

**III. Rig 52 Is a "Workplace in a State" Under Section 4(a).**

Section 4(a) extends OSHA's coverage to "employment performed in a workplace in a State." 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Rig 52 was drilling in Louisiana's territorial waters—waters within the geographic boundaries of a State for purposes of federal regulatory schemes. This Court has already rejected the notion that the presence of navigable water forecloses application of a federal workplace-safety or compensation scheme to activity occurring within state territorial waters. In *Calbeck v. Travelers Ins. Co.*, 370 U.S. 114, 117, 123–24 (1962), the Court held that the federal Longshoremen's and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act reaches injuries occurring on navigable waters within a State's territory, without regard to artificial jurisdictional lines between land and water or between state and maritime law. The same reasoning forecloses any argument that Rig 52's location on a Louisiana waterway removes it from "a workplace in a State" under §4(a).

**CONCLUSION**

Because the Coast Guard has not exercised statutory authority over the occupational hazards of drilling operations aboard uninspected vessels, and because Rig 52 is a workplace in the State of Louisiana, §4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA's jurisdiction. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
