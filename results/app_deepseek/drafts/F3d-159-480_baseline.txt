**ARGUMENT**

**I. Congress Exceeded Its Commerce Clause Authority in Enacting 18 U.S.C. § 1951 as Applied to Mr. Pearson’s Conduct**

The Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, criminalizes robbery that “in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce.” This Court must determine whether the government presented sufficient evidence that Mr. Pearson’s conduct substantially affected interstate commerce. *See United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559 (1995) (holding that Congress may regulate activities that substantially affect interstate commerce). In *Lopez*, the Supreme Court struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act because the regulated activity had “nothing to do with commerce or any sort of economic enterprise.” *Id.* at 561.

Here, the government’s sole evidence of an interstate commerce nexus was that Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas is part of a national chain, purchases goods from out-of-state suppliers, and suffered business losses after the robbery. But mere operation of a business that engages in interstate commerce does not transform every local robbery into a federal crime. This Court recognized in *United States v. Collins*, 40 F.3d 95, 100 (5th Cir. 1994), that the Hobbs Act requires a showing that the defendant’s conduct itself—not merely the victim’s business activities—had a “realistic probability of effect on interstate commerce.” The government failed to establish any such probability here.

In *United States v. Rivera*, 77 F.3d 1293, 1294 (10th Cir. 1996), this Court held that robberies of businesses engaged in interstate commerce satisfy the Hobbs Act’s jurisdictional element. However, *Rivera* involved a business that “regularly purchased inventory from out-of-state suppliers.” *Id.* at 1295. Even under *Rivera*, the government must prove a “minimal” but “realistic” effect on commerce. *Id.* at 1294. Here, the only evidence was that Mr. Goodcents purchases “goods from both Kansas and out-of-state companies.” This generic testimony fails to demonstrate any concrete effect on interstate commerce caused by this single, localized robbery. *Cf. United States v. Hickman*, 179 F.3d 230, 232 (5th Cir. 1999) (requiring “proof that the robbery itself affected interstate commerce”). Without such proof, Mr. Pearson’s conviction violates the constitutional limits on federal power.

**II. The District Court Erred by Refusing to Allow Mr. Pearson’s Tardy Alibi Defense**

The district court excluded Mr. Pearson’s alibi notice because it was untimely under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12.1. However, exclusion of evidence is a drastic sanction that must be justified by the defendant’s bad faith or prejudice to the government. *See Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400, 414-15 (1988) (recognizing that exclusion should be a last resort). Mr. Pearson’s alibi—that he was at a jewelry store at the time of the robbery—was central to his defense and supported by documentary evidence. The government suffered no prejudice because it had notice of the alibi before trial and could have investigated. The district court therefore abused its discretion. *See United States v. Myers*, 123 F.3d 350, 359 (6th Cir. 1997) (finding abuse of discretion where alibi evidence was excluded despite lack of prejudice).

**III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Support Mr. Pearson’s Convictions**

To sustain a conviction, the government must present evidence sufficient for a rational jury to find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). The only eyewitness identification of Mr. Pearson as the unarmed robber came from Angela Starks, whose testimony was inherently unreliable. Ms. Starks failed to identify Mr. Pearson from a photo array shortly after the robbery and only identified him after seeing his picture on television. She testified that Mr. Pearson was the only black male of the right age in the courtroom. Such suggestive circumstances render her identification constitutionally suspect. *See Neil v. Biggers*, 409 U.S. 188, 199 (1972) (evaluating reliability of identification). Without credible identification, the remaining evidence—accomplice testimony from a cooperating witness—was insufficient.

**IV. The District Court Erred in Its Felony Murder Instruction**

The district court instructed the jury that Mr. Pearson could be convicted of felony murder if the killing occurred during the commission of a robbery. But this instruction failed to require the jury to find that Mr. Pearson had the requisite mental state for the underlying felony. *See United States v. Pearson*, 159 F.3d 480, 483-84 (10th Cir. 1998) (reversing similar instruction). The error was not harmless.

**V. The District Court Erred by Refusing Lesser Included Offense Instructions**

Mr. Pearson requested instructions on robbery and attempted robbery as lesser included offenses. A defendant is entitled to such an instruction if a rational jury could convict on the lesser offense while acquitting on the greater. *See Schmuck v. United States*, 489 U.S. 705, 716 n.8 (1989). Given the jury’s inability to find Mr. Pearson guilty of the underlying robbery, the refusal was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse Mr. Pearson’s convictions.
