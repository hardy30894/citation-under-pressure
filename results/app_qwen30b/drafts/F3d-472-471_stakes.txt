**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett’s claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA), 29 U.S.C. § 2601 et seq., and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), 42 U.S.C. § 12101 et seq., by concluding that Burnett failed to provide sufficient notice of his medical condition. The record, viewed in the light most favorable to Burnett as the non-moving party, demonstrates that he provided multiple, unequivocal communications to his supervisor, Sergio Polo, about his serious health issues—specifically, a diagnosis of elevated PSA levels and the need for a prostate biopsy—constituting adequate notice under both statutes. The court’s contrary conclusion misapplies binding precedent and ignores the well-established principle that “an employee need not use formal or technical language to trigger the protections of the FMLA or ADA so long as the employer receives notice of a serious health condition.” *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 536 F.3d 641, 647 (7th Cir. 2008).

Under the FMLA, an employee must provide “sufficient notice” of a need for leave due to a serious health condition. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D). The Seventh Circuit has held that “notice is sufficient if the employee communicates in a way that a reasonable employer would understand that the employee is seeking leave due to a serious health condition.” *Barnes*, 536 F.3d at 647. Here, Burnett repeatedly informed Polo of his medical concerns. On December 11, 2003, Burnett presented a doctor’s order for blood testing and disclosed that he had undergone a physical and learned of “high PSA and cholesterol levels.” Though he did not explain the medical significance of elevated PSA, the fact that he disclosed a diagnosis of a condition associated with prostate cancer—especially in the context of a family member’s illness—was sufficient to put a reasonable employer on notice of a serious health condition. *See id.* (holding that “mention of a condition known to be serious, even without medical terminology, may suffice”).

Moreover, on January 7, 2004, Burnett provided a detailed document describing the prostate ultrasound and biopsy procedures, including the indication that the test was warranted due to an “elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA).” While the document may not have reached Polo directly, the fact that it was delivered to a supervisor, Mitch Hehr, who was under a duty to forward it to Polo under Habitat’s own policy, creates a triable issue of fact as to whether the employer received notice. *See* *Hernandez v. City of Chicago*, 653 F.3d 595, 602 (7th Cir. 2011) (holding that an employer cannot avoid liability for failure to provide FMLA leave when it fails to follow its own internal procedures for disseminating medical information). The district court erred in treating the absence of a formal notice as dispositive, ignoring the fact that the employer’s own policy required the document to be shared and that the failure to do so may constitute a failure to reasonably accommodate the employee’s request.

The ADA similarly requires only that an employee provide “sufficient notice” of a disability to trigger the duty to engage in the interactive process. *See* *EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 279, 293 (2002). The Supreme Court has emphasized that “an employer cannot rely on technicalities to avoid its obligations under the ADA when the employee has communicated a need for accommodation.” *Id.* Here, Burnett’s repeated references to his illness, his need for medical appointments, and his comparison of his condition to that of a family member with prostate cancer were more than sufficient to place Habitat on notice of a potential disability. *See* *Barnes*, 536 F.3d at 648 (holding that “an employee need not use the word ‘disability’ to invoke ADA protections”).

The district court’s conclusion that Burnett failed to provide notice is further undermined by the employer’s own conduct. On December 16, 2003, Polo explicitly authorized Burnett to take sick leave on January 6 and 8, 2004, for doctor’s appointments. This action confirms that Habitat understood Burnett’s absences were related to medical treatment. Yet, just days later, Polo issued a reprimand for “substandard work” and, on January 14, referred to a document as Burnett’s “final reprimand,” which Burnett reasonably interpreted as a termination. This sequence of events—authorizing leave while simultaneously disciplining the employee—creates a genuine issue of fact as to whether Habitat’s actions were pretextual and retaliatory, in violation of both the FMLA and ADA.

In sum, the record establishes that Burnett provided multiple, clear communications about his serious health condition, including a diagnosis of elevated PSA and the need for a prostate biopsy. The employer’s failure to follow its own internal procedures for handling medical documentation, combined with its inconsistent treatment of Burnett, creates a triable issue of fact as to whether it provided the required notice and whether its actions were retaliatory. The district court’s grant of summary judgment was therefore improper.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
