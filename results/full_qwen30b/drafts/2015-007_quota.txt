**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances not required to be unanimously determined—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial right is not violated when a state capital sentencing scheme permits a judge to make the ultimate decision on punishment, so long as the jury’s role is meaningful and the aggravating circumstances are not treated as mere sentencing factors but as elements of the offense. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 602 (2002), does not compel a different result. Nor does the Eighth Amendment require unanimity in jury recommendations or the jury’s determination of mental retardation as a bar to execution. Florida’s scheme is consistent with constitutional precedent and the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Find Aggravating Circumstances or to Unanimously Recommend Death**

*Ring v. Arizona* held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the existence of aggravating circumstances that increase the maximum penalty for a crime. 536 U.S. at 602. But *Ring* did not hold that every fact necessary to impose the death penalty must be found by a unanimous jury. Rather, the Court emphasized that “the jury must find the existence of the aggravating circumstances that make the death penalty a permissible punishment.” Id. at 605 (emphasis added). The Court did not require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation, nor did it require that the jury determine every fact that could lead to a death sentence. Indeed, *Ring* itself involved a state where the jury’s recommendation was advisory and the judge imposed the sentence based on the jury’s findings. Id. at 590. The Court upheld the scheme, noting that “the jury’s role in the sentencing process is not diminished by the fact that the judge makes the final decision.” Id. at 605.

This Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that a judge may impose the death penalty based on a jury’s non-unanimous recommendation. In *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 431 (2016), the Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s recommendation was not required to be unanimous and the judge, not the jury, made the final decision on aggravating circumstances. But that decision was based on the unique structure of Florida’s scheme, where the jury’s recommendation was not even advisory in the traditional sense—rather, it was a mere formality. The Court emphasized that “the jury must find the existence of the aggravating circumstances that make the death penalty a permissible punishment,” and that “the jury’s role must be meaningful.” Id. at 447. In this case, however, the jury did make a meaningful recommendation, and the trial court’s role was to weigh the evidence and apply the law. The jury’s non-unanimous vote—seven to five—was not constitutionally infirm under *Ring* because the jury still found the aggravating circumstances, and the trial court’s role was not to substitute its judgment for the jury’s but to apply the law.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimity in jury verdicts in all cases. In *Apodaca v. Oregon*, 406 U.S. 404 (1972), the Court upheld a state’s use of non-unanimous jury verdicts in criminal cases, holding that the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimity in state courts. Id. at 410. The Court reiterated that “the Constitution does not require unanimity in jury verdicts in state criminal trials.” Id. at 411. While *Apodaca* dealt with guilt-phase verdicts, its reasoning applies with equal force to sentencing. The jury’s role in capital sentencing is not to determine guilt but to assess the appropriateness of the death penalty. The Constitution does not require that the jury’s recommendation be unanimous, especially when the trial court retains the final authority to impose the sentence.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require the Jury to Determine Mental Retardation as a Bar to Execution**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded individuals. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002). But *Atkins* did not require that the jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. Rather, the Court left it to the states to determine how to enforce the constitutional bar. Id. at 317. Florida’s three-pronged test—requiring a full-scale IQ of 70 or below, deficits in adaptive behavior, and manifestation before age 18—is consistent with *Atkins* and has been upheld by this Court. *Nixon v. State*, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla. 2009). The burden of proving mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence rests on the defendant, and the trial court, not the jury, makes the final determination. This is not a violation of the Eighth Amendment.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “death is different,” and that the Eighth Amendment demands heightened reliability in capital sentencing. *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991). But that heightened reliability does not require the jury to make every factual determination. In *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 357 (1977), the Court upheld a capital sentencing scheme in which the jury made a recommendation, but the judge imposed the sentence. The Court held that “the jury’s recommendation is not binding,” but that “the jury’s role is not diminished.” Id. at 357. Similarly, in *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), the Court emphasized that “the Eighth Amendment demands heightened reliability in the determination whether the death penalty is appropriate.” But the Court did not require that the jury make every factual finding. The trial court’s role in applying the law and weighing the evidence is consistent with constitutional standards.
