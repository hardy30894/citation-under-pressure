# ARGUMENT

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that navigability for title purposes is determined at the time of statehood, and correctly applied that standard here. This Court's equal-footing doctrine jurisprudence has, for over a century, tied title to the riverbed to navigability *as it existed when the State entered the Union*. Petitioner's attempt to recast this settled temporal rule does not withstand scrutiny.

## I. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Fixes Title at the Moment of Statehood, Requiring Courts to Assess Navigability as of That Date.

This Court has long held that "[t]he shores of navigable waters, and the soils under them" pass to each new State upon its admission to the Union, "not by virtue of any clause in the Constitution, but as an inherent attribute of sovereignty." *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). Because title vests instantaneously upon statehood, this Court has consistently examined navigability at that fixed historical moment rather than at some other time.

In *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), this Court explained that the "test" for equal-footing purposes turns on "the fact of navigability of the river at the time of statehood." *Id.* at 10. The Court had no occasion to survey the river's navigability decades before statehood, nor at trial itself; it asked only whether the waterway was navigable "at the time" Utah joined the Union. This temporal anchor makes sense because the equal-footing doctrine is fundamentally about *sovereign succession*—the transfer of dominion from the United States to the incoming State occurs at statehood, and it is at that moment that the character of the riverbed must be assessed. *See Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26 (1894) (explaining that "absolute property in, and dominion and sovereignty over" submerged lands "pass[es] to the several States" upon admission).

This Court reaffirmed the statehood-date rule in *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), holding that navigability "is to be determined as of the time of the admission of the state." *Id.* at 56. And in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court again confirmed that the relevant inquiry is "the capacity of the streams for navigation at the time of the admission of the State." *Id.* at 75.

Nothing in *PPL Montana*'s theory of the case displaces this settled framework. Petitioner does not—and cannot—identify a single decision of this Court holding that present-day navigability, or navigability at some other date, controls title. The Montana courts properly applied the statehood-date test to the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers.

## II. The Segment-by-Segment Approach Petitioner Advocates Is Consistent With, Not Contrary To, This Court's Precedent, Provided the Statehood-Date Inquiry Remains Fixed.

While Respondent agrees that navigability must be assessed on a segment-by-segment basis, see *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 77 (evaluating "different sections" of the river separately), Petitioner errs in suggesting that this segment-specific inquiry somehow permits courts to abandon the statehood-date baseline. The proper application of *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), requires courts to ask, segment by segment, whether *each* stretch was navigable *at statehood*—not whether it is navigable today, nor whether portages or discontinuities elsewhere in the river's course somehow retroactively alter the historical record for the specific segment at issue.

The trial court did precisely what this Court's precedents require: it examined the historical record—including affidavits, historical publications, and government surveys documenting use of the rivers "at the time" of Montana's 1889 admission—to determine navigability segment by segment. This methodology tracks *Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. at 56, and *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75, both of which relied on historical evidence of the waterway's condition and use at the relevant historical moment.

## III. Petitioner's Reliance on Present-Day Recreational or Commercial Non-Use Is Legally Irrelevant Under This Court's Test.

Petitioner suggests that evidence of present-day conditions—including the existence of dams, portages around rapids, or modern recreational use—should inform or supplant the statehood-date inquiry. This Court rejected an analogous argument in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), where it held that the presence of occasional obstructions did not defeat navigability so long as the river was, "in fact, susceptible" to use as a highway for commerce "at the time" of statehood. *Id.* at 76. The proper test, as this Court articulated in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1871), asks whether the waters were used, or were "susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce," at the relevant time—here, 1889. *Id.* at 563.

The Montana Supreme Court's reliance on historical records—including territorial-era steamboat traffic, ferry operations, and log-driving—was fully consistent with *The Daniel Ball*'s susceptibility test and this Court's statehood-date framework. That such uses might not persist today, or that dams now obstruct portions of the rivers, has no bearing on the historical inquiry this Court's precedents demand.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed insofar as it correctly required navigability to be assessed at the time of statehood, consistent with *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845), *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1 (1894), *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), and *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971).
