**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from the bankruptcy estate because inherited IRAs are not “retirement funds” within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). Those provisions exempt only “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation” under specified Internal Revenue Code provisions. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The text requires more than a tax-favored account; it requires funds that function as retirement savings. That is why the Supreme Court has looked to the attributes of the account at issue, not merely its label or tax treatment. See Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 331–33 (2005).

An inherited IRA lacks the essential attributes of a retirement fund. Under the tax code, the beneficiary may not add contributions, may not roll the account over into another IRA, and must begin mandatory distributions shortly after inheritance, regardless of the beneficiary’s age. 26 U.S.C. §§ 402(c)(11)(A), 408(d)(3)(C), 401(a)(9)(B). As this Court has recognized in analogous settings, whether an asset counts for a federal statutory exemption depends on what the statute is trying to protect. In Rousey, the Court held that IRAs qualified under § 522(d)(10)(E) because they provided income “on account of age,” and because the debtors’ access to those funds was constrained until retirement. 544 U.S. at 331–36. In contrast, inherited IRAs are not savings reserved for the owner’s retirement; they are time-limited vehicles for current consumption. Clark v. Rameker, 714 F.3d 559, 562–64 (7th Cir. 2013). As the Seventh Circuit correctly explained, “an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” Id. at 562.

The statutory phrase “retirement funds” confirms that conclusion. Congress used that term in conjunction with tax-exempt accounts, but it did not define it to include every account that once held retirement savings. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The better reading is that the relevant inquiry is the character of the funds in the debtor’s hands when bankruptcy begins. Courts routinely interpret bankruptcy exemptions by reference to present ownership and use. For example, a debtor cannot claim a homestead exemption for property that is not the debtor’s residence at filing merely because someone else once lived there. See 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A); Clark, 714 F.3d at 565–66. Nor can a debtor convert nonexempt cash into exempt property after filing and thereby retroactively change its character. Id. at 566. The same logic applies here.

The Clarks’ reliance on the fact that the funds were once part of Heidi’s mother’s IRA is misplaced. The Supreme Court has rejected statutory arguments that elevate formal ancestry over present function. In Boggs v. Boggs, 520 U.S. 833, 845–47 (1997), the Court emphasized that Congress protects retirement interests through carefully drawn rules governing who may claim benefits, rather than by broad, relation-based assumptions. Likewise, in Patterson v. Shumate, 504 U.S. 753, 757–58 (1992), the Court construed bankruptcy exemptions with attention to the structure of the underlying federal regime. Here, the structure of the Internal Revenue Code makes clear that an inherited IRA no longer serves the beneficiary’s retirement. The beneficiary can withdraw the funds long before retirement, and must do so on a schedule that ensures the account functions as a decedent’s tax-deferral device, not the beneficiary’s retirement nest egg. Clark, 714 F.3d at 562–63.

The policy of the Bankruptcy Code reinforces the text. Exemptions are meant to protect assets needed for a debtor’s basic future support, not windfalls that are immediately available to creditors’ reach. See Rousey, 544 U.S. at 331–33. Inherited IRAs are different. They are available for use today, not only after the owner stops working. If Congress had wanted to exempt inherited IRAs, it could have said so expressly, as it has elsewhere when creating special protections for particular beneficiaries. See Boggs, 520 U.S. at 845–46. It did not.

Finally, petitioner’s contrary reading would produce an anomalous result. Under that view, an account that the beneficiary may freely spend, withdraw from, and never replenish would be treated as protected retirement savings simply because it originated as an IRA in another person’s hands. That would stretch the term “retirement funds” beyond its ordinary meaning and beyond the bankruptcy exemption’s purpose. The Seventh Circuit correctly rejected that reading and held that inherited IRAs are not exempt under § 522. Clark, 714 F.3d at 567–68.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
