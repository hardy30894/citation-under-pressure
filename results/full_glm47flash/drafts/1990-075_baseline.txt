**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT PROVIDES AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE GOVERNING THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY.**

The non-delegation doctrine is a fundamental principle of constitutional governance, but it is not a rigid bar to congressional action. The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may delegate legislative power so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This standard is “intelligible” in the sense of being sufficiently clear to guide the delegatee’s discretion, not in the sense of being a detailed legislative code. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 369 (1989).

Here, Congress provided a clear and specific standard for the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority under 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). The statute authorizes the Attorney General to schedule a substance on a temporary basis only if he determines that the substance poses an “imminent hazard to the public safety” after considering specific factors, including the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and duration of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These factors constitute an intelligible principle that limits the Attorney General’s discretion. The Court has repeatedly upheld similar delegations in complex regulatory fields, noting that “in our increasingly complex society, replete with ever changing and more technical problems, Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 654.

The need for such flexibility is evident in the facts of this case. The permanent scheduling process contained an inevitable lag time that allowed drug manufacturers to exploit unforeseen loopholes. Congress amended the Act in 1984 specifically to address this problem, authorizing the Attorney General to act swiftly to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This emergency power is analogous to the delegation upheld in *Yakus v. United States*, where the Court found that the President’s authority to fix maximum prices during wartime was a valid exercise of legislative power because it was “confined within narrow limits” defined by Congress. 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944). The temporary scheduling provision is similarly confined; it is limited in duration (one year with a possible six-month extension) and subject to specific procedural safeguards, including notice and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), (4).

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL PROPERLY SUBDELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DRUG ENFORCEMENT AGENCY.**

Even assuming arguendo that the Attorney General’s authority to temporarily schedule is legislative in nature, the Attorney General is fully authorized to delegate that authority to subordinate officers. The Constitution does not prohibit Congress from obtaining assistance from coordinate branches, nor does it prevent the Attorney General from delegating the performance of delegated functions to subordinates. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 654. The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA Administrator is a valid exercise of this authority.

In *Mistretta*, the Court upheld Congress’s delegation of sentencing guideline promulgation to the Federal Sentencing Commission, a body composed of members appointed by the President. The Court emphasized that the Constitution does not forbid Congress from delegating to coordinate branches. Id. at 654. Here, the Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA Administrator is a standard administrative function. The Attorney General has the statutory authority to promulgate regulations, and the regulation at issue, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), explicitly vests the functions of the Attorney General under the CSA in the DEA Administrator. This sub-delegation is a necessary mechanism for the efficient administration of the law and is consistent with the Attorney General’s broad supervisory powers.

**CONCLUSION**

The Controlled Substances Act provides an intelligible principle governing the Attorney General’s temporary scheduling authority, and the Attorney General properly exercised his authority to delegate that power to the DEA. Accordingly, the convictions of Daniel and Lyrissa Touby should be affirmed.
