**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Admitting the Expert Opinion on the Cause of the Line’s Parting**

The opinion of American Steamship’s expert was not proper expert testimony because it was not based on specialized knowledge beyond the ken of the average juror. Expert testimony is admissible only when the subject matter is “so distinctively related to some science, profession, business or occupation as to be beyond the ken of the average layman.” *United States v. 5 Cases*, 179 F.2d 519, 522 (2d Cir. 1950). The cause of a rope’s parting under ordinary conditions is a matter of common experience, not requiring expert elucidation. Moreover, an expert’s opinion must be “based on facts in evidence or on facts personally known to the expert.” *Id.* at 522. Here, the expert’s opinion rested on speculation about the line’s history and ice accumulation, not on any established facts. The opinion also lacked the “general acceptance” in the relevant scientific community required for novel scientific evidence. *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). Because the opinion invaded the province of the jury and lacked a proper foundation, its admission was reversible error.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook’s Motion for Directed Verdict on Unseaworthiness**

The evidence conclusively established that the vessel was unseaworthy as a matter of law. The duty to provide a seaworthy vessel is “absolute and nondelegable.” *Alaska S.S. Co. v. Petterson*, 347 U.S. 396, 400 (1954). Unseaworthiness exists when the vessel or its equipment is “not reasonably fit for its intended purpose.” *Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). The line supporting the bos’n’s chair parted without any apparent cause, demonstrating that it was unfit for its intended use. The shipowner is liable for injuries caused by unseaworthiness “regardless of fault.” *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 88 (1946). Moreover, the doctrine extends to the vessel’s equipment, including lines and rigging. *Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co.*, 321 U.S. 96, 100 (1944). Because no reasonable jury could find that the line was seaworthy, the district court should have granted judgment as a matter of law on this issue.

**III. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook’s Motion for Directed Verdict on Comparative Negligence**

The record contains no evidence that Cook was negligent. The burden of proving contributory negligence rests on the defendant. *The Arizona v. Anelich*, 298 U.S. 110, 123 (1936). Cook followed the master’s orders and used the torch to deice the boom under extreme time pressure and hazardous conditions. His actions were reasonable under the circumstances. Even if some negligence were shown, the seaman’s contributory negligence does not bar recovery but only reduces damages. *Socony-Vacuum Oil Co. v. Smith*, 305 U.S. 424, 430 (1939). Here, the jury’s 50% reduction was unsupported by any evidence of fault. The district court should have directed a verdict that Cook was not comparatively negligent.

**IV. The District Court Erred in Admitting Evidence of Cook’s Alcohol-Related Ailments**

Evidence of Cook’s alcohol-related ailments was irrelevant and highly prejudicial. In a Jones Act case, evidence of a seaman’s drinking habits is inadmissible to show contributory negligence unless there is a causal connection to the injury. *The Arizona v. Anelich*, 298 U.S. at 123 (holding that contributory negligence must be based on the seaman’s conduct, not his character). The defendant offered no evidence that Cook’s ailments caused or contributed to the accident. Such evidence served only to portray Cook as a person of poor character, inviting the jury to decide based on prejudice rather than facts. Because the evidence had no probative value and its prejudicial effect substantially outweighed any relevance, its admission was reversible error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
