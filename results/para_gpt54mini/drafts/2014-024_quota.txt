## Argument

Section 875(c) does not impose strict liability. A conviction for transmitting a threat requires proof that the defendant himself intended the communication as a threat, or at minimum knew it would be understood that way. The Third Circuit erred by affirming a conviction based on a mere reasonable-speaker standard. That approach conflicts with ordinary principles of criminal culpability, with this Court’s threat precedent, and with the First Amendment.

1. **Congress normally requires mens rea.** This Court has long “require[d] awareness of some wrongdoing” where a federal statute is silent as to intent. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994); see also *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-51 (1952). The presumption against criminal strict liability is especially strong where the statute carries serious penalties and sweeps in otherwise innocent conduct. *Liparota v. United States*, 471 U.S. 419, 426 (1985); *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 72-73 (1994). Section 875(c) contains no express dispensation from mens rea. It criminalizes transmitting “any communication containing any threat,” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c), but says nothing about punishing speakers who do not subjectively intend a threat. Under *Staples*, *Liparota*, and *X-Citement Video*, the statute must be read to require at least a subjective mental state as to the threatening character of the communication.

2. **The Third Circuit’s objective test is inconsistent with this Court’s threat cases.** In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), this Court held that the statute at issue did not reach political hyperbole and emphasized that context matters. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359-60 (2003), likewise explained that “true threats” are statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit unlawful violence. Although *Black* addressed a Virginia cross-burning statute, its reasoning is incompatible with a purely objective standard. The Court’s explanation of “true threats” turns on what the speaker “means,” not merely on how a listener might react. *Id.* at 359. The Third Circuit’s rule allows conviction even when the speaker lacked any intent to threaten so long as a hypothetical reasonable person might view the speech as threatening. That is broader than *Watts* and *Black* permit.

3. **The First Amendment requires a subjective mens rea for true threats.** This Court has repeatedly held that “the First Amendment needs breathing space.” *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Criminalizing speech based only on audience reaction chills protected expression, especially where the speech is crude, political, artistic, or therapeutic. *Hess v. Indiana*, 414 U.S. 105, 108-09 (1973) (per curiam), rejected liability where words were not shown to be an intent to produce imminent unlawful action. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (per curiam), likewise requires intent before punishing advocacy. Threat doctrine should not be more speech-restrictive than incitement doctrine. An objective-only test punishes speakers who neither intend harm nor seek to terrorize, contrary to the First Amendment’s protection for unpopular and unsettling speech. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949).

4. **The government’s contrary position rests on fear alone, but fear is not enough.** True-threat doctrine protects listeners from fear of violence, but the Constitution does not permit liability without culpable intent. *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 388-90 (1992), cautioned against content-based punishment even within unprotected categories. *Black* similarly rejected a rule that would permit punishment of speech without the requisite mental state. 538 U.S. at 359-60. A subjective-intent requirement properly balances protection of victims with protection of expression; an objective test does not.

5. **The district court’s instruction relieved the government of proving the essential mental element.** This Court has invalidated convictions where the jury was not required to find the defendant’s culpable mental state. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263; *Liparota*, 471 U.S. at 433-34. Here, the jury was told it could convict based on how a reasonable person would perceive Elonis’s posts, even if he intended them as rap lyrics, venting, or hyperbole. That instruction authorized conviction without proof beyond a reasonable doubt of subjective intent, violating due process. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

Because § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended to threaten, or at least knew the communication would be understood as a threat, Elonis’s conviction cannot stand. The judgment should be reversed.
