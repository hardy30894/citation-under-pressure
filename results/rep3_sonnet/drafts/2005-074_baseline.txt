# ARGUMENT

## I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO PETITIONER BECAUSE HIS RELEVANT CONDUCT—ILLEGAL REENTRY—OCCURRED BEFORE THE STATUTE'S ENACTMENT

Section 241(a)(5) of the INA, enacted as part of IIRIRA in 1996, provides that when an alien illegally reenters after having been deported, "the prior order of removal is reinstated." This Court has long held that "congressional enactments . . . will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result." *Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988). Section 241(a)(5) contains no clear statement that Congress intended it to reach aliens whose illegal reentry—the triggering conduct for the statute—occurred before its effective date.

This Court's retroactivity jurisprudence proceeds in two steps. First, courts ask "whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute's proper reach." *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994). Absent such express direction, courts must determine "whether the new statute would have retroactive effect, i.e., whether it would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party's liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Id.* If applying the statute would have retroactive effect, "our traditional presumption teaches that it does not govern absent clear congressional intent favoring such a result." *Id.*

Petitioner's illegal reentry in 1982 was the conduct that gave rise to any potential reinstatement liability. Section 241(a)(5) attaches new legal consequences—automatic reinstatement without any opportunity for relief—to that already-completed act of reentry. This Court has recognized that "the presumption against statutory retroactivity" applies with particular force where a new law "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70. Because Petitioner's reentry occurred fifteen years before § 241(a)(5)'s enactment, and because that reentry is the very act the statute purports to regulate, the statute's silence as to pre-enactment reentries must be construed against retroactive application.

The government's position—that reinstatement is triggered by the *agency's discovery* of the illegal reentry, rather than the reentry itself—cannot be squared with the statutory text. Section 241(a)(5) reinstates a prior order when an alien "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The operative verb is "reentered," a completed past act. Nothing in the statute suggests that Congress intended the relevant "event" for retroactivity purposes to be the moment of administrative reinstatement rather than the moment of reentry itself. As this Court explained in *Landgraf*, the proper focus is on "the conduct" regulated, not on "the events" of subsequent enforcement. 511 U.S. at 291.

## II. EVEN IF THE STATUTE APPLIES, ITS APPLICATION TO PETITIONER IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE

Even assuming § 241(a)(5) reaches pre-enactment reentries, its application here is unconstitutionally retroactive because it "attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment" and disturbs Petitioner's settled expectations. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270.

### A. Petitioner Possessed a Vested Interest in Seeking Adjustment of Status

Prior to § 241(a)(5)'s enactment, Petitioner—like similarly situated aliens—retained the statutory right to apply for adjustment of status under former INA provisions notwithstanding his illegal reentry. This Court has recognized that retroactivity concerns are heightened where new legislation "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Petitioner's marriage to a United States citizen and his subsequent application for adjustment of status reflected reasonable reliance on the legal landscape as it existed both when he reentered and for nearly two decades thereafter.

The Tenth Circuit's conclusion that Petitioner had no "protectable expectation" misapprehends the retroactivity inquiry. This Court's retroactivity analysis does not require a "vested right" in the constitutional-property sense; rather, it asks whether the new statute "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted." *Id.* Petitioner possessed, at the time of his reentry and throughout the ensuing nineteen years, the statutory eligibility to seek adjustment of status—a right categorically foreclosed by § 241(a)(5)'s bar on "relief under this chapter."

### B. Section 241(a)(5) Increases Petitioner's Liability for Past Conduct

Section 241(a)(5) does more than regulate primary conduct going forward; it retroactively transforms the legal significance of Petitioner's 1982 reentry by foreclosing avenues of relief that were available when he acted and for years afterward. This Court has cautioned that statutes "increas[ing] a party's liability for past conduct" implicate the "familiar considerations of fair notice, reasonable reliance, and settled expectations" that animate the presumption against retroactivity. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. Here, Petitioner built a life in the United States—marrying a citizen and establishing eligibility for adjustment—based on the reasonable expectation that his reentry, while unlawful, did not permanently foreclose all paths to lawful status. Section 241(a)(5) upends that expectation by operation of law, without any additional voluntary act by Petitioner after the statute's enactment.

### C. The Statute's Elimination of All Relief Confirms Its Retroactive Effect

The reinstatement statute's harshness—eliminating any possibility of adjustment, asylum, or other relief—is itself evidence of impermissible retroactivity. This Court has recognized that the "clearest case" for applying the presumption against retroactivity arises when a statute "would impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Petitioner's reentry was a completed transaction; § 241(a)(5) now attaches to that completed transaction a legal consequence—total ineligibility for relief—that did not exist when he acted.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
