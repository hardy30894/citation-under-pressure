ARGUMENT

A state prisoner may not rely upon a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel to excuse the default of an underlying constitutional claim. Exhaustion of state remedies is a prerequisite to federal habeas corpus relief. As this Court explained, the requirement “is not one of those requirements of which a petitioner may take piecemeal advantage.” Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 209 (1950). It demands that the prisoner present each federal claim to the state courts “in accordance with their procedural requirements.” Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. 114, 116 (1944).

Where a prisoner fails to comply with a state procedural rule, the default is an adequate and independent state ground that bars federal review. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953). The same principle governs successive defaults. A claim that itself has been defaulted cannot serve as the vehicle for excusing an earlier default, for that would permit the prisoner to evade the very procedural rules the exhaustion doctrine exists to enforce. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963) (recognizing that federal courts may decline to entertain claims when the prisoner has “deliberately bypassed” state procedures).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding would allow a prisoner to resurrect a forfeited sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim simply by asserting, out of time, that appellate counsel should have raised it. Such bootstrapping contradicts the settled rule that each constitutional claim must independently satisfy the exhaustion requirement. Darr, 339 U.S. at 209; Ex parte Hawk, 321 U.S. at 116. Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was itself dismissed as untimely by the Ohio courts, it supplies no basis for excusing the default of his sufficiency claim. The writ should therefore be denied.
