**ARGUMENT**

**I. CERCLA's Text and Purpose Confirm That Only a Genuine Resolution of Liability Triggers the Contribution Bar of Section 113(f)(3)(B).**

CERCLA offers two roads to recovery: a six-year cost-recovery action under section 107 and a three-year contribution action under section 113(f). Congress did not intend the shorter period to swallow the longer one whenever a PRP settles anything with the government. Where, as here, "the letter of the statute" would produce a result "not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers," this Court has long refused to enforce that letter mechanically. *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). The D.C. Circuit's reading—that any settlement touching any aspect of a response action forecloses cost-recovery relief regardless of whether liability was actually resolved—produces exactly the "absurd or futile result[]" this Court has cautioned against. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). CERCLA is remedial legislation designed to ensure that "responsible parties foot the bill," and it "should be construed broadly to effectuate its purposes," not narrowly to trap an innocent territory into a limitations period that expired before it could even sue. *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967).

**II. The Consent Decree's Express Disclaimer of Liability Precludes a Finding That Guam "Resolved" Its Liability.**

The 2004 Consent Decree states plainly that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." A consent decree, like any negotiated instrument, must be given effect according to the terms the parties actually chose, not the terms a court might imagine they intended. This Court has long insisted that such agreements be read as the parties wrote them, respecting bargains reached "with the consent of all concerned." *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 119 (1932). Guam and EPA bargained for a decree that resolved a Clean Water Act permitting dispute without resolving CERCLA liability for the underlying contamination. To hold that this disclaimer means nothing is to rewrite the parties' agreement rather than interpret it. The Circuit's approach effectively treats every settlement obligating a PRP to take remedial steps as an automatic admission of liability, no matter what the parties expressly said to the contrary—an approach inconsistent with the basic principle that agreements mean what they say.

**III. Contribution Rights Are Purely Statutory, and Ambiguous Settlements Should Not Be Read to Extinguish Guam's Independent Cost-Recovery Remedy.**

Before Congress acted, there was no inherent right of contribution among jointly liable parties; whether and when such a right exists is "a matter for Congress, not for [the courts,] to determine." *Halcyon Lines v. Haenn Ship Ceiling & Refitting Corp.*, 342 U.S. 282, 285 (1952). Section 113(f)(3)(B) is a narrow, congressionally defined exception that applies only when a party has actually "resolved its liability." Courts should not stretch that statutory trigger by implication to cut off a distinct statutory remedy—section 107 cost recovery—that Congress separately guaranteed with its own six-year clock. Limitations periods, moreover, are not to be applied with wooden rigidity where doing so defeats the equities Congress sought to protect; "this equitable doctrine is read into every federal statute of limitations." *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946). Guam did not sit on its rights; it could not have known, from a decree expressly disclaiming liability, that its contribution clock was running. Construing the statute to bar Guam's suit rewards ambiguity with forfeiture, a result section 113 does not compel and equitable principles counsel against.

**IV. Reading the Consent Decree to Extinguish Guam's Section 107 Remedy Raises Serious Due Process Concerns.**

Section 113(f)(2) shields a settling party from future contribution claims by non-settlors. If the 2004 Decree is deemed to have triggered section 113(f)(3)(B), then non-settling PRPs—including any other entities potentially responsible for the Ordot Dump—would have had their contribution exposure extinguished without notice that the Decree carried such consequence. Due process requires, at minimum, "notice reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties" that their rights are at stake. *Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co.*, 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). Nothing in the Decree's text—least of all its disclaimer of liability—provided such notice. Indeed, "the fundamental requisite of due process of law is the opportunity to be heard," *Grannis v. Ordean*, 234 U.S. 385, 394 (1914), an opportunity foreclosed for any party who might have relied on the Decree's plain statement that it resolved nothing regarding liability. The Circuit's construction thus risks silently stripping away rights and remedies through a settlement that, on its face, promised the opposite.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss should be reinstated.
