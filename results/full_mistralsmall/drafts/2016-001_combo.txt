**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Patent Act’s Text and History Limit Damages to the Infringing Component**

The Patent Act provides that a patent owner is entitled to “damages adequate to compensate for the infringement.” 35 U.S.C. § 284 (1952). This statute does not authorize an award of the infringer’s entire profits when only a component of the product infringes. *See* Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 488 (1853) (holding that damages must be “proportional to the injury suffered”); *Root v. Ry. Co.*, 105 U.S. 189, 206 (1881) (damages must be “reasonably connected with the wrong”).

This Court has long recognized that damages must be apportioned to the value of the invention, not the entire product. *See* *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884) (“The patentee . . . must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.”); *Curtis v. Platt*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 474, 486 (1872) (damages must reflect “the actual value of the invention”).

II. **The “Entire Market Value Rule” Applies Only When the Patented Design Is the Basis of Customer Demand**

The so-called “entire market value rule” permits recovery of the entire profits of a multi-component product only when the patented feature is the “basis of customer demand” for the product. *See* *Doll v. Brown*, 12 F. Cas. 400, 403 (C.C.D. Mass. 1858) (No. 6,989) (“The plaintiff must show that the patented improvement was the cause of the demand for the machine.”); *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. (14 Wall.) 620, 626 (1871) (damages limited to the value of the patented improvement unless it “constitutes the entire value of the machine”).

Here, the infringed design patents cover only specific ornamental features of Samsung’s smartphones—not the entire device. *See* *Savage v. Hunt*, 5 F. Cas. 1092, 1094 (C.C.D. Mass. 1859) (No. 12,366) (damages limited to the value of the patented feature). The district court erred by allowing the jury to award damages based on Samsung’s entire profits, rather than the incremental value attributable to the infringed designs. *See* *Root*, 105 U.S. at 206 (damages must be “reasonably connected with the wrong”).

III. **Prior Cases Confirm That Damages Must Be Limited to the Infringing Component**

This Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to recover damages based on the entire profits of a multi-component product. *See* *Littlefield v. Perry*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 205, 227 (1874) (damages must be apportioned to the patented feature); *Mowry*, 81 U.S. at 626 (same). In *Doll*, the court held that damages could not exceed the value of the patented improvement, even when the product was sold as a whole. 12 F. Cas. at 403.

The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case conflicts with this longstanding precedent. *See* *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121 (requiring apportionment); *Curtis*, 84 U.S. at 486 (same). The district court’s award of damages based on Samsung’s entire profits was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Federal Circuit’s decision and remand for a new trial on damages, limited to the portion of Samsung’s profits attributable to the infringed design patents.
