The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Bryant, holding that an uncounseled conviction in tribal court cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), is erroneous. The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, and the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) governs the rights of defendants in those courts. See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). The use of prior uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment, as established by this Court's decision in Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994).

In Nichols, this Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction can be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, as long as the conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment. Id. at 746-47. The same logic applies to the use of prior uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a). The fact that the convictions were obtained in tribal court, where the Sixth Amendment does not apply, does not render them invalid for purposes of § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit's reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), is misplaced. Ant predates Nichols and is inconsistent with this Court's subsequent holding. Moreover, Ant's reasoning is flawed because it imposes a Sixth Amendment right to counsel on tribal courts, which are governed by the ICRA, not the Sixth Amendment. See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987) (extending comity to tribal courts).

The use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) is consistent with Congress's intent to combat domestic violence on tribal lands. See 18 U.S.C. § 117(a); United States v. Lara, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (describing Congress's plenary power to pass legislation affecting Indian tribes). By enacting § 117(a), Congress exercised its authority to permit more vigorous federal prosecution of serious crimes against women on tribal lands.

The Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split and undermines the effectiveness of § 117(a) in combating domestic violence on tribal lands. See United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir.2011); United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir.2011). This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that prior uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

The reliability concerns that underpin the Sixth Amendment right to counsel are not implicated in the context of recidivist statutes like § 117(a), which penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant. See Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). Therefore, the use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment, and the Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed.
