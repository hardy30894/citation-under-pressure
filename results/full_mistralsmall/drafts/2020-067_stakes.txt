**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Applied the Plain-Error Standard and Did Not Exceed Its Authority by Considering the Entire Record

A. The Plain-Error Framework Requires Consideration of the Entire Record

Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), a court of appeals may correct a forfeited error only if it is plain and affects substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 731–32 (1993). To determine whether an error is prejudicial, the reviewing court must assess whether the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Id.* at 734. This inquiry necessarily requires examination of the entire trial record—not just the evidence presented to the jury—to determine whether the error undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. *See United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (explaining that plain-error review is not limited to the trial record when assessing prejudice).

The Eleventh Circuit followed this well-established precedent. It did not err by considering the entire record, including Greer’s prior convictions and the circumstances of his prosecution, to determine whether the Rehaif error prejudiced him. *See United States v. Vail-Bailon*, 868 F.3d 1293, 1300 (11th Cir. 2017) (holding that plain-error review permits consideration of the full record to assess prejudice).

B. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Engage in Improper Factfinding

Greer contends that the Eleventh Circuit improperly considered matters outside the trial record. This argument misapprehends the nature of plain-error review. The court did not make new factual findings; it evaluated the existing record to determine whether the error had a prejudicial effect. *See Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 469–70 (1997) (explaining that prejudice under plain-error review is assessed based on the record as a whole).

The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis was consistent with Supreme Court guidance. In *Rehaif*, the Court emphasized that the government must prove a defendant’s knowledge of both possession and status. 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2195 (2019). The Eleventh Circuit properly examined whether the record contained evidence of Greer’s knowledge of his felon status to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Davis*, 923 F.3d 436, 443 (11th Cir. 2019) (permitting consideration of the full record in plain-error review).

II. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Concluded That Greer Failed to Demonstrate Prejudice

A. The Error Did Not Affect Greer’s Substantial Rights

To establish prejudice under plain-error review, a defendant must show that the error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Resendiz-Patino*, 423 F.3d 1199, 1206 (11th Cir. 2005) (quoting *Johnson*, 520 U.S. at 469). Greer cannot meet this burden.

The record shows that Greer pleaded guilty to being a felon in possession of a firearm, and his prior convictions included aggravated burglary—an enumerated offense under the ACCA. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). Even assuming the Rehaif error occurred, the evidence of Greer’s status as a felon was overwhelming. *See United States v. Williams*, 919 F.3d 1208, 1215 (11th Cir. 2019) (holding that overwhelming evidence of a defendant’s status negates prejudice under plain-error review).

B. The Error Did Not Undermine the Fairness of the Proceedings

Greer argues that the lack of a jury instruction on knowledge of status deprived him of a fair trial. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that the Rehaif error is not structural and does not automatically require reversal. *See Rehaif*, 139 S. Ct. at 2195. The Eleventh Circuit correctly determined that the error did not undermine the fairness of the proceedings, given the overwhelming evidence of Greer’s felon status. *See United States v. Palacios-Quinonez*, 936 F.3d 1172, 1178 (11th Cir. 2019) (holding that overwhelming evidence of a defendant’s status negates prejudice).

III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Relief

The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that Greer failed to demonstrate prejudice is a factual determination entitled to deference. *See United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 631–32 (2002) (holding that plain-error review is deferential to the district court’s factual findings). Greer’s argument that the Eleventh Circuit improperly considered matters outside the trial record is without merit. The court properly examined the entire record to assess prejudice, as required by Supreme Court precedent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
