**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) entitles him to habeas relief when he was, in fact, aware of that right—must be answered in the negative. The Third Circuit correctly held that a violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review and that the error here was harmless because the defendant knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. This conclusion is compelled by precedent, the structure of the Federal Rules, and the fundamental principle that procedural violations do not warrant relief when they do not prejudice the defendant’s substantial rights.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires a district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal following a guilty plea. *Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2)*. However, the rule does not mandate automatic reversal or vacatur of a sentence for noncompliance. Indeed, the Supreme Court has long recognized that not every procedural error requires reversal. As this Court held in *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993), “a reviewing court may not reverse a conviction for a nonconstitutional error unless the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights.” The same standard applies to errors under the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure. *Id.* at 734.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is not clearly erroneous and is supported by the record. The defendant had been represented by counsel, who is presumed to have advised him of his appellate rights. *See United States v. Cronic*, 466 U.S. 648, 659 (1984) (counsel’s role includes informing defendant of rights). Moreover, Peguero did not file a direct appeal, but he did file a timely motion for habeas relief in 1996—over four years after sentencing—indicating that he understood the appellate process and the consequences of not appealing. His decision not to appeal, based on his awareness of the right, demonstrates that the failure to advise did not prejudice him.

The Third Circuit correctly applied the harmless-error standard. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002) (holding that Rule 32 errors are subject to harmless-error review). The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their right to appeal so they may make an informed decision about whether to pursue appellate review. *See United States v. Bynum*, 978 F.2d 1355, 1358 (D.C. Cir. 1992) (explaining that the rule “serves the function of ensuring that defendants are aware of their appellate rights”). That purpose was fully satisfied here. Peguero was aware of his right and chose not to exercise it. The absence of a warning did not alter the outcome.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the absence of a procedural safeguard does not justify relief when the defendant was not prejudiced. In *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004), the Court held that a defendant may not obtain relief under Rule 11 unless he can show that the error affected his substantial rights. The same principle applies here. The failure to advise of the right to appeal is not a structural error, nor does it implicate the fairness of the trial. *See id.* at 82 (distinguishing between structural and non-structural errors). It is a procedural defect that, like others, must be evaluated for harmlessness.

The respondent submits that the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. The district court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was not reversible error because it did not affect his substantial rights. He was aware of the right, understood its significance, and made a deliberate choice not to appeal. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was fulfilled. To hold otherwise would transform a procedural requirement into a jurisdictional bar, contrary to the Court’s settled jurisprudence.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
