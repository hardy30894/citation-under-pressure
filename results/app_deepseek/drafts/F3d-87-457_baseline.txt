ARGUMENT

**I. The Disclosure Provisions Are Facially Overbroad and Violate the First Amendment**

The compelled disclosure of all lobbying expenditures—including those made by principals and categorized by type—unconstitutionally chills protected speech and association. Under *Broadrick v. Oklahoma*, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973), a statute is facially invalid if it prohibits a “substantial” amount of protected speech relative to its legitimate sweep. The Florida statute fails this standard.

The disclosure requirements apply to any person who “lobby[s],” a term defined to encompass “influencing” legislative or executive action. Fla. Stat. § 11.045(1). This definition sweeps into the regulatory net not only direct advocacy but also grassroots communications, public education, and issue-based speech—core First Amendment activities. *See Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 14 (1976) (recognizing that issue advocacy is protected speech). The statute requires disclosure of “all lobbying expenditures” and their sources, including “research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging.” Id. § 11.045(3)(a). Such a broad mandate forces lobbyists and their principals to reveal sensitive information about their political views, strategies, and associations, deterring them from engaging in speech that might be unpopular. *See NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (compelled disclosure of membership lists can chill associational rights).

The State’s interest in preventing corruption is legitimate, but the disclosure provisions are not narrowly tailored. *Buckley* upheld campaign disclosure on the basis that it was “the least restrictive means” of combating corruption. 424 U.S. at 68. Here, Florida’s statute imposes no such limit. It requires disclosure of expenditures unrelated to direct lobbying, such as research and publications, without any showing that such information is necessary to detect improper influence. The availability of an advisory opinion procedure, Fla. Stat. § 11.045(4), does not cure the overbreadth. The procedure is discretionary and provides no clear safe harbor; it merely invites further uncertainty. *See Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 358 (1983) (a vague statute is not saved by an administrative procedure that does not resolve the ambiguity).

Because the statute inhibits a substantial amount of protected speech—including grassroots advocacy and public education—it is facially overbroad. *See City of Houston v. Hill*, 482 U.S. 451, 467 (1987) (overbreadth challenge can succeed where statute “sweeps within its ambit” a wide range of protected conduct).

**II. The Ban on Contingency-Fee Lobbying Violates the First Amendment**

The absolute prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying, Fla. Stat. § 11.047, is an unconstitutional prior restraint on speech and association. The statute bars a lobbyist from receiving compensation “contingent upon the outcome of any legislative or executive action.” This directly restricts the ability of lobbyists and principals to structure their advocacy relationships—a form of expressive association protected by the First Amendment. *See Buckley*, 424 U.S. at 64-65 (recognizing that spending money to communicate is itself protected speech).

Contingency fees are a common method of financing advocacy for those who cannot afford hourly fees. The ban eliminates this option entirely, placing a heavy burden on less wealthy individuals and groups seeking to influence government. *See Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414, 426 (1988) (striking down a ban on paying petition circulators because it “constrain[ed]” the ability to disseminate ideas). Similarly, in *Riley v. National Federation of the Blind*, 487 U.S. 781, 789 (1988), the Court invalidated a state law limiting fees for charitable solicitors, holding that the government cannot “justify any restriction on expression by a ‘benign motive’ if the restriction is not narrowly drawn.” The contingency-fee ban serves no such narrow tailoring.

The State asserts the ban prevents corruption or the appearance of corruption. But a flat prohibition is not the least restrictive means. The Court has upheld less restrictive alternatives, such as disclosure and criminal penalties for bribery. *See Buckley*, 424 U.S. at 26-27 (upholding disclosure of contributions, not a ban on contributions). Moreover, the ban applies regardless of whether any actual quid pro quo exists. It punishes a form of compensation that is itself a contractual arrangement between willing parties, not a corrupt act. *See United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625 (1954) (upholding lobbying disclosure but noting that “the regulation of conduct” must be “narrowly drawn”). The contingency-fee ban is a direct restriction on the content and manner of advocacy; it is not content-neutral and cannot survive strict scrutiny. *See Turner Broadcasting System, Inc. v. FCC*, 512 U.S. 622, 642 (1994) (content-based restrictions must be narrowly tailored to a compelling interest).

The State cannot demonstrate that the ban is necessary to combat corruption. Many other states have disclosure laws without a ban, and no evidence in the record shows that Florida’s prior history of abuse required such a drastic measure. The ban therefore violates the First Amendment rights of the League’s members to engage in paid advocacy.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and declare the disclosure and contingency-fee provisions of Chapter 93-121 facially unconstitutional.
