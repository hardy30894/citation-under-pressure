**ARGUMENT**

**A District Court’s Failure to Advise a Defendant of His Right to Appeal Under Rule 32 Does Not Warrant Habeas Relief When the Defendant Was Independently Aware of That Right and Elected Not to Appeal.**

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure are designed to ensure fairness, not to create traps for unwary courts. Rule 32(a)(2) requires a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal. But when a defendant already knows of that right and chooses not to exercise it, the purpose of the rule is fully served. A violation of Rule 32 is subject to harmless-error analysis, and where the defendant’s knowledge is established, any error is harmless.

**I. The Purpose of Rule 32 Is to Inform, and That Purpose Is Satisfied When the Defendant Already Knows of His Right to Appeal.**

The plain text of Rule 32(a)(2) requires the court to “advise the defendant of any right to appeal the sentence.” The rule’s purpose is to ensure that defendants are not deprived of their appellate rights through ignorance. But where a defendant already possesses that knowledge, the rule’s objective is met. This Court has long recognized that procedural rules are not ends in themselves; they are means to ensure fundamental fairness. *See, e.g., Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82, 86 (1946) (rejecting a claim that a failure to advise of the right to counsel automatically invalidated a conviction where the defendant had actual knowledge of that right). The logic of *Canizio* applies with equal force here: if the defendant knew of his right to appeal, the failure to advise him caused no prejudice.

**II. A Rule 32 Violation Is Subject to Harmless-Error Review.**

This Court has consistently held that violations of federal procedural rules are subject to harmless-error analysis unless the rule itself provides otherwise. *See, e.g., Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52, 58-59 (1985) (applying harmless-error analysis to a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel in the plea context). Rule 32 contains no language suggesting that its violation automatically entitles a defendant to relief. To the contrary, the rule’s advisory nature suggests that harmless-error review is appropriate. The Court of Appeals correctly held that the error here was harmless because Peguero knew of his right to appeal.

**III. Peguero’s Knowledge of His Right to Appeal Precludes Any Claim of Prejudice.**

The District Court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is entitled to deference. *See Blackledge v. Allison*, 431 U.S. 63, 74-75 (1977) (holding that factual findings after an evidentiary hearing are entitled to deference). Because Peguero knew of his right, the failure to advise him caused no prejudice. He cannot show that he would have appealed but for the court’s omission. *See Hill*, 474 U.S. at 59 (requiring a showing of prejudice in the plea context). Indeed, Peguero’s decision not to appeal was a knowing and voluntary choice.

**IV. Habeas Relief Is Not Available for Harmless Errors.**

Habeas corpus is an extraordinary remedy, reserved for fundamental miscarriages of justice. *See, e.g., Lane v. Williams*, 455 U.S. 624, 631 (1982) (holding that habeas relief is not available for errors that do not affect the legality of the detention). A harmless error, such as the one here, does not warrant such relief. *See also Swain v. Pressley*, 430 U.S. 372, 378 (1977) (noting that habeas relief is limited to cases where the remedy by motion is inadequate or ineffective). Peguero’s claim fails because he cannot demonstrate that the error caused him any actual harm.

**V. The Court of Appeals Correctly Applied Harmless-Error Review.**

The Court of Appeals held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review and that the error was harmless because Peguero knew of his right to appeal. This conclusion is consistent with this Court’s precedents. *See, e.g., Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 389 (1994) (applying harmless-error review to a procedural default). The court’s analysis was thorough and correct.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32 does not warrant habeas relief when the defendant was independently aware of that right and elected not to appeal. The error was harmless, and Peguero suffered no prejudice.
