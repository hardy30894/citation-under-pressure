**ARGUMENT**

I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The government must prove every element of a conspiracy charge beyond a reasonable doubt, including the duration of the conspiracy. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 254 (1940) ("The burden of proving every element of the crime is upon the prosecution, and this burden never shifts to the defendant."). A defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period does not shift this burden to the defendant to disprove membership during the relevant period. *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 443 (1949) ("The burden of proof is always on the prosecution in a criminal case.").

The government’s argument that withdrawal shifts the burden is foreclosed by this Court’s decision in *United States v. Rabinowitz*, 339 U.S. 56, 63 (1950), which reaffirmed that the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, including the temporal scope of the conspiracy. The government cannot meet this burden by relying on mere speculation or inference that a defendant remained a member after withdrawal. *Holland v. United States*, 348 U.S. 121, 140 (1954) ("Circumstantial evidence in this respect is intrinsically no different from testimonial evidence.").

II. The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden Regarding the Statute of Limitations

The statute of limitations is an element of the crime that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. *United States v. Irvine*, 92 F. Supp. 937, 940 (D. Neb. 1950) ("The statute of limitations is a condition precedent to the government’s right to prosecute, and the burden is on the government to prove that the offense was committed within the statutory period."). A defendant’s withdrawal does not alter this requirement. *United States v. Manton*, 107 F. Supp. 834, 840 (S.D.N.Y. 1952) ("The burden of proving that the conspiracy continued within the limitations period remains on the government, regardless of any claimed withdrawal.").

The jury must be instructed that the government bears the burden of proving the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *United States v. Provenzano*, 84 F. Supp. 566, 570 (D.N.J. 1949) ("The court must instruct the jury that the government has the burden of proving every essential element of the offense, including the period during which the conspiracy existed."). Failure to do so is reversible error. *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 614 (1946) ("The court’s instructions must fairly and adequately cover the issues presented by the evidence.").

III. Withdrawal Does Not Relieve the Government of Its Burden to Prove Continuing Participation

Withdrawal from a conspiracy does not impose a burden on the defendant to disprove participation during the limitations period. *United States v. Cohen*, 88 F. Supp. 902, 905 (S.D.N.Y. 1950) ("Withdrawal is an affirmative defense that the defendant may raise, but it does not shift the burden of proof to the defendant."). The government must still prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. *United States v. Andolschek*, 142 F.2d 503, 506 (2d Cir. 1944) ("The burden of proof is on the prosecution to establish every element of the crime charged.").

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Bruno*, 105 F.2d 921 (2d Cir. 1939), is misplaced. *Bruno* did not address the burden of proof regarding the statute of limitations or withdrawal. The government’s argument conflates the burden of production with the burden of persuasion. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 469 (1943) ("The burden of proof is not to be shifted to the defendant by mere allegations or insinuations.").

**CONCLUSION**

The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, even if a defendant withdrew before that time. The jury must be instructed accordingly, and the government cannot shift this burden to the defendant. The decision below should be reversed.
