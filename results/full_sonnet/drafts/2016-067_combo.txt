# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of Section 1692a(6) Confines "Debt Collector" to Persons Collecting Debts Owed to Another at the Time of Collection

The starting point—and, absent ambiguity, the ending point—of statutory interpretation is the text Congress enacted. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms."). Section 1692a(6) defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as one who "regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." The word "another" modifies the entire phrase that precedes it, so that the statute reaches only persons collecting debts that belong, at the time of collection, to someone else. *See Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920) ("When several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all.").

Petitioners do not dispute this grammar; they instead argue that "another" should be read to mean whoever originally held the debt, regardless of subsequent ownership. But nothing in the text supports substituting "originally owed" for "owed." Where Congress used a single word to describe a present relationship—ownership at the moment of collection—courts may not rewrite it to describe a historical one. As this Court has cautioned, "[t]o supply omissions transcends the judicial function," and a court asked to enlarge a statute's reach beyond what its words provide is not construing the statute but amending it. *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926). Petitioners' "default-at-acquisition" test is precisely such an amendment: it appears nowhere in the operative definitional clause of § 1692a(6) and cannot be conjured from silence.

## II. Petitioners' Default-Status Theory Impermissibly Converts a Narrow Exclusion Into an Independent Rule of Inclusion

Petitioners derive their rule not from the definition of "debt collector" itself, but from an exclusion to the definition of "creditor" in § 1692a(4) and an exclusion to the definition of "debt collector" in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). Both provisions speak only to who is *excluded* from a category once a person otherwise falls within it; neither purports to define who is *included*. This Court has repeatedly refused to allow specific statutory provisions addressing one problem to be stretched to control matters outside their terms, particularly where a general rule and a specific proviso occupy different textual roles. *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Prods. Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (general statutory language will not be extended to displace or redefine matters governed by a specific provision addressed to a distinct question). The (F)(iii) exclusion protects loan servicers handling *current* debt on behalf of others from being swept into the Act; it was never designed, and cannot be read, to independently establish that every purchaser of *defaulted* debt is automatically a debt collector. To hold otherwise would require treating an exception to a definition as though it were the definition—a result the structure of § 1692a(6) forecloses, since the statute directs courts first to determine whether a person satisfies one of the affirmative definitions before ever reaching the exclusions.

## III. Consistency of Statutory Language and Legislative Purpose Confirm That an Owner Collecting Its Own Account Is a Creditor, Not a Debt Collector

Where the same or related language recurs within a single enactment, this Court presumes it carries a consistent meaning. *Atlantic Cleaners & Dyers, Inc. v. United States*, 286 U.S. 427, 433 (1932) ("[T]here is a natural presumption that identical words used in different parts of the same act are intended to have the same meaning."). Throughout the FDCPA, the recurring axis is agency: a debt collector acts on behalf of another; a creditor acts on its own account. Santander, having purchased the receivables outright, occupies the latter position—it collects what it owns, not what it services for someone else.

That reading also serves the statute's evident purpose. Congress explained that it intended the term "debt collector" to "cover all third persons who regularly collect debts for others," not entities collecting their own accounts. Purpose illuminates text; it does not license courts to ignore text, but it confirms the natural reading here. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940) (the function of statutory interpretation is to give effect to the intent of Congress as expressed in the words chosen). Even under the more purpose-driven approach of *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) ("a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit"), petitioners' theory fails, for treating every purchaser of defaulted paper as a "debt collector" would contradict, not fulfill, Congress's design.

Finally, this Court has cautioned against reading regulatory statutes to reach conduct their jurisdictional terms do not plainly cover. *FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349 (1941). Section 1692a(6) does not plainly cover an owner collecting its own purchased debt. The judgment below should be affirmed.
