**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS Cannot Collect a Partnership’s Tax Deficiency from Individual Partners Without First Making Individualized Assessments Against Those Partners.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment be made by “recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. A “taxpayer” is “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). An individual partner and a partnership are distinct taxpayers, each with their own taxpayer identification number. *United States v. Galletti*, 299 F.3d 913, 919 (9th Cir. 2002).

The IRS timely assessed the unpaid employment taxes against the Partnership, but it never assessed the individual partners. The three-year statute of limitations for assessment under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) has long since expired. The assessment against the Partnership extended the collection period to ten years under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a), but only as to the Partnership. *Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 919. The statute of limitations for the individual partners was never extended because no assessment was made against them. *Id.*

The IRS argues that an assessment against the partnership suffices to collect from the partners. This Court has already rejected that argument. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), the IRS assessed unpaid taxes against a partnership and claimed a lien against an alleged partner. The district court held that “such a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property … unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This Court affirmed, albeit on other grounds. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 462-63. The reasoning of the district court remains sound: an assessment against a partnership is not an assessment against its individual partners.

The IRS relies on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), but that case is inapposite. There, a partner voluntarily paid a tax assessed against the partnership and then sought a refund. The Court held only that a partner who pays a tax cannot recover a refund merely because the assessment did not name him. *Id.* at 910. The Court did not hold that the IRS could *compel* payment from an unwilling partner without an individual assessment. *Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 920-21. As the Ninth Circuit explained, *Young* “supports Debtors’ position because their liability for the tax assessed against the Partnership is not at issue in this case.” *Id.* at 921. The partners concede liability; they argue only that the IRS is procedurally barred from collecting without an individual assessment. *Id.*

**II. The IRS Cannot Rely on State Partnership Law to Avoid the Federal Assessment Requirement.**

The IRS argues that California partnership law, which makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allows it to collect directly from the partners. But California law requires a creditor to obtain a judgment against a partner before satisfying a partnership debt from the partner’s assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) (“A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”). The IRS has obtained no judgment against the partners, and the three-year statute of limitations for bringing such an action has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a); *Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 923.

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *United States v. Wright*, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether the statute of limitations was tolled during the partnership’s bankruptcy. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562-63. The Seventh Circuit “had no opportunity to address the question before us.” *Galletti*, 299 F.3d at 916.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS failed to assess the individual partners within the three-year statute of limitations. The assessment against the Partnership does not extend the limitations period for the partners. State law does not provide an alternative path because the IRS never obtained a judgment against the partners. The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
