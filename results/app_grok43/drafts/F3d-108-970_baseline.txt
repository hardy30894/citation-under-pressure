ARGUMENT

The district court erred in entering judgment for Feltmann on her sex-discrimination and retaliation claims. Under the burden-shifting framework applicable to both Title VII and the MHRA, a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case by showing she was qualified for the position and suffered an adverse action under circumstances permitting an inference of discrimination. McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Feltmann failed to make this showing.

Sieben articulated legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for its actions: Feltmann’s gross profit per vehicle was the lowest among all consultants for five consecutive months, she repeatedly permitted unauthorized guest drives, and she violated the demo-car restriction for six months after written warning. These performance deficiencies constitute a legitimate reason for probation and termination. Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981) (employer need only produce evidence that the plaintiff was rejected for a legitimate reason). Feltmann’s sales volume, while occasionally competitive, did not rebut the undisputed evidence of chronically deficient profitability, which Rich attributed to her failure to explain vehicle features.

No evidence established that these reasons were pretextual. A plaintiff must show “both that the reason was false, and that discrimination was the real reason.” St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993). The record contains no comparator evidence that any male consultant maintained similarly low gross profits yet escaped discipline. The single vulgar remark by a non-supervisory employee at another franchise, promptly investigated and remedied, does not support an inference that Sieben’s later performance-based decisions were motivated by sex. See Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986) (isolated incidents insufficient to alter terms of employment).

Feltmann’s retaliation claim likewise fails for want of causation. Protected activity must be the but-for cause of the adverse action. Univ. of Tex. Sw. Med. Ctr. v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338, 360 (2013). The performance deficiencies predated her November 1992 complaint, and the January 1993 work-ethic memo addressed conduct Rich had already observed. Temporal proximity alone cannot establish causation when “the employer’s decision was based on non-retaliatory grounds that were in place before the protected conduct.” Clark Cnty. Sch. Dist. v. Breeden, 532 U.S. 268, 272 (2001) (per curiam). Because Feltmann did not rebut Sieben’s evidence of legitimate reasons, judgment should have been entered for the employer on all counts.
