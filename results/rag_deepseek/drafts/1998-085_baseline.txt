**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE COURT OF APPEALS CORRECTLY HELD THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE A SHOWING OF “MALICE OR RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE” INDEPENDENT OF THE CONDUCT’S EGREGIOUSNESS.**

Section 1981a(b)(1) of Title VII authorizes punitive damages where a plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). By its plain terms, this provision requires inquiry into the defendant’s state of mind, not the degree of outrageousness of its conduct. Nothing in the statute requires that the employer’s conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” as a freestanding prerequisite to punitive damages.

The Court has construed analogous punitive damages standards in other civil rights statutes to focus on the defendant’s mental state. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court held that under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, punitive damages may be awarded when the defendant’s conduct is “motivated by evil motive or intent” or involves “reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” *Id.* at 56. The Court expressly rejected the argument that a higher threshold—such as “actual malicious intent”—was necessary. *Id.* at 48–49. There, as here, the “key feature of punitive damages” is that they “are never awarded as of right, no matter how egregious the defendant’s conduct.” *Id.* at 52. Instead, the jury determines whether the defendant’s state of mind justifies punishment and deterrence.

Congress modeled Section 1981a(b)(1) on the *Smith* standard. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 283 (1994). By incorporating “malice or reckless indifference,” Congress directed courts to assess the employer’s knowledge that it was violating federal law, not whether the specific acts of discrimination were exceptionally severe. A contrary rule—importing an “egregiousness” requirement—would add words Congress did not write and would undermine the remedial purpose of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which Congress enacted to “deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981 note (Congressional Findings).

The legislative history cited by petitioner does not compel a different result. Statements by individual legislators, such as Senator Dole’s remark that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases,” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991), are not the law. *See United States v. Burke*, 504 U.S. 229, 237 (1992) (legislative history views of individual members are not controlling). The Dole memorandum was a last-minute effort to restrict a statute that, on its face, imposes no such limitation. The controlling text—which demands proof of “malice or reckless indifference”—reflects Congress’s intent to adopt the familiar *Smith* standard.

Petitioner’s egregiousness requirement would conflate the standard for liability with the standard for punitive damages in an unprecedented way. Under Title VII, proof of intentional discrimination is sufficient for compensatory relief. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Punitive damages demand more: the plaintiff must show that the employer acted with “malice or reckless indifference”—a higher mental state than the intent required for liability. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 283. This is the “higher hurdle” that Section 1981a erects. That requirement is sufficient to limit punitive damages to cases where the employer knew or should have known it was violating federal law, without demanding that the conduct be independently “outrageous.”

The Court of Appeals correctly recognized that a jury could find that petitioner acted with reckless indifference based on evidence that its executive director preselected a male candidate before the position was posted, failed to review respondent’s qualifications, and gave pretextual explanations for her rejection. Such evidence supports an inference that petitioner knew it was violating Title VII’s prohibition on sex discrimination. Whether the conduct was “egregious” is irrelevant to the statutory inquiry.

**II. THE COURT OF APPEALS PROPERLY REMANDED FOR A JURY DETERMINATION OF PUNITIVE DAMAGES.**

Where the evidence permits a reasonable jury to find that the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference, the punitive damages question must be submitted to the jury. *Curtis v. Loether*, 415 U.S. 189, 196 n.11 (1974) (punitive damages are appropriate for jury determination in civil rights cases). The district court erred by removing the punitive damages claim from the jury based on its own assessment that the evidence was insufficient. The Court of Appeals correctly reversed and remanded for trial on that claim.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
