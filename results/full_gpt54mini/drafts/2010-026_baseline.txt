**Argument**

The Ninth Circuit erred by stretching FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its text and Supreme Court precedent. Exemption 2 reaches only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that FOIA exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and “are to be interpreted narrowly.” *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm’r FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)); *Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976). By holding that Navy documents describing the effects of explosions are exempt because they are “predominantly” internal and might be used to “circumvent[]” agency regulation, the Ninth Circuit converted a limited personnel exemption into a broad safety-and-security exemption Congress did not enact. *Milner v. U.S. Dep’t of Navy*, 562 F.3d 1075, 1081-87 (9th Cir. 2009).

This Court should reject that reading. In *Rose*, the Court held that Exemption 2 covers only materials that are both internal and sufficiently trivial or administrative, and it emphasized that the statute does not reach information merely because disclosure might facilitate evasion of agency policy. 425 U.S. at 362-73. *Rose* recognized a narrow “Low 2” category for mundane workplace matters and left no warrant for a free-floating “High 2” exemption untethered to internal personnel rules and practices. *Id.* at 369-70. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), was not adopted by this Court and cannot be squared with the text Congress chose. See *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

The Navy’s ESQD documents are not “internal personnel rules and practices.” They are technical safety materials used to calculate blast effects, determine storage limits, and map explosion distances at a weapons depot. *Milner*, 562 F.3d at 1078-79. That is not personnel administration. As this Court explained in *Rose*, Exemption 2 concerns the relation of materials to agency “personnel rules and practices,” not simply whether an agency wants to keep the information in-house. 425 U.S. at 369. The Ninth Circuit’s approach collapses that statutory limitation into a mere “predominantly internal” inquiry and effectively rewrites “personnel” out of the statute. *Milner*, 562 F.3d at 1085-87.

Nor does any asserted risk of “circumvention” rescue the Navy. *Rose* did not create a stand-alone circumvention exemption; it construed the phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” in light of the statute’s narrow subject matter. 425 U.S. at 369-70. Other FOIA exemptions expressly address risks of endangerment, law-enforcement interference, and national-security harm. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), (b)(7)(F). Where Congress has specified those protections, courts may not enlarge Exemption 2 to do the same work. *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151; *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. The Navy’s theory that disclosure could aid a malicious actor is precisely the sort of risk Congress addressed elsewhere, not in Exemption 2.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also conflicts with this Court’s insistence that FOIA exemptions be construed against the agency. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. FOIA embodies “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure.” *Id.* at 360-61. The Navy bore the burden to justify withholding. 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B); *U.S. Dep’t of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164, 173 (1991). But the government cannot satisfy that burden by recasting safety planning documents as “internal” simply because they are used by agency personnel. That reasoning would permit withholding of countless technical manuals, engineering diagrams, and hazard analyses whenever disclosure might be inconvenient or strategically undesirable—an outcome incompatible with FOIA’s structure. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

The better rule is the one reflected in the statute and this Court’s cases: Exemption 2 covers only internal personnel rules and practices, not substantive operational or safety information merely because the agency would prefer secrecy. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369-73. Because the requested ESQD materials do not fall within that category, the Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be reversed.
