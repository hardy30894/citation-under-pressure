**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding. The Clause, by its text, prohibits a second jeopardy “for the same offence,” U.S. Const., amend. V, and the Supreme Court has long distinguished guilt adjudication from sentencing. In **Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949)**, the Court upheld sentencing based on information outside the trial record and explained that due process does not “freeze[] the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” Id. at 251. That basic premise remains controlling. See **Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389, 397-99 (1995)**; **United States v. Watts, 519 U.S. 148, 152-53 (1997) (per curiam)**.

This case is not governed by the capital-sentencing double jeopardy cases. In **Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981)**, the Court held that a capital sentencing proceeding resembling a trial could trigger double jeopardy protections when the sentencer’s decision functioned like an “acquittal” of death. Id. at 438-46. The Court repeated that rationale in **Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984)**, emphasizing the special trial-like structure of capital sentencing. Id. at 209-12. But the Court has repeatedly described those holdings as tied to capital sentencing’s “unique circumstances.” **Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania, 537 U.S. 101, 109-10 (2003)**; **Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721, 731-32 (1998)**. Noncapital recidivist proceedings are different. They determine only the appropriate punishment for an offense already established, not guilt or innocence of a separate crime. **Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948)**.

Monge’s own prior-conviction allegation was a sentencing issue, not a new prosecution. In **Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948)**, the Court rejected the claim that using prior convictions to enhance punishment constituted a second punishment for the earlier offense, explaining that “the sentence as a fourfold habitual criminal is not to be viewed as either a new jeopardy or additional penalty for the earlier crimes.” Id. at 732. Likewise, **Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554, 567-69 (1967)** recognized that recidivist statutes are constitutional even though they expose defendants to harsher punishment based on prior convictions. And in **Witte v. United States, 515 U.S. 389 (1995)**, the Court held that consideration of related conduct in sentencing does not constitute punishment for that conduct for double jeopardy purposes. Id. at 398-99.

The Court’s decisions in **North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711 (1969)** and **Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784 (1969)** do not support respondent. Pearce addressed punishment after reconviction for the same offense, not a sentencing enhancement based on a prior conviction allegation. 395 U.S. at 717-19. Benton incorporated the Double Jeopardy Clause against the States but did not suggest that every sentencing factfinding proceeding is a jeopardy-triggering prosecution. 395 U.S. at 794-96. Likewise, **Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1 (1978)** and **Greene v. Massey, 437 U.S. 19 (1978)** bar retrial after reversal for insufficient evidence at trial, but they concern guilt-phase prosecutions, not sentencing enhancements. See **Monge, 524 U.S. at 728-30**.

Nor does the evidentiary insufficiency found below transform this proceeding into an acquittal entitled to double jeopardy protection. The Court has cautioned that the label “acquittal” turns on whether a ruling actually resolves factual elements of a charged offense, not on whether the prosecution failed to prove a sentencing factor. **Smith v. Massachusetts, 543 U.S. 462, 467-68 (2005)**; **Evans v. Michigan, 568 U.S. 313, 318-19 (2013)**. But prior conviction allegations in noncapital sentencing are not “offenses” within the meaning of the Clause. **Monge, 524 U.S. at 728-34**.

Finally, allowing retrial here does not offend the Clause’s core purpose of preventing repeated attempts to convict and punish an accused for the same offense. **United States v. DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. 117, 127-29 (1980)** explains that a defendant has no legitimate expectation of finality in a sentence while authorized review remains available. A retrial on a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital enhancement proceeding is even less problematic: it is a limited sentencing inquiry, not a second prosecution. See **Monge, 524 U.S. at 728-34**; **DiFrancesco, 449 U.S. at 132-39**.

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial of Monge’s prior conviction allegation in this noncapital sentencing proceeding.
