**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals held that § 1113(2) bars suit only if the plaintiff had "actual knowledge both that those investments occurred, and that they were imprudent." That holding is wrong for two independent reasons. First, "actual knowledge of the breach or violation" requires knowledge of the underlying facts, not knowledge of the legal conclusion of imprudence. Second, even under the Ninth Circuit's standard, Sulyma actually accessed documents disclosing the investments and their strategy; his later denial of memory or comprehension cannot create a genuine factual dispute.

**I. The three-year clock begins when the plaintiff knows the facts; knowledge that the facts are legally "imprudent" is not required.**

Section 1113(2) is a statute of repose. *Riddlesbarger v. Hartford Ins. Co.*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 386, 390 (1868). Limitations laws like it rest on the "experience of mankind" that claims lose reliability as time passes. *Bell v. Morrison*, 26 U.S. (1 Pet.) 351, 360 (1828). When Congress repealed the separate constructive-knowledge provision of § 1113 in 1987, it did not thereby convert "actual knowledge" into knowledge of legal wrongdoing. The equitable discovery rule has always suspended a limitations period only where the plaintiff remains ignorant "without any fault or want of diligence or care," *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 21, 25 (1874), and only while he cannot learn the facts through "reasonable diligence," *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 140 (1879). The focus is on the facts a plaintiff knows or can discover, not the legal label attached to those facts.

The Ninth Circuit nonetheless required Sulyma to have known that Intel's conduct was "imprudent." But "imprudence" under § 1104 is the ultimate legal conclusion committed to the trier of fact. ERISA's prudent-investor rule descends from the common law requiring a fiduciary to exercise "a sound discretion," *Harvard College v. Amory*, 26 Mass. (9 Pick.) 446, 461 (1830), and whether discretion was sound is a judgment applied to facts, not a fact of which a beneficiary can have "knowledge." A plaintiff who knows what the fiduciary did has "actual knowledge of the breach"; he need not also predict that a court will call that conduct a breach. Justice Story defined "actual knowledge" as direct and positive knowledge of the thing itself, in contrast to constructive notice. *Sherwood v. Sutton*, 21 F. Cas. 1292, 1295 (C.C.D.N.H. 1828) (No. 12,782). The "thing" here is Intel's investment of Sulyma's retirement funds in alternative assets—facts that Intel disclosed and that Sulyma accessed.

**II. Sulyma had actual knowledge of the facts underlying his claim; his failure to read or recall does not defeat the bar.**

The record shows that Intel disclosed the challenged allocations in Fund Fact Sheets, a 2011 Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, a 2012 Summary Plan Description, and 2012 Annual Disclosures, and that Sulyma "accessed some of this information on the websites." That access is fatal to his claim of ignorance. A plaintiff who reads a disclosure has actual knowledge of its contents; he cannot later disavow comprehension or memory to avoid the statute. And a plaintiff who does not read the documents cannot be heard to complain, because the law charges parties with knowledge of what they had the means to know. *Upton v. Tribilcock*, 91 U.S. 45, 50 (1875). Sulyma's willful failure to read the plan documents, or his convenient inability to recall them, is exactly the "fault or want of diligence" that the discovery rule never excuses. *Bailey*, 88 U.S. at 25.

The Ninth Circuit's approach lets any plaintiff defeat summary judgment with a self-serving declaration that he was "unaware" or cannot "recall" the disclosures. But knowledge is not limited to independent recall. Courts have long allowed knowledge to be inferred from circumstances, and a studied effort to remain ignorant is no defense. *Spurr v. United States*, 174 U.S. 728, 735 (1899). Once a defendant proves that the plaintiff accessed or received documents disclosing the very facts now challenged, the statute runs; testimony of non-awareness does not create a jury question.

**III. The Ninth Circuit's rule defeats the purposes of § 1113(2) and of fiduciary law.**

ERISA fiduciaries act on information available when they act, and their judgments are to be evaluated in light of the circumstances then known. *Loring v. Palmer*, 118 U.S. 321, 336 (1886). If a participant may ignore, forget, or disclaim the disclosures that ERISA requires fiduciaries to make, and then sue a decade later, § 1113(2) means nothing. Fiduciaries will remain permanently exposed to suits over openly disclosed investments that were never challenged—the precise threat that statutes of repose exist to eliminate. *Riddlesbarger*, 74 U.S. at 390; *Bell*, 26 U.S. at 360.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
