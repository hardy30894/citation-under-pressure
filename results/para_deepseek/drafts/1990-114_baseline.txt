**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges Because They Are “Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level.”**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall squarely within this exclusion.

Judging is inherently a policymaking function. As the First Circuit recognized, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). In our common law tradition, judges establish general standards and specific rules of decision that bind future litigants and guide other branches of government. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, has adopted the system of comparative negligence, *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and resolved profound ethical questions regarding the withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment, *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc). These decisions are not mechanical applications of settled law; they are exercises of discretion and judgment that create policy.

Moreover, Missouri judges exercise substantial supervisory and administrative policymaking authority. The Chief Justice of the Missouri Supreme Court and the chief judges of the lower courts possess general supervisory powers. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. The Missouri Supreme Court adopts rules governing judicial conduct and the Missouri Bar, and it resolves disputes within the judicial hierarchy. *See, e.g., In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). These responsibilities confirm that appointed judges operate “on the policymaking level.”

Petitioners argue that the “policymaking level” exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees. But reading the exception so narrowly would leave no ADEA exemption for appointed judges, even though elected judges are expressly exempt. Congress could not have intended such an implausible result. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460-61 (D. Mass. 1988). The state judiciary is a powerful “agency” of state government, and its appointed members are precisely the type of high-level appointees Congress intended to exclude.

**II. Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause requires only that a classification bear a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 312 (1976) (per curiam). Age classifications are not suspect, and mandatory retirement laws are subject only to rational basis review. *Id.* at 313-14.

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for judges at age 70 satisfies this deferential standard. The state has legitimate interests in ensuring that judges possess the physical and mental faculties necessary for demanding judicial work, reducing the risk of delay caused by death or disabling illness of older judges, and avoiding the unpleasantness and expense of selectively removing aged judges. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir.), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), *cert. denied*, 449 U.S. 955 (1980). A mandatory age limit also eliminates the need for case-by-case competency evaluations, which are costly, embarrassing, and potentially inconsistent. *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988).

Petitioners may disagree with the empirical wisdom of these rationales, but that disagreement does not render the classification irrational. Under rational basis review, a state may “draw a line” at age 70, even if that line is imperfect. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 316. The Constitution does not require Missouri to prove that every judge over 70 is incompetent; it need only show that the classification is rationally related to a legitimate purpose. It has done so.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
