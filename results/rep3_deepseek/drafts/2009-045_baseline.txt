**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorney’s fee under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes: in the “rare” and “exceptional” case, such an enhancement is permissible when supported by specific evidence and detailed findings. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 898–901 (1984); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986) (plurality opinion) (“*Delaware Valley I*”).

**I. The Plain Language of Fee-Shifting Statutes Permits Enhancements for Exceptional Performance and Results.**

Fee-shifting statutes, such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, authorize a “reasonable” attorney’s fee. Reasonableness is not a rigid formula; it requires a holistic assessment of the value of the legal services rendered. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 434 (1983). The lodestar—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is the presumptively reasonable fee. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897. But the presumption is rebuttable. *Id.* at 898. In *Blum*, the Court expressly rejected the argument that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” *Id.* at 901. The Court explained that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (quoting *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 435). That language is not dicta; it is a direct holding that enhancements are legally permissible. *See id.* at 900–01.

**II. Supreme Court Precedent Confirms That Enhancements for Quality and Results Are Allowed in Rare Cases.**

*Blum* set the standard: an enhancement may be awarded in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional.” *Id.* at 899. The Court did not categorically bar such enhancements; it merely required a showing of exceptional circumstances. *Id.*

*Delaware Valley I* reaffirmed this principle. The plurality stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” 478 U.S. at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). Although the plurality cautioned that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” it left open the possibility of an extraordinary case. *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). The Court did not overrule *Blum*; it cited *Blum* with approval. *Id.* at 564, 568. Thus, the door remains open for enhancements based on superior performance and exceptional results.

**III. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Supported by Specific Evidence and Detailed Findings.**

The district court did not enhance the fee based on a vague sense of good work. It made detailed findings, relying on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court found that counsel’s “superb quality of representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Id.* at 1290. This is precisely the type of “specific evidence” *Blum* requires. 465 U.S. at 899. A district judge’s firsthand observation of counsel’s performance is a legitimate basis for such a finding. *See Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437 (recognizing the district court’s “superior understanding of the litigation”).

**IV. Every Circuit to Consider the Issue Agrees That Enhancements for Exceptional Performance Are Permissible.**

There is no circuit split. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all hold that a district court may enhance the lodestar for exceptional performance and results in rare cases. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008); *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). This uniform consensus reflects the sound principle that district courts, with their “unparalleled opportunity to observe the attorney’s performance,” are best positioned to identify exceptional advocacy. *Kenny A.*, 532 F.3d at 1246 (Wilson, J., concurring).

**V. The Petitioner’s Arguments Would Undermine the Purpose of Fee-Shifting Statutes.**

Fee-shifting statutes aim to “enable plaintiffs to secure legal assistance” in cases that serve the public interest. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897. A categorical bar on enhancements for exceptional performance would discourage attorneys from undertaking complex, high-stakes litigation where the lodestar may not fully compensate for extraordinary results. The Court has never imposed such a bar, and it should not do so now.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
