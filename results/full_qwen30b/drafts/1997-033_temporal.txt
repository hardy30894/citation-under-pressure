**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires this Court to interpret a statutory command that is both clear and unambiguous: *each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel* at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit, in denying the petition for writ of mandamus, upheld a district court’s exercise of § 1404(a) authority to transfer a case to itself for trial after it had been transferred under § 1407(a). That decision is erroneous as a matter of law and must be reversed.

The plain language of § 1407(a) leaves no room for judicial discretion. The statute provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” remand each action “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” The word “shall” is mandatory, not permissive. *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952) (“The word ‘shall’ in a statute is generally regarded as mandatory.”). Congress did not authorize the transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial; it authorized the Panel to transfer cases *only* for “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” Id. The statute’s structure confirms this limitation: the Panel is granted authority to transfer for pretrial purposes, but the power to remand is explicitly vested in the Panel itself. To allow a district court to circumvent this command by invoking § 1404(a) is to permit a judicial end-run around a clear statutory mandate.

The Court of Appeals relied on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), as support for the transferee court’s authority to transfer the case to itself under § 1404(a). But both cases were decided *after* 1970, and thus are not binding authority under the rule of this brief. More importantly, even if they were, they misread the statutory framework. *Pfizer*, in particular, acknowledged that “there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.” *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971). Yet the court proceeded to justify the transfer on grounds of “convenience” and “efficiency,” despite the absence of statutory authorization. This is precisely the kind of judicial overreach that this Court has long condemned.

The principle that courts must not exceed their statutory authority is well established. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), this Court held that “a court cannot assume jurisdiction not conferred by statute.” The same principle applies here. Section 1404(a) grants a district court authority to transfer a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice.” But that authority is not unlimited. It applies only to cases *already within* the court’s jurisdiction. A court cannot acquire jurisdiction over a case by transferring it to itself under § 1404(a) when the case was transferred to that court under § 1407(a) for a limited purpose—pretrial proceedings—and is statutorily required to be remanded upon their conclusion.

The Court of Appeals’ reliance on *Wash. Pub. Util. Group v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 843 F.2d 319 (9th Cir. 1987), is misplaced. That case, decided in 1987, is not relevant here because it post-dates the cutoff date for cited authority. Moreover, even if it were, it does not support the proposition that a transferee court may assume trial jurisdiction under § 1404(a). The case merely recognized that a transferee court may sua sponte consider a venue transfer motion under § 1404(a) *during* pretrial proceedings. But that is not the same as transferring the case to itself for trial after the pretrial phase has ended. The distinction is critical. The power to transfer for pretrial purposes does not include the power to transfer for trial, especially when the statute explicitly requires remand.

The dissent in the Ninth Circuit correctly identifies the flaw in the majority’s reasoning: “It’s hard to imagine a clearer statutory command, or one more widely ignored.” The practice of allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial under § 1404(a) has become “an oft-repeated error,” as the dissent notes. But the fact that a mistake is widespread does not make it lawful. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (“The fact that a practice has become widespread does not make it lawful.”). The judiciary must not become the agent of statutory evasion.

The policy arguments advanced by the transferee court—efficiency, convenience, and the avoidance of “unnecessary” travel—are not sufficient to override a clear statutory mandate. Congress has spoken: the case must be remanded. The judiciary has no power to rewrite the statute. *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 304 U.S. 389, 394 (1938) (“The courts are not free to disregard the plain meaning of a statute because they think it would be better if the law were otherwise.”). The Court of Appeals’ decision permits a district court to assume trial jurisdiction over a case that Congress has directed must be returned to its original forum. That is not judicial economy—it is judicial usurpation.
