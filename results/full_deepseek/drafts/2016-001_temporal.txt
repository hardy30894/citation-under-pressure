## ARGUMENT

The question presented calls for this Court to determine whether, when a design patent protects only a component of a multicomponent product, the patentee’s damages under 35 U.S.C. § 289 are limited to the portion of the infringer’s profits attributable to that component. The statutory text, the history of the provision, and this Court’s longstanding precedents all compel the answer that the “article of manufacture” to which the design is applied is the infringing component itself, not the entire product into which that component is incorporated.

### I. The Statutory Text and Its Historical Context

Section 289 provides that a design patent infringer “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit” from the “article of manufacture” to which the patented design was applied. The term “article of manufacture” is not defined in the statute, but its meaning must be drawn from the ordinary language of patent law and from this Court’s prior interpretations. A design patent, by its nature, is granted for a design “for an article of manufacture.” 35 U.S.C. § 171. The design is embodied in the article; it is not a claim to the entire product that contains that article. When the design is applied to a component—such as the front face of a smartphone—the “article of manufacture” is that component, not the entire smartphone with its many other unpatented features.

This understanding is consistent with the history of the statute. Congress enacted the predecessor to § 289 in 1887 in direct response to this Court’s decisions in *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), and *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886). In those cases, the Court had held that a design patentee could not recover the infringer’s total profits from the sale of a carpet when the design was applied only to a portion of the carpet. *Dobson*, 114 U.S. at 444–46. The Court required apportionment of profits between the patented design and the unpatented features of the carpet. *Id.* at 445. Congress responded by enacting the 1887 Act to allow the patentee to recover the infringer’s total profits from the “article of manufacture” to which the design was applied, thereby eliminating the need for apportionment. But Congress did not redefine “article of manufacture” to include the entire product when the design was applied only to a component. If the design is applied to a component, the “article of manufacture” remains that component, and the total profits from that component are recoverable. The statute does not authorize recovery of profits from unrelated components of the product.

### II. This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedents Define the “Article of Manufacture” as the Component Embodying the Design

Long before the 1952 codification, this Court consistently recognized that the “article of manufacture” for purposes of design patent damages is the discrete product that embodies the design. In *Dobson*, the Court rejected the patentee’s attempt to recover profits from the entire carpet because the design was applied only to a small border. 114 U.S. at 444–45. The Court explained that the patentee’s right was limited to “the profits made by the defendant from the sale of the carpets containing the patented design,” and that those profits must be “the profits actually made from the use of the patented design.” *Id.* at 445. The Court required the patentee to “show the profits made from the sale of the carpets” and then to “separate the profits made from the use of the patented design from the profits made from the sale of the carpet as a whole.” *Id.* This rationale compels the conclusion that the “article of manufacture” is the component to which the design is applied, not the entire product.

In *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), the Court reaffirmed this principle. The patent covered a design for a portion of a carpet, and the Court held that the patentee could not recover profits from the entire carpet. *Id.* at 16–17. The Court stated that “the patentee is entitled to recover only the profits which the infringer has made from the use of the patented invention,” and that those profits are “the profits made from the sale of the article of manufacture to which the design is applied.” *Id.* at 17. The “article of manufacture” was the component that embodied the design, not the larger product.

Similarly, in *Smith v. Whitney*, 116 U.S. 167 (1886), the Court addressed design patent damages for a design applied to a part of a machine. The Court held that the patentee’s recovery was limited to the profits from the part that embodied the design, because the design was “applied to a part of the machine, and not to the whole machine.” *Id.* at 173–74. The Court reasoned that the design patent protected only the “article of manufacture” that was “the subject of the patent,” and that the infringer’s profits from the entire machine could not be awarded. *Id.* at 174.

These cases establish a clear rule: the “article of manufacture” is the specific product or component that actually incorporates the patented design. When the design is applied to a component of a larger product, the component is the article of manufacture, and the patentee’s recovery is limited to the profits attributable to that component. The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach—allowing the jury to award the infringer’s entire profits from the sale of the complete smartphone—ignores this Court’s repeated holdings.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Cannot Be Reconciled with This Court’s Precedents

The Federal Circuit affirmed the district court’s damage award on the ground that Samsung did not challenge the sufficiency of the evidence. But the underlying legal question—whether the “article of manufacture” is the component or the entire product—is a matter of statutory interpretation that this Court must resolve. The Federal Circuit has never addressed this question in a published opinion, and its implicit assumption that the entire smartphone is the article of manufacture conflicts with *Dobson*, *Dornan*, and *Smith*. The jury was instructed to calculate damages based on Samsung’s total profits from the sale of the entire smartphone, even though the asserted design patents covered only the front face, the bezel, and the graphical user interface. Under this Court’s precedents, the “article of manufacture” for each design patent is the component that embodies that design, not the entire phone. The district court should have limited the jury’s consideration to the profits attributable to the infringing components.

### IV. Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the Federal Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial on damages, with instructions that the jury be directed to determine the “article of manufacture” for each design patent and to award only the profits attributable to that article.
