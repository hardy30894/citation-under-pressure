**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment guarantees the right to free speech and assembly, and when a government creates a limited public forum—by policy or practice—it may not exclude speech based on its religious content. Here, the Center Moriches School District has opened its facilities to non-religious community groups for after-hours use, thereby creating a limited public forum. The District’s blanket exclusion of religious speech from this forum violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech and Equal Protection Clauses. This Court should reverse the Second Circuit’s judgment and hold that the District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show religious-oriented films constitutes unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination.

**I. THE SCHOOL DISTRICT CREATED A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM BY POLICY AND PRACTICE, THEREBY OBLIGATING IT TO TREAT RELIGIOUS SPEECH ON EQUAL TERMS WITH NON-RELIGIOUS SPEECH**

The First Amendment does not permit a government entity to create a limited public forum and then exclude speech solely because of its religious content. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). When a government opens a forum for expressive activity, it must do so in a manner consistent with the First Amendment’s guarantee of viewpoint neutrality. *Regan v. Taxation With Representation of Wash.*, 461 U.S. 540, 547 (1983). In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that a state university could not deny access to student religious groups when it had created a forum open to other student organizations. The Court emphasized that “the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and thus “must be open to all comers on an equal basis.” *Id.* at 267, 102 S.Ct. at 273. Similarly, in *Board of Educ. of Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), the Court upheld the Equal Access Act, which requires schools receiving federal funds to allow student religious groups equal access to school facilities if non-curricular student groups are permitted to meet. *Id.* at 247, 110 S.Ct. at 2370. The Court made clear that “a school district cannot discriminate against a student group on the basis of the religious content of its speech.” *Id.* at 248, 110 S.Ct. at 2371.

Here, the District has repeatedly allowed non-religious community groups to use its facilities after hours. The record shows that the Southern Harmonizers performed at the high school auditorium in a non-religious context, and the Center Moriches Free Public Library sponsored a four-night lecture series on parapsychology and metaphysics—topics described by the speaker as “all science” and “not the purpose” of the lectures. These uses were not incidental; they were deliberate, recurring, and publicly advertised. The District’s own rules acknowledge that such uses are permissible: Rule No. 7, which prohibits “religious purposes,” is not applied to these programs. Thus, the District has created a limited public forum through both policy and practice. Once a forum is opened, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. *See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (“[T]he government may not exclude particular speakers or viewpoints from a forum it has opened.”).

**II. THE DISTRICT’S EXCLUSION OF LAMB’S CHAPEL IS VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION, WHICH VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT**

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a film series on family values—described as “practical insights about the family” and “every parent should see”—is not neutral. The District denied the request solely because the film was “church related,” despite the fact that the content was nonsectarian and focused on parenting, child rearing, and family stability. The District did not object to similar secular programming, including lectures on psychology and metaphysics. This selective exclusion based on the religious nature of the message constitutes impermissible viewpoint discrimination. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273, 102 S.Ct. at 276 (“A State may not impose a burden on the free exercise of religion simply because the religious aspect of a program is deemed offensive or controversial.”).

The District’s argument that no prior religious use occurred is irrelevant. The existence of a limited public forum is determined not by whether religious groups have used the space before, but by whether the government has opened the forum to others. *See Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 247, 110 S.Ct. at 2370. The District’s policy of allowing non-religious groups to use the facilities establishes a forum. The fact that no religious group has previously sought access does not justify excluding one now. To hold otherwise would allow governments to insulate themselves from constitutional scrutiny by refusing to open forums to any religious expression, even when they are open to all others. That is precisely what the First Amendment forbids.

**III. THE SECOND CIRCUIT ERRED IN APPLYING DEEPER LIFE PRECEDENT TO THIS CASE**

The Second Circuit relied on *Deeper Life Fellowship v. City of New York*, 925 F.2d 598 (2d Cir. 1991), to uphold the District’s denial. But *Deeper Life* involved a different factual context: the city had never opened its facilities to any community group, and thus the property remained a nonpublic forum. *Id.* at 602–03. Here, the District has repeatedly allowed non-religious groups to use its facilities. The distinction between a nonpublic forum and a limited public forum is critical. In a nonpublic forum, the government may regulate speech reasonably, but in a limited public forum, it must treat all speech equally. *See Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46, 103 S.Ct. at 955. The Second Circuit’s failure to distinguish *Deeper Life* from this case results in a fundamental misapplication of First Amendment doctrine.
