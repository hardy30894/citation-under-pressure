# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF THE FDCPA MAKES THE DEBT COLLECTOR/CREDITOR DISTINCTION TURN ON WHOSE DEBT IS BEING COLLECTED, NOT WHEN THE DEBT WAS ACQUIRED

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as "any person . . . who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); see Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting this text). The statute's focus is relational: it asks whether the collector is acting on behalf of someone else, not what condition the debt was in when the collector acquired it. Once Santander purchased petitioners' accounts outright, it was no longer collecting "debts owed or due . . . another"—it was collecting debts owed to itself. § 1692a(6).

Petitioners' contrary theory—that any debt "in default" at the time of transfer is automatically subject to FDCPA regulation in the hands of the transferee—finds no footing in the statutory text. Petitioners derive their rule from the definition of "creditor," which excludes "any person to the extent that he receives an assignment or transfer of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another." § 1692a(4) (emphasis added). But that exclusion, by its own terms, applies only to a transferee who takes defaulted debt "for another"—i.e., an agent or servicer, not a purchaser who buys the debt outright for its own account. Santander did not receive petitioners' loans "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection . . . for another"; it bought them, along with $3.55 billion in other receivables, for its own investment portfolio. Because Santander does not fall within the narrow agency-based exclusion of § 1692a(4), that provision says nothing about Santander's status.

Petitioners' reliance on the exclusion found in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii)—for persons "collecting or attempting to collect any debt . . . owed or due another . . . which was not in default at the time it was obtained"—suffers the same defect. An exclusion from the definition of "debt collector" is relevant only after a plaintiff has shown that the defendant otherwise satisfies the operative definition in the main clause of § 1692a(6). See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015) ("[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. [Instead] . . . he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements."). Petitioners never established that Santander was collecting debt "owed or due . . . another" in the first place; they simply assumed the conclusion by jumping to an exclusion that presupposes it. That inverts the statutory analysis.

## II. THE PHRASE "OWED OR DUE ANOTHER" MUST BE ASSESSED AT THE TIME OF THE CHALLENGED COLLECTION CONDUCT

Petitioners argue that because their debts were originally "due" CitiFinancial Auto, Santander's later collection efforts were necessarily collection of debt "due another," even after Santander purchased the accounts. That reading cannot be squared with the statute's purpose or its temporal logic. The FDCPA regulates conduct—specifically, the manner in which a person collects a debt at the time of collection. See § 1692e (prohibiting a "debt collector" from using deceptive means "in connection with the collection of any debt"). The relevant inquiry is therefore whether the debt is owed to another at the time of the collection activity, not whether it once was. See Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318 ("[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection."); Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) ("The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another.'"). Once CitiFinancial Auto sold the receivables to Santander, the debts were no longer "due" CitiFinancial Auto in any meaningful sense—Santander owned them.

## III. CONGRESS DESIGNED THE ACT TO REGULATE THIRD-PARTY COLLECTION AGENTS, NOT CREDITORS COLLECTING THEIR OWN ACCOUNTS

The structure of the Act confirms that Congress meant to reach agents collecting on behalf of others, while leaving creditors who collect their own debts largely outside its reach. The Senate Report accompanying the FDCPA explains that Congress intended the term "debt collector," "subject to the exclusions discussed below, to cover all third persons who regularly collect debts for others." S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977) (emphasis added). Congress likewise exempted loan servicers handling non-defaulted debt "so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing," id. at 3–4, precisely because such servicers act on another's behalf rather than for themselves.

Adopting petitioners' rule would produce an anomalous result inconsistent with this design: any consumer finance company that both services debt for others and purchases and collects debt for its own account—ordinary features of the secondary debt market—would become subject to FDCPA liability for its self-collection activities merely because it also, in some transactions, collects on behalf of others. Nothing in the statute's declared purpose "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors," § 1692(e), supports sweeping in creditors collecting their own accounts simply because the debt happened to be delinquent at the moment of sale.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
