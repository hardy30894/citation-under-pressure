**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Plainly Erred in Admitting FBI Agent Jones’s Testimony That Impermissibly Opined on Mr. Hill’s Credibility and Guilt**

Because Mr. Hill did not object at trial, review is for plain error. *Atkinson v. United States*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (a court may notice errors affecting substantial rights even if not raised below). The admission of Agent Jones’s testimony was error, it was plain, it affected Mr. Hill’s substantial rights, and it seriously impaired the fairness of the proceedings.

**A. Agent Jones’s Testimony Invaded the Province of the Jury**

No witness, lay or expert, may express an opinion on the credibility of another witness or the truthfulness of a defendant’s statements. *United States v. Smith*, 142 F.2d 785, 787 (10th Cir. 1944) (error to admit opinion that defendant’s story was “inconsistent” and “unworthy of belief”). The rule is settled: “The jury alone must determine the weight and credibility of the evidence.” *United States v. Collins*, 269 F.2d 171, 172 (9th Cir. 1959). Agent Jones went far beyond permissible expert testimony. He stated that many of Mr. Hill’s answers were “not worthy of credence” and “did not make sense.” He further testified that Mr. Hill displayed behaviors “common among the criminal element.” Such testimony is precisely the sort of “opinion as to the veracity of the accused” that is inadmissible. *United States v. Sauls*, 65 F.2d 886, 887 (5th Cir. 1933).

**B. Agent Jones Directly Opined That Mr. Hill’s Statements Were Deceptive and That He Was Guilty**

The agent’s statement that he had “never [seen] an innocent person” express a desire to die rather than face charges is a direct opinion on Mr. Hill’s innocence—a fact “for the jury to determine from all the evidence.” *Lindsey v. United States*, 133 F.2d 368, 370 (5th Cir. 1943). Similarly, Jones’s testimony that invoking God to support truthfulness is “deceptive” constitutes an expert pronouncement that Mr. Hill was lying. Such evidence “invade[s] the province of the jury” because it tells the jury whom to believe. *United States v. Bussey*, 139 F.2d 138, 139 (9th Cir. 1943). A jury’s function is to evaluate witness credibility; an expert cannot usurp that role. *Hickory v. United States*, 151 U.S. 303, 306 (1894) (opinion evidence “usurps the functions of the jury”).

**C. The Error Was Plain and Affected Mr. Hill’s Substantial Rights**

The rule against credibility opinions has been “consistently recognized” for decades. *Crawford v. United States*, 212 U.S. 183, 199 (1909) (improper to admit witness’s opinion on another’s veracity). Here, the government’s case was circumstantial. Mr. Hill was convicted only after a second trial, following a deadlocked first jury. The agent’s testimony—that Mr. Hill’s answers were unworthy of belief, that his behaviors were “criminal,” and that his invocations of God were deceptive—was the “functional equivalent of an expert’s testimony that the defendant is guilty.” *United States v. Butler*, 144 F.2d 769, 771 (3d Cir. 1944). Without it, the jury may well have reached a different verdict. The error therefore “affected the outcome of the trial.” *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160.

Moreover, Mr. Hill’s failure to object does not preclude relief because the error was “obvious and serious.” *United States v. Johnson*, 316 U.S. 623, 626 (1942) (plain error doctrine applies when an error “seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”). The district court had a duty to exclude such testimony sua sponte. *See Bussey*, 139 F.2d at 139. Its failure to do so constitutes reversible plain error.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse Mr. Hill’s conviction and remand for a new trial.
