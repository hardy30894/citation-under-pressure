**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT FUNDS EXEMPTION APPLIES TO INHERITED IRAS BECAUSE THEY ARE "RETIREMENT FUNDS" EXEMPT FROM TAXATION UNDER THE INTERNAL REVENUE CODE.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempts from a debtor’s estate “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not exempt because it is not “retirement funds” in the hands of the beneficiary. *See Clark v. Rameker* (In re Clark), 714 F.3d 559, 563-64 (7th Cir. 2013). The plain text of the statute requires only that the funds be held in an account that is tax-exempt under the Internal Revenue Code—it does not condition the exemption on the funds being earmarked for the debtor’s retirement. *See id.* at 563 (noting that § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) “are identical” and “exempt from creditors’ claims any ‘retirement funds’ to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation” under specified I.R.C. sections).

The Internal Revenue Code explicitly classifies inherited IRAs as tax-exempt retirement accounts. Section 408(d)(3)(C) of the I.R.C. provides that an inherited IRA retains its tax-exempt status, stating that “[a]ny amount distributed from an inherited individual retirement account shall be included in gross income.” I.R.C. § 408(d)(3)(C) (emphasis added). This provision confirms that inherited IRAs are tax-exempt accounts under § 408, which is one of the enumerated sections in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). *See also* I.R.C. § 408(e)(1) (providing that IRAs are not subject to tax on their earnings). Thus, inherited IRAs fall squarely within the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption for retirement funds held in tax-exempt accounts.

The Seventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that inherited IRAs are not “retirement funds” because they are not held for the beneficiary’s retirement—misreads the statute. The Bankruptcy Code does not define “retirement funds,” but it does not require that the funds be *currently* set aside for retirement. Instead, it exempts funds held in accounts that are tax-exempt under the I.R.C., regardless of their present use. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit’s focus on the beneficiary’s inability to hold the funds until retirement conflates the *purpose* of the exemption with its *mechanism*. The exemption is tied to the account’s tax status, not the debtor’s intended use of the funds. *See Chilton v. Moser* (In re Chilton), 674 F.3d 486, 491 (5th Cir. 2012) (“The exemption turns on the status of the funds as ‘retirement funds’ in the hands of the debtor, not on whether the funds are currently being used for retirement.”).

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance that exemptions should be construed broadly in favor of debtors. *See Owen v. Owen*, 500 U.S. 305, 308 (1991) (“Exemptions are to be construed liberally in favor of the debtor.”). The Seventh Circuit’s restrictive reading undermines this principle by imposing an unwritten requirement that the funds be held for the debtor’s retirement, despite the statute’s silence on the matter.

II. **THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH THE TEXT AND PURPOSE OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE’S RETIREMENT FUNDS EXEMPTION.**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision rests on the assumption that “retirement funds” must be funds *currently* set aside for retirement. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. However, this interpretation ignores the statute’s plain language and the structure of the Bankruptcy Code. The exemption applies to funds held in accounts that are tax-exempt under the I.R.C., not to funds that are *currently* being used for retirement. *See* 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit’s reliance on the fact that inherited IRAs must be distributed within a short period is misplaced, as the statute does not condition the exemption on the duration of the account’s tax-deferred status. *See id.*

Moreover, the Seventh Circuit’s decision creates an arbitrary distinction between inherited IRAs and other tax-exempt retirement accounts. For example, a surviving spouse who rolls over an inherited IRA into their own IRA retains the account’s tax-exempt status and can continue to hold the funds for their retirement. *See* I.R.C. § 408(d)(3)(C)(ii). Under the Seventh Circuit’s logic, this account would be exempt, but the same funds would lose their exempt status if inherited by a non-spouse. This distinction has no basis in the Bankruptcy Code and serves only to penalize beneficiaries who are not the surviving spouses of the original account holder.

The Seventh Circuit also erred in analogizing inherited IRAs to cash withdrawn from an IRA. *See Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. Unlike cash withdrawn from an IRA, inherited IRAs remain tax-exempt and subject to the distribution requirements of the I.R.C. *See* I.R.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). The funds in an inherited IRA are not freely available for current consumption; they are constrained by the I.R.C.’s distribution rules, which ensure that the funds are eventually taxed. *See id.* § 401(a)(9)(B). Thus, inherited IRAs are not equivalent to cash in a bank account and should not be treated as such for purposes of the Bankruptcy Code’s exemption.

III. **THE MAJORITY OF CIRCUITS TO ADDRESS THIS ISSUE HAVE HELD THAT INHERITED IRAS ARE EXEMPT UNDER § 522(b)(3)(C) AND (d)(12).**

The Seventh Circuit’s decision conflicts with the Fifth and Eighth Circuits, which have held that inherited IRAs are exempt under the Bankruptcy Code. *See Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 491; *Nessa v. Trans Union Corp.* (In re Nessa), 426 B.R. 312, 317 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 2010). These courts correctly recognized that the exemption turns on the account’s tax-exempt status, not the beneficiary’s ability to hold the funds until retirement. *See Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 491; *Nessa*, 426 B.R. at 317. The Seventh Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split and should be reversed to ensure uniformity in the interpretation of the Bankruptcy Code’s retirement funds exemption.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be reversed. The Bankruptcy Code’s retirement funds exemption applies to inherited IRAs because they are held in tax-exempt accounts under the Internal Revenue Code. The Seventh Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statute and creates an arbitrary distinction that is unsupported by the text or purpose of the exemption.
