**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an uncounseled tribal court conviction may be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—is governed by the settled precedent of the Supreme Court, which permits the use of uncounseled misdemeanor convictions in recidivist statutes so long as the conviction is valid and reliable. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to bar such use, based on a strained and erroneous reading of the Sixth Amendment, conflicts with *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), and undermines Congress’s clear intent in enacting § 117(a) to combat the epidemic of domestic violence in Indian country. This Court should reverse.

### I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRONEOUSLY EXTENDED THE SIXTH AMENDMENT TO TRIBAL COURT CONVICTIONS THAT ARE VALID UNDER THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT

The Ninth Circuit held that uncounseled tribal court convictions cannot serve as predicate offenses under § 117(a) because they “violate the Sixth Amendment” if they result in imprisonment. *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014). This conclusion is wrong. The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal court proceedings. *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323–24 (1978) (“The sovereign power to punish tribal offenders is the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty.”); *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008) (“It is beyond dispute that the Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes.”). Thus, tribal court proceedings are not subject to the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel.

Instead, tribal court proceedings are governed by the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a), which provides that “no person shall be deprived of liberty ... without due process of law.” The ICRA does not require a right to appointed counsel in all cases, but it does mandate that tribal courts afford “procedural safeguards” when they exercise expanded jurisdiction, including the prosecution of felonies and domestic violence offenses. *Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010*, Pub. L. No. 111-211, § 201, 124 Stat. 2172. Congress has thus recognized that tribal courts may prosecute serious crimes, including domestic violence, and has provided a framework for due process that is tailored to tribal sovereignty and self-governance.

The Ninth Circuit’s insistence that tribal court convictions must afford a right to counsel “coextensive” with the Sixth Amendment is not only unsupported by precedent but also an impermissible intrusion into tribal self-governance. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (“The federal courts must not intrude needlessly on tribal self-government.”). By imposing a federal constitutional standard that does not apply, the court has engaged in the very “judicial paternalism” that the Supreme Court has repeatedly condemned. *Wilson v. Marchington*, 127 F.3d 805, 811 (9th Cir. 1997) (“Federal courts must also be careful to respect tribal jurisprudence along with the special customs and practical limitations of tribal court systems.”).

### II. *NICHOLS* PERMITS THE USE OF UNCOUNSELED CONVICTIONS IN RECIDIVIST STATUTES, INCLUDING § 117(A)

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. *Ant* predates *Nichols* and was decided under a different legal framework. *Nichols* resolved the question now before this Court: “A prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction may be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, so long as the conviction is valid and reliable.” 511 U.S. at 746–47. The Court emphasized that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to misdemeanor convictions that do not carry a sentence of imprisonment. *Id.* at 741, 746. In *Nichols*, the defendant was convicted of a misdemeanor DUI, which did not result in a prison sentence, and the Court held that the absence of counsel did not invalidate the conviction for sentencing purposes.

The Court in *Nichols* explicitly distinguished between the guilt phase and the sentencing phase. At sentencing, the government need only prove a prior conviction by a preponderance of the evidence. *Id.* at 748. In contrast, at trial, the government must prove each element of an offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively elevates the reliability standard for prior convictions used in a recidivist statute to the level required at trial—despite the fact that *Nichols* made clear that such a standard does not apply to sentencing enhancements.

Section 117(a) is a recidivist statute. It imposes a felony penalty on a person who has been convicted of two prior domestic violence offenses. The punishment is for the *current* offense, not for the prior convictions. *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747; *Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. at 386 (“100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction. None is for the prior convictions.”). Thus, the use of prior convictions under § 117(a) is functionally identical to the sentencing enhancement at issue in *Nichols*. The Court’s holding in *Nichols* applies with equal force here.

### III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CREATES A CIRCUIT SPLIT AND UNDERMINES CONGRESS’S EFFORT TO COMBAT DOMESTIC VIOLENCE IN INDIAN COUNTRY

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a direct conflict with the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions may be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a).
