**ARGUMENT**

**Once a defendant produces evidence that he withdrew from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period began, the burden shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member during that period.**

**A. Conspiracy is a continuing offense, and withdrawal terminates a defendant's criminal exposure for the conspiracy's subsequent life.**

This Court has long recognized that conspiracy is not a single completed act but an ongoing "partnership in criminal purposes" that persists only so long as its members continue to pursue its objects. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910). Because the offense is continuing in nature, a defendant's membership in it is not a fixed historical fact but a status that can begin, persist, and end. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), confirms that withdrawal is legally consequential: a defendant who withdraws ceases to be criminally responsible for the conspiracy's later course. It follows that when a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, he is not raising a freestanding affirmative defense; he is disputing an element of the very offense charged against him for that period—namely, that he was a member of the conspiracy when the law says he must have been.

This Court's decision in *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946), applied that principle directly to the limitations problem now before this Court. There, the Court held that a defendant's past association with a conspiracy's unlawful aims does not, standing alone, establish that he remained a participant during the period Congress has deemed prosecutable; the government must show continuing adherence to the conspiracy's purposes within that period. *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440 (1949), reinforces the point from the opposite direction, holding that a conspiracy terminates once its central objectives are accomplished or abandoned, so that the acts and statements of former co-conspirators after that point cannot be imputed to a defendant no longer part of the venture. Read together, *Kissel*, *Hyde*, *Fiswick*, and *Krulewitch* establish that continued membership within the limitations period is part of what the government must prove, not something the defendant must disprove.

The courts of appeals confronting this precise question before the decision below reached the same conclusion. In *United States v. Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376 (2d Cir. 1964), Judge Friendly held that once a defendant comes forward with evidence of withdrawal, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's membership continued into the limitations period. The rule the D.C. Circuit applied below—requiring the defendant himself to prove withdrawal—cannot be squared with that sound and long-settled approach, nor with the constitutional principles discussed below.

**B. Requiring the defendant to bear the burden of disproving continued membership violates due process.**

The Constitution places the burden of proving every element of a crime on the government, and that burden does not shift merely because the defendant is the party who first introduces evidence bearing on an element. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court held that once a defendant introduces evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt as to an element of the offense—there, sanity—the government must overcome that doubt beyond a reasonable doubt; the burden of persuasion remains where the Constitution places it. This Court reaffirmed that framework in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), distinguishing between evidence that negates an element of the offense, governed by *Davis*, and truly independent affirmative defenses, as to which a State may constitutionally place the burden on the defendant. Continued conspiratorial membership within the limitations period is not an independent defense of the kind at issue in *Leland*; it is inseparable from the existence of the offense itself as to that defendant and that period.

*Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), teaches the same lesson from a different angle. This Court refused to allow a presumption to substitute for proof of a defendant's continuing culpable state of mind, explaining that "guilt is personal," id. at 250, and that the government may not use shortcuts to relieve itself of its evidentiary burden on an element of the crime. Requiring a defendant to prove his own withdrawal by a preponderance of the evidence is precisely such a shortcut: it allows the jury to convict on an inference of continued membership that the government itself has not proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

The constitutional stakes of misallocating this burden are substantial, not abstract. In *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958), this Court cautioned that the assignment of a burden of proof must account for "the risk of error" it imposes, id. at 525, and held that courts must be reluctant to increase that risk for the party whose fundamental liberty is at stake. Few risks are graver than requiring a criminal defendant to disprove, by a lesser standard than the government's own burden, that he remained a conspirator during years for which the prosecution's own charging decision already reflects doubt about its ability to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

**C. Conclusion**

*Kissel*, *Hyde*, *Fiswick*, and *Krulewitch* establish that withdrawal terminates the continuing offense of conspiracy as to the withdrawing defendant. *Davis*, *Leland*, *Morissette*, and *Speiser* establish that once evidence of withdrawal is in the case, due process places the burden of persuasion on the government to prove membership within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. The decision below, which inverted that burden and required petitioners to disprove their own continued participation, should be reversed.
