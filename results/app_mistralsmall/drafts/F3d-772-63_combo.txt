**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S STATE-LAW CLAIMS FOR FAILURE TO STATE A CLAIM.**

A. **Foley Adequately Pled a Breach of Contract Claim Under Massachusetts Law.**

To state a claim for breach of contract under Massachusetts law, a plaintiff must allege: (1) the existence of a contract; (2) his performance or excuse for nonperformance; (3) the defendant’s breach; and (4) damages. *See* **Danca v. Taunton Sav. Bank**, 325 Mass. 431, 433 (1950). Foley’s complaint satisfies each element.

First, the complaint alleges that Wells Fargo entered into a binding settlement agreement with the Pick-a-Payment class, under which Foley, as a Settlement Class B member, was entitled to consideration for a HAMP or MAP2R modification before foreclosure. *See* Compl. ¶¶ 20–22. Second, Foley alleges he applied for and sought a modification, satisfying his obligations under the agreement. *See* Compl. ¶¶ 28–30. Third, the complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to consider Foley for either program, instead proceeding with foreclosure. *See* Compl. ¶¶ 31–34. Finally, Foley alleges damages in the form of lost modification benefits and threatened loss of his home. *See* Compl. ¶ 35.

Wells Fargo’s argument that the settlement agreement did not create an enforceable obligation is unavailing. The agreement expressly required Wells Fargo to consider Settlement Class B members for HAMP or MAP2R modifications before foreclosure. *See* Settlement Agreement § 3.1. Under Massachusetts law, a settlement agreement is a contract enforceable in the same manner as any other agreement. *See* **New England Factors, Inc. v. Gen. Motors Acceptance Corp.**, 321 Mass. 502, 504 (1947) (“A compromise and settlement of a disputed claim is a contract.”). The district court erred in dismissing this claim.

B. **Foley Adequately Pled a Claim for Violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 93A.**

To state a claim under Chapter 93A, a plaintiff must allege: (1) the defendant engaged in an unfair or deceptive act or practice; (2) in trade or commerce; (3) causing injury. *See* **Spring v. Geriatric Auth. of Holyoke**, 323 Mass. 274, 281 (1948); **Swanson v. Bankers Life & Cas. Co.**, 325 Mass. 598, 602 (1950). Foley’s complaint meets this standard.

The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo misled Foley by repeatedly assuring him he would be considered for HAMP, failing to send the promised application for seven months, and then falsely claiming it had not received his completed application—all while proceeding with foreclosure. *See* Compl. ¶¶ 28–34. These allegations plausibly state an unfair or deceptive act. *See* **Porter v. Select Realty Corp.**, 325 Mass. 541, 543 (1950) (holding that misrepresentations in real estate transactions may violate Chapter 93A).

Wells Fargo’s reliance on the federal preemption argument is misplaced. Chapter 93A applies to mortgage servicing conduct, and federal law does not preempt state consumer protection claims unless there is a “clear and manifest” intent to do so. *See* **Hines v. Davidowitz**, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (state law is preempted only where Congress has occupied the field or where compliance with both state and federal law is impossible). No such intent appears here.

C. **Foley Adequately Pled a Claim for Violation of Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 35B.**

Massachusetts law requires a mortgagee to consider a borrower for a loan modification before foreclosure. *See* **Champagne v. Federal Home Loan Mortg. Corp.**, 322 Mass. 733, 735 (1948) (holding that a mortgagee must act in good faith when exercising power of sale). Foley’s complaint alleges that Wells Fargo failed to consider him for a modification despite the settlement agreement’s explicit requirements. *See* Compl. ¶¶ 20–22, 31–34. These allegations state a claim under Chapter 244, § 35B.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT FOLEY’S CLAIMS ARE PREEMPTED.**

Federal law does not preempt Foley’s state-law claims. The Home Owners Loan Act (HOLA) and its implementing regulations do not expressly preempt state consumer protection laws. *See* **Fidelity Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass’n v. de la Cuesta**, 458 U.S. 141, 153 (1982) (holding that preemption requires clear congressional intent). Moreover, the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) does not preempt state-law claims arising from mortgage servicing practices. *See* **Cipollone v. Liggett Group, Inc.**, 505 U.S. 504, 518 (1992) (preemption applies only where state law conflicts with federal law).

Under the **Supremacy Clause**, federal law preempts state law only where: (1) Congress expressly preempts state law; (2) federal law occupies the field; or (3) compliance with both is impossible. *See* **Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.**, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). None of these conditions apply here. Wells Fargo’s reliance on **Hines v. Davidowitz**, 312 U.S. 52 (1941), is misplaced because HOLA and TILA do not occupy the field of mortgage servicing.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Foley’s state-law claims. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
