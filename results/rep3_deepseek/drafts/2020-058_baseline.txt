**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Grant of Temporary Protected Status Constitutes an “Admission” Under 8 U.S.C. § 1255 Because Section 1254a(f)(4) Places TPS Recipients in the Same Legal Position as Nonimmigrants Who Were Lawfully Admitted.**

The text of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This provision does not merely confer a status; it equates TPS recipients with nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted. The Immigration and Nationality Act defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When Congress directed that TPS recipients be treated as nonimmigrants, it necessarily incorporated the procedural and legal consequences that accompany nonimmigrant status—including the requirement that such status be obtained through inspection and admission.

The Third Circuit erred by drawing an artificial distinction between “status” and “admission.” In *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that “[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.” This conclusion follows from the statutory scheme: nonimmigrant status under the INA is not granted in a vacuum but is predicated upon a lawful admission. See 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) (defining classes of nonimmigrant aliens as those “having a residence in a foreign country which he has no intention of abandoning” and who “is coming to the United States” for a specified purpose). The very concept of nonimmigrant status presupposes an admission.

**II. The Text and Structure of Section 1254a(f)(4) Confirm That TPS Recipients Are Deemed Admitted for Purposes of Adjustment of Status.**

Section 1254a(f)(4) states that TPS recipients “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” The phrase “considered as” is a deeming provision—a legal fiction that requires courts to treat TPS recipients as if they were nonimmigrants in all relevant respects. See *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 553 (6th Cir. 2013) (holding that § 1254a(f)(4) “places TPS recipients in the same position as nonimmigrants who have been lawfully admitted”). Because nonimmigrant status is inherently tied to admission, the deeming provision necessarily extends to the admission requirement.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on the distinction between “admission” and “status” in *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced. *Hanif* addressed whether a change in status retroactively altered the date of admission for purposes of § 1255(b), not whether a grant of TPS could satisfy § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement. The question here is not whether TPS retroactively changes the date of entry, but whether the legal fiction created by § 1254a(f)(4) deems TPS recipients to have been admitted. The Sixth and Ninth Circuits correctly answered that question in the affirmative. *Flores*, 718 F.3d at 553; *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 960.

**III. The Third Circuit’s Interpretation Renders Section 1254a(f)(4) Superfluous and Undermines Congress’s Intent.**

If, as the Third Circuit held, § 1254a(f)(4) does not satisfy the admission requirement of § 1255(a), then the provision serves no meaningful purpose for TPS recipients who entered without inspection. Such recipients would remain permanently ineligible for adjustment of status, rendering § 1254a(f)(4) a dead letter for a significant class of beneficiaries. Congress could not have intended such a result. See *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.”).

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s interpretation conflicts with the humanitarian purpose of TPS. Congress enacted TPS to protect nationals of countries experiencing ongoing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Forcing TPS recipients who entered without inspection to depart the United States and reenter lawfully before seeking adjustment of status would undermine this protective purpose, particularly when their home countries remain unsafe. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964.

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Reliance on Section 1254a(h) Is Misplaced.**

The Third Circuit suggested that reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a)’s admission requirement would circumvent § 1254a(h), which requires a supermajority vote in the Senate to adjust the status of TPS recipients through special legislation. But § 1254a(h) addresses Congress’s ability to enact *ad hoc* legislation adjusting the status of entire classes of TPS recipients. It does not limit the Secretary of Homeland Security’s authority to adjudicate individual adjustment-of-status applications under existing statutory criteria. The two provisions operate in different spheres and can coexist without conflict.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
