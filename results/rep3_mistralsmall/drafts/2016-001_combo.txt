**ARGUMENT**

I. The Patent Act’s Text and History Limit Damages to the Infringing Component

The Patent Act provides that a patent owner is entitled to “damages adequate to compensate for the infringement.” 35 U.S.C. § 284 (1952). This language does not authorize an award of the infringer’s entire profits when only a component of the accused product practices the patented design. The Supreme Court has long held that damages must be “reasonably related” to the infringement. *Root v. Railway Co.*, 105 U.S. 189, 210 (1882). In * Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480 (1853), the Court explained that damages must be “proportional to the injury suffered,” id. at 488, and cannot extend to unpatented or non-infringing aspects of the product. *See also* *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. 620, 627 (1871) (damages must be “limited to the actual injury sustained”).

This principle is embedded in the Patent Act’s structure. Section 284’s reference to “damages” in the plural contemplates apportionment where only a component is infringing. *See* *Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co.*, 235 U.S. 641, 647 (1915) (damages must be “measured by the pecuniary injury” caused by the infringement). The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to recover profits from unpatented or non-infringing parts of a product. *See* *Munson v. Mayor*, 85 U.S. 442, 446 (1873) (damages cannot include “profits derived from other and distinct parts” of a machine).

II. Design Patent Damages Must Be Limited to the Infringing Article of Manufacture

The Patent Act’s design patent provision, 35 U.S.C. § 289 (1952), authorizes an award of “the total profit” made from the infringing article. However, this provision does not permit recovery for profits attributable to non-infringing components. The Supreme Court has long interpreted similar statutory language to require apportionment. In * Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. at 488, the Court held that damages must be “limited to the article or part of the machine which is the subject of the patent.” *See also* *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. at 627 (damages must be confined to the “infringing part”).

This rule applies with equal force to design patents. In * Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Court held that damages for infringement of a patented carpet design could not include profits from the sale of the entire carpet when only the design was patented. The Court explained that damages must be “limited to the value of the invention” and cannot extend to “the entire machine or article.” Id. at 444. *See also* *Munson v. Mayor*, 85 U.S. at 446 (damages must be confined to the “infringing part”).

III. The Entire Market Value Rule Does Not Apply When the Patented Design Is Only a Component

The “entire market value rule” permits recovery of the infringer’s entire profits only when the patented invention is the “basis for customer demand” for the entire product. *See* *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884). This rule does not apply when the patented design is only one component of a multicomponent product. In * Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. at 488, the Court held that the rule does not apply when the patented improvement is “merely a part of a larger machine.” *See also* *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. at 627 (the rule does not apply when the patented invention is “only a part of the machine”).

The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case erroneously applied the entire market value rule to a multicomponent product. The rule requires a showing that the patented design drives demand for the entire product, not merely that it is a valuable component. *See* *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. at 121 (the rule applies only when the patented invention is the “primary basis for customer demand”). Here, the patented designs were only a small part of Samsung’s smartphones, and there was no evidence that they drove customer demand for the entire product. *See* *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. at 444 (the rule does not apply when the patented design is only a component).

IV. The District Court Erred in Failing to Instruct the Jury on Apportionment

The district court’s failure to instruct the jury on apportionment was reversible error. The Supreme Court has held that a jury must be instructed on the proper measure of damages. *See* *Root v. Railway Co.*, 105 U.S. at 210 (the jury must be instructed on the “proper measure of damages”). In * Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. at 488, the Court held that the jury must be instructed to limit damages to the infringing part. *See also* *Mowry v. Whitney*, 81 U.S. at 627 (the jury must be instructed to confine damages to the infringing part).

Here, the district court’s instruction allowed the jury to award damages based on Samsung’s entire profits, without requiring apportionment. This error was prejudicial because the jury could have awarded damages for non-infringing components. *See* *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. at 444 (prejudicial error to allow damages for non-infringing parts). The district court’s failure to instruct on apportionment requires reversal. *See* *Root v. Railway Co.*, 105 U.S. at 210 (failure to instruct on the proper measure of damages is reversible error).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Federal Circuit and remand the case for a new trial on damages, with instructions to limit damages to the profits attributable to the infringing components of Samsung’s smartphones.
