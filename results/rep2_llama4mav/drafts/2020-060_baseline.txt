The United States respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the district court's denial of its motion to dismiss Guam's CERCLA claims. The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for potentially responsible parties (PRPs) to seek recoupment of cleanup costs: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. 42 U.S.C. §§ 9607, 9613(f). The statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, while the statute of limitations for a remedial section 107 action is six years. Id. § 113(g)(2)(B), (g)(3).

The Court of Appeals correctly held that Guam's 2004 consent decree with the EPA triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), precluding it from now pursuing a claim under section 107. See Government of Guam v. United States, No. 19-5131, slip op. at 16-20 (D.C. Cir. Feb. 14, 2020). As the court explained, the consent decree's substantive provisions, including Guam's agreement to close the Ordot Dump and design a cover system, "resolved [Guam's] liability to the United States for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action." Id. at 18 (quoting CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B)).

Guam's arguments to the contrary are unavailing. First, Guam contends that the consent decree did not resolve its liability because it did not admit fault. However, as the Court of Appeals observed, "parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability." Asarco LLC v. Atl. Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017). The mere fact that Guam refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt the consent decree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B). Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (8th Cir. 2019).

Furthermore, Guam's assertion that the consent decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it concerned non-CERCLA pollutant discharges is incorrect. The EPA's complaint demanded that Guam "complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," which is a "remedial action" under CERCLA. CWA Compl. ¶ 29; CERCLA § 101(24).

Finally, Guam's due process argument is forfeited because it was not raised in the district court. Keepseagle v. Vilsack, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016). In any event, Guam lost the ability to bring a contribution claim not because it was deprived of due process, but because the statute of limitations ran.

The Court should reverse the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss the complaint. The plain language of CERCLA and the terms of the consent decree make clear that Guam's claim is time-barred under section 113(f)(3)(B). United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998); Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004).
