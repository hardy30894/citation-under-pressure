# ARGUMENT

## I. Having Opened Its Facilities to Community Groups for Expressive Use, the District May Not Exclude Speech Because of Its Religious Content.

The First Amendment's protection against content-based exclusion does not depend on whether the government property at issue is a street, a park, or a school auditorium; it depends on whether the government has opened that property to expressive activity by outside speakers. Streets and parks receive the highest degree of protection because they "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). But the same principle of open access applies with equal force wherever government has voluntarily thrown its property open to community expression—as the Center Moriches School District did here by regularly admitting civic clubs, cultural performers, and lecturers on subjects ranging from parapsychology to metaphysics. Having created such a forum, the District could not then draw a line excluding only religious speech.

That Lamb's Chapel's message took the form of a film does not weaken its claim to protection. This Court has long recognized that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas" entitled to full First Amendment protection. *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). The District cannot escape its obligations by treating a film on family values differently from a live lecture on the identical subject merely because the film's perspective happened to be religious. Indeed, "a function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute," *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), and it is precisely the District's discomfort with the religious perspective of Lamb's Chapel's message that led to its exclusion.

## II. Excluding Religious Speech While Tolerating Comparable Secular Speech Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly struck down government action denying access to public facilities because a speaker's message was religious, while similar secular activity was permitted. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court invalidated the denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses where other groups had used the same park for their own meetings and the denial rested on official disapproval of the applicants' religious views rather than any neutral standard. The Court reached the identical result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), condemning the discriminatory application of a park ordinance against a religious meeting while similar secular gatherings proceeded undisturbed. And in *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), the Court held that officials could not exercise licensing discretion to exclude a speaker because they disapproved of the religious content of his message.

The record here reveals the same constitutional defect. The District permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform and allowed a library-sponsored lecture series touching on "spiritual" and "metaphysical" topics, yet categorically barred anything "church related"—the very words its own clerk used to justify refusing Lamb's Chapel's application not once, but three times. That is viewpoint discrimination of the sort *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Kunz* forbid.

## III. The Establishment Clause Neither Requires Nor Permits the District's Discrimination Against Religious Speech.

Any suggestion that the Establishment Clause compelled the District to exclude Lamb's Chapel finds no support in this Court's precedent. The Establishment Clause requires government neutrality toward religion—it does not license hostility toward it. In *Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), the Court cautioned that the First Amendment "does not say that in every and all respects there shall be a separation of Church and State," and that government need not "be hostile to religion" to remain properly separate from it. Similarly, in *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947), the Court explained that the Establishment Clause requires the State to be neutral, not adversarial, toward religious belief and expression. Permitting Lamb's Chapel to use the auditorium on the same terms as any other community group would not constitute an establishment of religion; it would simply extend the neutral access already given to secular speakers. See also *School District of Abington Township v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963) (the Establishment Clause forbids only that government "advance" or "inhibit" religion, not that it treat religious speech identically to all other speech in a forum already open to comparable expression).

## IV. Public School Property Is Not an Enclave Exempt from First Amendment Scrutiny.

Finally, the fact that the property at issue belongs to a public school does not diminish the constitutional analysis. This Court has made clear that "State-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism," and that persons associated with such institutions "do not shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 506, 511 (1969). The collective, associational character of Lamb's Chapel's proposed use—a congregation seeking to share its views on family life with the broader community—is itself protected activity. See *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 430 (1963) (recognizing that advocacy of views through group activity enjoys full First Amendment protection).

## CONCLUSION

The District's exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Hague*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Kunz*, *Burstyn*, *Terminiello*, *Zorach*, *Everson*, *Schempp*, *Tinker*, or *Button*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
