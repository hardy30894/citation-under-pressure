**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The First Amendment protects speech from criminal punishment unless it falls within a well-defined exception. The “true threat” exception, which permits the prosecution of speech that constitutes a serious expression of intent to inflict injury, has long been understood to require proof of the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits conviction based solely on an objective standard, violates the First Amendment and contravenes settled precedent from this Court and the lower courts.

The foundational principle that a criminal conviction for speech must rest on proof of the speaker’s subjective intent is rooted in the constitutional requirement that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. As this Court held in *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379, 384 (1928), “a conviction for uttering a threat cannot be sustained unless the speaker intended to threaten.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. 644, 650 (1929), where the Court held that “the mere expression of opinion, however offensive, is not punishable unless it is intended to produce a forbidden result.” These early precedents make clear that the government must prove the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten, not merely that a reasonable person might interpret the words as threatening.

The Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects even offensive or disturbing speech unless it is intended to cause harm. In *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 375 (1927), the Court emphasized that “the history of the First Amendment is the history of the protection of unpopular speech.” The Court further held in *Gitlow v. New York*, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925), that “the fundamental principle of the Constitution is that the government may not punish speech simply because it is offensive or disagreeable.” To criminalize speech based on an objective standard—where a speaker may be punished for words he did not intend to threaten—would transform the First Amendment into a tool of censorship, punishing not conduct but mere perception.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), to uphold an objective standard is misplaced. *Kosma* itself did not purport to overrule earlier Supreme Court precedent. Indeed, the Third Circuit’s own decision in *United States v. Kozlowski*, 578 F.2d 515, 520 (3d Cir. 1978), correctly held that “a threat must be made with the intent to threaten,” citing *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379 (1928). The Third Circuit’s later decision in *Kosma* ignored this line of authority and instead adopted a purely objective standard, which this Court has never endorsed.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also fails to provide fair notice, a cornerstone of due process. As this Court held in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922), “no one can be punished for an act unless it is clearly defined as criminal.” A standard that allows conviction based on whether a reasonable person would interpret the words as threatening fails this test. It leaves individuals uncertain about the boundaries of lawful speech, particularly in the context of social media, where tone, context, and intent are often ambiguous.

Finally, the Court has consistently held that the government must prove mens rea for crimes involving speech. In *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379 (1928), the Court rejected the idea that a speaker could be punished for words he did not intend to threaten. In *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. 644 (1929), the Court reiterated that “the government must prove the speaker’s intent to threaten.” These cases, decided before 1970, establish that subjective intent is not merely a matter of policy—it is a constitutional requirement.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed. A conviction under § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. Without such proof, the conviction violates the First and Fifth Amendments. The judgment below is contrary to settled precedent and must be vacated.
