**ARGUMENT**

### I. The Bureau of Prisons exceeded its statutory authority by disqualifying Roussos from early release based on a sentencing enhancement for firearm possession.

Congress authorized a discretionary sentence reduction for prisoners convicted of a “nonviolent offense” who successfully complete substance abuse treatment. 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The statute says nothing about excluding inmates whose convictions were enhanced under the Sentencing Guidelines for weapon possession. Where Congress has used a term but not defined it, an agency may interpret only within the bounds of the statute and may not adopt a rule that defeats the statute’s evident purpose. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984); *MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 512 U.S. 218, 231 (1994).

The BOP’s own regulation ties eligibility to whether the inmate’s “current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). Section 924(c)(3) looks to the offense of conviction: a felony that either has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, or by its nature involves a substantial risk that force may be used in committing the offense. 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). A conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846 is not a crime of violence under that definition. This Court has recognized that drug trafficking offenses are not converted into crimes of violence merely because they may be accompanied by dangerous circumstances or by evidence of weapons. *United States v. Doe*, 960 F.2d 221, 225-26 (1st Cir. 1992) (a drug offense does not become a crime of violence by virtue of surrounding facts); *United States v. Dyer*, 325 F.3d 464, 467-68 (3d Cir. 2003) (categorical analysis looks to the offense, not the particular conduct, where the statutory text requires focus on the offense of conviction).

The BOP instead relied on Program Statement 5162.02, which classifies a § 846 conviction as a crime of violence if the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for firearm possession. That approach is inconsistent with the regulation and with the statute. An agency “may not rewrite” the statutory term it is charged to administer. *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 446-47 (1987). Nor may it create a rule that transforms a nonviolent narcotics conspiracy into a violent offense merely because the sentencing judge applied a guideline enhancement. The Supreme Court has cautioned that sentencing enhancements are not elements of the offense itself. *McMillan v. Pennsylvania*, 477 U.S. 79, 85-86 (1986); *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 156 (1997) (sentencing courts may consider conduct not resulting in conviction, but that does not alter the nature of the offense of conviction).

The firearm finding here was made only for guideline purposes. Roussos was convicted of a drug conspiracy; the guns were not a factor in his arrest or conviction. The sentencing enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 did not convert that offense into a violent crime. Because the BOP’s eligibility decision rests on an impermissible conflation of sentencing factors with offense elements, the decision conflicts with 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) and 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. See *Lopez v. Davis*, 531 U.S. 230, 244 (2001) (recognizing BOP discretion under § 3621(e)(2)(B), but only within the bounds of a valid exercise of delegated authority).

### II. Even if the BOP possesses some discretion under § 3621(e)(2)(B), Program Statement 5162.02 is arbitrary and contrary to the BOP’s regulation as applied to Roussos.

Interpretive program statements do not have the force of law and cannot override a duly promulgated regulation. *Christensen v. Harris County*, 529 U.S. 576, 587 (2000); *Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 212-13 (1988). Here, the regulation asks whether the “current offense is determined to be a crime of violence as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).” 28 C.F.R. § 550.58. Program Statement 5162.02 adds a separate rule: if a drug conviction received a firearm enhancement, the offense becomes violent. That extra condition is not found in the statute or regulation.

That mismatch matters. Under the statutory and regulatory text, the relevant inquiry is whether the conviction itself is for a violent offense. A § 846 conspiracy is not. The BOP may not bootstrap a guideline enhancement into a statutory disqualification. See *Reno v. Koray*, 515 U.S. 50, 61 (1995) (agency interpretation may guide administration only when consistent with the governing text). Program Statement 5162.02 therefore imposes a categorical exclusion where Congress authorized only a discretionary reduction for those convicted of nonviolent offenses.

The Court should reject the BOP’s effort to recast Roussos’s drug conspiracy as a violent offense and reverse the denial of habeas relief. At minimum, the case should be remanded with instructions to apply § 3621(e)(2)(B) and 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 according to their terms, without the added disqualifier created by Program Statement 5162.02.
