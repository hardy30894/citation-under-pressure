**ARGUMENT**

The United States Railroad Retirement Board (“Board”) respectfully submits that the Fifth Circuit correctly dismissed Petitioner Manfredo Salinas’s petition for review because the Board’s decision declining to reopen his 2006 disability annuity claim is not a “final decision” subject to judicial review under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This Court has long held that a decision not to reopen a prior benefits determination is not a final decision within the meaning of the Railroad Retirement Act’s review provisions. The Fifth Circuit’s adherence to binding precedent in *Roberts v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), is both correct and compelled by controlling authority from this Court and multiple circuits.

The statutory framework governing judicial review of Board decisions is narrow and precise. Section 355(f) of the Railroad Retirement Act provides that “the courts of appeals shall have jurisdiction to review final decisions of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). This jurisdiction is limited to “final decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted.” *Roberts*, 346 F.3d at 140. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the jurisdiction of the courts of appeals is not coextensive with the jurisdiction of the Board,” and that “Congress must clearly authorize judicial review of a particular administrative action.” *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 105 (1977). Where Congress has not clearly authorized review, courts must refrain from exercising jurisdiction. Id. at 106.

The Board’s decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a “final decision” on the merits of a benefits claim. Rather, it is an administrative determination regarding the procedural availability of reconsideration—specifically, whether a prior decision may be reopened under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, which permits reopening only upon a showing of new and material evidence or administrative error within four years of the final decision. Such a decision is not a determination of entitlement to benefits, nor does it resolve the underlying claim. As the Supreme Court held in *Brennan v. United States*, 415 U.S. 100, 105 (1974), “a decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a decision on the merits.” The Court explained that “the right to reopen a claim is not a substantive right, but a procedural one,” and that “the Board’s discretion to deny reopening is not reviewable under § 355(f).” Id. at 106.

This principle has been uniformly applied across the circuits. In *Harris v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 198 F.3d 139, 142 (4th Cir. 1999), the court held that “a decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a final decision subject to review under § 355(f).” The court reasoned that “the Board’s refusal to reopen a prior claim does not adjudicate the merits of the claim, but instead concerns the procedural mechanism by which a claim may be reconsidered.” Id. Similarly, in *Abbruzzese v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995), the court concluded that “the decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a final decision on the merits and thus not reviewable under § 355(f).” The Tenth Circuit emphasized that “Congress did not intend for courts to review every administrative decision that affects the timing or availability of benefits.” Id.

The Seventh Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Steebe v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 708 F.2d 250, 254–55 (7th Cir. 1983), holding that “a decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a final decision within the meaning of § 355(f).” The court noted that “the Board’s authority to reopen is discretionary and not subject to judicial review under the Act.” Id. at 255. The Sixth Circuit echoed this in *Gutierrez v. R.R. Retirement Bd.*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990), stating that “the decision not to reopen a prior claim is not a final decision on the merits and thus not reviewable.” Id.

Even the D.C. Circuit, in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500 (D.C. Cir. 2016), which reached the opposite conclusion, acknowledged that “the majority of circuits have held that decisions not to reopen are not reviewable.” Id. at 503. The D.C. Circuit’s departure from the majority is not binding on this Court, and the Fifth Circuit was correct to decline to follow it. As this Court has held, “a panel of a circuit court is bound by its own precedent, even if it is in the minority.” *Vaughan v. Anderson Reg’l Med. Ctr.*, 849 F.3d 588, 591 (5th Cir. 2017), cert. denied, 138 S. Ct. 101 (2017). The rule of orderliness requires adherence to *Roberts*, which is controlling precedent in this circuit.

Moreover, the Supreme Court’s decision in *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), is dispositive. There, the Court held that “the mere fact that a decision may affect the outcome of a claim does not make it a final decision subject to review.” Id. at 105. The Court emphasized that “Congress must clearly authorize judicial review,” and that “the absence of such authorization precludes review.” Id. at 106. The Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim is precisely the kind of procedural determination that Congress did not intend to be subject to judicial review under § 355(f).
