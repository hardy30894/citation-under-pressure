The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's case to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), effectively overruled the D.C. Circuit's precedent in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and dictates that judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction lies in district court.

In Kloeckner, the Supreme Court held that when the MSPB dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court. 568 U.S. at 56. The Court's reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals. The statute defines a mixed case as one in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" MSPB and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" certain federal laws. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The Kloeckner Court interpreted this language to mean that a mixed case is one that is "appealable to the MSPB" and involves a discrimination claim. 568 U.S. at 604.

The D.C. Circuit's attempt to distinguish between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is unpersuasive. The statutory language does not support a distinction between the two. In both cases, the MSPB's dismissal means that the employee's appeal was not ultimately "appealable to the MSPB." See Kloeckner, 568 U.S. at 604. The Federal Circuit's decision in Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111 (Fed. Cir. 2013), cert. denied, 134 S. Ct. 1495 (2014), which relied on a similar distinction, was wrongly decided.

Furthermore, the D.C. Circuit's reliance on the Justices' questions during the Kloeckner oral argument is misplaced. While the Justices did discuss the potential distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals, their questions do not provide a reliable indication of the Court's ultimate holding. See Transcript of Oral Argument at 21-24, Kloeckner, 568 U.S. 41 (No. 11-184).

Perry's arguments, which the D.C. Circuit declined to consider, are persuasive. The statute's deadlines and the need for certainty about the proper forum for judicial review suggest that the forum should not vary based on the MSPB's ultimate decision about its jurisdiction. See 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(2), (e)(1)(B), (e)(2). Moreover, jurisdictional dismissals grounded in the voluntariness of the challenged employment action can overlap with the merits of a discrimination claim, making it sensible for judicial review to reside in district court.

The D.C. Circuit should have reconsidered its precedent in Powell in light of Kloeckner. The Supreme Court's decision effectively overruled Powell, and Perry's case should be reviewed in district court. This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit's decision and hold that judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction lies in district court.
