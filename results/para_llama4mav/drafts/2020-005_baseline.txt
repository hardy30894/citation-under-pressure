The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that a state court may exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant like Ford Motor Company when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of doing business in the state, even if the specific claims at issue did not arise directly from those contacts. 

Specific jurisdiction is proper when the defendant's contacts with the forum state are continuous and systematic, and the plaintiff's claims "arise out of or relate to" those contacts. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County, 582 U.S. 255, 262-63 (2017). The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's continuous and systematic contacts with a state can subject it to specific jurisdiction, even if the specific claims at issue are not directly related to those contacts. International Shoe Co. v. State of Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 317 (1945).

Here, Ford Motor Company has extensive and continuous contacts with Montana, including advertising and selling parts within the state. By doing so, Ford has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana, thus subjecting itself to specific jurisdiction. The fact that the specific vehicle involved in the accident was not sold in Montana is not dispositive, as Ford's overall contacts with the state are sufficient to establish jurisdiction. See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770, 779-80 (1984) (holding that a defendant's regular and continuous contacts with a state can subject it to jurisdiction, even if the specific claim at issue is not directly related to those contacts).

Furthermore, the Due Process Clause does not require a "strict causal relationship" between the defendant's in-state activities and the plaintiff's claims. Bristol-Myers Squibb, 582 U.S. at 262-63. Instead, the court must consider whether there is a sufficient "connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue." Id. In this case, the Montana Supreme Court correctly found that such a connection exists, as Ford's activities in Montana are related to the claims at issue, even if the specific vehicle involved in the accident was not sold in the state.

The exercise of specific jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company in Montana is consistent with the principles of due process and fair play. Ford has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of doing business in Montana and has therefore submitted to the jurisdiction of the state's courts. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
