The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable for title purposes at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889. The equal footing doctrine, established by the Supreme Court in cases such as Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), and Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1 (1894), provides that upon admission to the Union, a state acquires title to the beds of navigable waters within its borders. The test for determining navigability for title purposes is whether a river was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931). 

The Montana Supreme Court applied this test correctly, relying on historical evidence to determine the navigability of the relevant stretches of the rivers. The court properly considered evidence such as newspaper articles, diaries, and other historical documents, which are admissible under various exceptions to the hearsay rule, including the public records and reports exception, M. R. Evid. 803(8), and the ancient documents exception, M. R. Evid. 901(8). See Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc., 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79-81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that historical evidence is relevant and probative in determining navigability for title purposes. In United States v. Utah, the Court considered evidence of commercial use, such as the transportation of goods and people, to determine whether a river was navigable. 283 U.S. at 77-82. Similarly, in The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557 (1870), the Court held that a river is navigable if it is "navigable in fact," meaning that it is "used, or is susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce." Id. at 563.

The Montana Supreme Court's decision is consistent with these precedents. The court considered a range of historical evidence, including accounts of commercial use, to determine that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. See Montana v. PPL Montana, LLC, 2010 MT 64, ¶¶ 32-45, 355 Mont. 452, 229 P.3d 421. This evidence included reports of fur traders and explorers, who used the rivers for transportation and commerce. Id. ¶¶ 36-37.

PPL's argument that the Montana Supreme Court erred in relying on hearsay evidence is unpersuasive. As noted above, the court properly considered historical documents under various exceptions to the hearsay rule. Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that historical evidence may be based on hearsay, and that such evidence is nonetheless relevant and probative in determining navigability for title purposes. See United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 23-24 (1935).

The Montana Supreme Court's decision is also consistent with the principle that the equal footing doctrine is intended to ensure that states have title to the beds of navigable waters within their borders. See Oregon ex rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co., 429 U.S. 363, 374 (1977). By holding that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood, the court ensured that Montana has title to the beds of these rivers, as required by the equal footing doctrine.

In sum, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the test for determining navigability for title purposes, relying on historical evidence to determine that the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. The court's decision is consistent with Supreme Court precedents, including United States v. Utah, The Daniel Ball, and Oregon ex rel. State Land Board v. Corvallis Sand & Gravel Co. The decision should be affirmed.
