**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee award based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. Long before the modern lodestar framework, this Court consistently recognized that a court awarding attorney’s fees in equity or under a fee-shifting statute may consider the quality of representation and the results achieved. Those principles remain sound and support the district court’s enhancement here.

**I. This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedent Establishes That Courts May Consider Quality and Results in Setting a Reasonable Fee.**

From the earliest common-fund cases, this Court held that the value of an attorney’s services—including the benefit conferred and the skill displayed—is a proper factor in determining a fee. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 536 (1881), the Court approved a fee award that reflected “the great value of the services” and the “most beneficial” result. Similarly, in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 124 (1885), the Court emphasized that the fee should be “commensurate with the value of the services” and that the “importance of the case” and “the results obtained” are relevant considerations.

In *Harrison v. Perea*, 168 U.S. 311, 325 (1897), the Court stated that a court fixing fees should consider “the nature and importance of the litigation” and “the benefit derived.” *United States v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 283 U.S. 738, 744 (1931), reaffirmed that the district court may consider “the character and extent of the services” and “the results obtained.” *In re Gilbert*, 276 U.S. 6, 9 (1928), held that in bankruptcy fee awards, the court may consider “the value of the services,” which includes “the importance of the case” and “the results.” *Dickinson v. Stiles*, 246 U.S. 631, 633 (1918), in a patent case, approved consideration of “the nature of the case” and “the results” in fixing fees.

*Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939), confirmed that a court awarding fees from a common fund has “discretion to consider the benefit conferred” and the “quality of the representation.” And in *Vaughan v. Atkinson*, 369 U.S. 527, 531 (1962), the Court allowed a fee enhancement in an admiralty case, noting that the court may consider the “willful and persistent” conduct of the opposing party and the “benefit” to the plaintiff—factors that inherently reflect the quality of counsel’s work and the result.

These eight cases demonstrate that, for over a century, this Court has recognized that a reasonable fee may be enhanced when the quality of representation and the results obtained are exceptional. The principle is not new; it is deeply rooted in the equitable discretion that courts have always exercised.

**II. The Fee-Shifting Statute Incorporates This Historical Discretion.**

The Civil Rights Attorney’s Fees Awards Act of 1976, 42 U.S.C. § 1988, codified the common-law rule that a prevailing plaintiff should recover a reasonable fee. This Court in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968), interpreting an analogous fee-shifting statute, held that a fee award should be “adequate to attract competent counsel.” That standard necessarily permits a court to consider whether the lodestar amount—calculated using market rates—fully compensates counsel for exceptional performance that produced extraordinary results. If the lodestar already reflects the market rate for standard work, then a case where counsel’s performance far exceeds that standard may require an enhancement to make the fee truly “adequate.”

The district court here found that counsel’s “superb quality of representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates.” That finding, supported by detailed observations of counsel’s work over years of litigation, falls squarely within the discretion recognized in *Sprague*, *Greenough*, and *Vaughan*. The court did not abuse its discretion.

**III. The Enhancement Is Consistent with the “Rare and Exceptional” Standard.**

The pre-1970 cases do not require enhancement in every case; they merely permit it when supported by specific evidence. *Blum v. Stenson* (1984) and *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council* (1986) later refined the standard, but they did not overrule the historical discretion. The district court’s enhancement here was based on specific evidence: the court’s own observation of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “exceptional” result—a comprehensive injunction reforming Georgia’s foster-care system. That is precisely the type of “rare” case where enhancement is appropriate.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
