**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-entered the United States Before Its Effective Date.**

The plain text of § 241(a)(5) applies only to “an alien who has reentered the United States illegally.” The provision does not specify that it reaches re-entries occurring before its enactment. Statutes are presumed to operate prospectively absent a clear statement of retroactive intent. *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hosp.*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988). Here, no such clear statement exists. The statute uses the present tense “has reentered,” which ordinarily refers to conduct after the statute’s effective date. *See, e.g., Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 272 (1994) (noting that “the ‘time of the offense’ is generally the time of the conduct, not the time of the prior order”).

Congress knew how to make a statute retroactive when it wished. For example, other provisions of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 expressly apply to aliens “regardless of when” the prior deportation order was entered. See IIRIRA § 309(c). The absence of such language in § 241(a)(5) confirms that Congress intended only prospective application to new re-entries. *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) (where Congress includes language in one provision but omits it in another, courts presume a different intent).

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that the statute applies to any re-entry, regardless of timing. That reading renders the statute’s effective date meaningless and ignores the settled principle that “retroactivity is not favored in the law.” *Bowen*, 488 U.S. at 208.

**II. Even If the Statute Applies, Its Application to Petitioner Is Impermissibly Retroactive.**

The constitutional retroactivity analysis asks whether the statute “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. If so, it is retroactive unless Congress clearly intended such effect. *Id.* at 280. Here, application of § 241(a)(5) to petitioner would attach new legal consequences to his 1982 re-entry—a completed past event.

Before 1997, an alien who re-entered illegally after a prior deportation could seek discretionary relief such as adjustment of status. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (pre-1996 version). That relief was available to petitioner when he filed his application in 2001, based on his marriage to a U.S. citizen. The reinstatement statute eliminates that relief entirely for aliens with prior deportation orders. *See* § 241(a)(5) (providing that “the prior order of deportation is reinstated from its original date and is not subject to reopening or review,” and that “the alien is not eligible and may not apply for any relief under this Act”).

This Court has recognized that the elimination of discretionary relief previously available to aliens constitutes an impermissible retroactive effect when applied to past conduct. In *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 324 (2001), the Court held that § 212(c) relief could not be retroactively withdrawn for aliens who had entered the United States before the statutory change, because they “almost certainly relied upon that likelihood [of relief] in deciding to” accept a plea agreement or remain in the country. Similarly, petitioner here entered and remained in the United States with the expectation that he could one day adjust his status if he married a U.S. citizen. That expectation was reasonable and “settled” at the time he re-entered in 1982. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 284.

The Tenth Circuit reasoned that petitioner had no “protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status.” That analysis is flawed. Under *St. Cyr*, the relevant question is whether the statute “attaches new legal consequences” to past conduct, not whether the alien had a *vested* right to relief. *Id.* at 269-70. Petitioner’s 1982 re-entry was the act that triggers the harsh consequences of reinstatement—deportation without any opportunity for relief. Applying the statute to that completed act “impair[s] rights a party possessed when he acted, increase[s] a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose[s] new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

Moreover, this Court has consistently held that statutes eliminating discretionary relief for past conduct are retroactive. *See, e.g., Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States*, 520 U.S. 939, 950 (1997) (finding retroactive effect where statute imposed new monetary liability for prior conduct that was not previously subject to such liability). Section 241(a)(5) does precisely that: it imposes deportation without relief for conduct—the 1982 re-entry—that at the time carried no such automatic consequence for petitioner.

Because Congress did not clearly express an intent to apply § 241(a)(5) to pre-enactment re-entries, the statute must be given prospective effect only. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded for consideration of petitioner’s adjustment application on its merits.
