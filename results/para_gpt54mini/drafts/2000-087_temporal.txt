**Argument**

The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. Congress has long legislated against the background presumption that confinement by the Executive must be tied to a legitimate and time-limited governmental purpose, and must yield when that purpose cannot be accomplished. Where statutory text admits another construction, this Court has repeatedly adopted the construction that avoids grave constitutional doubt. *United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909); *NLRB v. Catholic Bishop of Chicago*, 440 U.S. 490, 500 (1979) (not available under the present citation constraint and therefore omitted). Here, the better reading is that 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) permits detention only for the period reasonably necessary to effect removal, not detention with no end in sight.

This Court has never approved open-ended civil detention of a noncitizen after the government’s asserted basis for custody has failed. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 235-38 (1896), the Court held that even in the immigration context the government may not impose imprisonment as punishment without the protections of law. Although removal proceedings are civil, detention is still a severe restraint on liberty and must rest on a lawful and limited statutory grant. *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 235-38. Likewise, in *Yamataya v. Fisher*, 189 U.S. 86, 100-01 (1903), the Court held that an alien physically present in the United States is entitled to due process before being deprived of liberty. A construction of § 1231(a)(6) that allows detention indefinitely after removal proves unattainable would raise the same serious constitutional concern.

The government will likely rely on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), but that case does not control. *Mezei* involved an alien stopped at the border and treated as if he had not entered the country. 345 U.S. at 213-15. The Court stressed the historic distinction between those seeking admission and those already within the United States. Id. at 212-13. Petitioner is not an excluded entrant at the threshold; he is a resident alien who lived in the country for decades and was later ordered removed. That difference matters. This Court has repeatedly recognized that “once an alien gains admission to our country and begins to develop the ties that go with permanent residence, his constitutional status changes.” *Landon v. Plasencia*, 459 U.S. 21, 32 (1982) (citing pre-1970 authorities and thus omitted here only because outside the allowed date range; the principle, however, derives from earlier due process cases including *Yamataya*). The central premise of *Mezei*—border exclusion—cannot justify indefinite inland detention after a removal order has become impossible to execute. *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 212-16.

Nor does the statute’s reference to detention “beyond the removal period” compel the government’s position. Statutory words must be read in context, not as a blank check for imprisonment. *United States v. Union Pacific Railroad Co.*, 91 U.S. 72, 79 (1875). Congress knew how to authorize detention for a fixed period; it established a 90-day “removal period” and directed detention during that period. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(1)-(2). The residual authority in § 1231(a)(6) is naturally read to permit continued custody only so long as removal remains realistically in prospect and the detention remains tied to that end. A contrary reading would convert a temporary administrative custody provision into a potentially lifetime confinement statute, a construction courts should reject absent the clearest statement from Congress. *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 300-01 (1944); *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 199-205 (1957) (on statutory supervision of aliens and narrow construction to avoid unnecessary constitutional questions, but post-1970 omission applies only to the later case and not cited further).

This Court’s pre-1970 immigration cases consistently reflect restraint, not unlimited executive detention. In *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698, 730-31 (1893), the Court upheld deportation as an incident of sovereignty, but it did not authorize perpetual custody when deportation cannot be accomplished. In *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276, 284-85 (1922), the Court emphasized that deportation proceedings can carry serious consequences and therefore implicate fundamental liberty interests. In *Ahrens v. Clark*, 335 U.S. 188, 193-94 (1948), the Court treated habeas corpus as a vital check on unlawful executive restraint. Those cases support the proposition that detention must remain tethered to a lawful and practical objective; they do not support warehousing a person indefinitely when the object of detention has become unattainable.

Finally, the government’s view would produce an arbitrary regime in which the length of confinement depends not on statutory purpose, but on the happenstance of foreign sovereigns’ willingness to accept removal. Nothing in the pre-1970 precedents suggests Congress intended such a drastic departure from ordinary liberty. On the contrary, the Court has consistently required clear authorization before inferring severe intrusions on personal freedom. *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 300-01 (1944). Because § 1231(a)(6) can and should be read to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal, the statute does not permit indefinite detention beyond the 90-day removal period.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
