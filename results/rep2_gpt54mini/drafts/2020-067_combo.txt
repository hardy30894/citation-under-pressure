**ARGUMENT**

Under the plain-error doctrine, an appellate court may consider only the record made in the trial court. This Court has long held that “the settled rule is that matters not appearing in the record cannot be considered on appeal.” *United States v. Page*, 302 U.S. 447, 452 (1938). Plain-error review does not create an exception to that fundamental limitation. *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658-59 (1896), recognized only a narrow power to notice error apparent on the record, and *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936), likewise permitted review of “obvious errors” that are apparent from the record and that seriously affect the fairness of judicial proceedings. Nothing in those decisions authorizes appellate courts to supplement the trial record with extra-record materials to decide whether the error was prejudicial.

The same limitation follows from the structure of Rule 52(b). Plain error is an appellate doctrine for correcting forfeited errors “in exceptional circumstances.” *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189, 200 (1943). But the inquiry remains tethered to the trial record because appellate courts review what happened below, not what might be learned later. *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67-69 (1914), held that a conviction could not be set aside on matters outside the record, emphasizing the finality of judgments and the narrow grounds on which they may be disturbed. And *Gagnon v. United States*, 193 U.S. 451, 468 (1904), explained that review is limited to “the case as presented by the record.”

That principle is especially important when a defendant asks an appellate court to determine whether an unpreserved error affected substantial rights or the integrity of the proceeding. Those questions are ordinarily answered from the evidence and proceedings actually before the jury. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), held that harmless-error review asks whether the error had “substantial and injurious effect or influence” on the verdict, a judgment made from the trial record. Likewise, *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 82-83 (1935), considered prejudice from prosecutorial misconduct based on the proceedings below, not on extra-record evidence. Plain-error review is no different in kind. It asks whether the error affected the outcome or seriously impaired the fairness of the proceeding; it does not authorize a new evidentiary inquiry on appeal. *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160; *Johnson*, 318 U.S. at 200.

Greer’s contrary approach would convert appellate review into factfinding. That is not the role of an appellate court. *United States v. Landress*, 291 U.S. 557, 565 (1934), refused to go beyond the record to resolve a factual question on appeal. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942), likewise reviewed prejudice based on the trial record, not on post hoc information. And *DiCarlo v. United States*, 6 F.2d 364, 366 (2d Cir. 1925), explained that an appellate court “cannot consider evidence not in the record.” If a reviewing court could look outside the trial record to deny or grant plain-error relief, the jury’s verdict would be replaced by appellate speculation and later-developed facts.

This limitation is particularly compelling here. Greer’s Rehaif claim was unpreserved. Under long-settled precedent, the burden rests on the appellant to show that an error was prejudicial. *Smith v. United States*, 216 U.S. 158, 173-74 (1910), places the burden on the party seeking reversal to demonstrate harmful error. But that burden cannot be satisfied by materials never presented to the factfinder. If extra-record evidence could be used to assess prejudice, the government could defeat relief with new information, and defendants could seek reversal based on new factual assertions as well. That would erode the adversarial process and conflict with the principle that appellate review is confined to “the record.” *Page*, 302 U.S. at 452; *Mayer*, 235 U.S. at 67-69.

The better rule is the one grounded in these decisions: on plain-error review, a federal appellate court may consider only the trial record in deciding whether the error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. That approach respects the limited office of appellate courts, preserves the finality of judgments, and avoids converting plain-error review into a second trial. *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160; *Johnson*, 318 U.S. at 200; *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764-65.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
