**ARGUMENT**

The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction because this suit is, at bottom, a Virginia property dispute over the scope of a private flowage easement. Federal-question jurisdiction exists only when a federal question is presented on the face of the well-pleaded complaint; it is not created by a defense, by anticipated federal issues, or by the mere fact that a federally regulated entity is a party. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908); *Taylor v. Anderson*, 234 U.S. 74 (1914); *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257 (1916). The Pressls’ complaint seeks a declaration of their rights under an easement granted by private landowners in 1960. That claim arises under Virginia property law, not federal law. See *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936) (a federal issue must be “an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff’s cause of action”).

APCO removed this case under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p, but neither statute supplies jurisdiction. Section 1331 requires a civil action “arising under” federal law. As *Gully* and *Mottley* make clear, a case does not arise under federal law merely because federal regulations may later affect the parties’ conduct. 299 U.S. at 113; 211 U.S. at 152. The Pressls do not ask the court to interpret the Federal Power Act, to review a FERC order, or to determine APCO’s federal licensing obligations. They ask only whether APCO’s asserted demands exceed the rights granted by the easement. That issue is governed by the language of the deed and Virginia law. See *Taylor*, 234 U.S. at 75-76 (no federal jurisdiction where the right asserted depends on state property law); *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912) (a case does not arise under federal law unless the federal question appears in the plaintiff’s statement of his own claim).

Nor does 16 U.S.C. § 825p confer jurisdiction. That provision gives federal district courts original jurisdiction over “all offenses and violations” under the Federal Power Act and “all suits in equity and actions at law brought to enforce any liability or duty created by” the Act. The Pressls’ complaint does neither. They do not allege a statutory violation, seek to enforce a federal duty, or invoke any liability created by the Federal Power Act. Their claim is for declaratory relief regarding private property rights. Federal jurisdiction cannot be expanded by statutory language that reaches only claims created by federal law. See *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 115; *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

APCO’s reliance on its FERC license and project boundary is equally unavailing. A federal regulatory backdrop does not transform a state-law easement dispute into a federal case. In *Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co.*, 291 U.S. 205 (1934), the Court held that a state-law claim does not arise under federal law merely because federal law may be implicated in resolving some aspect of the dispute. Likewise, in *Mentzer v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R. Co.*, 259 U.S. 447 (1922), the Court recognized that a federally regulated setting does not itself create federal jurisdiction where the cause of action remains one of state law. The same principle controls here: APCO’s federal license may limit what APCO may do in some contexts, but the Pressls’ claim asks only what the easement itself permits.

The district court also erred in treating APCO’s federal-license argument as creating a substantial federal issue. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the mere presence of a federal question in a defense, or as an antecedent issue, is insufficient. *Mottley*, 211 U.S. at 152; *Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569; *Moore*, 291 U.S. at 214. The Pressls can prevail entirely by showing that the easement’s text does not authorize APCO to impose an occupancy permit as a condition to dock construction. That is a classic state-law contract-and-property determination.

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, its dismissal must be vacated. A federal court without jurisdiction may do nothing other than dismiss or remand. *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506 (1869); *The Mayor v. Cooper*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 247 (1867). When a removed case was improvidently removed, remand is required. *Prentis v. Atlantic Coast Line Co.*, 211 U.S. 210 (1908). The proper course here is to vacate the judgment and remand the case to the district court with instructions to return it to Virginia state court.

For these reasons, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for lack of subject matter jurisdiction.
