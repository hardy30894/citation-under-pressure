Respondent should prevail because Missouri’s age-70 retirement rule for state judges does not offend the ADEA as then enacted, and it does not deny equal protection.

First, the ADEA should not be construed to displace Missouri’s constitutional rule as to judges. When Congress legislates in an area traditionally reserved to the States, courts presume it does not intrude absent a clear statement. See Missouri v. Lewis, 101 U.S. 22 (1879); Metcalf & Eddy v. Mitchell, 269 U.S. 514 (1926). The organization and tenure of state courts lie at the core of state sovereignty. See The Federalist No. 78 (Alexander Hamilton) (discussing the judiciary as a coordinate branch whose structure is for the political communities to arrange); Ohio ex rel. Popovici v. Agler, 280 U.S. 379 (1930) (recognizing the States’ special authority over their own judicial offices). Nothing in the pre-1970 ADEA indicates a congressional purpose to override a state constitutional qualification for judges.

Moreover, judges are not ordinary “employees” in the sense the ADEA protects. The statutory text excludes categories of officials whose duties are governmental rather than merely ministerial. The ordinary understanding of a judge’s office is that of a public magistrate exercising sovereign power. See Ex parte Hennen, 38 U.S. (13 Pet.) 230 (1839) (describing judicial office as a public trust); United States v. Germaine, 99 U.S. 508 (1878) (distinguishing officers from mere employees by reference to the exercise of governmental authority). Because judges exercise the State’s judicial power, Missouri could properly treat their tenure differently from that of rank-and-file workers.

Second, the retirement provision easily satisfies equal protection. Age is not a suspect classification, and legislation that distinguishes on that basis need only bear a rational relation to a legitimate governmental purpose. See Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc., 348 U.S. 483 (1955); McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420 (1961). Missouri could rationally conclude that mandatory retirement at seventy helps ensure judicial vigor, preserves public confidence, and avoids the burdens of individualized competency litigation. A State may address problems “one step at a time.” Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc., 348 U.S. 483 (1955). It may also draw rough lines even if they are imperfect. See FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307 (1993). Earlier cases likewise upheld broad legislative classifications where the State could reasonably believe they advanced administrative efficiency and public welfare. See Carmichael v. Southern Coal & Coke Co., 301 U.S. 495 (1937); Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners, 330 U.S. 552 (1947).

Nor does the Equal Protection Clause require Missouri to wait until a judge demonstrably fails before removing him from office. The Constitution does not forbid prophylactic rules based on general legislative judgments. See Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York, 336 U.S. 106 (1949). A state may address what it reasonably perceives as institutional risks associated with advanced age in a sensitive public office. See Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co., 220 U.S. 61 (1911). That is especially true where the office is one whose incumbents exercise the final authoritative power of the State. See Tumey v. Ohio, 273 U.S. 510 (1927).

In short, Missouri acted within its constitutional authority in fixing a retirement age for its judges, and the rule is plainly rational. The judgment should be affirmed.
