**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the government must make an assessment against each individual partner before it may rely on the ten-year collection period in 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The Internal Revenue Code makes assessment taxpayer-specific. Section 6203 provides that an assessment “shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer,” and the regulations require identification of “the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203; 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. Section 6502(a) then extends the collection period only for “the assessment of any tax” that has been timely made. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Reading those provisions together, the statute extends the limitations period only for the taxpayer actually assessed. As the Ninth Circuit explained, “[t]he assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership.” United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. 114, 121-22 (2004) (quoting the court below). The partners here were separate taxpayers, and the IRS concededly never assessed them within the ordinary three-year period. Id. at 118-19.

A. Assessment against the partnership did not assess the partners.

This Court has long treated assessment as the mechanism that fixes the government’s administrative right to collect from a particular taxpayer. In Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935), the Court described assessment as the government’s “formal act” establishing the tax liability for collection purposes. Later, in United States v. Berman, 825 F.2d 1053, 1056 (6th Cir. 1987), the court recognized that “assessment” is the critical step that creates the government’s right to proceed against the assessed taxpayer. Nothing in the Code says that an assessment against one taxpayer automatically becomes an assessment against another simply because state law may make both liable for the same debt.

That distinction matters here. The partnership and the individual partners are separate “taxpayer[s]” under 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The IRS therefore had to assess each taxpayer it sought to hold administratively liable. As the Ninth Circuit put it, “[a]n ‘individual’ can be a partner but is distinct from a ‘partnership.’” United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 121. The government’s contrary theory would rewrite § 6502(a) to say that an assessment against any liable entity extends time against every other liable person. But Congress did not choose that language. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-23.

B. State-law partner liability does not dispense with the federal assessment requirement.

The government’s principal response is that, because California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, no separate assessment is necessary. But state-law substantive liability is not the same thing as federal procedural authority to collect. In Commissioner v. Stern, 357 U.S. 39, 45 (1958), the Court held that while state law determines whether a person is substantively liable for another’s tax debt, federal law governs the government’s collection procedures. Likewise, in United States v. Rodgers, 461 U.S. 677, 682-83 (1983), the Court recognized that federal tax collection remedies are creatures of federal law, even when state law supplies the underlying property interests.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied that distinction. California law may make a partner liable, but it does not eliminate the need for a judgment before the partner’s personal assets may be reached. United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123-24 (citing Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)). The IRS obtained no judgment here, and the time to obtain one expired. Id. at 124. Thus, even if the government possessed a substantive claim against the partners, it no longer possessed a timely enforceable federal collection claim.

C. The government’s reliance on partnership assessment cases is misplaced.

The government’s authorities do not hold that one assessment against a partnership automatically extends the limitations period against individual partners. In United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122, the Court explained that United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), involved assessments against both the partnership and the individual partners, and therefore did not decide the question presented here. Likewise, Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), did not hold that the IRS may collect from an unassessed partner. It merely rejected a refund claim where the taxpayer had already paid. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-23. And United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458-63 (9th Cir. 1961), likewise turned on different procedural defects and does not support bypassing assessment entirely.

This Court has repeatedly warned against treating the tax laws as if liability and collection are the same thing. They are not. An assessment identifies the taxpayer and starts the clock for collection against that taxpayer. 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 6502(a); United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 121-24. The IRS failed to assess respondents individually within three years. It therefore cannot invoke the ten-year collection period to recover from them now.

The judgment should be affirmed.
