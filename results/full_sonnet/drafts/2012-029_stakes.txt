ARGUMENT

I. The Texas Malpractice Action Does Not "Arise Under" the Patent Laws Within the Meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).

Section 1338(a) confers exclusive federal jurisdiction over "any civil action arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents." This Court has held that a state-law claim "arises under" federal patent law only when a "well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988). Neither branch of that test is satisfied here.

A. Patent Law Does Not Create Minton's Cause of Action.

Minton's suit sounds exclusively in Texas legal malpractice law. His right to relief arises from the duty a lawyer owes a client under state tort principles, not from any right created by Title 35. See Christianson, 486 U.S. at 808–09. That the malpractice claim requires proof of a "case within a case"—that Minton would have prevailed on the experimental-use exception but for counsel's asserted negligence—does not transform a state tort claim into one created by federal law. The cause of action, the duty breached, and the remedy sought are all creatures of Texas law.

B. The Embedded Patent Question Is Not "Necessary, Disputed, and Substantial" Within the Meaning of Grable.

Even where patent law does not create the cause of action, jurisdiction may lie if the claim "necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). This Court has been careful not to let federal question jurisdiction expand merely because a federal issue is "lurking in the background." Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117 (1936). Rather, courts must engage in "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Id. at 117–18. A suit does not arise under federal law "unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

Applying that standard, the hypothetical patent question embedded in this malpractice action is not substantial in the sense Grable requires. The Texas courts will not be adjudicating the validity of the '643 patent, enforcing any patent right, or binding the Patent and Trademark Office; they will be answering the backward-looking, case-specific question whether, on the record that existed in 2001, a reasonably competent attorney would have pled the experimental-use exception and whether doing so would have changed the outcome of Minton's own infringement suit. Nothing about that inquiry will "govern numerous" future disputes or fix the meaning of federal patent law for anyone beyond these parties; it is confined to the peculiar facts of one abandoned defense in one closed case. Because the outcome of the malpractice suit has no preclusive or practical effect on the '643 patent itself, or on patent law generally, the federal interest is far removed from the kind of substantiality this Court found present in Grable, where resolution of the federal question "would resolve" and "govern" recurring controversies over the meaning of a federal tax provision. See Grable, 545 U.S. at 313–14.

C. Federal Jurisdiction Here Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Courts.

Grable's fourth requirement asks whether the exercise of federal jurisdiction would "disturb[] any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. Legal malpractice, like other traditional tort and professional-responsibility claims, is a subject "'that States have relegated to their courts,'" and one in which the States possess an overwhelming interest in regulating attorney conduct within their own bars. Treating every malpractice claim that happens to involve a patent-related defense as one "arising under" the patent laws would sweep an entire category of state-law claims—malpractice suits touching copyrights, trademarks, or any other body of federal law raised as a defense in the underlying litigation—into the federal courts. Federal courts applying the Grable framework, including the Fifth Circuit in Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir. 2008), have recognized that "federal question jurisdiction exists where (1) resolving a federal issue is necessary to resolution of the state-law claim; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal jurisdiction will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." Minton cannot satisfy the third or fourth of these elements, and the court below erred in holding otherwise.

II. This Court Should Reject an Expansive Reading of § 1338(a) That Federalizes State Malpractice Claims Whenever a Patent Issue Is Incidentally Present.

Adopting the Texas Supreme Court's rule would mean that any malpractice plaintiff who once litigated a patent case must now litigate his malpractice claim in federal court, regardless of the claim's fundamentally local character. That result cannot be squared with Christianson's insistence that jurisdiction depends on what "necessarily appears in the plaintiff's well-pleaded complaint," 486 U.S. at 809, nor with Grable's insistence on substantiality and balance. This Court should reverse.
