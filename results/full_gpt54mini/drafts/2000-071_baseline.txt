**Argument**

Milford did not violate the Good News Club’s free speech rights by denying access to school facilities after hours because the Club sought to use the school for religious instruction and worship, not for a permitted secular activity. The Free Speech Clause does not require a school to transform a limited public forum into an open forum for all expressive activity. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802-06 (1985); *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44-46 (1983). Once a forum is limited to certain subject matters, the government may exclude speech falling outside the scope of that forum so long as its restriction is reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 106-07 (2001); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 392-94 (1993).

Here, Milford’s Community Use Policy permitted outside use for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but expressly barred use “for religious purposes.” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. at 102-04. The record shows that the Good News Club’s meetings consisted of prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, songs about Christ, and invitations to accept Jesus as Savior. Id. at 103-05. That is religious instruction and devotional activity, not merely discussion of morality from a religious perspective. The Second Circuit’s contrary approach—treating the Club’s activities as a permitted subject of “moral and character development” but excluding them because they were “quintessentially religious”—misunderstood the forum and the nature of the speech. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 514-16 (2d Cir. 2000), rev’d, 533 U.S. 98 (2001). Milford was entitled to conclude that the Club’s proposed use fell outside the scope of the forum it had created. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

In any event, Milford’s decision was viewpoint neutral. The school did not exclude the Club because it disagreed with the Club’s perspective on morals; it excluded the Club because its proposed program was religious worship and instruction. The Constitution “forbids the government to regulate speech in ways that favor some viewpoints or ideas at the expense of others,” *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394, but a government “does not create a public forum by inaction or by permitting limited discourse.” *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 802. Milford could allow secular youth groups to discuss moral development without opening its facilities to a Christian evangelistic program that used Bible stories, prayer, and conversion invitations as its central methods. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), does not hold otherwise; it recognized that the line between speech and worship may depend on context, but it did not require schools to sponsor religious worship in a limited forum.

Nor would granting access have violated the Establishment Clause. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a public school does not offend the Establishment Clause by allowing private religious speech on equal terms in a limited public forum. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 270-75; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395; *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 839-46 (1995). But those decisions do not compel access here, because the relevant constitutional question is whether the forum included religious instruction and worship. Milford reasonably determined that it did not. A school need not extend equal access where doing so would convert a limited forum into a platform for devotional exercises directed at young children. *Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 107-10, distinguished from *Widmar* and *Lamb’s Chapel*.

Finally, Milford’s Establishment Clause concern was at minimum a constitutionally permissible basis for its policy judgment. The government need not expose elementary-school children to private religious worship in order to avoid hostility to religion. *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592-99 (1992); *Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 302-10 (2000). Milford acted to preserve the boundary between its limited after-hours use policy and religious exercises that, in the school setting and among young children, could reasonably be perceived as school endorsement. See *Edwards v. Aguillard*, 482 U.S. 578, 583-84 (1987). The Constitution did not require Milford to risk that result.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
