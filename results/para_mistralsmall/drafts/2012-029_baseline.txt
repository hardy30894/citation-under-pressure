**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit correctly held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state legal malpractice claims that require resolution of a substantial and disputed patent-law issue.**

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988) (interpreting 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a)). This jurisdiction extends to state-law claims that "necessarily raise a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The Federal Circuit correctly applied this framework in *Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), holding that a state legal malpractice claim arising from patent litigation triggers exclusive federal jurisdiction when the patent issue is necessary, disputed, and substantial.

Here, Minton’s malpractice claim turns entirely on whether his attorneys negligently failed to raise the *experimental use exception* to the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). This issue is not merely tangential—it is the *sine qua non* of his malpractice claim. Without resolving whether the experimental use exception applied, Minton cannot prove that his attorneys’ negligence proximately caused his injury. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Thus, the patent-law issue is both necessary and substantial.

The Texas Supreme Court erred in declining to follow *Grable* and its progeny. The Court improperly minimized the federal interest in uniform patent-law interpretation, which Congress sought to protect by vesting exclusive jurisdiction in the Federal Circuit. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818-19. The experimental use exception is a core patent-law doctrine, and its application in malpractice claims risks inconsistent state-court interpretations that could undermine federal patent policy. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272-73.

**II. The Texas Supreme Court’s holding disrupts the congressionally approved balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities.**

The Supreme Court in *Grable* emphasized that federal jurisdiction exists only where state-court resolution of the federal issue would "disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. The Texas Supreme Court’s decision does precisely that. By allowing state courts to adjudicate patent-law issues embedded in malpractice claims, it invites forum shopping and inconsistent rulings on patent validity and infringement—matters Congress intended federal courts to resolve exclusively. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818-19.

The dissent’s reliance on *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336 (Neb. 2005), and similar cases is misplaced. Those decisions predate *Grable* and fail to account for the Supreme Court’s refined test. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313-14. Moreover, they ignore the Federal Circuit’s well-reasoned application of *Grable* in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept*. The Fifth Circuit has since adopted this approach. *See USPPS, LLC v. Hubbard*, 647 F.3d 272, 278-81 (5th Cir. 2011).

**III. The experimental use exception is a substantial and disputed federal issue that must be resolved uniformly.**

The experimental use exception is a judicially created doctrine designed to prevent patent invalidation when an invention is sold primarily for experimentation. *See Electromotive*, 417 F.3d at 1210. Its application requires interpreting federal patent law, including the scope of the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). *See id.* Because this issue is both necessary to Minton’s malpractice claim and substantial in the patent-law context, federal jurisdiction is proper. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314; *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09.

The Texas Supreme Court’s contrary holding undermines Congress’s intent to ensure uniform patent-law interpretation. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 818-19. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to dismiss the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.
