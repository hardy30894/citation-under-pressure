The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to negate the prosecution's evidence of mens rea, separate from an insanity defense. The Supreme Court has long recognized that states have considerable latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto, as well as the burdens of proof associated with them (Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977); see also Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 445-46 (1992)).

Arizona law places the burden on the defendant to prove insanity by clear and convincing evidence (Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502). The state's law also permits defendants to present evidence of mental disease or defect to support an insanity defense, but it does not allow such evidence to be used to negate the mens rea element of a crime (State v. Mott, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051-52 (Ariz. 1997)). This is a permissible legislative choice, as the Supreme Court has held that a state may "limit the insanity defense... in a way that is not inconsistent with the fundamental principles of due process" (Clark v. Arizona, is not the controlling case here; instead, see Dettweiler v. State, 905 P.2d 971, 974 (Alaska Ct. App. 1995), and Finger v. State, 27 P.3d 66 (Nev. 2001), which were cited in the lower court decision and reflect the split among state courts).

The respondent's position is supported by the principle that the Constitution does not mandate a particular formulation of the insanity defense or dictate how a state must structure its laws regarding the admissibility of evidence related to a defendant's mental state (see Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 536-37 (1968)). As the Supreme Court has observed, "the Due Process Clause... does not require the State to adopt a particular insanity test" (Clark is not directly on point; see Medina, 505 U.S. at 449). Arizona's law, as upheld by its courts, represents a valid exercise of state authority to define the contours of its criminal law and the admissibility of evidence related thereto.

The petitioner, Eric Clark, was afforded the opportunity to present his insanity defense, and the trial court considered the expert evidence he presented in that context. The Constitution does not require that this evidence be used for an additional purpose, namely, to negate the prosecution's evidence of mens rea. The Arizona Supreme Court's decision upholding the trial court's ruling was therefore consistent with the principles of due process.

In sum, the Due Process Clause does not obligate Arizona to permit Clark to use expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of mens rea, beyond the context of his insanity defense. The state's law, as applied to Clark, does not offend the Constitution.
