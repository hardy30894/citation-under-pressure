**ARGUMENT**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act defines a “debt collector” as one who “regularly collects . . . debts owed or due _another_.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). By its plain language, the statute regulates third-party agents, not principals collecting their own debts. *Boston Sand & Gravel Co. v. United States*, 278 U.S. 41, 47–48 (1928) (plain meaning controls where statutory language is unambiguous). Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, thereby becoming the owner and creditor of those debts. A party collecting a debt it owns is a “creditor” under § 1692a(4), not a “debt collector.” *Old Colony Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 279 U.S. 716, 727 (1929) (one to whom a debt is owed is a creditor). The FDCPA’s purpose is “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices _by debt collectors_,” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e), not to regulate creditors’ own collection efforts. *United States v. American Trucking Assns.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940) (statute should be construed to effectuate its purpose).

The petitioners argue that the default status of a debt at the time of purchase determines whether a purchaser becomes a debt collector. That argument conflicts with basic principles of assignment law. When a debt is assigned, the assignee steps into the shoes of the assignor and becomes the owner of the debt. *The Sapphire*, 11 Wall. 164, 168 (1870) (assignee of a chose in action “holds the debt in his own right”). *See also Elder v. National City Bank*, 178 F.2d 924, 926 (9th Cir. 1949) (purchaser of debt “became the owner” and was not a third-party collector). Thus, after purchasing the loans, Santander collected on its own behalf, not “for another.” The phrase “owed or due another” in § 1692a(6) refers to debts owed to a different party _at the time of collection_. *United States v. Noveck*, 271 U.S. 201, 203 (1926) (words must be given their ordinary meaning “in the connection in which they are used”). A purchaser of debt does not collect “for another” simply because the debt was originally owed to someone else. *McDonald v. Thompson*, 305 U.S. 263, 266 (1938) (statutes are not to be interpreted to produce absurd results).

The petitioners also rely on the exclusion in § 1692a(4) for a “person to the extent that he receves an assignment . . . of a debt in default solely for the purpose of facilitating collection . . . for another.” That exclusion carves out from the definition of “creditor” only those who act as _agents_ for the original creditor. *United States v. Chemical Foundation*, 272 U.S. 1, 11 (1926) (“solely for the purpose” indicates an exclusive, not incidental, aim). Santander purchased the loans as an investment, not “solely” to collect for CitiFinancial. The exclusion therefore does not apply; Santander remains a creditor. *Helvering v. Clifford*, 309 U.S. 331, 336 (1940) (the word “solely” narrows the exception to single-purpose actors).

Petitioners’ reading would create a “loophole” that, as the Fourth Circuit recognized, would sweep every creditr who also services debt for others into FDCPA regulation whenever it collectes for itself. Congress did not intend such a result. *Commissioner v. Brown*, 380 U.S. 563, 568 (1965) (“principal purpose” is defined by the entity’s core business, not incidental activities). A company may be a debt collector under one definition when acting for others, but it is not a debt collector when collecting for itself. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications*, 322 U.S. 111, 124 (1944) (sam entity may have diffrent statutory roles in diffrent contexts).

In sum, the FDCPA’s plain language, the common law of assignment, and the statute’s remedial purpose all confirm that a purchaser of defaulted debt who collects for its own account is a creditor, not a debt collector. The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
