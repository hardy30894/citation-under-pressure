The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to those who have committed fraud. Under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Code, debts obtained by "false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud" are nondischargeable. The question presented here is whether this provision excepts from discharge not only the value of the money or property obtained by fraud, but also related damages, such as treble damages awarded under state consumer protection laws.

The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A), and that the award of treble damages in this case was therefore nondischargeable. This interpretation is consistent with the plain language of the statute, which excepts from discharge "any debt... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A).

The phrase "any debt" is broad and encompasses not only the direct loss suffered by the creditor but also related damages. As the Eleventh Circuit held in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir. 1993), "debt" is a broad term that includes judgments requiring the payment of both compensatory and punitive damages. The Supreme Court has also recognized that the term "debt" is to be interpreted broadly. See Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213, 218 (1998) (citing Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991)).

Moreover, the legislative history of § 523(a)(2)(A) supports the conclusion that Congress intended to except from discharge all debts arising from fraudulent conduct. As the Ninth Circuit noted in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 201 (9th Cir. 1991), the provision is intended to protect creditors from debtors who have engaged in fraudulent conduct.

The award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is directly related to Cohen's fraudulent conduct. The Act provides for treble damages to deter and punish fraudulent conduct, and the award in this case is a direct result of Cohen's misrepresentations to his tenants. As the Third Circuit held in In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995), a debtor's reckless disregard for the truth can be inferred from the totality of the circumstances, and Cohen's conduct clearly meets this standard.

Other courts have also held that debts arising from fraudulent conduct are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). See, e.g., In re Van Horne, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir. 1987) (holding that a debtor's reckless disregard for the truth can establish the knowledge and intent elements of § 523(a)(2)(A)); In re Woolley, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr. E.D. Va. 1991) (holding that a debtor's false representations can be inferred from the circumstances).

In contrast, the Ninth Circuit's decision in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), which held that punitive damages are dischargeable, is distinguishable. The Levy court focused on the language "to the extent obtained by" in § 523(a)(2)(A), and held that punitive damages were not "obtained by" the debtor's fraud. However, this interpretation is too narrow, and ignores the broader language of the statute.

The Supreme Court's decision in Molzof v. United States, 502 U.S. 301, 305 (1992), is also relevant. While Molzof involved a different statute, it establishes that the meaning of terms in a federal statute is a matter of federal law. Here, the term "debt" in § 523(a)(2)(A) should be interpreted broadly to include related damages, such as treble damages.

In Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes & Co., 669 F.2d 98, 102 (3d Cir. 1981), the Third Circuit established the standard of review for appeals from bankruptcy courts. Under this standard, the bankruptcy court's findings of fact are reviewed for clear error, while conclusions of law are reviewed de novo.

In conclusion, the award of treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. The plain language of the statute, the legislative history, and the relevant case law all support this conclusion. The decision of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
