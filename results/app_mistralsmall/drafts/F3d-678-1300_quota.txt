**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING THE ASSERTED CLAIMS INVALID UNDER 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) BECAUSE LEADER’S LEADER2LEADER® PRODUCT DID NOT PLACE THE ASSERTED CLAIMS IN THE PUBLIC DOMAIN PRIOR TO THE CRITICAL DATE.**

A. **Public Use Under § 102(b) Requires More Than Mere Accessibility; It Requires Public Accessibility or Beneficial Use by a Third Party.**

The Federal Circuit has consistently held that “public use” under § 102(b) requires that the invention be “either in public use or on sale” and that such use must be “accessible to the public.” *Lough v. Brunswick Corp.*, 86 F.3d 1113, 1120 (Fed. Cir. 1996). The Supreme Court has further clarified that “public use” occurs when the invention is “made available to the public in such a way that the public could obtain knowledge of it and so that the public could use it.” *Pfaff v. Wells Elecs., Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 64 (1998) (quoting *Elizabeth v. Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 136 (1878)).

Here, the district court erred in concluding that Leader’s internal demonstrations and sales pitches to potential customers constituted a public use. The record shows that Leader’s Leader2Leader® product was demonstrated only to a limited audience—primarily government and commercial entities—and was never made generally available to the public. *See In re Klopfenstein*, 380 F.3d 1345, 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2004) (holding that confidential disclosures do not constitute public use).

B. **The Leader2Leader® Product Was Not “On Sale” Under § 102(b) Because It Was Not Ready for Patenting.**

The Federal Circuit has established a two-prong test for determining whether an invention was “on sale” under § 102(b): (1) the invention must be the subject of a commercial offer for sale, and (2) the invention must be ready for patenting. *Pfaff*, 525 U.S. at 67. The second prong requires either a reduction to practice or sufficient drawings or descriptions to enable one skilled in the art to practice the invention. *Id.*

Leader’s Leader2Leader® product was not ready for patenting prior to the critical date. The record demonstrates that the product was still in development, with no completed software or fully functional system available for sale. *See *Ferag AG v. Quipp, Inc.*, 45 F.3d 1562, 1565 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (holding that an invention is not ready for patenting if it is not reduced to practice or lacks enabling disclosures).

C. **The District Court Improperly Relied on Leader’s Internal Communications as Evidence of Public Use.**

The Federal Circuit has cautioned against inferring public use from internal company communications or limited demonstrations. *See *Eolas Techs. Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 399 F.3d 1325, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (holding that internal testing does not constitute public use). The district court’s reliance on Leader’s white paper and demonstrations to potential customers was misplaced, as these activities did not place the asserted claims in the public domain.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING LEADER’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW BECAUSE THE JURY’S VERDICT WAS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.**

A. **The Jury’s Verdict Was Based on Insufficient Evidence of Public Use or On-Sale Activity.**

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 50(a) permits a court to grant judgment as a matter of law if “a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the party on that issue.” *See *Reese v. CNH Am. LLC*, 574 F.3d 1356, 1362 (Fed. Cir. 2009). Here, the jury’s verdict was based on an erroneous legal standard and insufficient evidence.

The jury was instructed that Leader’s internal demonstrations and sales pitches constituted public use, which was contrary to controlling precedent. *See *Lough*, 86 F.3d at 1120. Additionally, the evidence presented at trial did not establish that the Leader2Leader® product was ready for patenting or commercially available prior to the critical date. *See *Pfaff*, 525 U.S. at 67.

B. **The District Court’s Denial of Leader’s Motion for a New Trial Was an Abuse of Discretion.**

A district court’s denial of a motion for a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion. *See *Uniloc USA, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 632 F.3d 1292, 1311 (Fed. Cir. 2011). Here, the district court abused its discretion by failing to recognize that the jury’s verdict was against the great weight of the evidence and contrary to law.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Leader respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
