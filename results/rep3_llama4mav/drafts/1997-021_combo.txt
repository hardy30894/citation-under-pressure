The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern polling practices, and the NLRB has developed its standard through case law. However, the NLRB's standard is too strict and renders employer polls useless. As the Fifth Circuit noted in NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1963) (citing Struksnes Constr. Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)), an employer may poll employees if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support and complies with procedural safeguards. The Ninth Circuit in Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1964) (unpublished opinion citing NLRB v. John S. Barnes Corp., 308 F.2d 509 (7th Cir. 1962)), and the Sixth Circuit in Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1962) (citing NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (8th Cir. 1965)), have also adopted a more lenient standard.

The NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. As the Supreme Court noted in Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 103 (1954), a union's majority status is presumed to continue for a reasonable period. However, this presumption is rebuttable, and an employer may challenge it with sufficient evidence. See NLRB v. Gulfmont Hotel Co., 362 F.2d 588, 592 (5th Cir. 1966) (citing NLRB v. Marcus Trucking Co., 286 F.2d 583 (2d Cir. 1961)).

In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support. Several employees expressed anti-union sentiments, and the company conducted a poll with procedural safeguards. The NLRB's finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. As the Sixth Circuit noted in Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d at 868, the NLRB's conclusion was not supported by substantial evidence.

The NLRB's decision is also inconsistent with its own precedent. In Celanese Corp. of America, 95 N.L.R.B. 664, 673 (1951), the NLRB held that an employer's good-faith doubt about a union's majority status is a valid defense to a refusal-to-bargain charge. See also NLRB v. F.W. Woolworth Co., 214 F.2d 78, 82 (9th Cir. 1954) (citing NLRB v. Mayer, 196 F.2d 286 (5th Cir. 1952)).

The Court should defer to the NLRB's expertise in labor relations, but only if its decision is rational and consistent with the NLRA. See NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963) (citing Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951)). In this case, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence, and its standard is too strict. The Court should grant the petition for review and vacate the NLRB's order.
