# ARGUMENT

## I. An Appellate Reversal for Insufficient Evidence Is the Equivalent of an Acquittal, and the Double Jeopardy Clause Forbids Retrial After Such an Acquittal.

This Court has long held that once a defendant has been acquitted, "however erroneous" the ruling, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second attempt to convict. Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100, 133 (1904). In Kepner, the Court held that permitting the government to appeal an acquittal and retry the defendant would place him "twice in jeopardy," regardless of the correctness of the acquittal. Id. at 130. The Court reaffirmed that principle in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962) (per curiam), holding that a directed verdict of acquittal, "right or wrong," could not be reviewed "without putting [the defendant] twice in jeopardy." Id. at 143. The rule traces to United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896), which established that a verdict of acquittal is "final, and could not be reviewed" through a second prosecution.

The California Court of Appeal's ruling that the prosecution's proof was legally insufficient to sustain the prior-conviction allegation is precisely such an acquittal. It was a determination that the People had failed, on the evidence presented, to carry their burden. Under Kepner, Ball, and Fong Foo, the government cannot obtain a second chance to prove what it failed to prove the first time, merely because the label on the proceeding is "sentencing enhancement" rather than "trial."

This Court's decision in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), confirms that the label attached to a proceeding does not control the double jeopardy analysis. There, the Court held that a defendant convicted of a lesser offense — and thus impliedly acquitted of a greater one — could not be retried for the greater offense after the lesser conviction was reversed on appeal. The Court explained that the Double Jeopardy Clause was "designed to protect an individual from being subjected to the hazards of trial and possible conviction more than once for an alleged offense." Id. at 187. That same hazard — a second attempt to prove the same allegation after the State's first attempt failed — is presented here.

## II. The Proceeding to Determine the Truth of the Prior-Conviction Allegation Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial, and Therefore Falls Within the Protection of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Double Jeopardy Clause's protections are not confined to the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution; they extend to any proceeding that functions as a trial of a disputed factual issue bearing on the defendant's liberty. In Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605 (1967), this Court held that where a sentencing statute conditions an enhanced sentence on a new finding of fact, due process requires the "full panoply" of trial protections — notice, the right to be heard, the right "to be confronted with witnesses against him," and the right to cross-examine those witnesses. Id. at 610. The Court distinguished Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), on the ground that Williams involved a judge's discretionary consideration of background information, not a discrete new finding of fact essential to the sentence. Specht, 386 U.S. at 608-609.

The prior-conviction proceeding here is squarely governed by the Specht line, not the Williams line. It requires proof, beyond a reasonable doubt, of a specific historical fact — that petitioner suffered the alleged prior conviction and personally used a deadly weapon or inflicted great bodily injury. It is conducted according to the rules of evidence, with a right to confrontation and cross-examination. In short, it is a trial in every respect but name. This Court has never suggested that a State may evade the Double Jeopardy Clause simply by relocating a factual determination that would otherwise be an element of a greater offense from the guilt phase to a nominal "sentencing" phase. To hold otherwise would allow the State to do by relabeling what it could not do directly — subject a defendant to multiple trials on the same factual allegation.

## III. The Purposes Underlying the Double Jeopardy Clause Compel Its Application Here.

The core purpose of the Double Jeopardy Clause is to spare a defendant "the ordeal of a second trial." Green, 355 U.S. at 187-88. Because "the State with all its resources and power should not be allowed to make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense," id. at 187, the Clause prevents the government from refining its case on retrial after an initial failure of proof. That same anxiety, expense, and risk of an emboldened prosecution attend a second attempt to prove a prior-conviction allegation. See Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173 (1874) (the constitutional guarantee protects against being punished twice for the same act). Nothing in the nature of a sentence-enhancement proceeding diminishes those concerns; if anything, because the allegation determines whether a defendant's sentence will be doubled under the Three Strikes law, the stakes for the defendant are substantial.

Just as this Court held in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), that the prohibition against double jeopardy is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," and in North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), that the Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal, this Court should hold that the Clause's protection extends to bar the State's second attempt to prove the prior-conviction allegation against petitioner. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
