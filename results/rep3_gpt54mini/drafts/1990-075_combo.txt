The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power.

Congress may “obtain[] assistance from coordinate branches,” so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). That settled standard has repeatedly been applied to uphold broad delegations where Congress has identified the policy, the subject matter, and the relevant factors to be considered. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 425-27 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 249-50 (1947); American Power & Light Co. v. Securities & Exchange Comm’n, 329 U.S. 90, 104-05 (1946); Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Div. of Dep’t of Labor, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941).

Section 811(h) satisfies that standard. Congress authorized temporary scheduling only “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), and it restricted the Attorney General to specified factors bearing on abuse and risk to public health. It also imposed short duration limits and required consideration of the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ views. Such detailed standards are far more definite than the guidance upheld in Yakus, where Congress permitted price controls to prevent inflation, 321 U.S. at 420-21, and in Fahey, where Congress authorized regulation of bank management in the public interest, 332 U.S. at 250.

Petitioners’ reliance on the 1935 decisions is misplaced. Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), are the only cases in which this Court struck down federal statutes for excessive delegation. But those statutes contained no comparable guidance: Panama Refining lacked any policy or standard to govern the President’s action, 293 U.S. at 415-16, and Schechter delegated a broad power to approve codes without meaningful limitations. 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h), by contrast, cabins discretion with substantive criteria, procedural safeguards, and temporal limits.

Nor is there anything impermissible about Congress’s choice to vest the temporary scheduling power in the Attorney General. The Constitution does not forbid Congress from entrusting administrative implementation to the Executive where Congress sets the governing policy. Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904); Cincinnati, W. & Z. R.R. Co. v. Commissioners of Clinton County, 1 Ohio St. 77, 88-89 (1852) (recognized in principle by later federal decisions); Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 693-94 (1892). Here, Congress legislated the core prohibition itself and left only the determination of whether particular substances pose the imminent hazard Congress defined.

The temporary scheduling authority was also properly subdelegated to the DEA. The Attorney General is empowered to delegate functions within the Department of Justice under 28 U.S.C. § 510, and this Court has recognized that where Congress entrusts an executive officer with authority, the officer may employ subordinate officials to carry out ministerial and administrative tasks. United States v. Giordano, 416 U.S. 505, 514-17 (1974), is inapposite because it construed a specific statutory limit enacted after the relevant delegation principles were already established; earlier cases confirm the broader rule that executive officials may act through subordinates unless Congress has plainly prohibited it. See United States v. Germaine, 99 U.S. 508, 510-11 (1879); United States v. Eaton, 169 U.S. 331, 343 (1898); United States v. Eaton, 169 U.S. at 343 (“not a head of a department”). Nothing in the CSA forbids the Attorney General from assigning internal enforcement functions to the DEA Administrator. To the contrary, the statute contemplates administration by the Department of Justice, and the DEA is the Attorney General’s principal drug-enforcement arm.

Finally, petitioners’ theory would revive a rigid nondelegation doctrine rejected by this Court’s cases. The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress supplied the policy and left execution to the Executive. See Buttfield, 192 U.S. at 496; St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. Taylor, 210 U.S. 281, 287-88 (1908); Hampton, 276 U.S. at 404-05; Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424-27; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250. Section 811(h) fits comfortably within that settled line. The judgment should be affirmed.
