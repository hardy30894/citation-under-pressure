## Argument

### I. Summary judgment was improper on Burnett’s FMLA claim because the record contains ample evidence that Habitat had notice of a potentially serious health condition and Burnett’s need for leave.

The FMLA requires only that an employee give notice sufficient to make the employer aware that the employee may need leave for an FMLA-qualifying reason; the employee need not invoke the statute or use “magic words.” 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1); 29 C.F.R. § 825.303(b). This Court has repeatedly held that notice is a practical, fact-specific inquiry turning on what the employer knew and whether the information provided would reasonably apprise it that FMLA leave might be needed. See, e.g., *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d 471 (7th Cir. 2006); *Stoops v. One Call Communications, Inc.*, 141 F.3d 309 (7th Cir. 1998); *Phillips v. Quebecor World RAI, Inc.*, 450 F.3d 308 (7th Cir. 2006).

Here, Burnett did far more than the district court acknowledged. He told Polo in October 2003 that he had a “weak bladder” and was seeing a doctor. In December he reported a week-long illness, produced a doctor’s order for blood testing, explained that he had learned he had “two serious problems,” and stated that he needed to see a urologist. On December 16 he said he had been “sick,” felt he was “getting sick or was sick,” and linked his condition to prostate cancer by referencing his brother-in-law’s illness. On January 7 he notified Habitat of an upcoming prostate biopsy and gave a supervisor a document explaining that elevated PSA could indicate prostate cancer. That is more than enough to create a triable issue on notice. See *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d 471 (7th Cir. 2006); *Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 473 F.3d 479 (7th Cir. 2007) (discussing sufficiency of notice under the FMLA).

This Court has rejected the notion that employees must diagnose themselves or provide detailed medical explanations before the statute applies. In *Burnett*, the Court explained that “the employee need not expressly assert rights under the FMLA or even mention the FMLA.” 472 F.3d at 478. In *Phillips*, the Court held that notice was sufficient where the employee’s statements and medical absences conveyed enough information for the employer to understand that leave may be for a serious condition. 450 F.3d at 311-13. Likewise, in *Byrne v. Avon Prods., Inc.*, 328 F.3d 379 (7th Cir. 2003), the Court emphasized that an employer’s awareness of an employee’s illness and related absences can suffice even when the employee does not provide a clinical diagnosis. See also *N. Ill. Rehab. Servs. v. S.C. Dep’t of Health & Human Servs.*, 527 F.3d 869 (7th Cir. 2008) (emphasizing practical notice principles).

At minimum, the evidence required a jury to decide whether Habitat had enough information to investigate further and whether Burnett’s statements and medical documentation reasonably signaled a need for leave. Summary judgment is inappropriate where competing inferences can be drawn from the record. See *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986); *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317 (1986). The district court impermissibly weighed the evidence against Burnett instead of construing it in his favor. See *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574 (1986).

### II. Summary judgment was also improper on the ADA claim because Habitat had sufficient notice of Burnett’s disability-related condition and should have engaged in the interactive process.

The ADA’s notice requirement is similarly practical: an employee must inform the employer of a disability and the need for accommodation in a manner sufficient to put the employer on notice. See 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(5)(A); *Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Cmty. Schs.*, 100 F.3d 1281 (7th Cir. 1996); *EEOC v. Sears, Roebuck & Co.*, 417 F.3d 789 (7th Cir. 2005). An employee need not identify the precise legal theory or use formal terminology if the employer is otherwise aware of the condition and the workplace problem it creates. *Bultemeyer*, 100 F.3d at 1285-86.

Burnett’s communications plainly put Habitat on notice. He told Polo he had a “weak bladder,” was under medical evaluation, had serious abnormal test results, and needed a urologist and prostate biopsy. He also explained that the proposed transfer would reduce restroom access because of his bladder condition. That is classic notice of a medical impairment affecting work and a need for accommodation. See *Bultemeyer*, 100 F.3d at 1285; *Sears, Roebuck*, 417 F.3d at 803-04. An employer cannot avoid liability by refusing to connect the dots when the employee has identified a physical limitation and a workplace accommodation issue. See *Hedberg v. Indiana Bell Tel. Co.*, 47 F.3d 928 (7th Cir. 1995).

The district court’s ruling also ignores the employer’s duty to participate in the interactive process once it knows of the disability and the need for accommodation. *Beck v. Univ. of Wis. Bd. of Regents*, 75 F.3d 1130 (7th Cir. 1996); *Ekstrand v. Sch. Dist. of Somerset*, 583 F.3d 972 (7th Cir. 2009). Burnett’s request to remain in a location with restroom access, coupled with his repeated references to bladder and prostate problems, created at least a factual dispute over whether Habitat should have explored accommodations rather than summarily disciplining him.

Finally, the suspicious sequence of reprimands following Burnett’s medical disclosures reinforces the need for a trial. See *Troupe v. May Dep’t Stores Co.*, 20 F.3d 734 (7th Cir. 1994). Because a reasonable jury could find that Habitat had adequate notice under both statutes and reacted by escalating discipline rather than accommodating Burnett, summary judgment should be reversed.
