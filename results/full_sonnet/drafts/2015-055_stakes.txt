# ARGUMENT

## I. The Rule Announced in Johnson Is Substantive and Therefore Applies Retroactively on Collateral Review

Under the framework established in *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules of criminal procedure generally do not apply retroactively to cases already final on direct review. *Id.* at 310. But this limitation applies only to *procedural* rules. As this Court has repeatedly explained, "[n]ew substantive rules generally apply retroactively." *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004). The touchstone is whether a rule "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Id.* at 353. If it does, the rule is substantive, "because '[a] court has no more power to enforce an unconstitutional law' after the trial than before it." *Id.* (citation omitted). Rules of criminal procedure, by contrast, "regulate only the manner of determining the defendant's culpability." *Id.*

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), fits squarely within the substantive category. The Court there held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act's definition of "violent felony"—covering conduct that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii)—"denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges" and thus "is unconstitutionally vague." *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. That holding did not create a new procedure for adjudicating whether a prior conviction qualifies as a predicate offense; it eliminated an entire *category* of predicate offenses from ACCA's reach altogether. Before *Johnson*, a defendant with three prior convictions falling within the residual clause faced a mandatory fifteen-year minimum sentence; after *Johnson*, that same defendant faces, at most, the ordinary ten-year statutory maximum under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g). The class of persons subject to ACCA's enhanced punishment has been narrowed as a matter of substantive law, not procedural mechanics.

This case closely parallels *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614 (1998), where the Court held that a decision narrowing the scope of a criminal statute—there, the "use" element of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(1)—applied retroactively on collateral review because it "narrowed the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms." *Id.* at 620. The Court explained that such decisions are retroactive because they may mean "that the conduct with which [the defendant] was charged did not violate the statute." *Id.* Just as the decision in *Bousley* removed certain conduct from the ambit of § 924(c), *Johnson* removed an entire clause—and the convictions it swept in—from the ambit of ACCA's enhanced-sentencing provision. The logic is identical: a defendant sentenced under a since-invalidated clause is, like the defendant in *Bousley*, subject to punishment the law no longer authorizes.

## II. Applying a Void Residual Clause Produces a Sentence Unauthorized by Law, and the Constitution Requires That Such Sentences Be Correctable on Collateral Review

This Court's recent decision in *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190 (2016), confirms that substantive rules of constitutional law must be given retroactive effect on collateral review as a matter of constitutional command, not mere judicial grace. The Court there held that "[a] conviction or sentence imposed in violation of a substantive rule is not just erroneous but contrary to law and, as a result, void," and that "[i]t follows that when a State's collateral review procedures permit prisoners to challenge the lawfulness of their confinement, States cannot refuse to give retroactive effect to a substantive constitutional right that determines the outcome of that challenge." *Id.* at 205. Although *Montgomery* concerned a state prisoner, the same due-process logic governs collateral review of federal sentences: a sentence imposed under an unconstitutionally vague clause exceeds what the law now permits, and the prisoner subject to it is "imprisoned in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States." 28 U.S.C. § 2255(a).

Because the residual clause never validly defined a class of predicate offenses to begin with—"the void-for-vagueness doctrine" requires treating the clause "as if it had never been enacted for purposes of imposing" the enhanced penalty—a defendant sentenced solely by reference to that clause was subject to a punishment "not authorized" by any valid law. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 606–07 (Kennedy, J., concurring in the judgment) (recognizing the settled principle that vague penal provisions cannot support conviction or sentence). Petitioner's sentence rests in material part on a predicate offense—Florida strong-arm robbery—that the Eleventh Circuit approved only by resort to the "serious potential risk of physical injury" language that *Johnson* invalidated. Refusing to apply *Johnson* retroactively would leave in place a sentence built on a constitutionally void foundation, contrary to the principle that "there is no juridical basis for treating differently" defendants convicted under the same invalid rule merely because their cases proceed on collateral rather than direct review. *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620.

## Conclusion

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* is substantive under *Teague v. Lane* and *Schriro v. Summerlin*, and it therefore applies retroactively to cases, like Petitioner's, on collateral review. The judgment below should be reversed.
