# ARGUMENT

## I. The Plain Text of § 1692a(6) Distinguishes Debt Collectors From Creditors Based on Whose Debt Is Collected, Not When the Debt Fell Into Default.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as "any person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). By its terms, this definition turns on an agency-like relationship: the collector acts on behalf of, or with respect to debts belonging to, someone else. A "creditor," by contrast, is "any person to whom a debt is owed." *Id.* § 1692a(4). When an entity collects debt owed to itself, it functions as a creditor, not a debt collector, because Congress's statutory language does not sweep in first-party collection. As this Court recognized in *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995), "[t]he Act's definition of the term 'debt collector' includes a person 'who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] ... another.'" The critical word is "another"—and Santander, having purchased the receivables outright, was collecting debts owed to *itself*, not to another.

Petitioners' theory—that the default status of a debt at the moment of acquisition permanently fixes an entity's status as a "debt collector" even after it becomes the owner of the debt—finds no textual support. Nothing in § 1692a(6)'s principal definition mentions default status at all; default appears only in the *exclusionary* clause, § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which exempts from debt-collector status persons collecting non-defaulted debt *owed to another*. That exclusion is irrelevant unless the threshold definition is first satisfied. See *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015) ("[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. [Instead] ... he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements"). Petitioners' argument inverts the statute's structure by treating an exclusion applicable only to third-party servicers as though it supplied an affirmative definition of who *is* a debt collector.

## II. Santander Became a Creditor When It Purchased the Loans, and Its Subsequent Collection Activity Was on Its Own Account.

The phrase "debts owed or due another" must be assessed at the time of the challenged collection conduct, not at the debt's origination. See *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1318 ("[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection"); *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) ("The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another'"). This reading follows the ordinary grammatical rule that a modifying clause—here "owed or due"—governs contemporaneously with the conduct being regulated. Cf. *Paroline v. United States*, 134 S. Ct. 1710, 1721 (2014) ("When several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all") (quoting *Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920)).

Once CitiFinancial Auto sold the receivables to Santander on December 1, 2011, the debts became "owed" to Santander, and Santander's post-purchase collection activity was undertaken on its own account, as creditor. That Santander may have previously acted as a servicer collecting on CitiFinancial's behalf does not taint its later, distinct status as owner of the debt. Congress regulated conduct, not persons in perpetuity; an entity is a "debt collector" only "when it engages in collection activity on behalf of another," and is "acting as a creditor, not a debt collector" when it "collect[s] its own debts."

## III. Petitioners' Interpretation Would Produce Results Congress Did Not Intend and Would Improperly Subject Ordinary Creditors to FDCPA Liability for Collecting Their Own Debts.

Congress's stated purpose was "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors," 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e), not to regulate creditors collecting their own accounts. Petitioners' rule—that default status at acquisition permanently brands a purchasing entity a "debt collector"—would sweep vast categories of consumer-finance companies, banks, and other creditors that purchase defaulted paper into FDCPA liability for garden-variety first-party collection, contrary to the statute's design. It would also create an unworkable "loophole" in reverse: rather than allowing entities to evade regulation, it would impose debt-collector status on entities engaged in numerous business lines—lending, servicing, and investing—merely because one activity happened to involve debt collected on behalf of others. Such an entity would be regulated as a "debt collector" for *every* activity, including collecting its own accounts, simply because it also regularly collects for others elsewhere in its business. Nothing in the statutory text compels, and the Act's purpose disclaims, such an expansive and indiscriminate result.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander, having purchased the plaintiffs' loans and thereafter collecting on them for its own account, was a creditor, not a "debt collector" under 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The judgment below should be affirmed.
