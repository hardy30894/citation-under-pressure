## Argument

The Bureau of Prisons ("BOP") erred in denying Victor M. Roussos eligibility for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) after he successfully completed a 500-hour drug treatment program. The BOP's decision was based on an overly broad interpretation of the term "crime of violence" in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), as applied through its regulations and Program Statement 5162.02.

The statute governing early release, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), allows for a reduction in sentence for prisoners convicted of a "nonviolent offense." Although the statute does not define "nonviolent offense," the BOP's regulations clarify that it is the converse of a "crime of violence" as defined in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). The definition in § 924(c)(3) is two-pronged, encompassing offenses that either have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force or involve a substantial risk that physical force may be used.

Roussos was convicted of conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846, which does not inherently involve the use or threat of physical force. Therefore, to classify his offense as a "crime of violence," it must be shown that it "by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force against the person or property of another may be used in the course of committing the offense." 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)(B).

The BOP's Program Statement 5162.02 categorizes certain drug offenses as crimes of violence if the sentence was enhanced for possession of a dangerous weapon. However, this interpretation is inconsistent with the statutory definition and relevant case law. In _United States v. Canon, 66 F.3d 1073 (9th Cir. 1995)_, the Ninth Circuit held that a conviction for possession with intent to distribute cocaine was not a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3) merely because a firearm was present. The court emphasized that the statute requires more than mere possession; it demands an element of force or a substantial risk thereof.

Similarly, in _United States v. Cruz, 805 F. Supp. 104 (D. Conn. 1992)_ (cited with approval in _United States v. Luster, 305 F.3d 199 (3d Cir. 2002)_), the district court held that a drug trafficking offense is not inherently a crime of violence. The mere possibility of violence associated with drug trafficking is insufficient to categorize the offense as a crime of violence under § 924(c)(3).

Roussos' conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics, without more, does not constitute a crime of violence. The enhancement of his sentence for possession of a firearm during the commission of the offense does not transform the underlying offense into a crime of violence. The BOP's reliance on Program Statement 5162.02 to deny Roussos early release eligibility is therefore inconsistent with the statutory definition of "crime of violence" and relevant case law.

The BOP's action is also inconsistent with the congressional intent behind 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), which is to provide an incentive for prisoners to participate in drug treatment programs. By denying Roussos eligibility for early release based on an overly broad interpretation of "crime of violence," the BOP undermines this intent.

In conclusion, the district court erred in denying Roussos' habeas petition. The BOP's interpretation of "crime of violence" as applied to Roussos is inconsistent with the statute and relevant case law. Roussos is entitled to relief, and this Court should reverse the district court's order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this argument.
