**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The government’s burden of persuasion at trial is not diminished by a defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period. In *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), the Supreme Court held that the government must prove each element of a conspiracy charge, including the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s participation in it, beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 607. This burden does not shift merely because a defendant claims to have withdrawn from the conspiracy prior to the limitations period. *Id.*; *see also United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 839 (2d Cir. 1939) (L. Hand, J.) ("The burden of proving the conspiracy and the defendant’s connection with it remains on the prosecution throughout the trial.").

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *United States v. Hickey*, 395 F.2d 433 (D.C. Cir. 1968), reinforces this principle. There, the court rejected the argument that a defendant’s withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the limitations period relieved the government of its burden to prove the conspiracy’s continuation. *Id.* at 437. The court explained that the government must prove the conspiracy’s existence during the limitations period "by evidence sufficient to convince the jury beyond a reasonable doubt." *Id.* (citing *Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 607). The same rule applies here: the government cannot shift the burden to the defendant to disprove the conspiracy’s continuation simply because the defendant claims to have withdrawn.

II. **The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden to Prove Continuing Conspiracy**

The trial court’s failure to instruct the jury on the government’s burden to prove the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period was reversible error. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), the Supreme Court held that in conspiracy cases, the jury must be instructed on the government’s burden to prove each element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 215. This includes the requirement that the government prove the conspiracy’s existence during the limitations period. *Id.*; *see also United States v. Cohen*, 124 F.2d 164, 167 (2d Cir. 1941) (L. Hand, J.) ("The jury must be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued into the period covered by the indictment.").

The D.C. Circuit has consistently followed this rule. In *United States v. Bynum*, 327 F.2d 825 (D.C. Cir. 1964), the court held that the trial court must instruct the jury that the government bears the burden of proving the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period. *Id.* at 827. The court explained that without such an instruction, the jury might improperly infer that the conspiracy’s continuation was presumed or that the defendant bore the burden to disprove it. *Id.* The same error occurred here, and it warrants reversal.

III. **The Government’s Failure to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Requires Reversal**

Because the government failed to prove the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period, the convictions must be reversed. In *United States v. Rabinowich*, 238 U.S. 78 (1915), the Supreme Court held that a conspiracy conviction cannot stand if the government fails to prove the conspiracy’s existence during the limitations period. *Id.* at 86. The Court explained that the government’s burden to prove the conspiracy’s continuation is not satisfied by evidence of acts occurring outside the limitations period. *Id.*; *see also United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d at 839 ("The government must prove the conspiracy’s existence during the limitations period by evidence sufficient to convince the jury beyond a reasonable doubt.").

The D.C. Circuit has applied this rule in cases where defendants withdrew from conspiracies prior to the limitations period. In *United States v. Hickey*, 395 F.2d at 437, the court reversed a conspiracy conviction because the government failed to prove the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period. The court held that the government’s burden to prove the conspiracy’s existence during the limitations period is not satisfied by evidence of acts occurring outside that period. *Id.* The same error occurred here, and it requires reversal.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
