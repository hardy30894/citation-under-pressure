**ARGUMENT**

I. **The district court erred in holding that Wasek failed to establish harassment because of sex under Title VII and the ELCRA.**

Title VII prohibits discrimination “because of . . . sex,” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and the ELCRA likewise bars employment discrimination “because of . . . sex.” Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2202(1)(a). A plaintiff need not prove that the harassment was motivated by sexual desire; the question is whether the conduct occurred “because of sex.” *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc.*, 523 U.S. 75, 80-81 (1998). The Supreme Court has made clear that same-sex harassment is actionable where the plaintiff shows the harasser’s conduct was sex-based. *Id.* at 79-80. This Court applies the ELCRA consistently with Title VII. *Radtke v. Everett*, 442 Mich. 368, 501 N.W.2d 155, 160-61 (1993); *Haynie v. State of Mich.*, 468 Mich. 302, 662 N.W.2d 849, 854-55 (2003).

Wasek met that standard. Ottobre did not merely engage in general workplace bullying. He repeatedly touched Wasek’s buttocks and rear end, poked him in the rear with tools, and accompanied that conduct with sexual comments such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” Those facts are not neutral teasing; they are overtly sexualized conduct directed at a male employee. In *Oncale*, the Court explained that same-sex harassment can be proved by evidence that the harasser was “motivated by general hostility to the presence of men in the workplace,” or by “explicit or implicit proposals of sexual activity.” 523 U.S. at 80-81. Ottobre’s conduct fits the latter category, and his repeated sexual touching and sexually charged remarks permit the inference that the conduct was because of sex. See *Walker v. Bain*, 257 F.3d 660, 667-68 (6th Cir. 2001) (same-sex harassment actionable where conduct was sexual in nature and could support inference of sex-based motivation).

The district court gave insufficient weight to the fact that Ottobre’s conduct was not isolated horseplay but persistent, sexualized abuse. This Court has recognized that the “critical issue” is whether members of one sex are exposed to disadvantageous terms or conditions of employment to which the other sex is not exposed. *Vickers v. Fairfield Med. Ctr.*, 453 F.3d 757, 763 (6th Cir. 2006) (quoting *Oncale*, 523 U.S. at 80). Here, Wasek was subjected to sexually explicit remarks, unwanted sexual touching, and ridicule tied to those sexualized acts. A reasonable jury could find that this treatment altered the conditions of his employment because he was male. *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986).

II. **The harassment was severe or pervasive enough to create a hostile work environment.**

To establish a hostile-work-environment claim, a plaintiff must show conduct that is sufficiently “severe or pervasive” to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment. *Meritor*, 477 U.S. at 67; *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21-23 (1993). This Court considers the totality of the circumstances, including the frequency and severity of the conduct, whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, and whether it unreasonably interferes with work performance. *Williams v. Gen. Motors Corp.*, 187 F.3d 553, 562-63 (6th Cir. 1999).

Wasek’s evidence satisfies that standard. Ottobre’s conduct was recurring, physically invasive, and humiliating. He grabbed Wasek’s buttocks, poked him in the rear with tools, and made repeated sexual remarks. Physical touching of intimate parts is especially probative of severity. See *Howley v. Town of Stratford*, 217 F.3d 141, 154-55 (2d Cir. 2000). The harassment was compounded by Tripp’s laughter and repeated refusals to intervene, despite direct complaints. Employer inaction may be considered in assessing whether the environment was hostile and whether the employer is liable. *Harris*, 510 U.S. at 23; *Meritor*, 477 U.S. at 72.

III. **Arrow Energy is liable because supervisors knew of the harassment and failed to act.**

An employer is liable when it knew or should have known of harassment and failed to take prompt remedial action. *Hafford v. Seidner*, 183 F.3d 506, 513-14 (6th Cir. 1999); *Williams*, 187 F.3d at 560. Under the ELCRA, a supervisor who “negligently fails to remedy” harassment may also expose the employer to liability. *Radtke*, 501 N.W.2d at 164-65.

Here, Tripp witnessed some of Ottobre’s conduct and laughed. When Wasek complained, Tripp warned him not to “make waves” or “whin[e]” to higher management. Smillie likewise responded to Wasek’s complaints by telling him to “just kick [Ottobre’s] ass” and later suggesting that he and Ottobre “duke it out.” Strong’s response was to chastise Wasek for “whining.” These responses are not prompt remedial action; they are evidence of indifference. A jury could readily find that Arrow Energy failed to address known harassment. See *Hafford*, 183 F.3d at 514-15.

IV. **Wasek’s resignation was legally supported by constructive discharge principles.**

A constructive discharge occurs when working conditions are so intolerable that a reasonable person would feel compelled to resign. *Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders*, 542 U.S. 129, 147-48 (2004). Under the ELCRA, the same intolerability standard applies. *Radtke*, 501 N.W.2d at 165-66. Wasek informed management of ongoing sexual touching and verbal abuse, was mocked for complaining, and was told in substance to fight it out himself. After repeated failures by supervisors to intervene, he left the job. A jury could find that Arrow Energy’s response left him no realistic alternative but to quit. *Suders*, 542 U.S. at 147.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
