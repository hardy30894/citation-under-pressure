# ARGUMENT

## I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the Tax Court's legal conclusions de novo and its findings of fact for clear error. *Gustashaw v. Commissioner*, 696 F.3d 1124, 1128 (11th Cir. 2012). Because the Tax Court's determination that Meruelo's advances did not constitute "bona fide indebtedness" running "directly" to him, Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i), rests on a misapplication of the governing legal standard to undisputed facts, plenary review applies.

## II. The Net Transfers Through Merco's Affiliates Constituted Bona Fide Indebtedness Running Directly to Meruelo

Subchapter S permits a shareholder to deduct corporate losses only up to the sum of his stock basis and "the shareholder's adjusted basis of any indebtedness of the S corporation to the shareholder." 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1)(B). The Treasury Regulations require that such indebtedness be a "bona fide indebtedness" that "runs directly to the shareholder." Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). This Court has already recognized, however, that indebtedness need not flow in a single, unbroken line from a bank to the corporation's books before it can be attributed to the shareholder. In *Selfe v. United States*, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985), this Court held that a genuine issue of material fact existed as to whether a bank loan nominally made to an S corporation should, in substance, be treated as a loan first made to the shareholder and then contributed by her to the corporation, where the shareholder had personally guaranteed the debt and the corporation's financial condition made her the primary obligor in substance. *Id.* at 774–75. The *Selfe* Court instructed that the proper inquiry looks past the form of the transaction to whether "the substance of the transaction" establishes that the shareholder, not the corporation, was the true source of the funds. *Id.*

The transactions at issue here present an even stronger case for finding indebtedness running directly to Meruelo than the guaranteed bank loan in *Selfe*. Unlike a mere guarantee, the funds that flowed to Merco from its affiliates were traceable to entities in which Meruelo held a direct and substantial ownership interest, and Meruelo's own accountant, Carreras, consistently treated Meruelo's proportionate share of the net payable as an advance against the $10 million line of credit that Meruelo personally extended to Merco. That the funds passed through intermediate affiliates before reaching Merco does not defeat the "runs directly" requirement of the regulation any more than a bank's role as intermediary defeated the shareholder's claim in *Selfe*. Other circuits confronting comparable multi-entity fact patterns have likewise recognized that back-to-back financing among commonly controlled entities may be recharacterized as a direct shareholder loan where the shareholder's interest in the paying entities and the consistent accounting treatment demonstrate that the shareholder was, in economic reality, the lender. *See Oren v. Commissioner*, 357 F.3d 854, 857–58 (8th Cir. 2004) (examining whether a shareholder's loan through a related entity should be treated as running directly to the S corporation). The Tax Court's rigid insistence on a single-entity transfer ignores this line of authority and the commercial reality of Meruelo's integrated business structure.

## III. Meruelo Satisfied the Actual-Economic-Outlay Requirement

Even accepting that some outlay must be shown independent of bona fide indebtedness, the Tax Court erred in concluding Meruelo made none. The economic-outlay inquiry asks whether the shareholder has undertaken an outlay that leaves him "poorer in a material sense" as a result of the transaction—the very concern animating this Court's remand instruction in *Selfe*, 778 F.2d at 775, that the district court determine whether the guarantee "was the equivalent of a direct loan" to the corporation. Meruelo's position is stronger than that of the *Selfe* guarantor, who had not yet paid anything to the bank when the case arose. Here, Merco's own books recorded, year after year, net accounts payable to affiliates substantially owned by Meruelo, and Carreras charged Meruelo's proportionate share of those net payables against the $10 million promissory line of credit Meruelo personally executed in favor of Merco. That contemporaneous, consistent accounting treatment—applied identically across five tax years and reflected on the returns of every entity involved—is precisely the sort of objective indicium of a real economic burden that distinguishes a genuine shareholder advance from the empty guarantee disregarded in cases such as *Estate of Leavitt v. Commissioner*, 875 F.2d 420 (4th Cir. 1989). Unlike the shareholders in *Leavitt*, who had contributed no capital and made no advances traceable to their own resources, Meruelo already funded Merco's initial $5 million deposit obligation with proceeds of his personal loan, establishing that the parties' course of dealing treated Meruelo, not his affiliates, as Merco's true creditor.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the Tax Court's decision should be reversed insofar as it disallowed Meruelo's claimed $9 million basis, and the case should be remanded for entry of judgment consistent with this Court's holding in *Selfe v. United States*, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985).
