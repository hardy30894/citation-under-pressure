# ARGUMENT

## FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME COMPLIES WITH THE SIXTH AND EIGHTH AMENDMENTS

### I. Ring v. Arizona Does Not Invalidate Florida's Sentencing Scheme Because the Jury Finds the Facts Necessary for Death Eligibility

This Court's decision in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), held that Arizona's capital sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it permitted a judge, sitting alone, to find the aggravating circumstances necessary to impose death. The Court explained that "the Sixth Amendment requires that the aggravating factor determination be entrusted to the jury." *Id.* at 609. Petitioner argues that Florida's scheme suffers the same defect. It does not.

Florida's capital sentencing procedure requires a jury to hear all evidence relevant to aggravation and mitigation and to render an advisory verdict recommending life or death. § 921.141(1)–(2), Fla. Stat. Critically, the jury's recommendation is not advisory as to whether an aggravator *exists*—the jury weighs the aggravating and mitigating evidence and renders its recommendation based on that weighing process. The trial judge, in turn, must give the jury's recommendation "great weight." *Tedder v. State*, 322 So. 2d 908, 910 (Fla. 1975). This is fundamentally different from Arizona's scheme, where the judge made independent factual findings regarding aggravators with no jury involvement whatsoever.

This Court has recognized that not every capital sentencing scheme involving judicial factfinding violates *Ring*. In *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504 (1995), the Court upheld Alabama's scheme, which similarly involved a jury recommendation followed by judicial sentencing, because "the Constitution permits the trial judge, acting alone, to impose a capital sentence." *Id.* at 515. While *Ring* limited *Harris* in the context of factfinding on aggravators, it did not disturb the principle that jury involvement short of unanimous factfinding on every element can satisfy the Sixth Amendment where, as in Florida, the jury plays a substantial and constitutionally adequate role in the process.

Moreover, this Court in *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), and *Ring* focused on the maximum sentence authorized by the jury's verdict. In Florida, the jury's conviction for first-degree murder, combined with the jury's recommendation process involving consideration of aggravating factors, provides the constitutionally required jury involvement. The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that *Ring* does not require unanimous jury findings on aggravators where the jury's recommendation, following its weighing of aggravation and mitigation, satisfies the constitutional floor established in *Ring*.

### II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Unanimous Jury Sentencing Verdicts

Petitioner's Eighth Amendment argument fares no better. This Court has never held that unanimity in capital sentencing is constitutionally mandated. In *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), this Court expressly upheld Florida's capital sentencing scheme, including its judge-override provisions, holding that "the Constitution does not require jury sentencing, even where the sentence is death." *Id.* at 462–63. While *Ring* limited *Spaziano*'s holding regarding factfinding, it did not disturb *Spaziano*'s broader holding that jury sentencing itself—let alone unanimous jury sentencing—is not constitutionally required.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Eighth Amendment requires "heightened reliability" in capital sentencing, *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), but reliability is achieved through Florida's comprehensive procedural safeguards: proportionality review, statutorily enumerated aggravators, requirement of weighing aggravation against mitigation, and appellate oversight. Nothing in this Court's Eighth Amendment jurisprudence, from *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), through *Kansas v. Marsh*, 548 U.S. 163 (2006), has required unanimous jury verdicts as a component of reliability. In *Gregg*, this Court upheld Georgia's statute despite the absence of any unanimity requirement beyond the aggravator finding, focusing instead on whether the scheme provided a principled means to distinguish cases warranting death. 428 U.S. at 195, 198.

That Florida may be an "outlier" among death penalty states, as the concurring justices below observed, does not establish an Eighth Amendment violation. This Court's proportionality analysis under the Eighth Amendment, articulated in *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584 (1977), and refined in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), examines national consensus regarding the *category* of offenders or offenses eligible for death—not the specific procedural mechanisms by which death is imposed. Florida's non-unanimous jury recommendation process is a procedural choice, not a substantive judgment about who may be executed, and thus falls outside the consensus-based analysis this Court employs under the Eighth Amendment.

### III. The Trial Court Properly Determined Hurst's Mental Retardation Claim

Under *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of the mentally retarded, but this Court "le[ft] to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction." *Id.* at 317. Florida did so through § 921.137, Florida Statutes, and Florida Rule of Criminal Procedure 3.203, which assign the trial court—not the jury—the responsibility of determining mental retardation as a threshold legal question of eligibility for the death penalty.

This allocation is consistent with *Atkins* itself, which imposed no jury-trial requirement for the retardation determination. Nor does *Ring* apply, because the mental retardation determination is not an aggravating factor increasing the maximum punishment; rather, it is an eligibility threshold analogous to age-based eligibility determinations, which courts have consistently found to be properly resolved by the trial judge. Hurst received a full evidentiary hearing on this issue, with competing expert testimony, and the trial court's finding that Hurst failed to establish mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence—supported by IQ scores ranging from 69 to 78 and conflicting expert assessments of adaptive functioning—was neither clearly erroneous nor constitutionally deficient.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
