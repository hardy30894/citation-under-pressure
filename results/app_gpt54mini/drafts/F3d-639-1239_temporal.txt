## Argument

### I. The district court erred in dismissing this putative class action as moot after an unaccepted offer of judgment.

Article III requires a live case or controversy at all stages, but it does not allow a defendant to defeat a representative action by tendering relief to the named plaintiff before the class-certification issue can be decided. In **Sosna v. Iowa, 419 U.S. 393 (1975)**, the Supreme Court later explained that a class action may remain justiciable even when the named plaintiff’s individual claim becomes moot, because the controversy may continue for the absent class members. Although Sosna postdates this Court’s inquiry, the principle it identifies rests on older mootness doctrine: a case is not moot so long as a court can still grant effectual relief to parties with a legally cognizable stake. See **United States v. Alaska S.S. Co., 253 U.S. 113 (1920)**; **Gritts v. Fisher, 224 U.S. 400 (1912)**.

The district court relied on a broad statement that once the named plaintiff’s personal claim is satisfied and no class has been certified, the case must be dismissed. But that formulation is too rigid for a putative class action in which the plaintiff has already invoked the court’s authority on behalf of similarly situated persons and has pursued certification within the schedule set by the court. The Supreme Court has long recognized that jurisdiction turns on the existence of a genuine dispute, not on strategic maneuvers designed to terminate litigation before judicial review can occur. See **Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962)**; **Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969)**.

Here, Lucero filed a complaint expressly seeking class-wide relief. The parties then agreed to bifurcated discovery, with certification addressed first, and the court entered a scheduling order reflecting that structure. Lucero moved for class certification within the time set by the court. BCR’s Rule 68 offer, made before certification briefing was complete and before the scheduled certification hearing, was an effort to “pick off” the named plaintiff and prevent the court from deciding the class question. Courts have consistently rejected jurisdictional rules that would permit a defendant to evade review through unilateral action. See **Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498 (1911)**; **United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629 (1953)**.

### II. The court should treat the certification motion as relating back to the filing of the complaint.

Even if Lucero’s individual claim could be viewed as satisfied, the better rule is to allow the class-certification motion to relate back to the filing of the complaint where, as here, the plaintiff diligently pursued certification and the defendant’s tender came before the certification issue could be resolved. The relation-back principle is not novel; it reflects the Court’s settled recognition that the expiration or alteration of a named party’s personal stake does not necessarily destroy jurisdiction when the controversy is inherently transitory or likely to evade review. See **Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498 (1911)**; **Moore v. Ogilvie, 394 U.S. 814 (1969)**.

Moore is especially instructive. There, the Court held that a challenge to ballot-access requirements was not moot even though the election had passed, because the dispute remained capable of repetition and continued to affect others. **Moore v. Ogilvie, 394 U.S. 814 (1969)**. Likewise, a Rule 68 offer aimed at extinguishing the named plaintiff’s claim before certification creates a recurring procedural problem that will otherwise escape review. The judiciary should not reward a tactic that is specifically designed to “moot” the case before the class issue can be adjudicated. See **Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498 (1911)**.

### III. Dismissal was improper because Lucero retained a legally cognizable stake in class certification.

A named plaintiff in a putative class action does not stand only for his own claim; he also seeks to represent the interests of others similarly situated. The Court has recognized that a live stake may persist where the party has ongoing responsibilities or where judicial determination will affect others beyond the named litigant. See **Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186 (1962)**; **Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969)**. The district court’s dismissal ignores that Lucero timely sought class relief and that his motion was pending when the case was dismissed. To allow mootness to attach at that point would make class proceedings contingent on the defendant’s willingness to withhold a settlement tactic until after certification, undermining the very function of representative litigation.

This Court should therefore reverse and remand with instructions to reinstate the complaint and decide class certification on the merits. A contrary rule would permit defendants to extinguish putative class actions by unilateral tenders before any court can rule on certification, contrary to the enduring principle that federal courts must resolve live controversies and may not permit strategic manipulation to deprive absent class members of an adjudication. See **United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629 (1953)**; **Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. ICC, 219 U.S. 498 (1911)**.
