**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This provision does not create a federal workers’ compensation regime that applies regardless of where the injury occurs. Rather, it applies only when the injury occurs *on* the outer Continental Shelf—either on the submerged lands seaward of state jurisdiction or on artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore even where there is only a “substantial nexus” to offshore operations, misreads the statutory text, disregards settled precedent, and undermines the geographic precision that Congress intended in defining the scope of federal jurisdiction over the outer Continental Shelf.

The plain language of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “on the outer Continental Shelf” is not a mere descriptive flourish; it is a jurisdictional predicate. The term “outer Continental Shelf” is defined in § 1331(a) as “all submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters.” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). This definition makes clear that the outer Continental Shelf is a *geographic* entity—specifically, submerged lands beyond the three-mile limit of state jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has consistently interpreted the OCSLA’s coverage to be *situs-based*, not *status-based* or *operation-based* alone. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), the Court emphasized that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” This principle is not mere dictum—it is the foundational rule of OCSLA’s reach.

The Court’s statement in *Tallentire* that § 1333(b) “has no bearing on this case” does not diminish its relevance. The footnote in *Tallentire* was not an isolated observation but a recognition that the *situs* of the injury—whether on the outer Continental Shelf or elsewhere—determines whether the federal compensation regime applies. The Court’s reference to a “situs requirement” in the context of § 1333(b) is not dictum in the sense of being irrelevant or unconsidered. It is, rather, a *predictive* statement grounded in the statutory structure and legislative intent. As the Court has long held, “general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used.” *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). The Court’s observation in *Tallentire* was made in the context of a case involving a helicopter crash on the waters above the outer Continental Shelf—precisely the kind of injury that would fall outside the OCSLA’s geographic scope. The Court’s conclusion that the choice-of-law provision did not apply to such an injury underscores the importance of *locale* in determining coverage.

This principle is further confirmed by *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), where the Court held that the “civil and criminal jurisdiction of the United States” extends to the outer Continental Shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” Id. at 355. This extension of federal jurisdiction is not abstract; it is tied to physical presence on the submerged lands or fixed structures. The Court has never held that federal jurisdiction extends to onshore facilities merely because they are used in support of offshore operations. To the contrary, in *United States v. Louisiana*, 363 U.S. 1, 10 (1960), the Court affirmed that the United States holds “dominion and power over, the lands, minerals, and other natural resources underlying the waters of the Gulf of Mexico more than three geographical miles seaward from the coast of each State.” This jurisdiction is *geographic*—it does not extend to lands within the three-mile limit, let alone to onshore facilities.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore, contradicts this settled principle. The Court has long held that the LHWCA applies only when the injury occurs “upon the navigable waters of the United States (including any dry dock).” *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251, 255 (1940). The term “upon” is not synonymous with “resulting from.” In *Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford Railroad*, 281 U.S. 128, 132 (1930), the Court held that the LHWCA applies to employees “employed in maritime employment, in whole or in part, upon the navigable waters of the United States.” The phrase “upon the navigable waters” is not a mere causal link—it is a *situs* requirement. Similarly, in *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244, 247 (1942), the Court affirmed that the injury must have occurred “upon navigable waters” to trigger compensation. The Court did not permit recovery for injuries occurring on land, even if the injury was related to maritime work.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. That decision, which rejected a situs-of-injury test, was based on a misreading of *Tallentire* and ignored the clear statutory language. The Third Circuit’s conclusion that a “but for” test suffices is inconsistent with the *situs* requirement established in *Nogueira*, *South Chicago*, and *Parker*.
