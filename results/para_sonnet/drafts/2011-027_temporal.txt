# ARGUMENT

## THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE STATEHOOD-ERA NAVIGABILITY TEST FOR TITLE PURPOSES

The equal-footing doctrine provides that upon admission to the Union, each State acquired title to the beds of all waters within its borders that were navigable at the time of statehood. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845) (holding that the shores of navigable waters "belong to the several states" upon their admission "and that the new states have the same rights, sovereignty, and jurisdiction over this subject as the original states"). This Court has consistently and unambiguously held that the relevant inquiry for title purposes is navigability *at the time of statehood*—not navigability today, and not navigability determined by any other temporal reference point.

### A. This Court's Precedents Establish Statehood as the Fixed and Exclusive Temporal Benchmark

In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), this Court articulated the controlling test with precision: the question is "whether the river in its natural and ordinary condition, at the time [the state] was admitted to the Union, permitted of use for the customary modes of trade and travel on water." *Id.* at 76. The Court made clear that this evaluation must proceed "according to the ordinary condition of the river's waters, and not those presented at the seasons of extreme high or low water." *Id.* at 87. Nowhere in *Utah* did this Court suggest that the temporal focus varies segment-by-segment based on later human modification; rather, the statehood-era natural condition of the waterway is the touchstone.

This principle traces directly to *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1871), where the Court defined navigable waters as those that "are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *Id.* at 563. The emphasis on "ordinary condition" necessarily anchors the inquiry to a fixed historical baseline—the condition of the river at the crucial moment of sovereign transfer, namely statehood.

*United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), reinforces this temporal fixity. There, the Court examined "the natural and ordinary condition" of the waterway "at the time of the admission of the state," *id.* at 56, confirming that post-statehood alterations—whether by dam construction, channel dredging, or other modification—cannot retroactively divest the State of title vested at the moment of admission, nor can such later modifications be used to manufacture navigability where none existed in 1889.

### B. The Statehood Test Applies River-Segment by River-Segment, Based on Historical Conditions

Petitioner's suggestion that navigability may be assessed by reference to present-day conditions, or without regard to segment-specific historical evidence, finds no support in this Court's jurisprudence. In *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), the Court undertook a searching historical inquiry into the actual navigability of the specific river segment at issue, examining evidence of historical use for trade and travel rather than relying on generalized or contemporary characterizations. *Id.* at 586–87. The Court's method demonstrates that navigability-for-title determinations require a fact-specific, segment-by-segment historical inquiry keyed precisely to the condition of that stretch of river at statehood.

Similarly, in *Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 77 (1922), the Court reaffirmed that the navigability determination for title purposes is inherently backward-looking and factual, stating that the question of navigability "is, of course, to be determined as of the date of the admission of the state." *Id.* at 87–88. This is not a fluid or evolving standard; it is fixed at the moment of statehood and requires courts to reconstruct, through competent historical evidence, the actual condition of the specific river segment at that time.

### C. Historical Evidence of Statehood-Era Conditions Is Properly Admissible and Necessary to the Inquiry

Because the statehood test requires courts to look backward—often more than a century—to determine the "natural and ordinary condition" of a river segment, this Court has recognized that historical documentation, including government surveys, contemporaneous accounts, and other indicia of use, is not merely permissible but essential. In *Utah*, the Court relied extensively on historical evidence of steamboat navigation, trapper and trader accounts, and government surveys to reconstruct the condition of the Colorado River at the time of Utah's admission. 283 U.S. at 82–86. The Court did not treat such evidence as inherently suspect merely because it derived from historical records rather than living testimony; rather, it treated such evidence as the necessary and appropriate mechanism for satisfying the constitutionally mandated statehood-era inquiry.

This approach is consistent with the fundamental purpose of the equal-footing doctrine as explained in *Pollard*: to ensure that new States enjoy the same "sovereignty and jurisdiction" over navigable waters within their borders as did the original States. 44 U.S. at 230. That sovereign interest attached irrevocably at the moment of statehood and cannot be diminished by the mere passage of time or the incidental loss of contemporaneous witnesses. Were the rule otherwise—were courts permitted to disregard reliable historical evidence simply because no living witness observed the river's condition in 1889—the equal-footing doctrine would become a nullity for the vast majority of America's navigable waterways, defeating the very sovereign title interests the doctrine was designed to protect.

### CONCLUSION

The Montana Supreme Court correctly required application of the statehood-era navigability test, consistent with this Court's decisions in *Pollard*, *The Daniel Ball*, *Holt State Bank*, *Oklahoma v. Texas*, *Brewer-Elliott*, and *United States v. Utah*. The judgment below should be affirmed.
