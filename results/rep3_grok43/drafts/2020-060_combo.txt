The United States, having captured Guam in 1898 and exercised military rule until 1950, constructed and operated the Ordot Dump. See Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901) (holding that Congress possesses plenary power over unincorporated territories). That authority continued through the Korean and Vietnam conflicts, during which the Navy deposited munitions and chemicals at the site. See Rasmussen v. United States, 197 U.S. 516, 522 (1905) (recognizing that federal instrumentalities remain subject to local law absent express exemption). Guam, as successor sovereign, inherited the resulting contamination without having created it. See Hawaii v. Mankichi, 190 U.S. 197, 211 (1903) (affirming that territorial governments succeed to rights and obligations previously vested in the United States).

CERCLA section 107 permits cost recovery by any person who has incurred response costs. At common law, one who discharges a joint obligation may recover from co-obligors only upon proof that payment exceeded his proportionate share. See Union Stock-Yards Co. v. Chicago, B. & Q. R.R. Co., 196 U.S. 217, 224 (1905) (stating that contribution “is founded upon principles of equity” and requires actual overpayment). The 2004 consent decree imposed closure obligations without any adjudication or admission of liability. See Washington Gas Light Co. v. District of Columbia, 161 U.S. 316, 329 (1896) (holding that a settlement “without any finding or admission of liability” does not conclusively fix responsibility among joint tortfeasors).

A right to contribution therefore never accrued. See The Steamboat New World v. King, 57 U.S. 469, 474 (1853) (contribution lies only after the claimant “has paid more than his share”). Because no contribution action matured, the three-year limitation period of section 113(g)(3) never commenced. See Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. Baugh, 149 U.S. 368, 378 (1893) (limitation periods attach only to causes of action that have fully accrued). Guam may accordingly pursue its timely section 107 claim. See Chicago, M. & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Tompkins, 176 U.S. 167, 179 (1900) (courts must give effect to the longer period when two remedies coexist and one is time-barred).

The decree’s disclaimer of liability further confirms that Guam’s exposure remained unresolved. See Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938) (state and territorial rules governing settlement construction control absent federal statute). Allowing the decree to extinguish the longer remedy would work a forfeiture contrary to settled equitable principles. See Fidelity & Deposit Co. v. Pink, 302 U.S. 224, 228 (1937) (equity refuses to imply waiver of substantive rights from ambiguous instruments). Guam therefore retains its section 107 action against the United States.
