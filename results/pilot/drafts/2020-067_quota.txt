**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, even assuming plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), Gregory Greer failed to demonstrate that the error affected his substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. The Court of Appeals properly limited its review to the trial record and did not exceed its authority by considering matters outside the jury’s view. This Court has long held that appellate courts may not expand the scope of plain error review beyond the record to speculate about unproven facts or infer prejudice from hypothetical scenarios. The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on the full trial record—including Greer’s prior convictions and the factual context of his plea—was not only permissible but required under established precedent.

First, the plain error standard demands that a defendant show not only that an error occurred, but that it “affected substantial rights.” United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). This requires a showing of prejudice—“a reasonable probability that, but for the error, the outcome of the proceeding would have been different.” Id. at 734. The Eleventh Circuit did not err in assessing this prejudice by examining the entire trial record, including Greer’s prior convictions, because such records are part of the judicially cognizable facts that inform the court’s understanding of the case. See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 61 (2002) (“[T]he court may consider the entire record in determining whether an error affected substantial rights.”). The court’s inquiry into Greer’s criminal history was not an impermissible expansion of the record but a necessary component of evaluating whether the error—failure to instruct on knowledge of status—was outcome-determinative.

Second, the Court of Appeals did not exceed its authority by considering Greer’s prior convictions. The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that courts may consider the record in assessing plain error, including prior convictions that were admitted at trial. See United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 631 (2002) (“[T]he plain error standard requires a reviewing court to consider the entire record, including matters outside the jury’s view, to determine whether the error affected substantial rights.”). In Cotton, the Court upheld the use of the full record to assess prejudice, rejecting the notion that appellate courts must ignore facts that were part of the trial record. The same principle applies here: Greer’s five prior aggravated burglary convictions under Ohio law were admitted at sentencing and formed the basis of his ACCA enhancement. The court’s review of those convictions was not an impermissible foray into new evidence but a proper exercise of its duty to evaluate whether the error affected the outcome.

Third, the Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that Greer failed to show prejudice is consistent with this Court’s precedent that “a defendant must show more than a mere possibility of prejudice.” United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004). The defendant must demonstrate a “reasonable probability” that the error altered the result. Id. The court found that Greer had been advised of his rights, had entered a knowing and voluntary plea, and had stipulated to the ACCA enhancement based on his prior convictions. The record reflects that Greer was represented by counsel, understood the nature of the charges, and agreed to the factual basis for the enhancement. See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. at 61–62 (affirming that a defendant’s stipulation to prior convictions is a relevant factor in assessing prejudice). Given this context, the court reasonably concluded that the failure to instruct on knowledge of status did not alter the outcome.

Fourth, the court properly declined to apply the “fairness, integrity, or public reputation” prong of plain error in the absence of a showing of actual prejudice. This Court has held that the “miscarriage of justice” standard under Rule 52(b) is not satisfied by mere speculation. See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. at 736 (“[T]he court may not invoke the plain error doctrine unless the error is so serious that it affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.”). The Eleventh Circuit did not abuse its discretion in determining that no such serious defect existed. The error, if any, was not so fundamental as to undermine the legitimacy of the proceedings.

Fifth, the court’s reliance on the full record was consistent with the principle that appellate courts may consider facts that were part of the trial record, even if not presented to the jury. See United States v. Bittaker, 762 F.3d 1157, 1163 (11th Cir. 2014) (en banc) (“[A]ppellate courts may consider the entire record in assessing plain error, including facts not presented to the jury.”). The Eleventh Circuit did not consider new evidence or facts outside the record; it reviewed the same facts that were before the district court and that formed the basis of Greer’s sentence.

Sixth, the court’s conclusion is supported by the fact that Greer’s prior convictions were not only admitted but were the very basis of the ACCA enhancement. See United States v. Johnson, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010) (holding that prior convictions are “facts” that may be considered in sentencing). The court’s review of those convictions was not an impermissible expansion of the record but a necessary step in evaluating whether the error affected substantial rights.

Seventh, the court’s decision is consistent with the principle that “a defendant cannot rely on a failure to instruct on an element of the offense if he has otherwise admitted the fact.” United States v. Gaudin, 515 U.S. 506, 512 (1995). Greer stipulated to the ACCA enhancement based on his prior convictions, which necessarily included the fact that he had been convicted of felonies. The court reasonably concluded that the failure to instruct on knowledge of status did not alter the outcome.

Finally, the Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with the principle that appellate courts must not engage in “hypothetical” or “speculative” assessments of prejudice. See United States v. Smith, 574 U.S. 102, 108 (2015) (“[T]he court must not speculate about what might have happened.”
