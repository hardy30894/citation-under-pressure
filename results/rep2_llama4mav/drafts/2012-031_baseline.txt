The District Court erred in certifying the respondent class because it failed to ensure that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis using admissible evidence. Rule 23(b)(3) requires that "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this predominance requirement, the plaintiffs must show that they can prove their claims, including damages, through common evidence. See Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 311-312.

The District Court's certification order was flawed because it accepted the respondents' damages model, which failed to isolate damages resulting from the alleged anticompetitive conduct. The respondents' expert, Dr. McClave, presented four damages models, three of which were based on theories of antitrust impact that the District Court ultimately rejected. Behrend, 264 F.R.D. at 165-166. The remaining model, which was based on the theory that Comcast's clustering deterred overbuilder competition, was not shown to be reliable or relevant to the claims at issue.

The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the District Court's certification order despite this flaw. The appellate court's decision conflicts with the Supreme Court's holding in Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin, 417 U.S. 156 (1974), which prohibits merits inquiries at the class certification stage except to the extent necessary to determine whether the Rule 23 requirements are met. Id. at 177-178. By failing to scrutinize the respondents' damages model, the District Court and the Court of Appeals effectively allowed a merits inquiry that was not necessary to determine whether the Rule 23 requirements were met.

Furthermore, the District Court's certification order is inconsistent with the "rigorous analysis" required by Rule 23. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 318. The court must conduct a thorough examination of the evidence and arguments to ensure that the plaintiffs can prove their claims through common evidence. Id. at 317. In this case, the District Court failed to conduct such an analysis, instead accepting the respondents' damages model without adequate scrutiny.

The Court of Appeals' decision should be reversed because it failed to apply the correct legal standard in reviewing the District Court's certification order. The appellate court should have reviewed the District Court's decision de novo to ensure that it applied the correct legal standard. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 312. By failing to do so, the Court of Appeals allowed a flawed certification order to stand, which could lead to improper class certification and potentially disastrous consequences for the petitioners.

In sum, the District Court's certification order was flawed because it failed to ensure that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis using admissible evidence. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming this order, and its decision should be reversed to prevent improper class certification.
