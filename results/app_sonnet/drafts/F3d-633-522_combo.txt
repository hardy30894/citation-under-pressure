# ARGUMENT

## I. Statutory Rights Created for a Remedial Public Purpose, Like Those Under the FLSA, the NLRA, and the FELA, May Not Be Bargained Away Through an Individual Release Obtained Under Economic Pressure.

The WARN Act's sixty-day notice requirement is not a private entitlement that DHL's employees were free to trade away for a severance check; it is a public-regarding command designed to give workers and their communities time to adjust to the loss of employment. Congress's judgment that such protections cannot be defeated by private contract has deep roots in this Court's labor jurisprudence. In *J.I. Case Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 332 (1944), the Supreme Court held that individual contracts "may not be availed of to defeat or delay" the protections that a governing labor statute was designed to secure, "no matter what the circumstances that induce their execution." *Id.* at 337. The same principle controls here: DHL cannot use individually signed releases, extracted while the company was simultaneously forcing drivers to make irreversible job-rebid decisions, to erase a notice obligation that Congress imposed for the benefit of the workforce as a whole.

This Court has applied the identical logic to releases of Fair Labor Standards Act claims. In *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O'Neil*, 324 U.S. 697 (1945), the Court refused to enforce a release of statutory overtime rights, explaining that the FLSA was enacted to correct the "unequal bargaining power" that exists "as a matter of common knowledge" between employer and employee, *id.* at 706-07, and that an employee's rights under such a statute cannot be abandoned by "a private transaction," *id.* at 704. The Court reaffirmed and extended that holding the following year in *D.A. Schulte, Inc. v. Gangi*, 328 U.S. 108 (1946), holding that a release supported by payment of less than the full statutory entitlement is void because the statute's benefits "cannot be bargained away." *Id.* at 114. WARN's notice obligation, like the FLSA's overtime guarantee, exists to protect employees as a class from precisely the kind of one-sided, take-it-or-leave-it bargain that DHL presented here.

## II. The Releases Were Not "Knowing and Voluntary" Because DHL's Compressed Timetable and Linked Job-Rebid Deadline Created the Functional Equivalent of Duress.

Even where a statute permits waiver of a federal right, the waiver must be knowing and voluntary, and courts examine the surrounding circumstances with care rather than accepting the document's recitals at face value. In *Dice v. Akron, Canton & Youngstown R.R. Co.*, 342 U.S. 359 (1952), the Court held that the validity of a release of a federal statutory claim under the FELA is a matter of federal law and depends on whether the employee "knew" what he was signing and "intended to release" the claim, not on the release's formal recitations. *Id.* at 361-62. Similarly, in *Callen v. Pennsylvania R.R. Co.*, 332 U.S. 625 (1948), the Court examined whether a release of a federal statutory claim was procured through overreaching, recognizing that the party urging the release bears the burden of showing it was fairly obtained. *Id.* at 630.

Judged by that standard, the releases signed by Ellis, Price, and the class cannot stand as knowing, voluntary waivers of their WARN Act claims. Drivers had to decide, in a matter of days, whether to accept the ten-week package while simultaneously completing a job-rebid form that itself required disclosure of that very decision — a structure that made the "choice" to sign the release inseparable from the choice to keep working at all. That is the kind of pressured, all-or-nothing predicament that this Court has recognized can amount to economic duress. In *United States v. Bethlehem Steel Corp.*, 315 U.S. 289 (1942), the Court explained that a contract is the product of impermissible "business compulsion" when the complaining party has no reasonably available alternative but to submit to the other side's terms. *Id.* at 300. Unlike the contractor in *Bethlehem Steel*, who had bargaining alternatives available to it, DHL's employees had none: refuse the release, and lose not only severance but also any orderly transition, given that DHL's collective bargaining agreements contained no severance provision at all. Where, as here, the employer controls both the timeline and the employees' only source of transitional income, the resulting release is not the voluntary act the law requires.

## III. Congress Did Not Authorize the WARN Act's Sixty-Day Notice Requirement to Be Extinguished by Severance Consideration Paid for Something Else.

Finally, even accepting that some waivers were "voluntary" in form, the severance packages were negotiated consideration for the loss of recall rights and seniority — not consideration specifically for release of the WARN Act's separate notice violation. That distinction matters. In *Wilko v. Swan*, 346 U.S. 427 (1953), the Court held that a provision purporting to waive a remedial statutory protection is unenforceable where enforcing it would allow "the very evils" the statute was designed to correct to survive intact. *Id.* at 435. And in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1 (1937), the Court recognized that statutes safeguarding the employment relationship serve interests broader than the individual worker's own pocketbook. *Id.* at 33. The WARN Act's notice requirement protects not only the individual employee's paycheck but the community's interest in orderly notice of mass layoffs — an interest DHL's severance payments never purported to purchase, and one that a hastily signed general release cannot lawfully extinguish.
