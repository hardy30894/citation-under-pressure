**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Genuine issues of material fact exist on each element of the plaintiffs’ retaliation claims under 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C).

Summary judgment is proper only when the movant shows no genuine dispute of material fact and is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 247 (1986); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmoving party. Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986).

To prevail on an FRSA retaliation claim, a plaintiff must prove protected activity, an adverse action, and that the protected activity was a contributing factor in the adverse action. Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co., 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014); Gunderson v. BNSF Ry. Co., 850 F.3d 962, 968 (8th Cir. 2017). Once shown, the employer may avoid liability only by proving by clear and convincing evidence that it would have taken the same action absent the protected activity. Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc., 708 F.3d 152, 158 (3d Cir. 2013); Coppinger-Martin v. Solis, 627 F.3d 745, 750 (9th Cir. 2010).

The plaintiffs engaged in protected activity when they reported unsafe conditions at the bridge in their written statements and recorded interviews. See 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C); Kuduk, 768 F.3d at 789. Kline and Snyder expressly noted the absence of side rails and lighting; Foster reported prior complaints about the location. These reports occurred days before BNSF initiated its investigation.

BNSF took adverse actions by imposing discipline and subjecting Kline and Snyder to increased, unannounced operations testing that altered their work assignments and earnings. An action is adverse if it “well might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge.” Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The Public Law Board’s later reversal of the discipline does not negate its interim effect. See Ray v. Union Pac. R.R. Co., 971 F. Supp. 2d 869, 882 (S.D. Iowa 2013).

A reasonable jury could find the protected activity contributed to these actions. Contributing-factor causation is shown through temporal proximity, shifting explanations, and evidence that the employer’s stated reasons are pretextual. Gunderson, 850 F.3d at 969; Bechtel v. Competitive Techs., Inc., 448 F.3d 469, 474 (2d Cir. 2006). The investigation notice followed the reports by only days; the hearing and discipline occurred months later while the plaintiffs remained under review. BNSF’s witnesses offered conflicting accounts of the crew-change location, and the plaintiffs received heightened scrutiny not imposed on others.

BNSF cannot establish by clear and convincing evidence that it would have disciplined the plaintiffs absent their reports. The company’s affirmative defense requires evidence that “separates the violation from the protected activity.” Araujo, 708 F.3d at 159. The record contains no such separation; the rule violations cited were known or observable before the reports, yet no discipline issued until after the safety complaints.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
