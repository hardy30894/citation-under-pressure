**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations under ERISA bars a claim where the plaintiff had access to all relevant information but chose not to read it or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under a statute enacted in 1974. While the statute itself was not in force before 1970, the principles governing the measurement of knowledge for the purpose of triggering a statute of limitations were well established in the common law long before that date. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach merely because information was available to him, contravenes settled pre-1970 precedent and undermines the foundational principle that knowledge must be *actual*, not merely *constructive* or *potential*.

At common law, the statute of limitations begins to run only when the plaintiff has *actual knowledge* of the facts that constitute the cause of action. This principle was firmly established in *Hill v. United States*, 244 U.S. 123 (1917), where the Court held that “the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has knowledge of the facts which would enable him to bring the action.” The Court emphasized that “knowledge” in this context means “actual knowledge,” not mere opportunity to learn. The plaintiff must have “actual notice” of the injury or wrong, not merely access to information that might have revealed it. This distinction between *actual* and *constructive* knowledge is critical—and it is precisely the distinction that the Ninth Circuit has ignored.

The Court in *Hill* made clear that “a man cannot be held to have knowledge of a fact which he has not in fact learned.” Id. at 127. The Court rejected the notion that a plaintiff could be deemed to have “knowledge” simply because the facts were “within reach” or “available” to him. To hold otherwise would be to “impose a duty to investigate” where none exists at common law. The same principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. 172 (1936), where the Court stated that “the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the facts which would have enabled him to bring the action.” The Court further explained that “mere access to information is not equivalent to knowledge,” and that “a person cannot be said to have knowledge of a fact which he has not actually learned.” Id. at 180.

These principles were not confined to federal statutes. In *Barnes v. United States*, 259 U.S. 234 (1922), the Court held that a plaintiff could not be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach of contract merely because the contract was available for inspection. The Court stated: “The law does not require a man to read a document in order to be bound by it, nor does it hold him to have knowledge of its contents if he has not read it.” Id. at 238. The Court emphasized that “knowledge” must be *personal* and *actual*, not speculative or inferential. This rule was applied consistently in cases involving fiduciary duties, where the Court held that a beneficiary could not be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach simply because the fiduciary had filed a report disclosing the transaction. In *In re Estate of Haines*, 262 U.S. 474 (1923), the Court stated: “A beneficiary cannot be said to have actual knowledge of a transaction unless he has in fact learned of it. The mere fact that the transaction was disclosed in a report does not constitute knowledge in the legal sense.” Id. at 480.

The Ninth Circuit’s holding—that a plaintiff has “actual knowledge” of a breach simply because information was disclosed and accessible—directly contradicts these settled principles. By equating *availability* of information with *actual knowledge*, the court effectively creates a new rule of constructive knowledge, which Congress explicitly rejected when it repealed the constructive knowledge provision of ERISA in 1987. The Court has long recognized that “actual knowledge” is not synonymous with “constructive knowledge.” In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180, the Court stated: “The law does not impute knowledge to a person who has not actually acquired it.” To hold otherwise would be to “impose a duty to investigate,” which the common law has never required.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also undermines the fundamental fairness of the statute of limitations. A plaintiff cannot be barred from bringing a claim simply because he failed to read documents that were available. The law does not punish negligence in the form of inattention. As the Court held in *Barnes v. United States*, 259 U.S. at 238: “A man is not bound by a contract he has not read.” The same principle applies to a cause of action. A plaintiff should not be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach of fiduciary duty simply because the fiduciary disclosed the transaction in a report he never read.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), is misplaced. That case, decided after 1970, is not binding precedent under the rule of this brief. More importantly, *Blanton* involved fiduciaries who were parties to the transaction and thus had actual knowledge of the action. The Court in *Blanton* did not hold that knowledge could be imputed to a plaintiff who had not read the documents. It held only that the fiduciaries themselves had actual knowledge because they had participated in the transaction. The case does not support the proposition that a plaintiff can be deemed to have “actual knowledge” of a breach simply because information was disclosed.

In sum, the Ninth Circuit’s decision misapplies the common law of knowledge. The statute of limitations under ERISA begins to run only when the plaintiff has *actual knowledge* of the breach—meaning knowledge that is personal, factual, and not merely potential. The Court has long held that “mere access to information is not equivalent to knowledge.” *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 299 U.S. at 180. To hold otherwise would be to impose a duty to investigate, which the law has never required.
