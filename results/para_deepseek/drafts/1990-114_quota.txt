## ARGUMENT

### I. The ADEA Does Not Preempt Missouri’s Mandatory Retirement Provision Because Appointed State Judges Are “Appointees on the Policymaking Level.”

Congress did not clearly express an intent to displace state control over judicial tenure. In “traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision.” *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The ADEA’s definition of “employee” excludes “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Appointed state judges fall squarely within that exclusion.

Judging inherently involves policymaking. “[P]olicymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). State judges exercise discretion and judgment that “create law” and resolve “issues previously unsettled.” *Id.* at 55–56. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court similarly held that judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” because they “make policy through their decisions.” *In re Stout*, 559 A.2d 489, 493 (Pa. 1989). The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court agreed, reasoning that judges “exercise substantial discretion in interpreting and applying the law” and “their decisions have the force of law.” *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145 (Mass. 1988).

Petitioners’ narrow reading would exclude the entire appointed judiciary from any ADEA exemption, a result Congress could not have intended. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 56. The legislative history confirms that the exemption covers “persons appointed … to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” Joint Explanatory Statement, 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179, 2180. State courts are agencies of state government. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 56. Because the ADEA does not clearly preempt Missouri’s constitutional provision, the state law must prevail.

### II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.

Age classifications are subject only to rational-basis review. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 314 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri’s mandatory retirement at age 70 is rationally related to legitimate state interests.

First, mandatory retirement “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged … judges.” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). It avoids the “anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence.” *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Second, it “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges,” the risk of which increases with age. *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). Third, it “assure[s] that important decisions … will be made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens.” *Apkin*, 517 N.E.2d at 146. Fourth, it “makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary.” *Id.*

These rationales satisfy rational-basis review. *Murgia* upheld mandatory retirement at 50 for state police because “physical ability generally declines with age.” 427 U.S. at 315. *Vance v. Bradley* upheld mandatory retirement at 60 for foreign service officers because “age may be a rough but valid proxy” for declining abilities. 440 U.S. 93, 109 (1979). The same logic applies to judges, whose work “makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.” *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 627. Missouri could rationally conclude that age 70 is an appropriate line.

Petitioners have no fundamental right to continued judicial employment. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313. The mandatory retirement provision is constitutional.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
