The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, but the NLRB has developed a standard for permitting employers to conduct polls. Several courts of appeals have rejected the NLRB's standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice (e.g., NLRB v. A.W. Thompson, Inc., 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1961); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295 (9th Cir. 1964), citing Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863 (6th Cir. 1962)). In A.W. Thompson, the Fifth Circuit held that an employer may poll its employees if it has "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, even if that evidence is not sufficient by itself to justify withdrawal" of recognition. 651 F.2d at 1145.

In NLRB v. John S. Swift Co., 302 F.2d 342 (7th Cir. 1962), the Seventh Circuit held that an employer's good faith is relevant in determining whether a poll is lawful. The court noted that the employer had a legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment. Similarly, in NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (8th Cir. 1965), the Eighth Circuit recognized the employer's interest in determining employee support for the union.

The NLRB's standard is overly restrictive and fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment. In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union (cf. NLRB v. Gulfmont Hotel Co., 362 F.2d 588 (5th Cir. 1966)).

The Supreme Court has held that the NLRB's decisions must be based on substantial evidence and that the court must review the NLRB's findings to ensure that they are not arbitrary or capricious (Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951)). In this case, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence, and the court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order (NLRB v. Pittsburgh S.S. Co., 337 U.S. 656 (1949)).

Furthermore, the NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships (NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1 (1937)). The court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful.
