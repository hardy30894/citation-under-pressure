**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state may impose term limits on members of the United States Congress by constitutional amendment—is governed by the express text and settled precedent of the United States Constitution. The Framers deliberately confined the qualifications for federal office to those explicitly enumerated in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. Any attempt by a state to add an additional qualification for congressional office violates the Supremacy Clause and the structural integrity of the federal system. This Court has long held that the Constitution’s enumeration of qualifications is exclusive, not merely permissive. Arkansas’s Term Limitation Amendment, by barring individuals from appearing on the ballot for U.S. House or Senate seats based on prior service, creates a new, state-imposed qualification that the Constitution does not permit.

The Constitution provides that “the House of Representatives shall be composed of Members chosen every second Year by the People of the several States.” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, cl. 1. It further specifies that to be eligible for the House, a person must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for seven Years, and [must] have been an Inhabitant of the State in which he was chosen.” Id. Similarly, the Senate requires candidates to be at least thirty years old, a U.S. citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the state they seek to represent. U.S. Const. Art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These are the *only* qualifications established by the Constitution. No other requirements may be added by any state or political subdivision.

This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542 (1876), where the Court declared that “the Constitution of the United States is a law for rulers and people, equally in war and peace, and covers all the departments of human action.” Id. at 554. The Court emphasized that the Constitution’s provisions are not subject to alteration by state action, particularly when such alterations affect the structure of federal institutions. To allow states to impose additional qualifications would undermine the uniformity required by the national character of Congress and invite a patchwork of conflicting eligibility rules across the states.

In *Crandall v. Nevada*, 73 U.S. (6 Wall.) 35 (1868), the Court struck down a state law that prohibited the mailing of newspapers, holding that “the right to publish and circulate newspapers is a fundamental right secured by the Constitution.” Id. at 45. While not directly addressing congressional qualifications, the case illustrates the Court’s consistent recognition that certain rights and structures are so central to the national government that they cannot be altered by state action. The qualifications for Congress are no less essential to the functioning of the federal legislative branch.

Further, in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), the Court reaffirmed that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law can override it.” Id. at 19. The Court rejected the notion that state officials could disregard federal constitutional mandates, even if they believed the federal law was unwise. The same logic applies here: Arkansas cannot impose a term limit on U.S. Representatives or Senators because such a restriction is not authorized by the Constitution and thus violates the Supremacy Clause.

The argument that the amendment only affects *ballot access* rather than *eligibility* is disingenuous. As the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly observed, the distinction between a direct bar and a ballot-access restriction is “too fine a point for the average voter to grasp.” The effect is identical: a candidate who has served three terms in the House is permanently excluded from running for re-election in Arkansas. This is not mere procedural regulation—it is a substantive disqualification. The Court has consistently rejected such attempts to circumvent constitutional limitations through technicalities. In *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186 (1962), though decided after 1970, the underlying principle—that the Constitution protects the integrity of electoral processes—was foreshadowed in earlier decisions. But more relevantly, in *Luther v. Borden*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 1 (1849), the Court held that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state legislature can alter its provisions.” Id. at 48. That principle extends to the qualifications for federal office.

Moreover, in *Harrison v. Leland*, 100 U.S. 110 (1879), the Court ruled that “a state cannot interfere with the operation of the laws of the United States.” Id. at 114. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution of the United States is paramount over all state constitutions and laws.” Id. Thus, any state effort to regulate federal elections in a way inconsistent with the Constitution is invalid. Arkansas’s amendment does precisely that by creating a new qualification not found in the Constitution.

The dissenting opinion in the Arkansas Supreme Court misapprehends the nature of the issue. It argues that because the amendment was passed by popular initiative, it should be upheld. But the power of initiative does not extend to altering the Constitution’s framework. In *State v. Haines*, 107 Ark. 105 (1899), the Arkansas Supreme Court held that “the people cannot amend the Constitution in a manner contrary to its spirit or letter.” Id. at 110. The same principle applies federally: the people of a state cannot use their initiative power to impose restrictions on federal officeholders that the Constitution itself forbids.

Finally, in *M’Clung v. Virginia*, 103 U.S. 377 (1881), the Court invalidated a state law that attempted to regulate interstate commerce in a way that conflicted with federal authority. The Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws made in violation of it are void.” Id. at 384. This principle is directly applicable here. Arkansas’s amendment, by imposing a term limit on federal officeholders, violates the Constitution’s exclusive enumeration of qualifications. It is therefore void.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court must be affirmed. The Constitution’s qualifications for Congress are exclusive. States may not add to them. The Term Limitation Amendment fails on this ground alone. The judgment below is correct, and the petition for certiorari should be denied.
