# ARGUMENT

## I. THE STATUTE REQUIRES AN ASSESSMENT AGAINST EACH INDIVIDUAL TAXPAYER, AND A PARTNER IS A SEPARATE "TAXPAYER" FROM THE PARTNERSHIP

The Internal Revenue Code's ten-year extended limitations period applies only to collection efforts against the taxpayer who was timely assessed. Section 6502(a) provides that "[w]here the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto, such tax may be collected by levy or by a proceeding in court" for ten years after assessment. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). This extension necessarily runs to the identity of the assessed taxpayer, because assessment itself is defined as "recording the liability of the taxpayer in the office of the Secretary." 26 U.S.C. § 6203 (emphasis added). The implementing regulation confirms that a valid assessment "shall provide identification of the taxpayer." 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

Congress defined "taxpayer" broadly enough to include both partnerships and individuals, but as separate and distinct persons. A "taxpayer" is "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and "person" is defined to include "an individual" as well as "a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). An individual partner is thus a taxpayer wholly distinct from the partnership itself; each has "its, his, or her own taxpayer identification number." Because the IRS assessed only the Partnership, and never separately assessed the Gallettis or the Briguglios, the ten-year extension of § 6502(a) never attached to them. The three-year limitations period of § 6501(a) therefore governs, and it expired long before the IRS filed its proofs of claim in bankruptcy.

## II. NINTH CIRCUIT PRECEDENT CONFIRMS THAT AN ASSESSMENT AGAINST AN ENTITY DOES NOT SUBSTITUTE FOR AN ASSESSMENT AGAINST, OR A JUDGMENT AGAINST, THE INDIVIDUAL WHOSE ASSETS THE GOVERNMENT SEEKS TO REACH

This conclusion is confirmed by this Court's decisions in Young v. Riddell and United States v. Coson. In Young, the district court recognized that even though a partner may be substantively liable under state law for a partnership's tax debt, the government still needed "to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action" through either an individual assessment or a judgment. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff'd, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). This Court's affirmance did not disturb that reasoning; it merely held that a partner who had already paid could not obtain a refund on the theory that the assessment failed to name him personally. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960). Young thus stands only for the narrow proposition that liability for the underlying debt is not defeated by the absence of an individual assessment — not that the government may collect from an unassessed individual without ever obtaining an assessment or judgment against him.

United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), reinforces this reading. There, the district court explained that a lien "does not exist against a particular individual's property pursuant to §[§] 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203." United States v. Coson, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This Court's affirmance rested on a different, but related, procedural ground, confirming that the government's collection mechanisms against an individual's specific assets require some individualized process — assessment or judgment — directed at that individual, not merely at an entity of which he happens to be a member.

## III. CALIFORNIA PARTNERSHIP LAW DOES NOT SUPPLY AN INDEPENDENT BASIS FOR COLLECTION ABSENT A JUDGMENT AGAINST THE INDIVIDUAL PARTNER

The IRS's alternative theory — that state law joint-and-several liability permits it to proceed directly against the partners on the Partnership's assessment — fares no better. California law expressly provides that "[a] judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner," and that "[a] judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner's assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner." Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). A creditor's right to reach a partner's individual assets for a partnership debt is thus contingent on first obtaining a judgment against that partner. See 9 B.E. Witkin, Summary of California Law § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001).

The Bankruptcy Code permits disallowance of any claim "unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor, under ... applicable law." 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1); see also 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). Because the IRS never obtained a judgment against Debtors — and because the three-year period in which it might have sought one has long since expired, 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) — its claim is unenforceable under the very state law on which it relies.

## IV. UNITED STATES v. WRIGHT DOES NOT SUPPORT THE GOVERNMENT'S POSITION

The government's reliance on United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In Wright, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners, extending the six-year limitations period as to each. United States v. Wright, 868 F. Supp. 1070, 1071 & n.1 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The only question was whether tolling during the partnership's bankruptcy extended the period applicable to the partners as well. Wright, 57 F.3d at 562–63. The Seventh Circuit never confronted, and had no occasion to address, whether an assessment against a partnership alone suffices to extend the limitations period against unassessed individual partners. Wright is accordingly inapposite.

## CONCLUSION

Because the IRS assessed only the Partnership, and never timely assessed or obtained a judgment against Debtors individually, the ordinary three-year limitations period governs, and it has expired. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
