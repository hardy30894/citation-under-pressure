**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal "arising under" jurisdiction over patent issues is narrow and does not reach a state malpractice claim merely because it requires resolution of a hypothetical patent question.**

Congress gave federal district courts jurisdiction over cases "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), but this Court has long cautioned against reading "arising under" so broadly that any state-created claim touching a federal issue is swept into an exclusive federal forum. As Justice Holmes explained, "a suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action." *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Minton's suit against his former lawyers is a Texas tort—legal malpractice—created entirely by Texas law. That the case-within-a-case requires the state court to ask what would have happened had counsel raised the experimental-use exception does not transform the claim into one "arising under" federal patent law.

This Court's well-pleaded-complaint doctrine confirms the point. In *Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908), the Court held that federal jurisdiction depends on the plaintiff's own statement of his claim, and that a claim does not arise under federal law simply because a federal defense or federal issue may be anticipated in the complaint. Minton's petition alleges negligence and the elements of Texas malpractice law; it states no patent-law cause of action. Even an embedded federal issue must genuinely and substantially implicate the meaning or application of federal patent law before it can support jurisdiction otherwise reserved to the States. *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569–70 (1912). The experimental-use question here is a fact-bound, backward-looking inquiry confined to two former litigants; it does not implicate the meaning or scope of the patent statute for anyone beyond the parties to this malpractice action.

This Court has likewise cautioned against opening the arising-under door indiscriminately. In *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court explained that not every dispute in which federal law might play some part qualifies for federal jurisdiction, and that courts must distinguish the substantial federal questions warranting a federal forum from those that are incidental or remote. *Id.* at 117–18. A hypothetical defense never adjudicated by the Patent Office or any patent tribunal is not "substantial" in the sense *Gully* demands; it is, at most, an incidental strand in an ordinary state malpractice case.

**II. Precedent confirms that suits requiring only incidental application of patent principles remain within the States' traditional jurisdiction.**

Long before the modern "arising under" test was refined, this Court held that suits requiring courts to apply patent concepts to resolve contract or accounting disputes do not thereby become federal patent cases. In *Hartell v. Tilghman*, 99 U.S. 547 (1879), the Court held that a suit for an accounting under a patent license agreement was not a case arising under the patent laws, because the plaintiff's right to relief rested on the contract between the parties rather than on the patent grant itself. *Dale Tile Manufacturing Co. v. Hyatt*, 125 U.S. 46 (1888), reaffirmed that principle, holding that a dispute over royalties owed under a patent license sounded in contract and did not require exclusive federal jurisdiction merely because a patent underlay the parties' agreement. And *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255 (1897), likewise confirmed that a suit to enforce contractual obligations tied to a patent license does not require exclusive federal jurisdiction merely because resolving it may turn on patent-law questions.

The same logic controls here. Minton's right to relief flows entirely from Texas's law of professional negligence; the patent question is, at most, background necessary to prove or disprove causation—just as license and royalty terms were background to the contract claims in *Hartell*, *Dale Tile*, and *Pratt*.

**III. Exercising federal jurisdiction here would disturb, rather than preserve, the balance between federal and state judicial responsibilities.**

Embedded-issue jurisdiction is appropriate only where a federal forum may entertain the question without disturbing the balance Congress has struck between state and federal courts. That balance tips toward federal jurisdiction only in the rare case—like *Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co.*, 255 U.S. 180 (1921)—where the federal question carries broad, prospective importance, there the constitutionality of a federal bond program affecting countless future transactions. Cf. *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 671–73 (1950) (jurisdiction is not enlarged merely because resolution may turn on an antecedent federal question). No comparable stakes exist here. The Texas court's resolution of Minton's hypothetical defense binds no one but the parties, will not affect the Patent and Trademark Office or other patentees, and will not disturb the uniform administration of the patent laws. Texas courts are fully capable of applying settled patent doctrine to decide this backward-looking malpractice claim, consistent with the States' traditional authority over the practice of law within their borders.

For these reasons, the Texas courts possessed—and should have exercised—jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim.
