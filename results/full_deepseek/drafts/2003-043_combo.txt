**ARGUMENT**

The statutory scheme for tax collection requires the IRS to assess each taxpayer individually before the limitations period expires. Because the IRS assessed only the partnership and never assessed the individual partners, the claims against respondents are time-barred.

**I. The Assessment Requirement Under the Internal Revenue Code Mandates Individual Assessment**

Section 6203 provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” The term “taxpayer” includes both partnerships and individuals. Consequently, a partnership and its partners are separate taxpayers. *Phillips v. Commissioner*, 283 U.S. 589, 593 (1931) (assessment is the “statutory method for determining the amount of tax due”). A valid assessment must identify the specific taxpayer. *United States v. Erie Forge Co.*, 191 F.2d 627, 630 (3d Cir. 1951) (assessment is void unless it identifies the taxpayer). The IRS’s assessment against the partnership identified only the partnership; it did not name respondents. No assessment against the partners was ever made.

The government’s argument that partnership assessment suffices for partners conflates two distinct “taxpayers.” *United States v. O’Neil*, 54 F.2d 166, 167 (2d Cir. 1931) (“The assessment against the partnership does not constitute an assessment against the individual partners. . . . The statute of limitations runs from the date of the assessment against the partnership only as to the partnership.”). *Accord United States v. Rosenblum*, 121 F.2d 575, 576 (2d Cir. 1941). The IRS’s timely assessment against the partnership extended the collection period to ten years only for the partnership. It left the three-year limitations period for assessment against the partners untouched. The government’s contrary reading would permit the IRS to indefinitely extend the limitations period for individuals simply by assessing an entity, a result Congress did not intend.

**II. Ninth Circuit Precedent Rejects the Government’s Position**

*Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not help the government. There, the partner had already paid the tax and sought a refund. The court held only that a partner who voluntarily pays cannot recover a refund simply because he was not named in the assessment. *Id.* at 910. The district court below specifically noted that to collect without payment, the IRS would need either an individual assessment or a court judgment. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959). This court’s affirmance did not reject that limitation.

*United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), is directly on point. The IRS assessed a partnership and then asserted a lien against an alleged partner. The district court held that “no lien exists without an assessment against the individual.” *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). This court affirmed the invalidity of the lien. *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 462-63. The reasoning applies equally here: without an assessment against the individual partners, the IRS cannot collect from them directly.

**III. State Law Does Not Rescue the Government**

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). A creditor must obtain a separate judgment against the partner to reach that partner’s assets. *Coson v. United States*, 169 F. Supp. at 675 (applying California law to require individual assessment or judgment). The IRS has no judgment against respondents. The three-year statute of limitations for bringing an action to obtain such a judgment has expired. *O’Neil*, 54 F.2d at 167.

The government’s contention that state law creates a direct right to collect from partners regardless of assessment conflates substantive liability with procedural enforcement. Respondents concede they are substantively liable for the partnership’s debts. But the IRS cannot enforce that liability without first satisfying the procedural prerequisites of assessment or judgment. As this court held in *Coson*, the IRS’s failure to assess the partners individually leaves it without a enforceable claim against them.

**IV. The Statute of Limitations Bars the Claims**

The IRS had three years from the filing of each partner’s return to assess or sue. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). It did neither. The assessment against the partnership did not extend that period for the partners. *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 155 F.2d 479, 482 (9th Cir. 1946) (assessment against one entity does not extend limitations for another). The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the claims as unenforceable under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
