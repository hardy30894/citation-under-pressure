**ARGUMENT**

**I. A credible showing of actual innocence is itself a sufficient equitable basis to excuse AEDPA’s limitations bar.**

Equity has long refused to allow procedural rules to defeat a meritorious claim where strict enforcement would produce an unjust result. Courts of equity act to prevent “unconscionable” outcomes and are not confined to wooden formalism. *Holladay v. Daily*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 606 (1813); *Boyle v. Zacharie*, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 348 (1832). The office of equity is to provide relief where legal forms would otherwise work a manifest wrong. *Russell v. Southard*, 53 U.S. (12 How.) 139 (1851); *Hendrickson v. Hinckley*, 58 U.S. (17 How.) 443 (1855).

That principle is especially forceful in habeas corpus, “the great writ” protecting liberty from unlawful restraint. *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75 (1807); *Ex parte Yerger*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 85 (1869). The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that habeas is not a narrow technical remedy but one designed to reach unlawful detention. *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193 (1830); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874). Where a petitioner makes a credible showing that he is actually innocent of the crime of conviction, equity cannot sensibly require continued adherence to a filing bar that would prevent any merits review.

This Court has already held that actual innocence may equitably toll AEDPA’s limitations period. That rule is consistent with longstanding equitable doctrine: a litigant invoking equity must ordinarily show diligence, but equity also recognizes exceptions where a rigid diligence requirement would itself produce injustice. *Piatt v. Vattier*, 34 U.S. (9 Pet.) 405 (1835); *Badger v. Badger*, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 87 (1864). The point of tolling is to prevent an inequitable forfeiture, and forfeiture is disfavored where the underlying claim is that the petitioner did not commit the offense at all. *U.S. Bank of Pennsylvania v. Bank of Georgia*, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 333 (1825).

**II. Pace v. DiGuglielmo does not add a diligence prerequisite to an actual-innocence tolling claim.**

The Warden relies on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), but that case does not govern here. *Pace* addressed whether a petitioner who sought statutory tolling could benefit when his state petition was not “properly filed.” Id. at 417. It did not involve a freestanding actual-innocence gateway or hold that innocence-based tolling is unavailable absent reasonable diligence. A rule developed to define statutory tolling cannot be transposed mechanically to extinguish equitable relief in the extraordinary innocence context. See *Bank of United States v. Dandridge*, 25 U.S. (12 Wheat.) 64 (1827); *Hardin v. Boyd*, 113 U.S. 756 (1885).

Nor does general equity doctrine compel the extra diligence requirement the Warden seeks. Equity often considers delay, but delay is not an absolute bar when enforcement would perpetrate injustice. *Mackall v. Casilear*, 137 U.S. 556 (1890); *Hammond v. Hopkins*, 143 U.S. 224 (1892). The touchstone is whether the equities favor relief, not whether a petitioner has demonstrated perfect promptness in every respect. *Mackall*, 137 U.S. at 566; *Pomeroy v. Fullerton*, 117 U.S. 222 (1886). In an actual-innocence case, the balance decisively favors review because the alternative is to allow a potentially innocent person to remain imprisoned without any merits adjudication.

**III. Perkins’s new evidence warrants equitable tolling and merits review.**

Perkins submitted affidavits that, taken together, tend to show that Jones—not Perkins—confessed to the killing and possessed bloody clothing consistent with the murder. That is precisely the type of evidence that can justify equitable relief where a limitations bar would otherwise foreclose review. Courts have long recognized that newly discovered evidence may warrant relief when it reveals that the judgment rests on a grave factual mistake. *Baker v. Lorillard*, 76 U.S. (9 Wall.) 528 (1869); *Moffat v. United States*, 112 U.S. 24 (1884). The question is not whether the proof is conclusive at the tolling stage, but whether it is sufficiently credible to justify opening the courthouse door to a merits examination.

Because Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence, the limitations period should be equitably tolled without imposing an additional diligence requirement. To hold otherwise would subordinate innocence to procedural finality in a manner equity has never required. *Ex parte Yerger*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 85 (1869); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874). This Court should reverse and remand for consideration of Perkins’s habeas petition on the merits.
