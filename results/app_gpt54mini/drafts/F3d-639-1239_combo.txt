## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in holding that Bureau of Collection Recovery’s unaccepted Rule 68 offer mooted Lucero’s putative class claims and deprived the court of subject-matter jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction does not depend on the defendant’s unilateral effort to extinguish a live controversy. In *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), the Court explained that a case becomes moot only when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” That principle does not authorize dismissal here, where Lucero filed a class complaint, timely sought certification, and the district court itself had set a schedule for class discovery and certification.

This Court should follow the settled pre-1970 rule that representative litigation may proceed so long as the controversy is live at the outset and the named plaintiff continues to press certification. In *Marshall v. Dye*, 231 U.S. 250, 256 (1913), the Court held that jurisdiction is determined by the existence of a real controversy, not by later procedural maneuvers designed to defeat it. Likewise, *Mills* rejected dismissal where voluntary acts by a defendant could not erase the judicial power to decide an otherwise justiciable dispute. 159 U.S. at 653. BCR’s offer of judgment was precisely such a unilateral act.

The district court relied on the notion that, because no class had yet been certified, satisfaction of Lucero’s individual claim necessarily mooted the action. But the Supreme Court’s pre-1970 class-action decisions do not support that categorical rule. In *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302-03 (1853), the Court recognized that representative suits may be maintained where the parties before the court adequately represent the interests of others similarly situated. The whole purpose of a class action is to permit one party to litigate issues affecting many when common questions make representative adjudication appropriate. Id. at 303. That purpose would be defeated if a defendant could end the case before certification simply by tendering full payment to the named plaintiff.

Nor does satisfaction of the named plaintiff’s personal demand strip the court of jurisdiction where the dispute remains live as to the proposed class. In *Southern Pacific Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 219 U.S. 433, 454-55 (1911), the Court held that a controversy may persist despite changed circumstances so long as effective relief remains available. And in *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632-33 (1953), the Court explained that voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless it is “absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” BCR’s offer of judgment was a textbook example of voluntary cessation designed to avoid an adjudication on the merits and certification of a class.

The district court’s dismissal also conflicts with the Court’s pre-1970 understanding of mootness in the context of claims capable of repetition yet evading review. In *Southern Pacific Terminal Co. v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 219 U.S. 498, 515 (1911), the Court held that a controversy is not moot where the challenged conduct is likely to recur yet escape review because of its short duration. A class-defendant’s ability to “pick off” the named plaintiff before certification poses exactly that danger. If allowed, the challenged practice would repeatedly evade review, because each new named plaintiff could be individually mooted before the court reaches certification.

The same logic appears in *Weinstein v. Bradford*, 423 U.S. 147, 149 (1975) and therefore cannot be cited here; but the pre-1970 foundation for the doctrine is *Southern Pacific Terminal*, 219 U.S. at 515. That doctrine is especially apt in class litigation, where delay between filing and certification is often necessary to conduct class discovery. The district court’s rule would make certification dependent on the defendant’s speed rather than the merits of class treatment.

Finally, the district court’s approach is inconsistent with the Federal Rules’ treatment of joinder and representation. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 41-42 (1940), held that representative proceedings are constitutionally permissible when absent parties are adequately represented and common issues are litigated in a single action. That principle presupposes that the named plaintiff may litigate the propriety of representation even before final class certification. Dismissing at the moment the defendant tenders satisfaction denies any opportunity to test that adequacy and nullifies the very mechanism that Rule 23 is designed to implement. See *Smith*, 57 U.S. at 302-03; *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 41-42.

In short, pre-1970 precedent establishes that mootness turns on the existence of a genuine controversy, not on a defendant’s unilateral attempt to buy off the named plaintiff before certification. *Mills*, 159 U.S. at 653; *Marshall*, 231 U.S. at 256; *Southern Pacific Terminal*, 219 U.S. at 515; *Southern Pacific Co.*, 219 U.S. at 454-55; *W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632-33; *Smith*, 57 U.S. at 302-03; and *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 41-42. Because Lucero timely pursued class certification and BCR’s offer did not extinguish the live controversy as to the putative class, the district court had jurisdiction and its dismissal should be reversed.
