# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of the Immigration and Nationality Act Compel the Conclusion That a Grant of Temporary Protected Status Satisfies § 1255(a)'s Admission Requirement

Congress provided that once TPS is granted, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This is not language of mere sufferance; it is a legislative command that courts must take at face value. Where Congress enacts a deeming provision, that provision must be given the same legal effect that any other operative statutory text receives. A statute "ought, upon the whole, to be so construed, that, if it can be prevented, no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant." *Market Co. v. Hoffman*, 101 U.S. 112, 115 (1879). To read § 1254a(f)(4) as conferring "lawful status" while leaving § 1255(a)'s independent "admission" requirement wholly untouched empties the deeming clause of practical consequence for the very population—aliens who entered without inspection—for whom the clause matters most. Congress does not enact "considered as" fictions that operate on no one.

This Court has long recognized that a statute's letter must yield to its evident design when a literal construction produces a result Congress could not have intended. "[A] thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). Congress designed the TPS program to permit aliens fleeing catastrophe to remain, work, and build lives in the United States under color of law. Reading § 1255(a)'s admission clause to nullify § 1254a(f)(4)'s deeming language for the class of TPS holders who entered unlawfully—precisely the aliens Congress most needed to protect from the destabilizing effect of undocumented status—cannot be reconciled with "the whole legislation" or "the circumstances surrounding its enactment." *Id.* Statutes must be construed, where fairly possible, "as a symmetrical and coherent regulatory scheme," and a fictional grant of "lawful . . . status" that leaves an alien permanently unable to invoke the ordinary consequences of that status is neither symmetrical nor coherent. *Cf. United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940) (courts must look to the statute "as a whole" and to its purpose when literal text yields an unintended result).

This Court's treatment of analogous legal fictions in the immigration context confirms that Congress may define "admission" through operation of law rather than through the bare physical fact of crossing the border. In *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), this Court treated a resident alien's brief absence and return as if he had never left the country for purposes of his legal status, recognizing that Congress and the courts may treat an alien's legal position as a matter of statutory construction rather than as dictated solely by the physical facts of entry. The same principle applies here: Congress's declaration that a TPS recipient is "considered as being" lawfully present as a nonimmigrant is itself the operative legal event that satisfies § 1255(a).

## II. Any Doubt Concerning the Interaction of §§ 1254a and 1255 Must Be Resolved in Petitioners' Favor

Even if the statutory text left room for the Third Circuit's contrary reading, settled canons governing immigration statutes require resolution of that doubt for the alien. This Court has repeatedly instructed that because "the stakes are considerable for the individual, we will not assume that Congress meant to trench on his freedom beyond that which is required by the narrowest of several possible meanings of the words used." *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). This principle applies with equal force to eligibility for lawful permanent residence, an entitlement Congress created to allow humanitarian migrants a path to permanence. *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637 (1954), applied the identical rule of strict construction against the government where ambiguity existed in provisions bearing on an alien's status. And this Court has confirmed that the Act's family- and status-related provisions are to be read generously in light of Congress's remedial aims, rather than narrowly against the alien. *INS v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214 (1966); *Costello v. INS*, 376 U.S. 120 (1964). These canons do not evaporate merely because the provision at issue concerns eligibility for adjustment rather than deportation; the animating concern—that Congress's protective purposes not be defeated by grudging interpretation—applies with full force to the TPS program, a statute enacted expressly to shelter vulnerable aliens from the disorder of unlawful status during periods of upheaval in their home countries.

## III. The Third Circuit's Contrary Reading Defeats the Remedial Purpose of the TPS Program

Interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) as petitioners urge harmonizes the statute's text with its evident humanitarian purpose, avoids rendering the deeming clause superfluous, and honors this Court's longstanding instruction that ambiguities in the Act be resolved in favor of the alien. The judgment below should be reversed.
