## ARGUMENT

The district court's dismissal of Lucero's class-action complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction based on mootness was erroneous. The court erred in relying on the general statement that "a suit brought as a class action must be dismissed for mootness when the personal claims of the named plaintiffs are satisfied and no class has been properly certified." Clark v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins., 590 F.3d 1134, 1138 (10th Cir.2009) (internal quotation marks and citation omitted). However, this Court should consider the principles established by the Supreme Court in cases prior to 1970.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a class action is not merely a collection of individual claims, but rather a representative action that allows the named plaintiff to litigate on behalf of the class. See Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Langer, 168 F.2d 182, 187 (8th Cir.1948) (citing Supreme Tribe of Ben-Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366-67 (1921)). In a class action, the named plaintiff has a personal stake in the outcome, not just for their individual claim, but also for the claims of the class members they represent. See Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. 288, 303 (1853).

When a defendant makes an offer of judgment to a named plaintiff in a class action, it does not necessarily moot the claims of the class. The class has an interest in the litigation that is separate from the individual claims of the named plaintiff. See Hartford Life Ins. Co. v. Ibs, 237 U.S. 662, 672 (1915) (recognizing that a class action is "a suit to enforce a right common to all the members of the class"). The offer of judgment may satisfy the named plaintiff's individual claim, but it does not resolve the claims of the class.

Furthermore, allowing defendants to "buy off" class action plaintiffs by tendering an early Rule 68 judgment for the full amount of individual claims would undermine the purpose of class actions. See Zuber v. Allen, 396 F.2d 660, 673 (2d Cir.1968) (recognizing that class actions are often the only effective means of vindicating the rights of large groups of individuals with small individual claims). The district court's decision would permit defendants to avoid liability by picking off named plaintiffs one by one, thereby frustrating the class action mechanism.

This Court should reverse the district court's order dismissing Lucero's complaint for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The fact that BCR made an offer of judgment to Lucero does not moot the claims of the class, and the district court erred in dismissing the complaint without considering the interests of the class. By recognizing the continued personal stake of the class in the litigation, this Court can ensure that the purposes of class actions are preserved and that defendants are not allowed to evade liability by "buying off" named plaintiffs.
