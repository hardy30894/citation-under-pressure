# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act Withdraws OSHA's Jurisdiction Wherever Another Federal Agency Has Statutory Authority to Regulate Occupational Safety and Health, and the Coast Guard Possesses and Exercises Such Authority Over Vessels Like Rig 52.

Congress did not intend OSHA to blanket every American workplace without regard to other federal regulatory schemes. Section 4(b)(1) provides that the Act "shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Congress vested exactly that kind of authority in the Coast Guard. Under 14 U.S.C. § 2, the Coast Guard "shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States covering all matters not specifically delegated by law to some other executive department." This is not a narrow or contingent grant; it is a comprehensive charge to safeguard life and property on the navigable waters of the United States, a charge the Coast Guard has fulfilled with respect to vessels of every stripe—inspected and uninspected alike. Congress specifically authorized the Coast Guard to issue safety regulations for uninspected vessels covering fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors and backfire traps, ventilation of engine and fuel-tank compartments, and emergency alerting and locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. The Coast Guard has exercised that authority, promulgating rules on lifesaving equipment, fire-extinguishing equipment, backfire flame control, ventilation, and emergency instructions applicable to uninspected vessels. 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq.; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. And it is undisputed that the Coast Guard exercised its statutory authority to investigate the very casualty at issue here pursuant to 46 U.S.C. § 6301. Section 4(b)(1) therefore withdraws OSHA's authority over Rig 52 regardless of whether Rig 52 happened to fall within the subcategory of vessels subject to the Coast Guard's most exacting inspection regime.

## II. Fifth Circuit Precedent Correctly Holds That the Coast Guard's Regulatory Authority Over Seamen's Working Conditions Is Exclusive and Draws No Distinction Between Inspected and Uninspected Vessels.

This Court need not write on a blank slate. In Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), the Fifth Circuit confronted a seaman injured aboard a drilling barge—the same species of vessel at issue here—who sought to introduce OSHA regulations to establish the standard of care. The court held that "OSHA regulations... do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," id. at 1122, because the Coast Guard is the agency "with statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen," and that authority "included standards governing the safety and health of persons working on vessels." Id. Nothing in Clary limited its holding to inspected vessels; the court announced a categorical rule keyed to the status of the worker as a seaman and the vessel as a vessel in navigation, not to any Coast Guard inspection classification.

The Fifth Circuit reaffirmed and elaborated this rule in Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), holding flatly that "[i]t is the law of this circuit that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation," id. at 826, and that "[n]othing in OSHA shall apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels." Id. at 827. The court explained that the Coast Guard's "comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen's working conditions" creates "an industry-wide exemption [from OSHA] for seamen serving on vessels operating on navigable waters." Id. at 826. Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray, 361 F.2d 432 (5th Cir. 1966), and Colomb v. Texaco, Inc., 736 F.2d 218 (5th Cir. 1984), confirm that workers of Mallard's kind—crew members serving aboard a drilling barge navigating territorial waters—are seamen for purposes of this exemption. Rig 52 is a drilling barge; its crew are seamen; the casualty arose from working conditions aboard a vessel in navigation. Clary and Donovan control, and they draw no line between inspected and uninspected hulls.

## III. Sound Statutory Policy Confirms That a Single, Uniform Regulator—Not a Patchwork Dependent on Inspection Status—Must Govern the Maritime Workplace.

The Fifth Circuit's rule also vindicates the structural purpose of § 4(b)(1). As Donovan recognized, "[s]ection 4(b)(1) evidences a general Congressional intent to forbid overlapping regulation of working conditions in any given industry by multiple federal agencies," because "[s]uch redundant control programs offer little except confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise." 720 F.2d at 827. Dual regulation keyed to a vessel's inspection status would produce exactly the incoherence Congress sought to avoid, risking the "anomaly... of steaming in and out of OSHA coverage" as a vessel crosses in and out of inspected status, or as its cargo or mission changes. Id. at 829. Congress intended "a single, uniform set of rules" to "govern the maritime workplace," and because OSHA's "geographic limitations" preclude it from filling that role at sea, "it must be" the Coast Guard's rules that govern. Id. A rule turning OSHA jurisdiction on and off according to inspection classification would recreate precisely the confusion the exclusivity principle was designed to eliminate. The judgment below should be affirmed.
