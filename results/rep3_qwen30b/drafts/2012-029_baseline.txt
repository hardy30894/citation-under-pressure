**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between federal patent policy and the constitutional balance of state and federal judicial authority. The Supreme Court of Texas correctly concluded that federal courts do *not* possess exclusive jurisdiction over such claims, and that the state court properly exercised subject-matter jurisdiction. The Court of Appeals erred in deferring to the Federal Circuit’s expansive interpretation of § 1338(a), which disregards the Supreme Court’s clear directive in *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363, 162 L.Ed.2d 257 (2005), that federal jurisdiction must be exercised only when a state-law claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” The Court of Appeals’ decision, and the Federal Circuit’s precedents upon which it relied, fail this test on all four prongs.

**I. The Patent Issue Is Not “Necessarily” Raised in Minton’s Malpractice Claim**

The first prong of the *Grable* test requires that resolution of the federal issue be “necessary” to the state-law claim. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies to the TEXCEN lease—is not necessary to Minton’s malpractice claim. Minton’s claim rests on the negligence of his attorneys in failing to raise a defense that was not legally viable at the time. As the trial court correctly found, there was no evidence that the lease was for experimental purposes. The lease was commercial in nature: it provided for a monthly fee based on Stark’s gross revenues, included performance warranties, and was negotiated with a major securities firm for use in its business operations. The fact that Minton may have *believed* the software was experimental is irrelevant; the law requires an objective determination of the *purpose* of the sale. See *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed.Cir.2005) (the exception applies only where the “primary purpose” of the sale was experimental).

Because the experimental use exception was not a viable defense under the facts known at the time, Minton cannot establish that the failure to raise it caused his loss. His malpractice claim depends not on the resolution of a federal patent issue, but on the factual determination of whether his attorneys breached their duty of care under Texas law. That determination is governed entirely by state law and does not require a federal court to interpret or apply patent law. As the Supreme Court held in *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117, 57 S.Ct. 96, 81 L.Ed. 70 (1936), a claim does not “arise under” federal law merely because it “touches upon” a federal question. The patent issue here is merely *incidental*, not *necessary*.

**II. The Patent Issue Is Not “Actually Disputed” or “Substantial”**

The second and third prongs of *Grable* require that the federal issue be “actually disputed” and “substantial.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. Here, the issue was not actually disputed in the state court. Minton’s own evidence showed that the lease was commercial, and his new counsel did not assert the experimental use exception until after the federal court had already ruled on the on-sale bar. The Federal Circuit’s decision in *Minton v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373 (Fed.Cir.2003), affirmed the district court’s denial of reconsideration precisely because the exception was not timely raised. The issue was not genuinely contested in the underlying litigation, and the state court was not required to resolve it.

Moreover, the issue is not substantial. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that “a federal issue is not substantial merely because it is federal.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. The experimental use exception is a narrow, fact-specific doctrine that does not implicate the uniformity of patent law. As the Fifth Circuit recognized in *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 340 (5th Cir.2008), “the resolution of a patent law issue in a malpractice case does not threaten the uniformity of patent law enforcement.” The same is true here. Even if a federal court were to rule on the experimental use exception, it would not affect the validity of the patent or the scope of its enforcement. The patent was already invalidated under the on-sale bar, and the issue of whether the exception could have saved it is a matter of historical fact, not a question of national patent policy.

**III. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities**

Finally, federal jurisdiction would disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363. State courts are well-equipped to adjudicate legal malpractice claims, which are inherently state-law matters governed by state standards of professional conduct, causation, and damages. The Federal Circuit’s approach—expanding federal jurisdiction over state malpractice claims based on a single federal issue—threatens to “swallow” state courts whole. As the Supreme Court warned in *Grable*, “the federal courts are not to be used as a general repository for state-law claims that happen to involve federal issues.” 545 U.S. at 313, 125 S.Ct. 2363.
