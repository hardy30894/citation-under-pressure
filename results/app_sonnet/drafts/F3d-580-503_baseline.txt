**ARGUMENT**

**The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Denying Winbush a Continuance and a Defense Fingerprint Expert, Thereby Depriving Him of His Sixth Amendment Right to Present a Complete Defense.**

A criminal defendant has a fundamental right under the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments "to a fair opportunity to defend against the State's accusations." *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973). That right includes the ability to present evidence that undermines the government's theory of the case. *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986) (recognizing the "fundamental" right to present a defense). Here, the district court's eleventh-hour denial of Winbush's motions for a fingerprint expert and a continuance stripped him of the only avenue by which he could challenge the government's forensic narrative linking him to the gun and crack found blocks away from his arrest.

**A. The denial of the continuance was an abuse of discretion.**

A trial court's decision to deny a continuance is reviewed for abuse of discretion, but that discretion is not unlimited. *Ungar v. Sarafite*, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964) (explaining that the answer to whether a continuance was properly denied "is not to be found in mere` numbers of days'" but must be judged "in the light of the circumstances presented"). Even though "not every denial of a request for more time . . . violates due process," *Ungar*, 376 U.S. at 589, a defendant is nonetheless entitled to relief where the denial results in "actual prejudice" to his defense. *Morris v. Slappy*, 461 U.S. 1, 11–12 (1983).

The Seventh Circuit has instructed that courts evaluating a continuance denial must weigh factors including the extent of the requested delay, whether other continuances were sought, the inconvenience to witnesses and the court, whether the delay was purposeful, and the likelihood that a continuance would have served the defendant's needs. *United States v. Miller*, 327 F.3d 598, 601 (7th Cir. 2003). Winbush's request was not purposeful gamesmanship; it arose directly from the government's own late notice, filed October 3, 2007, less than six weeks before trial. Winbush's counsel moved promptly thereafter for a fingerprint expert of his own once it became apparent that the government intended to use its expert and its Deputy Commander to explain away the absence of any fingerprints on the gun and the leather jacket. The magistrate judge's characterization of the request as "woefully late" ignored that the underlying need for a defense expert was created by the government's own late-noticed strategy of using two witnesses—Shay and Reilly—to neutralize the most obviously exculpatory fact in the case: that Winbush's fingerprints were on none of the physical evidence tying him to the firearm and crack.

**B. The denial of a defense fingerprint expert deprived Winbush of the means to rebut the government's forensic theory.**

Although the parties stipulated to the substance of Shay's negative fingerprint findings, that stipulation did not address the meaning of those findings—precisely the subject on which the government intended to call Deputy Commander Reilly, an expert whose sole purpose was to explain that the absence of fingerprints was not surprising or exculpatory given "the difficulties in obtaining latent fingerprints from different materials and surfaces." Winbush was left with no expert of his own to counter that narrative, even though the identity of whoever dropped the jacket, the gun, and the Tennessee and Kentucky crack was the central contested issue in this case. No witness placed the gun in Winbush's jacket pocket at the time he possessed it, and no witness saw him drop the Tennessee crack. The government was permitted, through its own two experts, to explain away the one piece of forensic evidence that might have raised reasonable doubt, while Winbush was barred from offering any expert testimony to bolster the inference that his own defense sought to draw from that same absence of prints.

This asymmetry is precisely the kind of imbalance that offends the principle, recognized in *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 76 (1985), that meaningful access to justice requires that indigent defendants have access to the raw materials—including expert assistance—integral to building an effective defense when such assistance is necessary to a fair adversarial process. While *Ake* arose in a different procedural posture, its animating concern—that a defendant not be forced to rebut the government's expert theory unarmed—applies with full force here. The government fielded two witnesses on fingerprint evidence; Winbush was permitted none. That imbalance was not the product of Winbush's own delay; it followed directly from the government's own late disclosure of its trial strategy.

**C. The error was not harmless.**

Because identity was hotly contested and the physical evidence tying Winbush to the firearm and the additional crack was entirely circumstantial, the erroneous denial of expert assistance cannot be deemed harmless. *Chambers*, 410 U.S. at 302 (reversing where exclusion of evidence "denied [defendant] a trial in accord with traditional and fundamental standards of due process"). The jury never heard any defense counter to the government's explanation for the absent fingerprints, an explanation that, if left unrebutted, effectively converted potentially exculpatory evidence into a nonissue.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Winbush respectfully requests that this Court vacate his convictions and remand for a new trial at which he is afforded the expert assistance and continuance necessary to present a complete defense.
