The respondent, Ohio's warden, respectfully submits that a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The Sixth Circuit's decision to the contrary is inconsistent with this Court's precedents.

In Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), this Court held that a federal habeas court may not review a state prisoner's claim if the state court has declined to address the claim due to a procedural default, unless the prisoner can demonstrate cause and prejudice. Id. at 750. The Court further explained that attorney error short of ineffective assistance of counsel does not constitute cause. Id. at 752-53. It follows that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim.

The Court's decision in Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), is not to the contrary. In Carrier, the Court held that ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause to excuse a procedural default, but it did not address whether a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim can serve as cause. Id. at 488. The Court's subsequent decision in Coleman makes clear that a procedurally defaulted claim cannot serve as cause.

The Sixth Circuit's decision in this case is also inconsistent with the principles of procedural default. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse the procedural default of another claim would undermine the state court's procedural rules and create an exception that would swallow the rule.

The Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit's decision and hold that a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. This holding is consistent with the Court's precedents and the principles of procedural default.

In Harris v. Reed, 489 U.S. 255 (1989), the Court held that a federal habeas court will presume that a state court's decision rested on the same grounds as its opinion, unless the state court's opinion clearly indicates that it relied on a procedural bar. Id. at 261-62. In this case, the Ohio courts clearly invoked a procedural bar when they dismissed Carpenter's application to reopen his direct appeal as untimely. Therefore, the federal habeas court should have honored that procedural bar and not considered Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.

The Court's decision in O'Sullivan v. Boerckel, 526 U.S. 838 (1999), is also relevant. In Boerckel, the Court held that a state prisoner must exhaust all available state remedies before seeking federal habeas relief. Id. at 845. Carpenter's failure to raise his ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim on direct appeal or in a timely application to reopen his direct appeal means that he has not exhausted his state remedies, and his claim is procedurally defaulted.

In conclusion, the respondent respectfully submits that the Sixth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the Court should hold that a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim.
