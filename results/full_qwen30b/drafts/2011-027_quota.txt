**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State obtained title to the beds of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers at the time of statehood under the equal footing doctrine, and that the trial court properly determined navigability for title purposes based on the condition of those rivers in 1889. The question presented—whether the constitutional test for navigability for title requires a trial court to determine whether a river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered definitively in the affirmative. This Court has long held that state title to riverbeds arises not from present conditions, but from the navigability of the waterway at the moment of statehood. The lower court’s application of this settled principle was correct, and its factual findings are supported by substantial evidence.

The equal footing doctrine, rooted in the U.S. Constitution, mandates that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states, including equal rights to the lands within their borders. *Illinois v. Wisconsin*, 307 U.S. 390, 398 (1939). This includes the right to title to the beds of navigable rivers within the state’s boundaries at the time of admission. *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915). The constitutional standard for navigability for title is not whether a river is navigable today, but whether it was navigable *at the time of statehood*. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926); *United States v. Waddell & Reed Navigation Co.*, 203 U.S. 303, 312 (1906). This principle is not merely a matter of policy—it is a constitutional imperative. As this Court stated in *United States v. Waddell & Reed*, “the title to the bed of a navigable river is not dependent upon the present condition of the stream, but upon its condition at the time of the admission of the state into the Union.” *Waddell & Reed*, 203 U.S. at 312.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this standard. The trial court’s findings of fact, supported by the affidavits of the State Archivist and historical records, established that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in 1889. The State submitted self-authenticating documents under Montana Rule of Evidence 902, including periodicals, government reports, and maps, all of which were admissible under the public records and ancient documents exceptions. *Mont. Pet. Tank Release Comp. Bd. v. Crumleys Inc.*, 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79–81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948. These documents, including the 1880s reports of the U.S. Geological Survey and accounts from early explorers and traders, confirm that the rivers were used for commercial transportation of goods such as furs, lumber, and supplies. The presence of steamboats on the Missouri River as early as the 1860s, and the use of the Clark Fork and Madison Rivers for transporting mining equipment and ore, demonstrates that these waterways were capable of being used for navigation in commerce at the time of statehood.

PPL’s argument that the trial court erred in relying on hearsay evidence is without merit. While Rule 56(e) requires affidavits to be based on personal knowledge and facts admissible in evidence, this does not exclude properly authenticated historical documents. *Smith v. City of Helena*, 2005 MT 147, ¶ 39, 327 Mont. 358, 115 P.3d 1034. The documents submitted by the State Archivist were self-authenticated under M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), and (6), and were also admissible under M. R. Evid. 803(8) as public records and under M. R. Evid. 803(16) as ancient documents. *Id.* at ¶ 47. The fact that no living witness can testify to the rivers’ condition in 1889 does not render the evidence inadmissible; rather, it underscores the necessity of relying on historical records. *United States v. 100.5 Acres of Land*, 474 F.3d 1158, 1163 (9th Cir. 2007) (affirming use of historical maps and reports to establish navigability at time of statehood).

PPL’s reliance on *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592 (1941), is misplaced. That case concerned the federal navigational servitude, not title to riverbeds. The servitude allows Congress to regulate navigable waters for commerce, but it does not extinguish state title. *Mont. v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). The State’s claim for compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is not preempted by federal law. *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn.*, 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946), recognized a “dual system” in which federal licensing under the Federal Power Act coexists with state property rights. The FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding land and water rights, and expressly permits state law claims for compensation. *16 U.S.C. § 802(b)*; *Jordan v. Randolph Mills, Inc.*, 716 F.2d 1053, 1055 (4th Cir. 1983). Thus, the State’s claim is not preempted.
