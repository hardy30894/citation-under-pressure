ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Striking Appellants’ Experts and in Excluding the Testimony of Drs. Schroeder and Watson.

A district court may impose discovery sanctions only when noncompliance results from “willfulness, bad faith, or any fault.” Societe Internationale Pour Participations Industrielles et Commerciales, S.A. v. Rogers, 357 U.S. 197, 212 (1958). The record shows that Appellants’ experts had not yet formulated opinions when noticed for deposition; the district court’s subsequent order setting a July 30, 1993 deadline did not account for this circumstance. Striking four experts and later excluding two others therefore exceeded the bounds of permissible discretion.

The exclusion of Drs. Schroeder and Watson under Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579 (1993), likewise rested on an incomplete record. Daubert requires only that expert testimony be both relevant and reliable; it does not demand that every supporting document be reviewed before an opinion is formed. Id. at 589-95. The district court’s contrary ruling was error.

II. Summary Judgment Was Improperly Granted.

Once the experts were excluded, the district court granted summary judgment. Summary judgment is appropriate only when “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and … the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c); Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986). Because the exclusion of the experts was erroneous, genuine issues of material fact remained. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). The grant of summary judgment must therefore be reversed.

III. Appellate Jurisdiction Exists.

Although Appellants’ notice of appeal was filed before the March 1, 1995 order disposing of the remaining claims, the notice became effective upon entry of that final order. See FirsTier Mortgage Co. v. Investors Mortgage Ins. Co., 498 U.S. 269, 273-76 (1991). This Court therefore has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291.
