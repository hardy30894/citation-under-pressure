**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s remedial order, which directed the state to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity, is a constitutionally permissible exercise of judicial authority under the Eighth Amendment and is fully consistent with the PLRA’s framework. The Ninth Circuit correctly upheld the order as a necessary and narrowly tailored remedy for a long-standing, systemic violation of prisoners’ constitutional rights. This Court should affirm.

### I. The District Court’s Population Reduction Order Is a Valid Exercise of Judicial Authority Under the Eighth Amendment

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishment,” which includes conditions of confinement that inflict unnecessary suffering due to deliberate indifference. *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976). When a prison system is so overcrowded that it renders medical care, mental health services, and basic safety impossible, the conditions become unconstitutional. *Helling v. McKinney*, 509 U.S. 25, 35 (1993). The district court’s finding that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of constitutional violations was supported by overwhelming evidence, including expert testimony, medical reports, and years of documented failures to provide adequate care. The court did not act in a vacuum; it was responding to a pattern of deliberate indifference that had persisted for decades.

The remedy imposed—reducing the prison population to 137.5% of design capacity—was neither arbitrary nor excessive. It was a carefully calibrated response to a proven constitutional violation. The court did not mandate mass release without regard to public safety or sentencing policy. Instead, it required the state to implement a structured, data-driven reduction plan that prioritized nonviolent offenders and those nearing release. The order was not a command to release all inmates, but a directive to bring the system into compliance with constitutional standards. As this Court has held, “a court may fashion a remedy that requires a state to take affirmative steps to correct a constitutional violation.” *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 111 (1983).

### II. The PLRA Does Not Prohibit the Court’s Population Reduction Order

The PLRA, enacted in 1996, imposes procedural and substantive limits on federal court intervention in prison conditions litigation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626. The Act’s central provision, § 3626(a)(1), states that a court may not issue an injunction that “requires the State to increase spending” unless the court finds that the state has failed to provide constitutionally adequate care and that the remedy is “narrowly drawn” and “least intrusive.” The State argues that the population reduction order violates this provision because it compels the state to spend money on new facilities and release inmates—actions that, it claims, constitute “increased spending.”

This argument misreads the PLRA. The Act does not prohibit courts from ordering population reductions as a remedy for overcrowding. In fact, *Helling v. McKinney*, 509 U.S. 25 (1993), recognized that overcrowding itself can constitute cruel and unusual punishment, and that courts may order remedies to address it. The PLRA does not override this constitutional principle. The Supreme Court has made clear that the PLRA “does not limit the power of federal courts to enforce constitutional rights.” *Jones v. Bock*, 549 U.S. 199, 217 (2007). The PLRA’s “narrowly drawn” and “least intrusive” standards are not absolute barriers to relief; they are procedural guardrails that ensure remedies are proportionate and necessary.

Here, the district court’s order was both narrowly tailored and the least intrusive available remedy. The court considered alternatives, including increased funding and facility construction, but found that these were insufficient to address the crisis. The court concluded that reducing the population was the only viable path to constitutional compliance. The order was not a command to spend money; it was a directive to eliminate a condition that rendered existing spending ineffective. As the Ninth Circuit observed, the order “followed from consent orders and a receivership that had not been contested by the State at the time the Order issued.” *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 558 F.3d 915, 922 (9th Cir. 2009). The State’s failure to appeal the receivership or the underlying consent decrees precludes it from now challenging the remedial order on PLRA grounds.

### III. The Order Is Consistent with the PLRA’s Purpose and Structure

The PLRA was not intended to insulate states from constitutional accountability. Its purpose is to prevent frivolous litigation and ensure that remedies are reasonable and enforceable. *See id.* at 215. It does not create a constitutional shield for states that fail to provide basic human dignity to prisoners. The district court’s order was not a “blank check” for federal overreach; it was a response to a documented, ongoing violation of the Constitution. The court’s order was subject to ongoing judicial oversight, required periodic reporting, and allowed for adjustments based on new evidence. It was not a one-time decree, but a dynamic, evolving remedy.

Moreover, the PLRA explicitly permits courts to order population reductions when necessary. *See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(B)* (authorizing injunctions that “require the State to take action to correct a violation of the rights of prisoners”). The court’s order falls squarely within this provision. It required the state to take action—specifically, to reduce the population—to correct a violation of prisoners’ rights. The PLRA does not prohibit such action; it requires courts to consider it when appropriate.
