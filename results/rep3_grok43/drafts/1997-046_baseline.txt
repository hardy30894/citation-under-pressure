**ARGUMENT**

**I. An inmate possesses no protected life or liberty interest in clemency proceedings.**

Clemency is “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). The Due Process Clause does not create an interest in procedures that “have not traditionally been the business of courts.” Id. Ohio’s clemency process therefore confers no life or liberty interest arising from state law. See id. at 465 (“[A] state-created right to clemency . . . is not a constitutionally cognizable ‘liberty’ interest.”).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that clemency forms “an integral part of the . . . system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant”—misapplies Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). Evitts addressed the first appeal as of right, a stage the Court has repeatedly distinguished from post-conviction clemency. See Ross v. Moffitt, 417 U.S. 600, 610–11 (1974). Dumschat itself rejected the identical claim that commutation decisions constitute “a regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process” requiring due process. 452 U.S. at 469 (Stevens, J., dissenting) (describing the rejected view). Because the Governor’s power remains “almost unfettered,” no protected interest attaches.

**II. The voluntary interview option does not compel self-incrimination.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits only compelled testimony. The challenged regulation grants the inmate the option to request an interview and expressly permits him to “say as much or as little . . . as he wishes.” No provision authorizes the parole board to compel answers. Where participation is voluntary, “the Constitution does not forbid the asking of questions.” Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976).

Any hypothetical future use of statements in a subsequent prosecution is “premature” for federal adjudication. Id. at 324. The inmate “does not contemplate that this action will have any effect on his state convictions,” and no criminal proceeding is pending or certain. The district court therefore correctly declined to enjoin a procedure that imposes no present compulsion.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
