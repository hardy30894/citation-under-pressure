**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE PLAIN LANGUAGE OF 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) REQUIRES THAT THE INJURY, NOT MERELY THE OPERATIONS, OCCUR ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF.**

The text of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) is unambiguous. Section 1333(b) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” necessarily links the injury—not just the operations—to the shelf. Congress could have written “injury resulting from operations on the shelf” if it intended no situs requirement. It did not. Instead, it drafted a provision that requires a causal connection between the injury and shelf-based operations, while the injury itself remains tethered to the shelf’s geographic ambit through the word “occurring.”

This Court has long recognized that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). The Court’s observation that “the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale” applies with full force to § 1333(b). *Id.* Indeed, the Court expressly noted that “§ 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. While the Ninth Circuit dismissed this statement as “unconsidered dictum,” this Court should treat it as what it is: a considered statement about the structure of the statute.

**II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS” TEST REPLACES A CLEAR STATUTORY STANDARD WITH AN UNWORKABLE JUDICIAL INVENTION.**

The court below held that an onshore injury is compensable under § 1333(b) so long as there is “a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf.” *Valladolid v. Pac. Operations Offshore, LLP*, 604 F.3d 1126, 1135 (9th Cir. 2010). This standard appears nowhere in the statute. Congress did not say “substantial nexus.” It said “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s test invites confusion, inconsistent application, and endless litigation over how close a connection between an onshore injury and offshore operations is “substantial” enough.

The Fifth Circuit, by contrast, adopted a straightforward situs-of-injury test that respects the statute’s geographic limitation. *Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). Under *Mills*, an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf. *Id.* That test is administrable, predictable, and faithful to the statutory text.

**III. THE THIRD CIRCUIT’S “BUT FOR” TEST, WHICH THE NINTH CIRCUIT EXPANDED, IS INCONSISTENT WITH OCSLA’S STRUCTURE.**

Even the Third Circuit, which rejected a situs-of-injury requirement, did not adopt the “substantial nexus” test. In *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), the court held that a claimant need only show that the injury would not have occurred “but for” operations on the outer continental shelf. *Id.* at 809. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is even more expansive, requiring only a “close causal connection” between the injury and shelf operations. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1135.

This Court should reject both tests. The “but for” test is too broad; it would cover an employee injured in a car accident while commuting to work, as *Curtis* itself held. 849 F.2d at 811. The “substantial nexus” test is even more amorphous. Neither test has any anchor in the statute’s language, which ties coverage to injuries on the shelf.

**IV. OCSLA’S LEGISLATIVE HISTORY CONFIRMS THAT CONGRESS INTENDED A GEOGRAPHIC LIMITATION ON COVERAGE.**

Congress enacted OCSLA in 1953 to extend federal law to the outer continental shelf, an area beyond state jurisdiction. The workers’ compensation provision was designed to “furnish the same compensation for injuries to employees working on the shelf as they would receive under the Longshoremen’s Act.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 12 (1953). The LHWCA itself applies to injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). Congress cross-referenced this maritime situs requirement when it extended LHWCA coverage to the shelf. There is no evidence that Congress intended to cover onshore injuries when the LHWCA itself would not.

**V. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S RULING CREATES A CIRCUIT SPLIT THAT SHOULD BE RESOLVED IN FAVOR OF THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S APPROACH.**

Two circuits have now addressed this question. The Fifth Circuit requires the injury to occur on the outer continental shelf. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 362. The Ninth Circuit does not. *Valladolid*, 604 F.3d at 1135. This Court should resolve the conflict by adopting the Fifth Circuit’s situs-of-injury requirement. The Fifth Circuit’s approach is consistent with the plain language of § 1333(b), this Court’s observation in *Tallentire*, and the administrative need for a clear, bright-line rule.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
