The Due Process Clause does not require a separate, heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The Constitution requires that a defendant be competent, but it has never constitutionalized a special psychiatric or decisional-quality test beyond the standard announced in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), and applied to related competency questions in subsequent cases. In Dusky, this Court held that the test is whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and has “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” Id. at 402. That standard governs competency to stand trial. Nothing in the Due Process Clause requires courts to add a free-standing inquiry into whether a defendant can make the “best” or most “reasoned” choice among alternatives.

This Court’s cases addressing guilty pleas and waiver of counsel confirm that the relevant constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent, not whether the defendant’s choice is especially wise. In Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court held that a guilty plea must be an intentional relinquishment of known rights, and that the record must show the plea was entered knowingly and voluntarily. Id. at 242-44. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of constitutional rights as an “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” Id. at 464. And in Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), the Court held that a defendant may waive counsel so long as he does so “knowingly and intelligently.” Id. at 835. None of these decisions imposes a heightened competence-to-choose standard.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” formulation is not grounded in this Court’s precedents. The court of appeals relied on its own cases, including Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and Evans v. Raines, 800 F.2d 884 (9th Cir. 1986), to state that competency to plead guilty or waive counsel requires “the capacity for ‘reasoned choice’ among the alternatives available.” But that formulation cannot be squared with this Court’s later decision in Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993), which rejected precisely that approach. The Court held that the competency standard is the same for standing trial, pleading guilty, and waiving counsel, and that due process does not require a higher level of competency for the latter decisions. Id. at 398-99. The Court explained that competency and the validity of a waiver are distinct questions: competency asks whether the defendant has the capacity required by Dusky, while whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary asks whether the defendant actually understood the rights he was abandoning. Id. at 400-01.

That distinction is dispositive here. A defendant may be competent under Dusky yet still fail to enter a valid waiver if the plea colloquy is inadequate or the record does not show understanding. Boykin, 395 U.S. at 242-44; Brady v. United States, 397 U.S. 742, 748 (1970). But the solution is a careful waiver inquiry, not a heightened competency test. Godinez, 509 U.S. at 400-01. The Constitution does not require trial judges to evaluate whether a defendant’s decision is “reasoned” in some independent sense. Id. at 398-99.

Applying that principle, the state court was required to determine whether Moran met the ordinary competency standard and whether his waivers were knowing and voluntary. The Due Process Clause did not require the court to go further and inquire into a separate “reasoned choice” capacity. To hold otherwise would improperly conflate competency with wisdom and would create a constitutionally mandated standard that this Court has already rejected. Godinez, 509 U.S. at 398-401.

Accordingly, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
