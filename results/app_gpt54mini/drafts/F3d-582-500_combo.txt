I. Erdman Presented a Triable Issue on FMLA Eligibility

Summary judgment should be reversed because the record, viewed in Erdman’s favor, permits a reasonable jury to find that she satisfied the FMLA’s 1,250-hour requirement. Summary judgment is proper only when “there is no genuine issue as to any material fact,” and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). But where the evidence supports competing inferences, “the matter is for the jury.” Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). On appeal from summary judgment, the Court must resolve all reasonable inferences for Erdman. Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372, 378 (2007).

That rule is especially important where the employer’s own conduct created the factual dispute. Nationwide initially allowed Erdman to work extra hours and treat them as “comp” time. Its supervisors repeatedly authorized or acquiesced in those hours, and no one told Erdman until February 10, 2003 that she could no longer use them. A jury could find that Nationwide’s belated disallowance of previously permitted hours cannot erase work already performed, particularly where the employer had benefited from the labor. Equity has long refused to permit a party to take advantage of its own wrong. See United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 487 (1868); Griggs v. Allegheny County, 369 U.S. 84, 89 (1962).

The District Court’s contrary approach also conflicts with the established principle that remedial statutes are to be applied in a manner that effectuates their protective purpose. The FMLA was enacted to balance workplace demands against family needs, and the eligibility rules should not be construed to defeat that purpose by crediting an employer’s after-the-fact attempt to characterize previously approved work as nonexistent. Cf. Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459-60 (1892); United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) at 487. While statutory text controls, interpretation must avoid “an absurd and unjust result.” Church of the Holy Trinity, 143 U.S. at 459.

The record here easily supports a finding that Erdman had accumulated the required hours. She had been a long-term Nationwide employee, she regularly worked beyond her scheduled part-time hours, and her supervisor consistently authorized that work as payable overtime or comp time. Getgen did not object when Erdman explained in January 2003 that she had used comp time, and only later reversed course. The question whether those hours count is one of fact and application, not a basis for summary judgment. See Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment should be used sparingly where motive and intent are at issue); Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627-28 (1944) (issues turning on credibility and inferences are for trial). Nationwide’s asserted timekeeping records are not dispositive where the surrounding evidence suggests the records omitted or misclassified hours actually worked. See Lavender v. Kurn, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946).

II. Nationwide’s Pretextual Firing Supports Erdman’s FMLA Claims

Erdman also produced evidence from which a jury could conclude that Nationwide terminated her because she sought FMLA leave. Her April 2003 request for leave was followed within weeks by discharge. Temporal proximity, combined with the shifting explanations and the absence of contemporaneous objection to her leave request, permits an inference of retaliatory motive. Cf. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 147-48 (2000) (even where employer offers a reason, disbelief of that reason can support liability); Poller, 368 U.S. at 473.

Nationwide’s reliance on Erdman’s alleged misconduct does not entitle it to judgment as a matter of law. The evidence must be viewed in context. Erdman disputes the alleged insubordination, and the only undisputed incident cited at the end involved a profane disclaimer on a personal call, after company policy stated that personal calls were not to be monitored for quality purposes. A jury could find that this was a convenient post hoc justification rather than the true reason for discharge. See Bailey v. Central Vermont Railway, Inc., 319 U.S. 350, 354 (1943) (courts should not permit formalism to defeat substantive rights); Lavender, 327 U.S. at 653.

In short, the summary judgment record contains classic factual disputes: whether Erdman had enough qualifying hours, whether Nationwide had approved or acquiesced in the extra time, and whether the termination was motivated by her FMLA request. Those disputes must be resolved by a jury, not on summary judgment. See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255; Sartor, 321 U.S. at 627-28; Poller, 368 U.S. at 473. The judgment on the FMLA claim should therefore be reversed.
