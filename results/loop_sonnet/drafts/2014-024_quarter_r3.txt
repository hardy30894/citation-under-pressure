**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long presumed that criminal statutes require proof of wrongful intent even where Congress has not expressly included such a requirement, particularly where the offense carries the stigma and penalties of a true crime rather than a mere regulatory violation. *United States v. Murdock*, 290 U.S. 389, 394–95 (1933) (construing "willfully" to require proof of a culpable state of mind rather than mere inadvertence).

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter. This Court has permitted strict construction of intent requirements only for statutes regulating inherently dangerous or heavily regulated conduct, where Congress weighed the risk of punishing an innocent actor against the danger to the public from unregulated activity. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 253–54 (1922). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from that regulatory context; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma — precisely the setting in which this Court has insisted on a subjective mens rea element.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty or punish the unwary. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the plurality read a "willfully" requirement into a federal civil-rights statute to avoid convicting a defendant who lacked a bad purpose, reasoning that the statute should not be construed to punish one who had no "awareness of the fact that his conduct is unlawful," *id.* at 101–02 (plurality opinion), precisely to avoid punishing defendants for conduct they did not appreciate as unlawful. Likewise, this Court has cautioned that criminal statutes touching on protected liberty must be construed to avoid punishing conduct the defendant could not reasonably know was forbidden, and that fair notice of what is prohibited is essential to due process. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); *see also Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 509–10 (1948). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." This Court has long recognized that certain narrowly limited classes of speech fall outside constitutional protection, but has insisted that such categories be defined with precision so as not to sweep in protected expression. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 309–10 (1940). This Court's own application of the true-threat concept in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), confirms that the inquiry cannot rest on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous or hyperbolic language. There, the Court held that "we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by [the] petitioner fits within that statutory term," *id.* at 708, even though a literal, objective reading of the words could have been understood by some listeners as menacing. The Court's focus was on the character of the speech itself and the speaker's evident meaning, not simply on audience perception.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression — dark humor, hyperbole, rap lyrics, venting — because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has repeatedly recognized that statutes touching on speech must be construed narrowly to avoid deterring protected expression, because speakers of ordinary prudence will steer clear of a broad zone of lawful activity rather than risk prosecution under an uncertain standard. *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 97–98 (1940). Where scienter requirements are absent, this danger of overdeterrence is especially acute, restricting protected speech along with the unprotected. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959). Vague, purely objective liability standards create the same risk of overdeterrence that this Court condemned when procedural burdens threatened to cause speakers to avoid more expression than necessary to escape prosecution. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before punishment may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense — punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium. *Lanzetta*, 306 U.S. at 453; *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–02 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
