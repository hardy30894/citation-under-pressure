**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class without requiring plaintiffs to demonstrate, through admissible evidence, that damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement. Rule 23(b)(3) demands that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This predominance inquiry “tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation.” Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). A court may not certify a class unless it is “satisfied, after a rigorous analysis,” that the prerequisites are met. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011) (quoting Gen. Tel. Co. of Sw. v. Falcon, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982)).

That analysis must extend to damages. Where individual damage calculations would “overwhelm questions common to the class,” predominance is defeated. Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013). Plaintiffs therefore bore the burden of producing admissible evidence that damages “may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis.” Id. at 30. The district court failed to discharge this obligation.

Plaintiffs’ expert offered four regression models purporting to calculate overcharges attributable to Comcast’s conduct. Yet the court itself limited the liability theory to a single claim: that clustering deterred overbuilder entry. The models, however, incorporated variables reflecting multiple theories of harm that the court had rejected. Because the regressions did not isolate the effect of the sole remaining theory, they could not establish that damages were “capable of measurement on a classwide basis.” Id. at 34. The district court’s acceptance of this mismatch rested on the erroneous premise that any plausible methodology suffices, without demanding that the methodology correspond to the theory of liability actually certified.

The court likewise declined to test the models’ admissibility under the standards governing expert testimony. It treated the regressions as common evidence even though they rested on assumptions about market definition and competitive effects that varied across thousands of individual households. Such variation precludes the “common answers” that Wal-Mart requires. 564 U.S. at 352. A finding of predominance cannot rest on expert evidence that fails to produce a common result for the class as a whole.

The Third Circuit compounded the error by applying a deferential standard of review that insulated these deficiencies. It held that the district court’s acceptance of the models was not clearly erroneous, without examining whether the models actually measured damages traceable to the certified theory. That approach conflicts with the requirement that a court “probe behind the pleadings” and resolve disputes necessary to the certification decision. Id. at 351.

Because plaintiffs failed to supply admissible evidence that damages could be measured class-wide, the predominance requirement was not satisfied. The certification order should be reversed.
