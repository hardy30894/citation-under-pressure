**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Arbitrary and Contrary to the National Labor Relations Act**

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only if it possesses “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. *See Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board’s justification—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—is insufficient to justify a standard that renders polling virtually useless. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). The Board’s own rationale in *Texas Petrochemicals* undermines its position: if the Board permits employers to withdraw recognition or seek an RM election upon a lesser showing, it cannot simultaneously deny employers the right to poll under the same or similar circumstances. *Id.* at 1063.

The Board’s standard conflicts with the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *See NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990). By imposing a higher threshold for polling than for withdrawal of recognition, the Board creates an anomaly: employers may withdraw recognition based on less evidence than would permit them to poll. This disparity serves no legitimate purpose and frustrates the Act’s balance of interests. *See id.* at 785-86.

This Court should defer to the Board’s policy choices only when they are “rational and consistent with the Act.” *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978). The Board’s polling standard fails this test. It is arbitrary to permit employers to act on their reasonable doubts through withdrawal or RM elections but not through polling, a less intrusive means of testing employee sentiment. *See Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 42 (deferring to Board only where its interpretation is “rational and consistent with the Act”).

**II. The Board Erred in Concluding That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt**

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The record contains ample objective evidence raising doubts about the union’s majority status:

- Seven employees explicitly repudiated the union. *See Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc.*, 325 N.L.R.B. 1263, 1265 (1998).
- Two employees (Bloch and Mohr) reported that multiple coworkers opposed the union. *Id.* at 1266.
- The union’s bargaining unit had been reduced from 45 to 32 employees, and the new employer had no prior relationship with the union. *Id.* at 1264.

The Board improperly discounted this evidence. It refused to credit statements made during job interviews, but such statements are probative of employee sentiment. *See Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978) (rejecting Board’s categorical exclusion of interview statements). The Board also ignored the fact that the bargaining unit had never been tested for majority support under the new employer. *See Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 36-41 (successor employer inherits bargaining obligation but may rebut presumption of majority support).

The Board’s reliance on *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), is misplaced. That case involved an employee’s dissatisfaction with union services, not opposition to representation. *Id.* Here, employees explicitly rejected the union. The Board’s factual finding is therefore unsupported by substantial evidence. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 488.

**III. The Board’s Standard for Polling Is Inconsistent with Circuit Precedent**

Three circuits have rejected the Board’s standard, holding that employers may poll if they have “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.” *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1140, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). These courts recognized that polling is a legitimate tool for employers with sincere doubts, provided procedural safeguards are met. *Id.*

The Board’s refusal to adopt this standard is arbitrary. The Board’s own rationale—that polling lacks the safeguards of an RM election—applies equally to withdrawal of recognition, yet the Board permits withdrawal under a lesser standard. *See Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061-62. The Board’s inconsistency demonstrates that its polling standard is not a rational exercise of its discretion. *See Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 786.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should grant the petition for review and deny enforcement of the Board’s order.
