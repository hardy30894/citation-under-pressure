**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does not add or alter any constitutionally enumerated qualification for service in Congress. Rather, it regulates ballot access—a permissible state power—without purporting to impose additional qualifications. *See United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 320 (1941) (holding that states may regulate primary elections as part of their power to regulate elections generally); *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 392 (1879) (recognizing state authority to regulate the manner of holding elections for federal offices). The qualifications clauses of the Constitution—Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 (House) and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 (Senate)—are exclusive and exhaustive. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (holding that Congress may not add qualifications beyond those enumerated in the Constitution). However, the Arkansas Amendment does not add qualifications; it merely conditions ballot access on prior service. *See Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 733 n.6 (1974) (noting that states may impose "reasonable, non-discriminatory restrictions on the availability of the ballot").

The distinction between qualifications and ballot access is well established. *See Rosario v. Rockefeller*, 410 U.S. 752, 760 (1973) (holding that a state may impose a durational residency requirement for independent candidates without violating the Constitution). The Arkansas Amendment operates in the same manner—it does not prevent any person who meets the constitutional qualifications from running for office; it merely requires that such persons appear on the ballot. *See Lubin v. Panish*, 415 U.S. 709, 713 (1974) (holding that a state may require a filing fee for ballot access as long as it does not operate to exclude candidates based on wealth). The Amendment is thus a permissible regulation of the electoral process, not an impermissible alteration of qualifications. *See Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23, 31 (1968) (holding that states may impose ballot access restrictions so long as they are reasonable and nondiscriminatory).

**II. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT IS A VALID EXERCISE OF THE STATE’S RESERVED POWERS.**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states all powers not delegated to the federal government. *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (reaffirming the principle that powers not delegated to the federal government are reserved to the states). The regulation of elections, including ballot access, is a traditional state function. *See Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932) (holding that the state’s power to regulate elections is a reserved power under the Tenth Amendment). The Arkansas Amendment is a valid exercise of this reserved power, as it does not interfere with the federal government’s authority to determine the qualifications of its members. *See McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316, 405 (1819) (holding that states may not interfere with the operations of the federal government).

**III. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FEDERAL ELECTIONS CLAUSE.**

The Elections Clause of the Constitution—Article I, Section 4, Clause 1—grants states the power to regulate the "Times, Places and Manner" of holding elections, subject to congressional override. *See Roudebush v. Hartke*, 405 U.S. 15, 24 (1972) (holding that the Elections Clause grants states broad authority to regulate elections). The Arkansas Amendment regulates the "Manner" of holding elections by conditioning ballot access on prior service. *See Oregon v. Mitchell*, 400 U.S. 112, 124 (1970) (plurality opinion) (holding that states may regulate the manner of holding elections). This is a permissible exercise of state authority under the Elections Clause. *See Davis v. Hildebrant*, 241 U.S. 565, 568 (1916) (holding that states may regulate the manner of holding elections).

**IV. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT IS NOT PRE-EMPTED BY FEDERAL LAW.**

Federal law does not preempt the Arkansas Amendment. The federal Constitution and statutes do not occupy the field of ballot access regulations. *See Purcell v. Gonzalez*, 549 U.S. 1, 4 (2006) (holding that states retain authority to regulate elections unless Congress has expressly preempted state law). The Arkansas Amendment does not conflict with any federal law, as it does not purport to regulate the qualifications of members of Congress. *See Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1, 210 (1824) (holding that federal law preempts state law only where there is a direct conflict).

**V. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT IS SEVERABLE.**

Even if Section 3 of the Amendment were invalid, the remainder of the Amendment would remain in effect. The severability clause in Section 4 of the Amendment provides that if any provision is held invalid, the remainder shall stand. *See Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Comm’n of Oklahoma*, 286 U.S. 210, 234 (1932) (holding that severability clauses are valid and enforceable). The Arkansas Supreme Court has held that severability clauses are enforceable unless the invalid provision is so essential to the statute that the legislature would not have enacted the remainder without it. *See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 812 (1964) (holding that the test of severability is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion). The invalidation of Section 3 does not require the invalidation of Sections 1 and 2, as the latter regulate state offices and are entirely severable. *See Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 108 (1976) (holding that severability clauses are enforceable unless the invalid provision is essential to the statute).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment is a valid exercise of the state’s reserved powers and does not violate the federal Constitution. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be reversed.
