# ARGUMENT

## I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS 1997 ENACTMENT

Congress is presumed to legislate prospectively, and courts will not read a statute to reach conduct that predates its enactment absent a clear statement of that intent. This canon is not new; it is "deeply rooted," to borrow a phrase this Court has long applied to statutes affecting settled expectations arising from past acts. In *Chew Heong v. United States*, 112 U.S. 536 (1884), the Court refused to apply a certificate requirement of the Chinese Exclusion Act to a laborer who had departed the country before the requirement was enacted, holding that Congress must speak plainly before a statute will be understood to attach new burdens to a completed act. The same principle controlled in *United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co. v. United States ex rel. Struthers Wells Co.*, 209 U.S. 306 (1908), where the Court refused to give retrospective effect to a statute absent unmistakable evidence that Congress intended such an operation, and in *Union Pacific R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190 (1913), where the Court likewise construed an ambiguous provision to operate only prospectively.

This Court reaffirmed the same rule in the modern era. In *Claridge Apartments Co. v. Commissioner*, 323 U.S. 141 (1944), the Court held that a tax provision would not be read to reach transactions completed before its passage without clear congressional direction. And in *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149 (1964), the Court applied the identical presumption to an executive regulation, explaining that fairness to those who acted in reliance on the law as it stood requires courts to demand a clear statement before construing new enactments to reach back and attach new legal consequences to completed conduct.

Section 241(a)(5) contains no such clear statement. Petitioner's re-entry in 1982 was a completed act, governed at the time by a regulatory regime that permitted him to seek discretionary relief notwithstanding his prior deportation. Nothing in the statutory text unambiguously instructs that Congress meant to reach back fifteen years to attach new, harsher consequences—automatic reinstatement of the old deportation order and forfeiture of all relief—to that already-completed act of re-entry. Under *Chew Heong*, *Struthers Wells*, *Union Pacific*, *Claridge Apartments*, and *Greene*, that silence should be resolved against retroactive application.

## II. EVEN IF THE STATUTE REACHES PRE-ENACTMENT RE-ENTRIES, ITS APPLICATION TO PETITIONER IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE

Petitioner's position is strengthened, not weakened, by the fact that he is a long-resident alien who built his life in the United States—married, and sought lawful status—based on a legal regime that permitted him to seek adjustment. This Court has consistently recognized that aliens who have entered and resided in this country accumulate constitutionally cognizable interests that Congress may not casually extinguish through retroactive legislation. As the Court explained in *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 n.5 (1953), quoting *Bridges v. Wixon*, 326 U.S. 135 (1945), "once an alien lawfully enters and resides in this country he becomes invested with the rights guaranteed by the Constitution to all people within our borders." That principle reflects the recognition, dating to *Ng Fung Ho v. White*, 259 U.S. 276 (1922), that deportation "may result also in loss of both property and life; or of all that makes life worth living," *id.* at 284, and that the deprivation worked upon a resident alien by deportation proceedings is substantial enough to demand fair process, including fair notice of the legal consequences attached to past conduct.

The government's reliance on the plenary power over deportation, reflected in cases such as *Fong Yue Ting v. United States*, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), does not answer petitioner's claim. Even under the broadest formulation of the plenary power doctrine, this Court has never held that Congress may attach unforeseeable, categorically harsher legal consequences to conduct completed years before the new rule existed, when the alien organized his affairs—marriage, family, years of residence—around the reasonable expectation that adjustment of status remained available to him. In *Galvan v. Press*, 347 U.S. 522 (1954), the Court upheld retroactive application of a new deportation ground only after emphasizing that the alien's own conduct (Communist Party membership) remained the same regardless of when the statute was enacted, and that Congress had long asserted the power to define deportable conduct by association. That is a different case from this one: petitioner is not challenged for conduct whose legal character never changed, but is stripped—by a change in law occurring sixteen years after he acted—of the very avenue of relief, adjustment of status through marriage to a citizen, that existed when he re-entered and that induced his reliance.

Because Section 241(a)(5), as applied to petitioner, attaches a new disability—total forfeiture of relief—to a completed act of re-entry undertaken under a materially different legal regime, and because petitioner is a resident alien whose settled expectations are entitled to the protection recognized in *Ng Fung Ho*, *Bridges v. Wixon*, and *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, this application is impermissibly retroactive.
