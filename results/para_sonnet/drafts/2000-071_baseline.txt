# ARGUMENT

## I. MILFORD'S EXCLUSION OF THE CLUB WAS REASONABLE, VIEWPOINT-NEUTRAL REGULATION OF A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM.

When a school district opens its facilities for after-hours use, it creates a "limited public forum" in which access "need not be permitted to all who wish to use" the property. *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). Within such a forum, the government may draw content-based lines "as long as the restrictions are reasonable and are not an effort to suppress expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker's view." *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford's Community Use Policy permits discussion of "the welfare of the community," including character and moral development, but excludes "religious purposes." That line is drawn at the category of activity—religious instruction and worship—not at any viewpoint on a permitted topic.

This case therefore differs fundamentally from *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993). There, the district allowed private groups to use school facilities to show films addressing family and child-rearing issues from a secular perspective, yet forbade a church from showing a film addressing the identical subject from a religious perspective. This Court held that exclusion impermissible because the district had "open[ed] its property for use by community groups," yet "den[ied] this opportunity" to a group merely "because his topic is presented from a religious standpoint." *Id.* at 393-94. Milford has done nothing comparable. Milford did not permit secular discussions of "prayer" or "religious conversion" while excluding the Club's religious discussion of the same activities. The Club's proposed use—prayer, proselytizing invitations to accept Christ, and scripture memorization directed at effecting individual salvation—is not a religious viewpoint on a secular subject like child-rearing; it is itself the practice of religion. As the court below correctly recognized, there is a "significant distinction between religious *instruction* and religious *perspective*." The Policy's exclusion of the former, while permitting groups such as the Boy Scouts and 4-H to teach values generally, including values informed by religious sensibility, is subject-matter line-drawing of exactly the sort *Cornelius* sanctions.

Nor does *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819 (1995), compel a different result. This Court there stressed that the University had not funded "religious activities" as such, but rather a "forum" for "the free flow of ideas" among student publications, and specifically noted that "we do not confront a case where the University has provided funding directly to a church." *Id.* at 842-43. Here, by contrast, Milford would be furnishing its own facility, immediately after the school day, as the site of the very religious exercise itself—prayer, conversion, and scriptural devotion—not merely as a forum for the discussion of ideas that happen to be religiously grounded.

## II. EVEN IF THE POLICY EFFECTED SOME LINE-DRAWING ON THE BASIS OF RELIGIOUS CONTENT, MILFORD'S WELL-FOUNDED ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE CONCERN JUSTIFIED THE EXCLUSION.

This Court has long recognized that a school may decline to open its forum to religious activity where doing so would risk an Establishment Clause violation. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court held that equal access for a student religious group did not violate the Establishment Clause—but only after emphasizing the relevant audience consisted of "college students," whom the Court deemed "less impressionable than younger students" and unlikely to perceive the University's neutral policy as an endorsement of religion. *Id.* at 274 n.14. That reasoning does not extend to Milford's forum, which is populated by children as young as six.

This Court has repeatedly distinguished the constitutional treatment of religious exercise involving young schoolchildren from that involving mature students. In *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992), the Court held that a graduation prayer violated the Establishment Clause because "adolescents are often susceptible to pressure from their peers towards conformity," *id.* at 593, and that public schools may not effectively compel attendance at religious exercises even indirectly. Likewise, in *School District of Abington Township v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), and *Engel v. Vitale*, 370 U.S. 421 (1962), the Court struck down school-sponsored religious exercises precisely because of the setting's coercive potential for impressionable children. The Good News Club would convene immediately after the school bell rang, on school property, using the same classrooms and the same authority structure the children associate with the school day. Under the endorsement inquiry this Court has applied, *County of Allegheny v. ACLU*, 492 U.S. 573, 592 (1989), a reasonable young observer could easily perceive the Club's proselytizing prayer meetings as bearing the school's imprimatur. Milford's judgment that granting access under these circumstances risked exactly the Establishment Clause violation this Court's precedents forbid was reasonable, and it should not be second-guessed by imposing First Amendment liability for that good-faith constitutional judgment.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
