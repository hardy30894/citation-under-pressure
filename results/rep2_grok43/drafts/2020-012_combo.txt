The Railroad Retirement Act provides for judicial review of final decisions of the Board. Under the plain language of the governing review provision, jurisdiction extends to any Board action that conclusively resolves a claim after exhaustion of available administrative remedies. Rochester Telephone Corp. v. United States, 307 U.S. 125, 132 (1939) (review lies where agency action “has the force of law” and “affects legal rights”).

A Board order refusing to reopen a prior denial meets this standard. It leaves the claimant with no further administrative recourse and fixes the rights of the parties with respect to the earlier application. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc. v. United States, 316 U.S. 407, 416 (1942) (order is final when it “imposes an obligation” or “denies a right” with “finality”). The same principle governs orders that terminate proceedings on a particular application even if later claims remain possible. Chicago & Southern Air Lines, Inc. v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 333 U.S. 103, 112-13 (1948).

Precedent confirms that an agency’s refusal to reconsider or reopen constitutes reviewable final action when no further internal relief is available. Leedom v. Kyne, 358 U.S. 184, 188 (1958) (review available where agency action is “in excess of its delegated powers” and leaves “no other means” of vindication); Myers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp., 303 U.S. 41, 50-51 (1938) (exhaustion not required where further proceedings would be “futile”). An order declining to disturb a prior denial on grounds of timeliness or new evidence likewise disposes of the matter finally. United States v. Storer Broadcasting Co., 351 U.S. 192, 199 (1956).

This Court has repeatedly held that agency determinations ending the administrative process on a claim are subject to immediate judicial scrutiny. Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 148 (1967) (finality turns on whether the action “mark[s] the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process”); Frozen Food Express v. United States, 351 U.S. 40, 44 (1956) (order is final when it “has an immediate and practical impact” on the parties). The Board’s refusal to reopen satisfies every element of these tests: it is the last word on the 2006 application and directly affects the claimant’s entitlement.

No sound distinction exists between a merits denial and a refusal to reopen when both leave the claimant without further administrative remedy. Endicott Johnson Corp. v. Perkins, 317 U.S. 501, 509 (1943) (review lies once agency has “finally determined” the issue presented). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion cannot be reconciled with these settled authorities.
