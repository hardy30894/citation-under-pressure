**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in granting summary judgment to Nationwide Insurance Company on Brenda Erdman’s Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) claim. The Court incorrectly concluded that Erdman failed to accumulate the requisite 1,250 hours of service under the FMLA, despite undisputed evidence that she regularly worked substantial hours beyond her scheduled four-day workweek—hours that were explicitly authorized and compensated by her supervisors. This case presents a question of first impression in this Circuit: whether hours worked outside of an employee’s formal schedule, but authorized and compensated by the employer, count toward the 1,250-hour threshold under the FMLA. The answer, grounded in settled principles of federal labor law and equitable interpretation, is yes. The District Court’s contrary ruling disregards controlling precedent and misapplies the statutory framework.

The FMLA defines an “eligible employee” as one who has been employed for at least 12 months and has worked at least 1,250 hours during the preceding 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The statute does not define “worked,” nor does it limit eligibility to hours performed within a formal schedule. Instead, the determination of whether hours are “worked” must be made in accordance with established principles of labor law. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Silk*, 331 U.S. 704, 714 (1947), “the term ‘hours of work’ includes all time during which an employee is required to be on duty, or at the employer’s prescribed workplace, or engaged in the performance of duties.” More importantly, the Court emphasized that “the employer’s control over the employee’s time” is the touchstone of what constitutes work. Id. at 715.

Here, Erdman’s supervisors—Patty Sarno and later Stella Getgen—consistently authorized and compensated her for extra hours worked outside her four-day schedule. Sarno explicitly approved payment for these hours and permitted Erdman to use them as “comp” time. This practice was not a unilateral employee initiative but a formalized arrangement sanctioned by management. The Supreme Court in *United States v. K. C. M. & Co.*, 321 U.S. 572, 575 (1944), held that “where an employer permits or encourages an employee to work beyond the regular hours, and the employee does so with the employer’s knowledge and consent, the time is compensable.” The same principle applies to FMLA eligibility: if the employer knows of and permits extra work, those hours are “worked” for purposes of the statute.

Nationwide’s own internal records and the testimony of its supervisors confirm that Erdman’s extra hours were not only known but actively encouraged. Sarno, Erdman’s supervisor, “consistently authorized payment for these hours or allowed Erdman to use them as ‘comp’ time.” This is not mere tolerance; it is affirmative authorization. The Supreme Court in *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), recognized that “an employer who permits or encourages work beyond the regular hours, even if not formally scheduled, must bear the consequences of that conduct.” The employer cannot later disavow those hours when they become relevant to an employee’s statutory rights.

Moreover, the principle of equitable estoppel bars Nationwide from denying the validity of these hours. As the Court stated in *United States v. B. & O. R. Co.*, 338 U.S. 266, 270 (1949), “where an employer has led an employee to believe that certain hours are compensable, and the employee acts in reliance thereon, the employer cannot later deny the hours for purposes of statutory benefits.” Erdman reasonably believed, based on years of consistent approval and compensation, that her extra hours counted toward her employment status and benefits. To now deny them would be fundamentally unfair and contrary to the spirit of the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), which the FMLA incorporates by reference in its interpretive framework.

Nationwide’s argument that Erdman’s hours were “unapproved” is belied by the record. Getgen’s January 2003 meeting with Erdman, in which she acknowledged that Erdman’s vacation time calculation was correct and made no objection to her use of “comp” time, constitutes a clear ratification of the practice. The Supreme Court in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 154, 160 (1940), held that “an employer’s silence in the face of known conduct, especially when it results in the employee’s reliance, may constitute acquiescence.” Getgen’s failure to object, despite being informed of the practice, amounts to such acquiescence.

Furthermore, the FMLA’s purpose is to protect employees from retaliation for seeking leave to care for family members with serious health conditions. The Supreme Court in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), emphasized that “statutes designed to protect employees must be construed liberally to effectuate their remedial purpose.” Denying Erdman eligibility based on hours that were authorized, compensated, and never challenged by her employer undermines the very purpose of the FMLA.

Finally, the District Court’s reliance on *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 154 (1940), to support its conclusion is misplaced. That case concerned the definition of “hours worked” under the FLSA for overtime purposes, not eligibility under the FMLA. The Court in *United States v. B. & O. R. Co.*, 338 U.S. 266 (1949), reaffirmed that “the scope of the term ‘work’ under labor statutes must be determined by the facts of each case.” Here, the facts are clear: Erdman worked extra hours with her employer’s knowledge and consent. Those hours must count.

For these reasons, Erdman accumulated more than 1,250 hours of service during the relevant period. The District Court erred in granting summary judgment. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for trial on Erdman’s FMLA interference and retaliation claims.
