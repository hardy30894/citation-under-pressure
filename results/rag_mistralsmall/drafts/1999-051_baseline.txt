**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Carpenter’s procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court has repeatedly recognized that a prisoner must demonstrate both cause and prejudice to overcome a procedural default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 485 (1986) (“[I]n appropriate cases the principles of comity and federalism counsel against federal court review of a state prisoner’s claims when the state court has refused to consider them because of a procedural default.”). The Court has further held that “[a]n ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may constitute cause for a procedural default if the claim is ‘so novel’ that the prisoner could not reasonably have been expected to raise it on direct appeal.” *Id.* at 488. However, the Court has never suggested that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim itself can serve as cause to excuse another default.

In *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991), the Court emphasized that procedural defaults must be excused only in exceptional circumstances, such as where the defaulted claim is “so novel” that it could not have been raised earlier. *Id.* at 750. The Court did not extend this principle to allow a defaulted claim to excuse another default. Similarly, in *Harris v. Reed*, 489 U.S. 255 (1989), the Court reiterated that a federal habeas court must defer to state procedural rules unless the prisoner demonstrates cause and prejudice. *Id.* at 262. The Court has never countenanced the notion that a defaulted claim can serve as cause for another default.

The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s precedent by allowing a defaulted claim to excuse another default. This approach undermines the finality of state court judgments and the comity principles underlying *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977). The Court should reject this novel expansion of the cause-and-prejudice doctrine.

**II. Even if Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim were not procedurally defaulted, it cannot excuse the default of his sufficiency claim.**

Assuming arguendo that Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim is not procedurally defaulted, it still cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. The Sixth Circuit relied on *Terrell v. Morris*, 493 U.S. 1 (1989) (per curiam), to support its holding. However, *Terrell* does not stand for the proposition that an ineffective-assistance claim can excuse another default. Instead, *Terrell* addressed whether a state procedural rule could be applied retroactively to bar a claim. *Id.* at 2. The Court held that the state could not retroactively apply its procedural rule to bar Terrell’s ineffective-assistance claim because the rule postdated his appeal. *Id.* at 3. *Terrell* does not address whether an ineffective-assistance claim can excuse another default.

Moreover, the Court has consistently required that cause must be something external to the prisoner’s own conduct. *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488. An ineffective-assistance claim, by definition, arises from the prisoner’s own counsel’s conduct and thus cannot serve as an external justification for a procedural default. The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflates the cause requirement with the underlying merits of the ineffective-assistance claim, a distinction this Court has carefully preserved.

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s prejudice analysis is also flawed.**

The Sixth Circuit found prejudice from counsel’s failure to raise the sufficiency claim on direct appeal. However, the Court has held that prejudice in the context of procedural default requires a showing that the defaulted claim is “substantial.” *Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 494. The Sixth Circuit’s analysis conflates the prejudice inquiry with the merits of the sufficiency claim, which is not the proper framework for evaluating procedural default. The Court should clarify that prejudice in this context requires a showing that the defaulted claim is substantial, not merely that it has merit.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.
