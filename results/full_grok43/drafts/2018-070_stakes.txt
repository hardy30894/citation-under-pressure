**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates the civil and criminal laws of adjacent States as surrogate federal law only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The statute’s text and structure make plain that state law supplies a rule of decision solely when federal law leaves an actual gap; it does not operate whenever state law merely “pertains to the subject matter” and is not expressly preempted.

In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court explained that “the purpose of the [OCSLA] was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” *Id.* at 355. Congress rejected both the extension of general maritime law and the wholesale incorporation of the adjacent States, choosing instead a regime in which “existing federal law” would be “exclusive” except where it proved “inadequate.” *Id.* at 357. The Court therefore held that state wrongful-death statutes could apply only because “no federal statute” supplied a remedy for the non-maritime accidents at issue. *Id.* at 360.

The same premise—that state law fills voids, not supplements comprehensive federal schemes—underlies *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Department of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19 (1988). There the Court observed that OCSLA “borrows” adjacent-state law “only for the limited purpose of providing a rule of decision” where federal law is silent. *Id.* at 29. Nothing in the statute’s text or history suggests that borrowed state law may coexist with, or displace, an already operative federal regulatory regime.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies so long as it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—cannot be reconciled with these authorities. The phrase “to the extent that they are applicable” presupposes an area in which federal law does not already govern; otherwise the qualifier is surplusage. *See also* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1) (extending federal law “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State”). The Fifth Circuit has long understood the statute this way, requiring a “significant void or gap” before state law may be assimilated. *Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Insurance Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969).

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies precisely such a comprehensive scheme for wage-and-hour claims. Its savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws only within the territorial jurisdiction of the States; it does not authorize their extraterritorial application to federal enclaves on the outer Continental Shelf. Because the FLSA leaves no gap, California’s wage-and-hour statutes are not “applicable” within the meaning of § 1333(a)(2)(A). The district court correctly so held; the Ninth Circuit erred in vacating its judgment.
