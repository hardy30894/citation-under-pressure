**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires Only That a Defendant Be Competent Under the Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not Under a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has long defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* did not purport to create a floor applicable only to standing trial; it announced the constitutional measure of mental competency itself.

Nothing in the competency inquiry changes when the defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, chooses to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard has no textual or doctrinal source in this Court's due process jurisprudence. It rests instead on the mistaken premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether the defendant possesses the mental capacity to understand and participate—not whether the choice he makes is wise, or is the choice a rational and counseled defendant would make. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine safeguards a defendant against being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend or participate in; it does not superintend the soundness of the particular decisions a competent defendant elects to make. *Id.* at 108.

The same principle governs a defendant's decision to forgo the assistance of counsel. In *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), this Court recognized that the right to counsel exists to protect defendants who lack the understanding necessary to conduct their own defense, but the Court did not suggest that relinquishing that assistance requires a mental capacity beyond the ordinary competency standard. Nor does *Uveges v. Pennsylvania*, 335 U.S. 437 (1948), which examined whether due process required counsel in light of the seriousness of the charge and the defendant's own capacities, suggest any separate, elevated threshold of rationality distinct from the defendant's basic capacity to understand the proceedings.

**II. The Standards Governing Waiver of Counsel and Guilty Pleas Concern the Voluntariness and Knowledge of the Waiver, Not a Separate, Elevated Mental-Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry is whether the waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent—not whether the defendant met some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that a defendant's relinquishment of the right to counsel must be knowing, and that courts must not presume such a waiver from a silent record. *See id.* at 464–65. That holding asks about the defendant's knowledge and intent respecting his rights, a question distinct from the *Dusky* capacity inquiry, and one that in no way implies a separate, more demanding competency standard.

*Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required a searching inquiry into the circumstances surrounding a waiver of counsel, examining the defendant's understanding of the charges, possible defenses, and the consequences of proceeding without a lawyer, *see id.* at 723–24—again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry, not a mental-capacity inquiry.

*Moore v. Michigan*, 355 U.S. 155 (1957), reinforces the point: the Court there examined whether an uncounseled defendant's guilty plea reflected an intelligent and competent waiver of the right to counsel under the circumstances presented, remanding for further proceedings on that voluntariness question rather than announcing any heightened standard of mental capacity. *See id.* at 159–65. And *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), reaffirmed that a court may not presume waiver of counsel from a silent record but must instead determine, from the totality of the circumstances, that the defendant understood his right and relinquished it. *See id.* at 516. Nothing in *Carnley* suggests that this determination requires proof of a mental capacity beyond that required by *Dusky*.

Guilty pleas are treated no differently. In *Smith v. O'Grady*, 312 U.S. 329 (1941), this Court recognized that a plea of guilty obtained without adequate understanding of its consequences may deprive a defendant of due process, but the inquiry the Court described was one of voluntariness and understanding of the plea's consequences—not a separate, elevated measure of rational capacity.

**III. No Decision of This Court Supports a Substantive Competency Standard Higher Than *Dusky*.**

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard finds no footing in this Court's precedents concerning the adequacy of waivers. Each of the decisions discussed above—*Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, *Moore*, *Carnley*, and *Smith*—addresses whether the record demonstrates that a waiver was voluntary and intelligent, not whether the defendant possessed some heightened quantum of rational capacity beyond the *Dusky* standard. The voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry required by these cases operates independently of, and in addition to, the competency inquiry described in *Dusky* and *Massey*; it does not transform that competency inquiry into a more demanding one. Conflating the two, as the Ninth Circuit did, imposes a standard this Court has never articulated and that finds no support in the due process cases governing either competency or waiver.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard that governs whether a defendant may stand trial, articulated in *Dusky* and *Massey*, likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional inquiries required by *Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, *Moore*, *Carnley*, and *Smith* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
