# ARGUMENT

The question here is whether a consent decree containing an express disclaimer of liability nonetheless "resolved" Guam's liability within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby extinguishing Guam's six-year cost-recovery remedy under § 107 in favor of a three-year contribution remedy that had already expired. Because the Decree's plain terms disclaim the very liability the court of appeals found it to "resolve," and because settled principles of statutory construction and equity counsel against reading ambiguous settlement language to forfeit a party's remedy against the responsible polluter, this Court should reverse.

## I. A Consent Decree Must Be Construed According to Its Own Terms, and Its Express Disclaimer of Liability Controls.

A consent decree is, in essence, a negotiated agreement between the parties, and its meaning must be derived "within its four corners," not from what a court speculates the parties might have intended. *Hughes v. United States*, 342 U.S. 353, 357 (1952). This Court has long recognized that such decrees "must be read to give meaning to the language actually used" rather than to import obligations the parties never accepted. *Swift & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 311, 326 (1928). Here, the 2004 Consent Decree was entered "based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." That disclaimer is not surplusage—it reflects the parties' considered decision *not* to resolve liability, and it should be honored according to its terms. To hold, as the court of appeals did, that the Decree nonetheless "resolved" Guam's liability is to rewrite the bargain the parties struck, contrary to the settled rule that consent decrees bind parties only to what they agreed to and no more.

## II. Section 113(f)(3)(B) Must Be Applied According to Its Plain Terms, Which Require an Actual Resolution of Liability.

Congress chose the word "resolved" in § 113(f)(3)(B), and this Court's longstanding interpretive practice requires that statutory language be enforced as written. "[T]he meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Similarly, this Court has cautioned that "there is . . . no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Congress did not write that any settlement referencing a response action triggers § 113(f)(3)(B); it required a "resolution" of liability. A decree that expressly refuses to adjudicate liability cannot, consistent with the statute's plain terms, be deemed to have resolved it.

## III. Ambiguities in Statutes of Limitations Should Be Construed to Preserve, Not Forfeit, a Litigant's Remedy.

Even if the Decree's language were ambiguous as to whether it "resolved" Guam's liability, that ambiguity should be resolved against forfeiture of Guam's remedy. This Court has repeatedly held that limitations periods attached to remedial statutes are to be construed generously in favor of the party seeking relief, not used as technical traps. *See Burnett v. New York Central R.R. Co.*, 380 U.S. 424, 427 (1965) (statutes of limitations are not to be applied so as to bar a plaintiff's claim on technical grounds where doing so would defeat the remedial purpose of the underlying statute). Likewise, in *Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231, 232–33 (1959), this Court refused to allow a defendant's own conduct to be leveraged into a limitations bar against the very party it wronged. The same principle applies with even greater force here: the court of appeals' construction converts an EPA settlement—entered while Guam remained under the cloud of American sovereignty and dependent on federal environmental enforcement—into a trap that extinguished Guam's remedy against the Navy years before Guam could have known its rights were at risk.

## IV. Contribution Rights Are Creatures of Statute and Should Not Be Presumed to Displace a Party's Cost-Recovery Remedy Absent Clear Authorization.

This Court has long held that a right of contribution among responsible parties does not exist absent clear statutory authorization. *Halcyon Lines v. Haenn Ship Ceiling & Refitting Corp.*, 342 U.S. 282, 285 (1952) (declining to fashion a contribution remedy in the absence of legislative action); *United States v. Atlantic Mutual Insurance Co.*, 343 U.S. 236, 238 (1952) (contribution among joint tortfeasors is available only where Congress has so provided). Because Congress created § 113(f)(3)(B) as a limited, statutorily defined avenue for contribution, that provision should not be read expansively to swallow the broader § 107 cost-recovery remedy whenever a settlement merely touches on remediation. Where, as here, the settlement does not clearly resolve liability, the default cost-recovery remedy under § 107 remains available.

## V. Equity Favors Placing the Cost of Contamination on the Party Responsible for Creating It.

Finally, principles of equity underlying CERCLA's cost-allocation scheme counsel against permitting the Navy—the party that operated the Ordot Dump and disposed of hazardous military waste there for decades—to escape responsibility through a forfeiture theory. This Court has recognized that equitable considerations govern the allocation of costs between the government and other parties when the government's own conduct created the hazard at issue. *United States v. Standard Oil Co. of California*, 332 U.S. 301, 305–06 (1947). Guam's position as a former colonial possession, compelled to accept the Navy's waste-disposal practices without meaningful sovereign control, makes this equitable concern especially acute.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
