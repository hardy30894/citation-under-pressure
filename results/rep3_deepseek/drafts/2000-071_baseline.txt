**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club did not violate the Free Speech Clause because the school created a limited public forum and reasonably excluded religious worship and instruction.**

When the government creates a limited public forum, it may restrict speech to the categories that define the forum, as long as the restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Milford’s Community Use Policy opened school facilities only for “social, civic and recreational meetings” and “other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” while expressly excluding “religious purposes.” The record is clear that the Good News Club’s meetings—featuring prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—constituted religious instruction and worship, not secular moral or character development. As the Second Circuit correctly held, “the Club’s activities are quintessentially religious,” and the school properly categorized them as falling “outside the bounds of pure ‘moral and character development.’” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000).

Because Milford did not open its forum for religious uses, its exclusion of the Club was a permissible subject-matter restriction. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393 (1993) (upholding exclusion of religious film series where school’s forum was limited to “social, civic, and recreational uses”). The Club’s activities—prayer, proselytizing, and Bible instruction—are not comparable to the secular character-building programs of the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, or 4-H. Milford reasonably distinguished between secular and religious uses without discriminating against any viewpoint. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (forum restrictions need only be reasonable, not the most desirable).

**II. Permitting the Good News Club’s activities would have violated the Establishment Clause, providing an independent justification for Milford’s decision.**

Even if Milford’s exclusion were subject to stricter scrutiny, the school’s decision was compelled by the Establishment Clause. Elementary school children are a uniquely impressionable audience. *See Edwards v. Aguillard*, 482 U.S. 578, 584 (1987) (recognizing “the State’s power to preserve the integrity of the school’s curricular mission”). The Good News Club’s meetings were held on school premises immediately after the school day, and the Club targeted young children ages six to twelve. The challenged activities included prayer, Bible memorization, and invitations to “accept Jesus as your personal Savior”—a message that a reasonable observer would perceive as school endorsement of religion. *See Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308 (2000) (inquiry is whether “an objective observer” would perceive state endorsement).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has recognized that “the State may ‘preserv[e] the atmosphere of religious tolerance and pluralism that is conducive to the educational process.’” *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 n.10 (1981) (quoting *Tilton v. Richardson*, 403 U.S. 672, 685 (1971)). In the elementary school context, “the potential for undue influence is far greater than at the university level.” *Id.* at 274 n.14. Milford had a compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation, and the exclusion of the Club was narrowly tailored to serve that interest. *See St. John’s Lutheran Church v. Home Ins. Co.*, 520 U.S. 123, 128 (1997) (government may avoid establishment risks by excluding religious worship from a limited public forum).

**III. Milford’s policy did not constitute viewpoint discrimination.**

The Good News Club argues that its message is a “religious viewpoint” on the secular subject of morals and character. But this argument conflates subject matter with viewpoint. The Club’s activities—prayer, Bible instruction, and invitations to accept Christ—are not a viewpoint but a subject: religious worship and instruction. As this Court recognized in *Rosenberger*, viewpoint discrimination occurs when the government “targets not a subject matter, but particular views taken by speakers on a subject.” *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829. Here, Milford did not exclude the Club because of its religious perspective on morals; it excluded the Club because its proposed use was religious worship and instruction—a subject matter outside the forum’s boundaries. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94 (distinguishing between teaching about a subject and engaging in religious worship). The dissent’s claim that “the subject matter does not change when it is informed by viewpoints that are sectarian,” *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 514 (Jacobs, J., dissenting), proves too much: if correct, any religious group could demand access to a limited public forum by characterizing its worship as a viewpoint on any secular topic. The First Amendment does not mandate that result. *See Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 809-10 (government may reserve a forum “for certain groups or for the discussion of certain topics”).

Accordingly, Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was constitutional. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Second Circuit.
