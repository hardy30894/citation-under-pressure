**ARGUMENT**

The Internal Revenue Code requires the government to assess a tax against each separate taxpayer within three years of the return’s filing. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). An assessment “shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer,” 26 U.S.C. § 6203, and must “provide identification of the taxpayer,” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. A “taxpayer” is “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” expressly includes both an “individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because each Debtor is a distinct taxpayer with a separate identification number, the assessments against the Partnership extended the ten-year collection period of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) only as to the Partnership itself.

This Court has long recognized that an assessment operates like a judgment against the identified taxpayer. *Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247, 260 (1935). The Ninth Circuit therefore correctly held that the government may not collect directly from unassessed partners. *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (lien invalid where assessment failed to name the individual partner). The Seventh Circuit reached the same result on materially identical facts, noting that assessments had been made against both the partnership and the partners. *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562 (7th Cir. 1995).

The government’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. There the partner had already paid the tax; the court merely refused a refund. The district court opinion affirmed in *Young* expressly acknowledged that, absent payment or judgment, “the only reason for naming such individual … is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action.” 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). No such payment or judgment exists here.

California law supplies no alternative route to collection. A judgment against a partnership “is not by itself a judgment against a partner,” and a partner’s personal assets may not be reached “unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The government obtained no such judgment within the three-year period of § 6501(a). Because the claims are therefore “unenforceable against the debtor,” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), the bankruptcy court properly disallowed them.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
