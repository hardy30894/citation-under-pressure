ARGUMENT

The district court's judgment in favor of Feltmann should be reversed because the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support her claims of sex discrimination and retaliatory discharge under Title VII, the MHRA, and Missouri common law.

First, Feltmann failed to establish a prima facie case of sex discrimination. The record shows that Rich's criticisms of Feltmann's sales methods were legitimate and not pretextual. As the court held in Texas & N.O.R. Co. v. Brotherhood of Ry. & S.S. Clerks, 281 U.S. 548, 559 (1930), an employer's good faith criticisms of an employee's performance can justify adverse employment actions. Here, Rich's memos to Feltmann regarding her "work ethic" and use of demo cars were based on her poor sales performance and excessive use of company resources. See also NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 45-46 (1937) (employer's right to criticize employee performance is essential to efficient operation).

Moreover, Feltmann's claim that she was treated differently than male consultants is not supported by the record. As the court noted in FTC v. Gratz, 253 U.S. 421, 427 (1920), a plaintiff must show that the defendant's actions were motivated by a discriminatory intent. Here, Rich's actions were motivated by Feltmann's poor sales performance and failure to follow company policies. The fact that male consultants may have occasionally taken their demo cars out of the St. Louis area does not establish that Rich's memo to Feltmann was discriminatory.

Feltmann's retaliatory discharge claim also fails. The record shows that Sieben took prompt action in response to Feltmann's complaint about Anzalone's vulgar comment. As the court held in Pennsylvania R. Co. v. United States, 323 U.S. 612, 616 (1945), an employer's good faith response to an employee's complaint can defeat a retaliation claim. Here, Sieben's owners met with Feltmann, reprimanded Anzalone, and warned him that future incidents would result in termination.

Furthermore, Feltmann's discharge was not causally linked to her complaint about Anzalone. As the court noted in Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52, 119 (1926), a plaintiff must show a clear causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse employment action. Here, Feltmann's discharge was based on her poor sales performance and failure to follow company policies, not her complaint about Anzalone. See also United States v. Wm. Cramp & Sons Ship & Engine Bldg. Co., 206 U.S. 118, 128 (1907) (employer's decision to discharge employee must be based on legitimate reasons).

Additionally, the court in Adair v. United States, 208 U.S. 161, 174-75 (1908), established that an employer has the right to discharge an employee for any reason, as long as it is not discriminatory or retaliatory. Sieben's decision to discharge Feltmann was based on her poor performance, not her sex or her complaint about Anzalone.

The court in Hitchman Coal & Coke Co. v. Mitchell, 245 U.S. 229, 251 (1917), also held that an employer's actions are presumed to be lawful unless shown to be otherwise. Feltmann failed to show that Sieben's actions were unlawful or discriminatory.

Finally, as the court noted in Virginian Ry. Co. v. System Federation No. 40, 300 U.S. 515, 558 (1937), an employer's rules and policies are entitled to a presumption of validity. Sieben's policies regarding demo car use and sales performance were reasonable and applied equally to all consultants.

In conclusion, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support Feltmann's claims of sex discrimination and retaliatory discharge. Sieben's actions were legitimate and non-discriminatory, and the district court's judgment should be reversed.
