**ARGUMENT**

**Fourth-Prong and Third-Prong Plain-Error Review Permits Consideration of the Entire Record, Including Matters Outside the Trial Evidence.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct a "plain error that affects substantial rights" only if the error also "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993) (quoting *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985)). This Court has repeatedly instructed that Rule 52(b) review is not confined to the four corners of the trial transcript, but permits — indeed requires — courts of appeals to consult the whole record to determine whether an unpreserved error actually prejudiced the defendant or undermined the proceeding's legitimacy.

In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), this Court squarely held that "a silent defendant has the burden to satisfy the plain-error rule and ... a reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights." *Id.* at 59. The Court reasoned that because the defendant who fails to object bears the burden of demonstrating prejudice, fairness requires that the government likewise be permitted to point to record evidence — wherever it appears — that forecloses any reasonable probability of a different outcome. *Id.* at 74-75. Limiting review to only the evidence a jury actually heard would artificially handicap that inquiry and reward defendants who withhold objections strategically.

This principle finds direct application in the context of *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), which held that a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) requires the government to prove the defendant "knew he belonged to the relevant category of persons barred from possessing a firearm." *Id.* at 2200. Where, as here, a defendant fails to object to the omission of a knowledge-of-status instruction, he must satisfy all four prongs of plain-error review, including the requirement that the error affected his "substantial rights." *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734. That showing demands a "reasonable probability that, but for the error," the result of the proceeding would have been different. *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 83 (2004) (internal quotation marks omitted).

This Court's decision in *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), confirms that such an inquiry properly draws on evidence beyond what was presented to the jury. There, reviewing an indictment defect for plain error, the Court found the fourth prong unsatisfied because "the evidence that the drug quantities involved in the conspiracy exceeded the statutory maximums was 'overwhelming' and 'essentially uncontroverted,'" a conclusion drawn from the full record rather than the charging instrument alone. *Id.* at 632-33. Similarly, in *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court declined to notice a forfeited materiality error because the evidence of materiality was "overwhelming," such that reversal would not "seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* at 470. Nothing in *Johnson* or *Cotton* restricted the reviewing court's gaze to only the evidence formally placed before the jury; both decisions examined the record as a whole, including undisputed background facts, to assess whether the error mattered.

The same logic governs Rehaif claims raised for the first time on appeal. A defendant convicted of being a felon in possession who has, in fact, served substantial prior terms of imprisonment for multiple felonies cannot plausibly claim unawareness of his status. Where the record — including the presentence report, prior plea colloquies, and undisputed criminal history — reveals that a defendant knew he was a felon, there is no "reasonable probability" that a properly instructed jury would have reached a different verdict. *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 83. To ignore that reality and confine the inquiry to the trial evidence alone would convert plain-error review into a formalistic exercise divorced from the actual likelihood of prejudice — precisely what *Olano* and *Vonn* forbid.

This approach also respects the structure and purpose of plain-error review. As this Court explained in *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258 (2010), the plain-error standard exists to ensure that appellate courts do not lightly upset final judgments for errors that, viewed in context, caused no real harm. *Id.* at 262. And in *Rosales-Mireles v. United States*, 138 S. Ct. 1897 (2018), the Court reaffirmed that the fourth prong retains "discretion" for courts of appeals to consider "the particular facts and circumstances of the case," a standard incompatible with a rule that blinds the court to obviously probative facts simply because they were not formally admitted at trial. *Id.* at 1908-09.

Petitioner's proposed rule — that a reviewing court may look only at what the jury heard — finds no support in *Olano*, *Vonn*, *Cotton*, or *Johnson*, all of which examined the broader record to determine whether forfeited errors caused genuine harm. The Eleventh Circuit therefore correctly considered Greer's undisputed criminal history, including his prior felony convictions and years of imprisonment, in concluding that he could not show a reasonable probability that a jury, properly instructed under *Rehaif*, would have acquitted him, nor that the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial. This Court should affirm.
