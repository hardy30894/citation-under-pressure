### Argument

The judgment should be affirmed because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal does not warrant habeas relief when the defendant already knew of that right and chose not to appeal. Habeas corpus is not a vehicle for correcting every procedural omission; it reaches only those errors that render the proceeding fundamentally unfair or unlawful. See *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202–03 (1830); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 181 (1947) (“The writ of habeas corpus is not to be used as a substitute for the regular appellate process.”). A petitioner therefore must show more than a bare violation of a rule; he must show prejudice of a kind that justifies collateral relief. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (“collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a rule).

Rule 32(a)(2) was intended to safeguard a defendant from unknowingly forfeiting an appeal. That purpose is fully satisfied when the defendant already knows of the right and intentionally declines to invoke it. The Court has long rejected automatic reversal for nonprejudicial errors. In *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), the Court explained that appellate intervention is not warranted for technical error that does not affect substantial rights. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760 (1946), the Court held that an error does not justify setting aside a judgment unless it had a substantial influence on the outcome. And in *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), the Court framed the harmless-error inquiry in terms of whether the error had any real impact. Here, the district court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero knew of his appeal right at sentencing. On that finding, the judge’s omission did not cause him to lose an appeal he otherwise would have taken.

That conclusion is reinforced by the basic distinction between a procedural lapse and a miscarriage of justice. In *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942), the Court made clear that errors must be evaluated in context rather than treated as automatic grounds for reversal. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), likewise recognizes that only errors affecting substantial rights justify relief. And *Riddle v. Dyche*, 262 U.S. 333, 335–36 (1923), warns against undoing judgments for defects that do not undermine the legality of the conviction or sentence. A defendant who knew of his appeal right and chose not to appeal cannot plausibly claim that the omitted advisement changed the result or deprived him of any opportunity the rule was designed to secure.

Petitioner’s contrary view would transform Rule 32 into an automatic-vacatur rule, untethered from prejudice. Nothing in this Court’s precedents supports that approach. To the contrary, the Court has long recognized that final judgments should not be lightly disturbed. *Baldwin v. Traveling Men’s Assn.*, 283 U.S. 522, 525 (1931), emphasizes the necessity of finality; *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67–68 (1914), similarly limits postjudgment relief to serious defects going to the validity of the proceeding. Allowing habeas relief whenever the district court omitted an appeal advisement, even when the defendant already knew of the right, would grant collateral review a function it was never meant to serve.

The district court’s factual finding that Peguero knew of his right to appeal is also entitled to deference. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). Once that finding is accepted, there is no basis to conclude that the omission had any effect on his decision not to appeal, and no basis for habeas relief. The rule’s purpose was satisfied, the omission caused no prejudice, and the judgment therefore should stand.

For these reasons, the decision below should be affirmed.
