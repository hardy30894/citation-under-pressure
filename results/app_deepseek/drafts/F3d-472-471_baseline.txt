**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because a Genuine Dispute Exists as to Whether Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the FMLA.**

The FMLA entitles an employee to leave for a “serious health condition” that renders the employee unable to perform the functions of his position. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D). To invoke FMLA protection, an employee must provide notice sufficient to make the employer aware that the absence is due to a qualifying condition. *Stevenson v. Hyre Elec. Co.*, 505 F.3d 720, 724-25 (7th Cir. 2007). The notice need not explicitly invoke the FMLA or use legal terminology; it is enough that the employer receives enough information to conclude that the employee’s condition might be FMLA-qualifying. *Id.* at 725.

Here, Burnett repeatedly supplied Habitat with information that, viewed in the light most favorable to him, plainly indicated a serious health condition. In October 2003, he told Supervisor Polo about his “weak bladder” and that he was seeing a doctor. In December 2003, Burnett gave Polo a doctor’s order for blood tests, explained he had high PSA and cholesterol levels, and said he needed to see a urologist. On December 16, Burnett explicitly compared his condition to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer, stating he felt “sick” and “was sick.” Habitat’s own internal email on that day acknowledged Burnett should be granted sick leave for upcoming appointments. In January 2004, Burnett provided a document describing prostate biopsy procedures and their indications, including elevated PSA as a screening test for prostate cancer.

This cumulative information was sufficient to alert Habitat that Burnett’s condition might be a serious health condition requiring ongoing medical care. A reasonable jury could find that Habitat knew or should have known that Burnett’s absences and requests for leave were FMLA-qualifying. *See Burnett v. LFW Inc.*, 472 F.3d 471, 478 (7th Cir. 2006) (holding that an employee’s statements about prostate problems and a biopsy can constitute sufficient notice). The district court’s conclusion that Burnett failed to provide notice because he did not use the phrase “serious health condition” or explicitly link his absences to the FMLA ignores the well-settled principle that “the critical question is whether the information imparted to the employer is sufficient to reasonably apprise it of the employee’s need to take FMLA leave.” *Stevenson*, 505 F.3d at 724 (internal quotation marks omitted).

**II. The District Court Also Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Burnett’s ADA Claim Because He Provided Sufficient Notice of a Disability.**

Under the ADA, an employee must inform the employer that he has a disability and request a reasonable accommodation. *Jovanovic v. In-Sink-Erator Div. of Emerson Elec. Co.*, 201 F.3d 894, 899 (7th Cir. 2000). However, the employee need not use specific terminology; “[t]he employer must know of both the disability and the employee’s desire for an accommodation or at least know enough to trigger a duty to inquire further.” *Byrne v. Avon Prods., Inc.*, 328 F.3d 379, 381 (7th Cir. 2003).

Burnett’s communications with Habitat satisfied this standard. He told Polo about his “weak bladder” and high PSA, indicating an underlying prostate condition. He provided medical documentation and discussed the need for diagnostic procedures. Shortly thereafter, Burnett sought a transfer to a midnight shift—a request that, given his disclosed medical condition, can be reasonably understood as a request for an accommodation to manage his symptoms. When Polo denied the transfer, Burnett pursued a union grievance, further signaling his need for a change due to his health.

A reasonable jury could find that Habitat had enough information to know Burnett had a disability (prostate cancer or a related condition) and that he sought an accommodation—or at least that Habitat had a duty to engage in an interactive process to clarify his needs. *See Bultemeyer v. Fort Wayne Cmty. Schs.*, 100 F.3d 1281, 1285 (7th Cir. 1996) (holding that employer must make a reasonable effort to determine appropriate accommodation once disability is known or suspected). The district court’s ruling improperly required Burnett to explicitly articulate a request for accommodation, which the ADA does not demand.

**III. Conclusion**

Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to Burnett, a genuine issue of material fact exists as to whether he provided adequate notice under both the FMLA and the ADA. Accordingly, this Court should reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
