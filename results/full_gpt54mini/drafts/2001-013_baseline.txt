**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because the Occupational Safety and Health Act applies unless another federal agency has actually exercised statutory authority over the specific working conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This Court has repeatedly held that § 4(b)(1) requires more than a general grant of maritime authority; it preempts OSHA only where another agency has exercised authority to prescribe or enforce standards “affecting occupational safety and health” for the relevant working conditions. *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 241-42 (2002); *Southern Pacific Transportation Co. v. Usery*, 539 F.2d 386, 390-91 (5th Cir. 1976).

The Fifth Circuit erred by treating the Coast Guard’s broad regulatory responsibility over vessels as automatic and exclusive preemption. That approach conflicts with this Court’s instruction that § 4(b)(1) turns on “actual exercise” of authority, not potential authority. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243-44; *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119, 123-24 (2d Cir. 1978) (agency must have exercised authority over the working conditions in question). The Coast Guard had authority to investigate the accident here, but investigation is not the same as promulgating and enforcing occupational safety standards. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 246-47; *Herman v. Tidewater Pacific, Inc.*, 160 F.3d 1239, 1244-45 (9th Cir. 1998).

Nor does the Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels displace OSHA. Congress expressly authorized the Coast Guard to issue only specified safety requirements for uninspected vessels, including fire extinguishers, life preservers, and certain ventilation and signaling equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102; *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 245-46. That narrow regulatory scheme does not amount to comprehensive regulation of all occupational hazards aboard such vessels. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 246-48. Indeed, this Court has recognized that where another agency regulates only a subset of hazards, OSHA remains free to fill the gaps. *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d at 124-25; *Southern Pacific Transportation Co.*, 539 F.2d at 391-92.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), cannot stand against this Court’s decision in *Chao*. Those cases reasoned from the premise that the Coast Guard’s maritime authority created an “industry-wide exemption,” *Donovan*, 720 F.2d at 826-29, but this Court rejected that categorical reading of § 4(b)(1). *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 244-45. Section 4(b)(1) does not create a vessel-wide exemption; it displaces OSHA only to the extent another agency has actually regulated the particular working condition at issue. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 242-44.

That principle is dispositive here. The citations concern hazards arising from drilling operations aboard a barge in territorial waters—hazards not comprehensively governed by Coast Guard standards for uninspected vessels. *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 245-48. Because the Coast Guard had not exercised authority over those specific occupational safety conditions, OSHA retained jurisdiction under the Act. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 248-49.

The government’s position also accords with the OSH Act’s purpose of ensuring “so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions.” 29 U.S.C. § 651(b); *Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall*, 445 U.S. 1, 12 (1980). Congress chose a presumption of OSHA coverage, subject only to specific displacements under § 4(b)(1). *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 243-44; *Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Commission*, 548 F.2d 1052, 1055-56 (D.C. Cir. 1976).

Because the Coast Guard had not exercised comprehensive authority over the cited hazards, OSHA’s citations were lawful. The Court should affirm OSHA’s jurisdiction and uphold the citations.
