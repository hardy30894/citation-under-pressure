The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to apply this heightened standard, known as the "reasoned choice" standard, violated Moran's due process rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's competency to stand trial is not the same as their competency to waive constitutional rights. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that a trial court must hold a hearing to determine a defendant's competency to stand trial if there is a good faith doubt about their competency. Similarly, in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), the Court defined the standard for competency to stand trial as whether the defendant has a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and is capable of assisting their counsel.

However, as the Ninth Circuit correctly noted, the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights is higher than that required to stand trial. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150 (1966), the Court implied that a separate determination of competency is required for a defendant to waive their right to counsel. The Ninth Circuit has consistently held that a defendant must have the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the alternatives available to them to waive constitutional rights validly. See, e.g., Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973); Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512 (9th Cir. 1981) (citing Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948)).

The trial court's failure to apply the "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was error. The court's inquiry into Moran's competency was limited to a determination of whether he was competent to stand trial, which is not sufficient to ensure that Moran was capable of making a valid waiver of his constitutional rights. See Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105 (1954). The psychiatric evaluations conducted prior to Moran's plea hearing were also limited to determining his competency to stand trial and did not address his ability to make a reasoned choice among the available alternatives.

The state court's post-conviction hearing did not cure this error, as it applied the wrong legal standard to evaluate Moran's competency. The state court's reliance on the psychiatric evaluations, which were conducted to determine Moran's competency to stand trial, was misplaced. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938) (emphasizing the importance of a knowing and intelligent waiver of constitutional rights).

In conclusion, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to apply this standard violated Moran's due process rights. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's decision and ensure that defendants are afforded the protections guaranteed by the Due Process Clause.
