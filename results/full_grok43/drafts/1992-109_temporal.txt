The Due Process Clause does not impose a distinct competency standard for a defendant's decision to plead guilty or waive counsel. In Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), this Court held that the test for competency is whether the defendant "has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Id. at 402. That formulation governs the capacity to stand trial and extends to the entry of a plea or the relinquishment of counsel, for each requires the same threshold of comprehension and assistance.

Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirms that due process is satisfied when a court applies this standard and conducts a hearing if the evidence raises a bona fide doubt about the defendant's ability to understand the proceedings or assist in his defense. Id. at 385. The opinion nowhere prescribes an elevated test keyed to the gravity of the choice presented.

Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), likewise treats the validity of a guilty plea as turning on whether the record affirmatively shows that the plea was made voluntarily and intelligently. Id. at 242. The Court did not announce a separate or more demanding measure of mental capacity; it simply required that the same constitutional safeguards applicable to the trial itself be observed at the plea stage.

Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), supplies the governing rule for waiver of counsel: the accused must "competently and intelligently" forgo the right. Id. at 468. That language aligns with the Dusky formulation and does not demand a heightened inquiry into the defendant's ability to make a "reasoned choice" among strategic alternatives.

Because the Constitution equates competency to stand trial with competency to plead guilty or waive counsel, the trial court's application of the Dusky standard fully satisfied due process. No additional or more rigorous test is required.
