**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” REQUIRES KNOWLEDGE OF BOTH THE TRANSACTION AND ITS ALLEGED IMPRUDENCE.**

The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) is inconsistent with this Court’s precedent and the statutory text. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), this Court held that “actual knowledge” is triggered by knowledge of the transaction constituting the alleged breach, not knowledge of its legal consequences. *Id.* at 991-92. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case departs from that principle by requiring knowledge of the imprudence itself—a standard unsupported by the statute or prior precedent.

The statute’s plain text requires only “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not knowledge of its legal characterization. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit’s holding conflates the breach (the imprudent investment) with the plaintiff’s legal conclusion about its imprudence. This misreading ignores that a plaintiff need not understand the legal significance of a transaction to have “actual knowledge” of it. *See Blanton*, 760 F.2d at 991-92; *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (holding that actual knowledge is triggered by knowledge of the transaction, not its legal effect).

II. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S RULING CONTRADICTS CONGRESS’S REPEAL OF THE CONSTRUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE PROVISION IN 1987.**

Before 1987, § 1113 included both an “actual knowledge” and a “constructive knowledge” provision. *See* Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. Congress repealed the constructive knowledge provision, leaving only the actual knowledge requirement. *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively reinstates a constructive knowledge standard by requiring plaintiffs to have understood the legal implications of disclosed information—a standard Congress explicitly rejected. *See Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523, 535 (2015) (noting Congress’s intent to limit the statute of limitations to cases of actual knowledge).

III. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING DISRUPTS SETTLED EXPECTATIONS AND ENCOURAGES LITIGATION OVER MERITORIOUS DEFENSES.**

The Ninth Circuit’s construction invites plaintiffs to avoid the limitations period by claiming ignorance of legal conclusions, even when they had full knowledge of the underlying facts. This undermines the purpose of statutes of limitations—to provide repose and prevent stale claims. *See United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 117 (1979) (noting that statutes of limitations “promote justice by preventing surprises through the revival of claims that have been allowed to slumber until evidence has been lost”). The Ninth Circuit’s rule will encourage plaintiffs to delay filing claims, knowing they can later argue they lacked “actual knowledge” of a legal conclusion.

IV. **THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT SULYMA HAD ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE OF THE ALLEGED BREACH.**

Sulyma undisputedly received multiple disclosures detailing Intel’s investment strategy, including the allocation of retirement funds to alternative investments. These disclosures provided him with actual knowledge of the transactions constituting the alleged breach. *See Blanton*, 760 F.2d at 991-92. The fact that Sulyma chose not to read or recall the disclosures does not negate his actual knowledge. *See Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that actual knowledge is triggered by receipt of information, regardless of whether the plaintiff acted on it).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that Sulyma’s claims are time-barred under § 1113(2).
