**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of the Internal Revenue Code and settled principles of tax law. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Government may not collect a partnership’s unpaid taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The Government’s failure to do so, and the resulting expiration of the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a), bars any collection attempt against the partners, even if the partnership was timely assessed. This conclusion is compelled by the plain language of the Code, the regulatory framework, and binding precedent.

**I. THE GOVERNMENT MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS PARTNERS TO COLLECT FROM THEM; THE PARTNERSHIP ASSESSMENT DOES NOT EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS FOR INDIVIDUALS**

The Internal Revenue Code treats partnerships and individual partners as distinct taxpayers. Section 7701(a)(1) defines “person” to include “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation,” and § 7701(a)(14) defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax.” Because each partner is a separate “person” and “taxpayer,” the Government must assess each partner individually to trigger the ten-year collection period under § 6502(a). The statute does not permit the Government to extend the limitations period for individual partners merely by assessing the partnership.

This interpretation is reinforced by the regulatory requirement that an assessment must “provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The IRS’s assessment against the partnership named only the entity, not the individual partners. Thus, no valid assessment was made against the partners themselves. Without such an assessment, the statute of limitations for collecting from the partners remains fixed at three years under § 6501(a). The Government concedes that it did not assess the partners within that window. The mere fact that the partnership was assessed does not alter this result.

The Government’s contrary argument—that the partnership assessment “extends” the limitations period to the partners—confuses the nature of liability with the procedural requirements for collection. The Government may not collect from a taxpayer without first assessing the tax or bringing a timely action. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The partnership assessment did not constitute a valid assessment against the partners, nor did it constitute a judicial action. The Government’s attempt to collect from the partners through a bankruptcy claim is therefore barred by the expiration of the three-year period.

**II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING IS CONSISTENT WITH BINDING PRECEDENT**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision is fully supported by our precedent. In *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), we affirmed a district court’s holding that a tax lien against a partner’s property was invalid because the IRS had never assessed the partner individually. The district court held that “a lien does not exist against a particular individual’s property pursuant to § 6321 and 6322 unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against him under § 6203.” 169 F.Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958). We affirmed on other grounds, but the district court’s reasoning—that an individual must be assessed before the Government may collect from them—remains sound and binding.

Similarly, in *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), we affirmed a district court’s refusal to grant a refund to a partner who had paid his share of a partnership tax. The court held that the partner was liable under state law, but emphasized that “it is unnecessary and superfluous to name the individual partners in the assessment in order to create liability” *only because the tax had already been paid*. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054. The court explicitly acknowledged that the Government would have needed to assess the partner or obtain a judgment to collect the tax in the absence of payment. Id. at 76,050. Thus, *Young* does not support the Government’s position; it confirms that individual liability does not eliminate the need for individual assessment or judgment.

The Government’s reliance on *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. 868 F.Supp. 1070, 1071 (S.D. Ind. 1994). The Seventh Circuit did not address whether a partnership assessment alone could extend the limitations period to partners. The case is therefore inapposite. The Ninth Circuit correctly distinguished *Wright* on this ground.

**III. CALIFORNIA LAW DOES NOT CREATE A RIGHT TO COLLECT WITHOUT JUDGMENT**

The Government argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this allows the Government to collect directly from partners. But California law does not permit such collection without a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.” This is not a mere technicality—it is the law. As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, the Government must obtain a judgment against each partner before it may collect from their personal assets.

The Government has not obtained such a judgment. The three-year limitations period under § 6501(a) has expired. The Government cannot now bring a new action or obtain a judgment. The bankruptcy court properly disallowed the claims because they are “unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor” under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).
