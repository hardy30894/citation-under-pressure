The court below held that the First Amendment permits a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) whenever a reasonable speaker would foresee that his statement would be interpreted as a threat. That standard is irreconcilable with this Court’s repeated holdings that true threats—the category of speech that may be punished—must be defined by the speaker’s subjective intent. Because the district court instructed the jury on an objective standard, and the government never proved that Elonis meant to communicate a threat, his conviction must be reversed.

I. Section 875(c) requires proof of a subjective intent to threaten.

A. The statute must be read against the background presumption of mens rea.

Section 875(c) criminalizes transmitting “any communication containing any threat.” It does not specify a mens rea. Under this Court’s “longstanding presumption” that “Congress intends to require a defendant to possess a culpable mental state,” Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994), the statute must be construed to require a culpable state of mind. As Morissette v. United States explains, “the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion; it is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will.” 342 U.S. 246, 250–51 (1952). The presumption of mens rea has added force where the statute regulates speech. United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994) (applying the presumption to a statute criminalizing distribution of sexually explicit materials). Section 875(c) must therefore be read to require the defendant to intend to make a threat, not merely to utter words a listener might perceive as threatening.

B. The true-threats exception requires a subjective intent to threaten.

The First Amendment permits punishment of “true threats.” In Virginia v. Black, this Court defined a true threat as a statement “where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). That inquiry focuses on the speaker’s purpose, not on how a reasonable listener would react. Black further held that “intimidation” is a true threat only when “the speaker directs a threat to a person or group of persons with the intent of placing the victim in fear of bodily harm or death.” Id. at 360. These formulations require subjective intent. The court below instead applied an objective “reasonable speaker” test derived from United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), treating Black as not controlling. That was error. Black’s language—“means to communicate” and “with the intent”—is irreconcilable with negligence or foreseeability. See United States v. Bagdasarian, 652 F.3d 1113, 1123 (9th Cir. 2011) (holding that a true threat “must be made with the intent to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence” (quoting Black, 538 U.S. at 359)).

C. The objective standard criminalizes protected speech.

Without a subjective intent requirement, § 875(c) will sweep in speech protected by the First Amendment. In Watts v. United States, this Court reversed a conviction under § 875(c) because the speaker’s words were “political hyperbole,” not a “true threat.” 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). Watts’ protection would be meaningless under an objective standard: a reasonable listener could take a statement literally. The Court instead required that the communication be “interpreted in its context,” id., which includes what the speaker intended to convey.

This Court has consistently required a subjective intent element when punishing speech that advocates lawlessness. In Brandenburg v. Ohio, the Court held that government may not “forbid or proscribe advocacy of the use of force or of law violation except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969). The phrase “directed to” demands a subjective purpose. Similarly, in Hess v. Indiana, the Court struck down a conviction because the State failed to prove that “the speaker intended to produce imminent disorder,” rather than merely “advocat[ing] illegal action” at some indefinite future time. 414 U.S. 105, 108–09 (1973). True threats are not an exception to this principle; they too must be intended by the speaker.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule would punish a defendant for speech he did not intend to threaten—for example, rap lyrics, dark humor, or emotional venting. That is precisely what happened here. Elonis testified that his Facebook posts were rap lyrics and artistic expressions. The objective standard allowed the jury to convict based on the listeners’ reactions, not on Elonis’ intent. A listener’s fear, while often genuine, cannot substitute for the defendant’s constitutionally required mental state. The Government must prove that Elonis meant to communicate a serious expression of an intent to inflict harm. Because the jury was never asked to find that, his convictions must be reversed.
