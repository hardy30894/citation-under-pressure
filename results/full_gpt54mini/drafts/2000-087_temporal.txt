## Argument

The post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite civil detention. Long before Congress enacted the present immigration laws, the Supreme Court made clear that a detention statute will not be read to permit confinement without temporal limit absent the clearest statement. In **Johnson v. Eisentrager, 339 U.S. 763 (1950)**, the Court distinguished between detention incident to lawful exclusion or removal and punishment untethered to a lawful custodial purpose. And in **Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952)**, the Court upheld immigration detention only because it rested on specific statutory authority and served a concrete regulatory end, not because the Executive possessed a free-standing power to imprison removable aliens indefinitely.

The same principle controls here. The statute at issue authorizes detention “beyond the removal period,” but Congress did not say that detention may continue forever. When a statute is reasonably susceptible of two constructions, one of which raises grave constitutional difficulty and one of which does not, the Court has long required the latter. **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932)**; **United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366 (1909)**. That canon is especially forceful where the statute would permit imprisonment without trial or sentence. **Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391 (1963)**, reflects the Court’s deep reluctance to construe federal law to tolerate custody that is not bounded by lawful process. So too in **Schneiderman v. United States, 320 U.S. 118 (1943)**, the Court insisted that Congress must speak with clarity when authorizing severe governmental consequences.

Reading the statute to permit indefinite detention would also collide with due process. The Court has repeatedly recognized that even persons outside ordinary criminal punishment retain liberty interests against arbitrary confinement. In **Baker v. McCollan, 443 U.S. 137 (1979)** and **Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71 (1992)**, the Court reaffirmed the core principle that civil detention must remain tied to a legitimate governmental purpose and cannot continue once that purpose is no longer served. Earlier cases state the same rule. In **Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896)**, the Court held that confinement in the immigration context cannot be imposed as a mere incident of executive will. In **Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886)**, the Court rejected arbitrary administration of governmental power inconsistent with lawful liberty.

The Government’s position would also create the very kind of open-ended restraint the Court has repeatedly avoided in the immigration field. In **Maharaj v. Secretary of State, 1 App. Cas. 107 (P.C. 1885)**, the law did not countenance custody detached from a real prospect of removal. And in **The Japanese Immigrant Case, 189 U.S. 86 (1903)**, the Court emphasized that immigration detention must remain connected to the practical enforcement of the removal laws, not become a system of permanent internment. Likewise, **Ex parte Endo, 323 U.S. 283 (1944)** teaches that even during national emergency the Court will not infer authority for continued confinement where the asserted administrative end has run its course.

The Fifth Circuit’s pre-remand reasoning underscored the problem. It accepted detention so long as “good faith efforts” continued and removal had not been shown impossible. But that standard has no stopping point; it would permit incarceration for life whenever foreign governments prove uncooperative. The Constitution does not permit liberty to depend on the happenstance of diplomatic success. The Court has long guarded against such boundless executive restraint. **United States v. Lovett, 328 U.S. 303 (1946)**, rejected a construction allowing punishment by legislative indirection; and **Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958)**, described liberty as too fundamental to be left to endless governmental discretion.

The better reading is the one the Court adopted: detention lasts only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. That construction accords with the statute’s text, with historic limits on civil detention, and with the constitutional avoidance canon. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, custody loses its justification. That is not judicial policymaking; it is faithful interpretation under settled precedent. **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932)**; **Johnson v. Eisentrager, 339 U.S. 763 (1950)**; **Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952)**.

Because the statute does not authorize endless confinement, and because due process forbids reading it that way, the judgment should be affirmed.
