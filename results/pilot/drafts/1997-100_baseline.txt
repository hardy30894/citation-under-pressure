**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *U.S. Const., amend. V; Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional guarantee is not limited to the trial on guilt or innocence; it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense” after an acquittal or a reversal based on insufficient evidence. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings involving prior conviction allegations—directly conflicts with this settled principle and must be reversed.

The Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a defendant is subjected to a second prosecution for the same offense after a prior conviction has been reversed on appeal. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). In *Bullington*, the Court held that a defendant could not be retried on the issue of whether he was a persistent offender after the state’s initial attempt to enhance his sentence was reversed due to insufficient evidence. The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding in a capital case “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 439. The Court reasoned that the sentencing hearing, like a trial on guilt, “involves the determination of facts that are critical to the imposition of a greater punishment,” and that the defendant has a legitimate interest in avoiding the risk of multiple prosecutions for the same offense. *Id.* at 440.

The California Supreme Court attempts to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it was a capital case. But the Court has never held that *Bullington*’s reasoning is limited to capital proceedings. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), the Court observed that *Bullington* and *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), were “capital cases,” and that the reasoning in those cases was “based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Id.* at 392. This language, however, was not a holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply in noncapital cases. It was a contextual observation made in the course of a separate inquiry into whether a new rule of law should be applied retroactively under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). The Court did not purport to overrule or limit *Bullington*’s broader principle. Indeed, in *U.S. v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 155 (1997), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” without qualification based on the severity of the punishment. The Court has consistently held that the Clause applies whenever a defendant is subjected to a second prosecution for the same offense, regardless of whether the punishment is capital or noncapital.

The California Supreme Court further argues that because the state has granted defendants a statutory right to a jury trial on prior conviction allegations under Penal Code § 1025, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply. This argument is flawed. The existence of a statutory right does not diminish the force of a constitutional right. The fact that California has chosen to provide greater procedural protections in sentencing hearings—such as a jury trial, the right to cross-examine witnesses, and the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt—does not mean that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 245–246 (1949) (holding that due process does not require the same procedural protections at sentencing as at trial, but not that constitutional protections are absent). The Double Jeopardy Clause is not a creature of statute; it is a constitutional safeguard. Its application should not turn on whether a state chooses to provide additional procedural rights.

Moreover, the California Supreme Court’s distinction between capital and noncapital cases is legally unsound. The *Bullington* Court did not limit its analysis to capital cases. It focused on the nature of the proceeding: whether it involved the determination of facts that are “critical to the imposition of a greater punishment.” *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. The same is true in noncapital cases. A prior conviction allegation, when proven, can double a defendant’s sentence under California’s Three Strikes law. The enhancement is not a mere formality; it transforms a non-life sentence into a life sentence. The stakes are no less significant than in a capital case. The defendant’s liberty is at stake, and the state seeks to impose a harsher penalty based on the same factual determination that was previously rejected. To allow retrial under these circumstances is to permit the state to “re-litigate the same issue” after a reversal based on insufficient evidence—precisely what the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids.

The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that a sentencing proceeding is “not a trial” for purposes of constitutional protections. *See Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 606 (1967) (holding that due process does not require a hearing at sentencing, but not that double jeopardy does not apply). The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply in noncapital sentencing proceedings is therefore inconsistent with the Court’s precedent and undermines the fundamental protection against multiple prosecutions for the same offense.

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court must be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings when a prior conviction allegation is litigated and later reversed due to insufficient evidence. The state may not retry the defendant on the same factual issue after a reversal based on the prosecution’s failure to meet its burden of proof. The judgment of the Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
