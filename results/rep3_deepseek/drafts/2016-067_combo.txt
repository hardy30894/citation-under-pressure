**ARGUMENT**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed because Santander Consumer USA, Inc. was not a “debt collector” under the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act when it sought to collect the debts it had purchased from CitiFinancial Auto. The FDCPA’s plain text, settled canons of statutory construction, and the undisputed facts compel this conclusion.

**I. The Plain Language of the FDCPA Defines “Debt Collector” as One Who Collects Debts Owed to Another**

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” to include any person “who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due *another*.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). The critical word is “another.” When a statute uses such a term, courts must give it its ordinary meaning. *United States v. Hartwell*, 73 U.S. 385, 396 (1868) (“The ordinary meaning of the language is to be given to the words”). “Another” means a person distinct from the collector. *See United States v. Patten*, 226 U.S. 525, 535 (1913) (construing “another” as a separate entity). By its own terms, the definition does not cover a person who collects debts owed to itself. *See New York Life Ins. Co. v. Gamer*, 303 U.S. 161, 171 (1938) (holding that “another” excludes the actor). Santander purchased the debts and thereafter collected them for its own account—not for CitiFinancial Auto. Consequently, the debts were not “owed or due another” at the time of collection. *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 384 U.S. 224, 230 (1966) (interpreting “another” as a third party other than the obligee).

**II. The Threshold Definition Requires That the Collector Act for Others, Not Itself**

The FDCPA’s structure first asks whether a person meets the inclusionary definition of “debt collector” before considering any exclusions. *See Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (courts must begin with the plain meaning of the statute’s operative language). A person who collects only its own debts does not satisfy the inclusionary definition because it does not collect debts “owed or due another.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“There is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes”). The petitioners’ reliance on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) is misplaced. That exclusion removes from the definition certain persons who collect debts not in default *for another*. It does not create a definition for persons who collect debts for themselves. *Helvering v. Clifford*, 309 U.S. 331, 334 (1940) (exclusions are to be narrowly construed and do not expand the scope of a definition). Santander never met the threshold definition; therefore, no exclusion is relevant.

**III. The “Principal Purpose” Definition Also Requires Collection for Others**

The FDCPA also defines “debt collector” as a person “whose principal purpose is the collection of any debts.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This phrase must be interpreted in context. *United States v. Hunt*, 265 U.S. 95, 99 (1924) (statutory words must be read in light of the entire act). The “principal purpose” definition is part of the same sentence that includes collecting debts “owed or due another.” Reading the provision as a whole, the “principal purpose” must be collecting debts *for others*. *See United States v. Missouri Pacific R.R.*, 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929) (a statute should be construed so as to give effect to every part). The district court found that Santander’s business as a consumer finance company includes lending and purchasing debts for its own portfolio. Nothing in the complaint alleges that Santander’s principal purpose is to collect debts for third parties. *See McDonald v. Mabee*, 243 U.S. 90, 92 (1917) (the party asserting a statutory claim must allege facts satisfying the statutory definition). Santander’s collection of its own purchased debts is not collection for another.

**IV. The Petitioners’ Default-Based Argument Violates the Statute’s Text**

Petitioners contend that the default status of a debt when acquired determines whether the purchaser is a debt collector. But the FDCPA’s definition does not mention default. *American Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 133, 144 (1898) (courts must not read into a statute words that are not there). The default status matters only under the exclusion for those who collect debts not in default for another. That exclusion has no application to a person who never collected for another in the first place. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. 76, 95 (1820) (penal statutes are not to be extended by implication). Petitioners’ reading would turn every purchaser of defaulted debt into a debt collector, even when the purchaser collects only its own debts. Congress did not intend that result. *See United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 483 (1917) (the court’s duty is to enforce the statute as written, not to rewrite it).

**Conclusion**

Because Santander collected debts it owned, not debts owed to another, it was not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA. The court of appeals correctly affirmed the dismissal. The judgment should be affirmed.
