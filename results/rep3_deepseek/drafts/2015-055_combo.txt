**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson* is substantive and therefore applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This Court has long held that a court is bound to take notice of the law as it exists at the time of its decision. *United States v. Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103, 110 (1801). That principle governs here. *Johnson* did not announce a new procedural rule; it held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is void for vagueness. A sentence based on a void statute is a fundamental defect that cannot stand.

The retroactivity inquiry turns on the purpose of the new rule, the extent of reliance on the old rule, and the effect on the administration of justice. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 636 (1965); *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293, 297 (1967). Where the purpose of the new rule is to invalidate a statute that fails to give fair warning, the rule is substantive and must be applied retroactively. The Due Process Clause requires that a criminal statute give fair warning of the conduct that it makes a crime. *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 350 (1964). No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). A penal statute must be sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct will render them liable to its penalties. *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). The residual clause is such a statute. By holding it void, *Johnson* placed certain conduct beyond the power of the government to punish.

The rule is substantive, not procedural. It does not regulate the manner in which trials are conducted; it defines the scope of criminal liability. A sentence imposed under a void statute is no sentence at all. No one can be punished except for a violation of a valid law. *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2, 119 (1866). The government’s reliance on the old rule cannot justify continued punishment under a statute that violates due process. The purpose of the rule—to protect against arbitrary and unpredictable punishment—outweighs any reliance interest. *Linkletter*, 381 U.S. at 636. This Court has consistently distinguished procedural rules, which are not retroactive, from substantive rules, which are. *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244, 250 (1969). *Johnson* falls on the substantive side of that line.

Habeas corpus is the appropriate vehicle to challenge a detention based on a void statute. The writ exists to liberate those imprisoned without sufficient cause. *Ex parte Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807). It reaches convictions obtained in violation of the Constitution. *Frank v. Mangum*, 237 U.S. 309, 331 (1915); *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 91 (1923). A prisoner may challenge the legality of his detention on federal constitutional grounds. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 458 (1953). Welch’s sentence is illegal because it rests on a vague and invalid statute.

The lower court’s refusal to apply *Johnson* retroactively cannot be squared with these principles. The court denied Welch a certificate of appealability before *Johnson* was decided. Now that *Johnson* has been decided, Welch is entitled to relief. The rule is substantive, and it must be applied to his case.
