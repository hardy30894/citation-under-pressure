The Qualifications Clauses of the Constitution prescribe the exclusive criteria for membership in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; art. I, § 3, cl. 3. Those criteria may not be supplemented by the States. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (qualifications “are fixed in the Constitution and are exclusive”).

This Court has repeatedly confirmed that the power to define congressional qualifications resides solely in the federal charter. See McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819) (“the government of the Union” is “supreme” within its sphere); Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 211 (1824) (state power “cannot interfere with” federal authority over national objects). Because the Clauses speak directly to eligibility, any state attempt to enlarge them is void. Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 394 (1879) (state law “in conflict with the Constitution” yields).

The Arkansas amendment illustrates the prohibition. By rendering ineligible any person who has served three House terms or two Senate terms, Amendment 73 adds an age, citizenship, or residency requirement nowhere found in Article I. Such a restriction “imposes additional qualifications” and therefore “violates the Qualifications Clauses.” See Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, 788 n.9 (1983) (state ballot rules may not “impinge upon” federal eligibility standards).

The same principle governs ballot-access measures that operate as de facto qualifications. Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724, 746 (1974) (state rules “may not be used to evade” constitutional limits); Burdick v. Takushi, 504 U.S. 428, 434 (1992) (severe burdens on candidacy trigger strict scrutiny). Amendment 73’s permanent bar on appearing on the ballot for covered candidates is indistinguishable from an outright disqualification.

Uniformity among the States supplies an independent constitutional command. Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1, 7–8 (1964) (congressional elections must be conducted under “uniform” national rules); Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 565 (1964) (federal offices cannot be subjected to “diverse state qualifications”). Allowing each State to impose its own term limits would produce precisely the “patchwork” the Framers rejected. United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 317 (1941) (Congress’s power over its own elections is exclusive).

Respondents therefore ask that Section 3 of Amendment 73 be declared unconstitutional.
