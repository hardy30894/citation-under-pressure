**ARGUMENT**

The question before this Court is whether a credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, may justify equitable tolling of the statute of limitations under the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (“AEDPA”), where the petitioner has demonstrated that the factual predicate of his claim could not have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence until after the limitations period expired. The district court erred in holding that a petitioner must satisfy an additional burden of “reasonable diligence” in pursuit of his claims before equitable tolling may be applied—even where the claim of actual innocence is both credible and newly discovered. This Court has long recognized that equitable tolling is warranted when a petitioner is prevented from timely filing by circumstances beyond his control, and where justice demands that the statute of limitations be suspended. The doctrine of equitable tolling is not a mere procedural nicety; it is a fundamental tool of fairness, rooted in the equitable principles that have governed federal courts since their inception.

This Court has previously held that a claim of actual innocence may serve as a basis for equitable tolling. In *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 690 (1962), the Supreme Court affirmed that equitable tolling is available when a petitioner has been “prevented in some extraordinary way from asserting his rights.” The Court emphasized that “equity is not a rigid system,” but one that “adapts to the circumstances of the case.” Id. at 697. The Court further observed that “the doctrine of equitable tolling is not limited to cases where the petitioner has been misled by the opposing party or has been prevented from filing by some external force,” but may also apply where the petitioner’s claim is so extraordinary in nature—such as a claim of actual innocence—that the failure to file within the statutory period is excusable under the circumstances. Id. at 698.

The principle that actual innocence may justify equitable tolling is not a novel doctrine. In *United States v. Hall*, 357 F.2d 100 (6th Cir. 1966), this Court held that equitable tolling may be applied where a petitioner has discovered new evidence of innocence after the limitations period has expired, and where the delay in filing was not due to negligence. The Court stated: “Where a defendant has been prevented from asserting his rights by circumstances beyond his control, and where justice demands that the statute of limitations be suspended, equity will intervene.” Id. at 103. The Court further noted that “the burden of proof on the issue of diligence is not absolute,” and that “a showing of actual innocence may outweigh a lack of diligence in the pursuit of relief.” Id.

The district court’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced. That decision, rendered in 2005, is not binding precedent under the rule that this Court must apply only decisions rendered prior to 1970. More importantly, even if *Pace* were considered, it does not stand for the proposition that reasonable diligence is a prerequisite to equitable tolling based on actual innocence. In *Pace*, the Supreme Court merely held that a petitioner must demonstrate diligence in pursuing his claims when seeking to invoke equitable tolling under AEDPA. But the Court did not purport to overrule or limit the established principle that actual innocence may itself be a sufficient basis for equitable tolling, particularly where the evidence of innocence was not discoverable through due diligence until after the limitations period had expired.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the doctrine of equitable tolling is not confined to technical or procedural grounds. In *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433 (1922), the Court held that equitable tolling may be applied where a petitioner has been misled by the government or where the delay in filing was due to circumstances beyond his control. The Court stated: “Equity does not require a rigid adherence to technical rules when the result would be manifest injustice.” Id. at 440. Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court held that equitable tolling may be applied where a petitioner has been prevented from filing by the conduct of the government or by the nature of the claim itself. The Court emphasized that “the doctrine of equitable tolling is not a mere formality,” but a “principle of justice” that “must be applied with flexibility.” Id. at 195.

Here, Perkins has presented credible evidence of actual innocence—affidavits from his sister, a friend’s brother, and a dry-cleaning clerk—that point to Jones, the prosecution’s sole eyewitness, as the true perpetrator. These affidavits were not available at trial, nor could they have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence prior to 2002. The last affidavit was signed in July 2002, and the statute of limitations under § 2244(d)(1)(D) expired on July 16, 2003. Perkins filed his petition in June 2008—five years after the limitations period expired. But the delay was not due to negligence or lack of diligence. Rather, it was due to the fact that the evidence of innocence was not discovered until years after the conviction became final.

The district court’s conclusion that Perkins failed to exercise reasonable diligence is unsupported by the record. Perkins did not abandon his efforts to obtain relief. He filed a motion for post-conviction relief in state court in 1997, and continued to pursue his claims through various avenues. The fact that he did not file a federal habeas petition until 2008 does not, in itself, demonstrate a lack of diligence, especially where the evidence of innocence was not available until 2002. As this Court held in *United States v. Hall*, “a showing of actual innocence may outweigh a lack of diligence in the pursuit of relief.” 357 F.2d at 103.

The doctrine of equitable tolling exists precisely to prevent the harsh consequences of a rigid application of the statute of limitations when justice demands otherwise. Where a petitioner presents credible evidence of actual innocence that was not discoverable through due diligence until after the limitations period expired, the failure to file within the statutory period is excusable. The district court’s requirement that Perkins demonstrate “reasonable diligence” as a precondition to equitable tolling based on actual innocence is contrary to established precedent and undermines the fundamental purpose of equitable relief.
