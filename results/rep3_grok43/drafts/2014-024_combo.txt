ARGUMENT

The First Amendment forbids criminal punishment of speech absent proof that the speaker subjectively intended to produce the harm the statute proscribes. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (speech may be restricted only when “intended to produce a clear and imminent danger”). This principle applies with full force to 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925) (statutes “must be construed” to require “the specific intent” to bring about the prohibited result).

Precedent uniformly demands that the government prove the defendant’s purpose to threaten. Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 376 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring) (advocacy is punishable only when “intended to incite” immediate lawless action). Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368 (1931) (statute invalid where it “might be construed” to reach speech without “specific intent”). Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309 (1940) (conviction reversed because record failed to show “intent to insult” or “to cause a breach of the peace”).

The same subjective standard governs every category of unprotected speech. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573 (1942) (“fighting words” require words “which by their very utterance inflict injury” uttered with intent to do so). Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (speech loses protection only when “intended to produce” violence). Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 318 (1957) (advocacy of violence punishable only upon “specific intent” to accomplish it).

New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 279-280 (1964) confirms that even in defamation—closely analogous to threats—the Constitution requires proof of the speaker’s actual state of mind. Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (per curiam) (speech may be proscribed only if “directed to inciting” imminent lawless action). These holdings establish that an objective “reasonable person” test supplies insufficient mens rea for a criminal conviction under the First Amendment.

Section 875(c) therefore cannot be satisfied by evidence that a hypothetical listener would have felt threatened. The statute’s text and the Constitution alike require the government to prove that Elonis subjectively intended his words as threats. Because the jury was instructed otherwise, the convictions must be reversed.
