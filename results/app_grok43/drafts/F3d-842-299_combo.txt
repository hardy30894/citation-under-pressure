The district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. A federal question must appear on the face of a well-pleaded complaint. Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). The complaint here seeks only a declaration of rights under a Virginia flowage easement and the parties’ retained rights to possess and use the land. Such a claim arises under state law. American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916).

A federal defense or anticipated federal issue cannot confer jurisdiction. Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 113 (1936); Tennessee v. Union & Planters’ Bank, 152 U.S. 454, 461 (1894). The complaint does not invoke the Federal Power Act or any FERC license; it requests construction of the easement according to its terms and Virginia property law. Shoshone Mining Co. v. Rutter, 177 U.S. 505, 507 (1900).

Removal was therefore improper. A defendant may remove only when the plaintiff’s statement of his own cause of action shows a federal question. Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets, 313 U.S. 100, 108 (1941); Great N. Ry. Co. v. Alexander, 246 U.S. 276, 281 (1918). The district court’s reliance on the existence of a federal license or project boundary does not alter the state-law character of the pleaded claim.

State law governs the interpretation of the easement and the scope of retained rights. Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938). A federal court sitting in diversity or supplemental jurisdiction must apply Virginia rules of construction. Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99, 108 (1945). The complaint presents no substantial federal question that would support original jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 or 16 U.S.C. § 825p.

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, the judgment must be vacated and the case remanded to state court.
