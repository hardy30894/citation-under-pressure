**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was fairly presented to the state courts or its own default is independently excused. That rule is required by the Court’s exhaustion and procedural-default precedents, which preserve the States’ first opportunity to correct constitutional error. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944); *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204-05 (1950); *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 485-86 (1953); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963).

The habeas petitioner bears the burden of respecting state procedures. Where a state judgment rests on an adequate and independent state ground, federal review is barred absent a recognized excuse. *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447-48 (1965); *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87-91 (1977). “Cause” is not an open-ended equitable label; it must be grounded in an external factor that legally excuses the default. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986). When the asserted cause is ineffective assistance of counsel, the Court has squarely held that the ineffectiveness claim itself must be exhausted and fairly presented to the state courts before it can serve that function. *Id.* at 489.

That requirement controls here. Petitioner’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was defaulted in state court. He seeks to excuse that default by invoking appellate counsel’s alleged ineffectiveness in failing to raise the sufficiency claim on direct review. But the ineffective-assistance claim on which he relies was itself not properly presented in state court; it was dismissed as untimely. Under *Carrier*, a defaulted ineffectiveness claim cannot automatically become cause for another defaulted claim. 477 U.S. at 489. To hold otherwise would allow a petitioner to end-run the state’s procedural rules simply by recasting one forfeited claim as the explanation for another.

The Court’s exhaustion cases foreclose that maneuver. In *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971), the Court held that exhaustion requires fair presentation of the same federal claim to the state courts. *Anderson v. Harless*, 459 U.S. 4, 6-7 (1982) (per curiam), reaffirmed that a petitioner must present the operative facts and controlling federal legal theory to the state tribunal. A claim not fairly presented is not available for federal use as though it had been. And because ineffective assistance is itself a distinct Sixth Amendment claim governed by *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687, 694 (1984), it must be separately exhausted before it can serve as cause.

The Court’s older habeas cases point the same way. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464-65 (1938), described waiver of a constitutional right as “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.” *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947), emphasized that habeas is not a substitute for orderly appellate review. *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101, 104-05 (1942), recognized that habeas may reach constitutional error, but it did not dispense with exhaustion or procedural default. Those decisions confirm that federal collateral review is structured, not free-floating. A petitioner who fails to comply with state rules cannot obtain federal review merely by asserting a different defaulted claim as “cause.”

Petitioner’s rule would also collapse the distinction between cause and prejudice and swallow the finality interests reflected in *Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 493-94 (1976), and *Francis v. Henderson*, 425 U.S. 536, 542-43 (1976). If any defaulted ineffectiveness allegation could excuse any other default, the state courts would be deprived of the first chance to address both the underlying claim and the asserted excuse. That is precisely what *Darr*, *Picard*, and *Carrier* forbid. The better rule, and the one the Court’s cases require, is that the ineffectiveness claim must itself be properly preserved or independently excused before it may constitute cause for another default.

Petitioner may contend that an attorney’s constitutionally ineffective performance is itself an external cause. But *Carrier* answered that point: attorney error rises to cause only when it amounts to a Sixth Amendment violation, and even then the ineffectiveness claim must be presented to the state courts in the first instance. 477 U.S. at 488-89. Otherwise, the federal court would be adjudicating a defaulted constitutional claim without the state courts ever having had a fair opportunity to resolve it.

Because petitioner’s ineffectiveness claim was itself procedurally defaulted and not excused, it cannot serve as cause to overcome the default of the sufficiency claim. The court of appeals’ contrary rule conflicts with *Carrier* and the Court’s longstanding exhaustion doctrine. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
