The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court erred in failing to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a defendant's competency to stand trial is a critical aspect of due process. In Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that a trial court must hold a hearing to determine a defendant's competency to stand trial when there is a bona fide doubt about the defendant's competency. The Court emphasized that "the failure to observe procedures adequate to protect a defendant's right not to be tried or convicted while incompetent to stand trial deprives him of his due process right to a fair trial" (Pate, 383 U.S. at 385).

While Pate established the standard for competency to stand trial, the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that a higher standard applies when a defendant seeks to waive constitutional rights. In Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), the Court defined the standard for competency to stand trial as whether the defendant has "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding – and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him" (Dusky, 362 U.S. at 402). However, waiving constitutional rights requires a more stringent standard, as it involves a defendant's ability to make a reasoned choice among available alternatives.

The trial court's failure to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights was error. Moran's behavior and circumstances raised significant doubts about his competency, including his recent suicide attempt, his ingestion of multiple medications, and his monosyllabic responses to the court's questions. Under Pate, the trial court had a duty to initiate a competency hearing to ensure that Moran's waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent.

The state court's post-conviction hearing did not cure this error, as it applied the wrong legal standard to evaluate Moran's competency. The state court's reliance on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, rather than his competency to waive constitutional rights, was misplaced. As the Ninth Circuit noted, the psychiatric evaluations did not address the specific issue of Moran's ability to make a reasoned choice among available alternatives.

The Due Process Clause requires that a trial court apply a heightened standard to determine a defendant's competency to waive constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court erred in failing to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency, and the state court's post-conviction hearing did not cure this error. Therefore, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
