# ARGUMENT

## I. A COURT REVIEWING FOR PLAIN ERROR MAY LOOK BEYOND THE VERDICT-FORM RECORD TO DETERMINE WHETHER AN UNPRESERVED ERROR AFFECTED SUBSTANTIAL RIGHTS OR THE FAIRNESS OF THE PROCEEDING

Petitioner asks this Court to erect an artificial wall around plain-error review, confining appellate courts to only that evidence the jury actually heard when assessing prejudice and the fairness of the proceeding under the third and fourth prongs of plain-error analysis. That position cannot be squared with the settled principles that govern harmless-error and plain-error review, nor with the practical realities of appellate adjudication.

### A. The Established Framework for Reviewing Unpreserved Error Permits Consideration of the Full Record

When a defendant fails to preserve an objection at trial, relief is available only if the defendant satisfies the four-part test requiring a showing that an error occurred, that it was plain, that it affected substantial rights, and that it seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Nothing in that standard—nor in this Court's precedents applying analogous harmless-error principles—suggests that reviewing courts must artificially blind themselves to matters in the record that bear directly on whether the error mattered.

This Court's decision in *Morris v. Mathews*, 475 U.S. 237 (1986), illustrates the point. There, the Court considered whether a defendant convicted of aggravated murder in violation of the Double Jeopardy Clause could nonetheless have his conviction sustained as the lesser offense of murder, examining whether "all the facts relating to the aggravated robbery of which he was convicted are excluded from consideration," 475 U.S. at 237, and looking to the trial record as a whole to determine whether the error was harmless. The Court did not limit its inquiry to only the evidence formally submitted to the jury on the tainted charge; it examined the totality of the record to determine whether the defendant was prejudiced.

Similarly, in *Washington v. Recuenco*, 548 U.S. 212 (2006), this Court held that even the failure to submit an element of a sentencing enhancement to the jury—precisely the type of *Apprendi*-style structural-sounding error petitioner invokes here—is "not structural error" and is instead "subject to harmless-error analysis." *Id.* If the omission of an essential factual finding from the jury's purview does not categorically bar consideration of what the record as a whole reveals about the strength of the evidence, there is no principled basis to treat an unpreserved *Rehaif* error differently on plain-error review, where the defendant's burden is even higher than under ordinary harmless-error review.

### B. Confining Review to the Trial Record Alone Would Produce Absurd and Unworkable Results

Petitioner's rule would require appellate courts to ignore overwhelming evidence—including the defendant's own stipulations, prior convictions, and sentencing admissions—that make plain the defendant knew of his felon status, simply because the jury was not asked to make that specific finding due to the era's understanding of the law. This Court has consistently rejected such formalistic approaches to error review in favor of asking whether the error actually mattered to the outcome or to public confidence in the proceeding.

The logic of *Recuenco* forecloses petitioner's position: this Court there rejected the argument that a distinction between labeled offenses and their armed variants should produce different harmless-error treatment, stating that such a result "defies logic." 548 U.S. at 220. So too here: it defies logic to hold that a court assessing whether an unpreserved knowledge-of-status error affected substantial rights must pretend that the defendant's own record-evidenced history of felony convictions does not exist.

Moreover, this Court's remand practice itself confirms that appellate courts routinely revisit records in light of intervening decisions and must look to what the full record shows. In *Trest v. Cain*, 522 U.S. 87 (1997), the Court addressed a Court of Appeals' *sua sponte* invocation of a procedural bar not raised by the parties, remanding for the court to determine in the first instance how to proceed—demonstrating that reviewing courts retain flexibility to examine the full circumstances of a case, not merely the narrow question presented by the parties' framing. And in *Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33 (1988), this Court examined the totality of a sentencing record—including evidence of prior convictions and the government's proof at the original hearing—to determine the consequences of an evidentiary error, rather than confining its inquiry to a hypothetical trial stripped of all disputed evidence.

### C. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Applied This Framework

The Eleventh Circuit did nothing more than what this settled framework permits: it examined "the entire trial record and Greer's previous convictions" to determine whether the *Rehaif* error affected Greer's substantial rights or the fairness of his trial. Greer's record contained multiple felony convictions, including convictions for which he had served substantial prison time—facts that make it not merely likely but overwhelmingly certain that Greer knew of his felon status when he possessed the firearm.

Requiring courts to disregard such record evidence would transform plain-error review from a searching but deferential inquiry into a hollow formalism, one that elevates hypothetical juror ignorance over demonstrated factual reality. This Court's precedents, from *Morris* through *Recuenco*, counsel against precisely that kind of rigid, record-blind approach.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
