**ARGUMENT**

**The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Prohibit the Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders, and *Penry v. Lynaugh* Controls.**

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely held that the Eighth Amendment does not impose a per se bar on the execution of mentally retarded persons. *Id.* at 340. Instead, the Court concluded that mental retardation is a mitigating factor that the sentencer must be allowed to consider and give effect to, but it does not render the defendant categorically ineligible for the death penalty. *Id.* at 328–329. That holding governs this case, and the Court should reaffirm it.

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is measured by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). This Court has consistently looked to objective indicia, such as state legislation and jury sentencing practices, to determine whether a national consensus has emerged against a particular punishment. *See Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370–371 (1989). When *Penry* was decided in 1989, only two states—Georgia and Maryland—had enacted statutes barring the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 334. The Court found that this “relatively small number” of states did not demonstrate a national consensus. *Id.* at 335. Since *Penry*, a few additional states have joined this minority, but the number remains far below the threshold that this Court has recognized as establishing a national consensus. *See, e.g., Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 313–315 (2002) (noting that by 2002, 18 states had enacted such bans, but the Court in *Atkins* overruled *Penry*). At the time of the petitioner’s trial, the landscape was essentially unchanged. The absence of widespread legislative action confirms that society’s “evolving standards” have not yet categorically condemned the execution of mentally retarded offenders.

Moreover, the jury here considered and rejected the mitigating evidence of Atkins’ mental retardation. The Virginia Supreme Court, relying on *Penry*, correctly held that the Eighth Amendment required only that the sentencer be able to consider and give effect to mitigating evidence—not that mental retardation automatically precludes a death sentence. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328. The jury in this case was fully apprised of Atkins’ condition, and it nonetheless found that the aggravating circumstances warranted the ultimate penalty. That individualized determination is precisely what the Eighth Amendment demands.

The dissenters in *Penry* and subsequent cases have urged the Court to overrule that decision, but the Court should not lightly discard settled precedent. *See, e.g., Smith v. Francis*, 474 U.S. 925 (1985) (Brennan, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari). The fact that some Members of this Court believe the death penalty cannot constitutionally be applied to mentally retarded offenders does not establish a constitutional rule. The Eighth Amendment leaves such judgments to the legislatures, which are better equipped to assess evolving standards of decency. *See Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 378 (plurality opinion). Until a clear national consensus emerges, this Court should adhere to *Penry* and affirm the judgment below.

Finally, the petitioner’s claim that his execution would be disproportionate to his culpability is a matter for the jury to resolve under proper instructions. The jury did so here, and the Virginia Supreme Court properly upheld the sentence. The Eighth Amendment does not require more.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
