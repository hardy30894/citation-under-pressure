The federal courts lack exclusive jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. Jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) exists only when federal patent law creates the cause of action or the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897). A suit does not arise under the patent laws merely because it involves a patent; the controversy must concern the validity, construction, or effect of a patent upon which the result depends. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

State-created rights remain enforceable in state courts even when federal questions appear incidentally. American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). The well-pleaded complaint rule confines the inquiry to the face of the plaintiff's pleading, unaided by anticipated defenses. Smith v. Kansas City Title & Trust Co., 255 U.S. 180, 199-200 (1921). Here the complaint alleges only common-law negligence; any patent issue arises solely as a defense or as part of the "case within a case" proof of causation.

Federal jurisdiction does not extend to every case that might touch upon federal law. Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 823 (1824). The mere presence of a federal ingredient does not convert a state claim into one arising under federal law. Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 115 (1936). Nor does the possibility that a federal court might later interpret patent principles in a hypothetical retrial suffice. Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co., 291 U.S. 205, 214-15 (1934).

Congress has not displaced the historic presumption that state courts are competent to adjudicate claims arising under state law. Bell v. Hood, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946). Allowing federal jurisdiction here would enlarge the limited category of cases Congress intended to remove from state courts. Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 378 (1821). Because the malpractice claim rests on duties created by state law and the patent questions are neither necessary nor dispositive on the face of the complaint, the state courts retain jurisdiction.
