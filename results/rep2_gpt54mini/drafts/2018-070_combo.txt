**Argument**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act permits state law to operate on fixed platforms only as surrogate federal law, and only when the adjacent state law is actually needed because federal law is absent. The statute does not authorize courts to sweep in any state rule that merely concerns the same subject matter and is not expressly inconsistent with a federal enactment. That is the reading compelled by the statutory text, the Act’s structure, and this Court’s precedents.

OCSLA provides that the civil and criminal laws of the adjacent State are adopted only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “applicable and not inconsistent” must be read together. A law cannot be “applicable” in the OCSLA sense if Congress has already supplied governing federal rules covering the same subject. Congress enacted OCSLA to create a body of law for the outer Continental Shelf, not to preserve concurrent state regulation whenever a state statute addresses the same topic. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-66 (1969).

This Court’s OCSLA decisions confirm that state law enters only as a gap-filler. In Rodrigue, the Court held that the Act was designed to make the fixed structures on the Shelf function like federal enclaves and to apply local law only where federal law did not already govern. 395 U.S. at 355-66. The Court explained that OCSLA was intended to furnish the law applicable to the seabed and the fixed structures attached to it, not to subject them to a free-floating blend of state and federal regulation. Id. at 355, 365-66. That understanding was reinforced in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), which recognized that OCSLA makes adjacent state law applicable as surrogate federal law only where federal law is not controlling. And in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court again emphasized that OCSLA’s special relationship to the shore community does not displace the statute’s text and limited operation. Id. at 218-19.

Petitioner’s reading also follows from the broader federal-enclave principle that state law supplies rules only when Congress has not itself legislated. In United States v. Paul, 6 Pet. 141 (1832), the Court recognized that when federal authority occupies a field, state law yields. Likewise, in Prigg v. Pennsylvania, 16 Pet. 539 (1842), the Court held that state law cannot add to or qualify a federal scheme. And in Wisconsin v. Pelican Ins. Co., 127 U.S. 265 (1888), the Court confirmed that state authority cannot override the federal character of the governing law where federal law controls.

Congress did not leave wage-and-hour matters unresolved on the outer Continental Shelf. The Fair Labor Standards Act sets federal wage and overtime rules. See Overnight Motor Transportation Co. v. Missel, 316 U.S. 572 (1942); Walling v. A.H. Belo Corp., 316 U.S. 624 (1942). Where Congress creates a comprehensive federal wage standard, state rules on the same subject are not “applicable” under OCSLA merely because they might coexist in some abstract sense. They are displaced because Congress has already spoken. See Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); Houston, E. & W. Tex. Ry. Co. v. United States (The Shreveport Rate Cases), 234 U.S. 342 (1914).

Newton’s contrary approach would convert OCSLA into a general incorporation statute for adjacent state labor laws whenever those laws are not directly inconsistent with a federal enactment. That reading would collapse the statute’s distinction between federal law and surrogate state law. But this Court has long rejected the notion that state law may operate alongside federal law merely because the two are not in direct conflict. See Missouri Pacific R.R. Co. v. Porter, 273 U.S. 341 (1927); Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52 (1941). Under OCSLA, state law is borrowed to fill the interstices of federal law, not to duplicate or supplement a federal regulatory regime already in place.

Because the FLSA supplies the governing wage-and-hour standards, California’s meal-period, overtime, and wage-payment rules do not apply on the Shelf as surrogate federal law. The court of appeals’ rule allows state law whenever it is merely “not inconsistent” with federal law, but OCSLA’s text requires more: state law must be both applicable and needed because federal law does not already occupy the field. That is the only construction faithful to Rodrigue and to Congress’s objective of establishing a uniform federal regime for the outer Continental Shelf.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
