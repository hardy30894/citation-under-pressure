# ARGUMENT

## A District Court's Failure to Advise a Defendant of His Right to Appeal Under Rule 32(a)(2) Does Not Warrant Habeas Relief Where the Defendant Independently Knew of That Right

The question presented turns on a simple and well-established principle: relief is unwarranted where a procedural omission causes no prejudice to the party seeking to invoke it. Petitioner Manuel Peguero asks this Court to grant habeas relief for a Rule 32(a)(2) violation notwithstanding the District Court's factual finding, made after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero *knew* of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. This claim must fail because the very purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)'s notice requirement was fulfilled through Peguero's independent knowledge.

### I. This Court Has Consistently Required a Showing of Prejudice Before Granting Habeas Relief for Procedural Defects

This Court's precedents make clear that habeas relief is not automatically available for every technical violation of a procedural rule; rather, a petitioner must demonstrate that the violation actually harmed him. In *Hill v. Lockhart*, 474 U.S. 52 (1985), this Court held that a habeas petitioner alleging deficient advice from counsel must show "the kind of prejudice from the allegedly incompetent advice" that would entitle him to relief. The Court denied relief because, despite the attorney's admitted failure to properly advise the defendant, the defendant could not establish that this failure caused him actual harm.

The same principle governs here with even greater force. If a criminal defendant must show prejudice when his own attorney fails to give him legally required advice, surely a defendant must make the same showing when a court's oversight causes no actual harm because the defendant already possessed the relevant information. Peguero cannot make that showing. The District Court found, after a hearing, that Peguero knew of his right to appeal notwithstanding the court's failure to recite it. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—ensuring defendants know they may appeal—was accomplished through means other than the court's own advisement.

### II. This Court's Decision in *Canizio v. New York* Confirms That Actual Knowledge of a Right Forecloses Collateral Relief for a Court's Failure to Advise of That Right

The circumstances of this case closely track those in *Canizio v. New York*, 327 U.S. 82 (1946). There, a defendant sought to vacate his sentence on collateral review because the trial court had failed to advise him of his right to counsel and had not determined whether he wished to have counsel appointed. This Court examined the record to determine whether the defendant's rights were nonetheless respected in substance, notwithstanding the court's failure to make the formal inquiry required.

The lesson of *Canizio* is that the touchstone for collateral relief is whether the defendant's underlying right was substantively honored—not whether the trial court recited the procedural formality designed to protect that right. Where, as here, the District Court found that Peguero was actually aware of his appellate rights, the procedural omission worked no deprivation of any substantive right. The Rule's protective function was served through an alternate channel, just as counsel's constitutional function can be served notwithstanding an isolated procedural lapse.

### III. Collateral Consequences Doctrine Confirms That Relief Is Unavailable Absent a Demonstrated Injury Flowing From the Violation

This Court's jurisprudence concerning collateral consequences further illuminates why Peguero's claim fails. In *Lane v. Williams*, 455 U.S. 624 (1982), this Court examined whether habeas petitioners could obtain relief where they could not identify any "collateral consequences" flowing from the challenged action. The Court's analysis proceeded from the premise that habeas relief exists to remedy actual injury—not to vindicate abstract procedural correctness untethered from any demonstrated harm to the petitioner.

Peguero's situation presents an even weaker case for relief than the petitioners in *Lane*, because Peguero cannot point to any collateral consequence whatsoever arising from the District Court's omission. He knew of his right to appeal. He made a knowing decision not to exercise it. The Rule 32(a)(2) violation, however regrettable as a matter of the District Court's compliance with procedural formalities, deprived Peguero of nothing.

### IV. Permitting Relief Without a Showing of Prejudice Would Undermine the Structural Principles Governing Habeas Corpus Review

This Court has repeatedly cautioned against expanding habeas relief beyond its proper function of correcting fundamental unfairness. In *Blackledge v. Allison*, 431 U.S. 63 (1977), this Court emphasized that habeas petitions must be evaluated based on whether the alleged violation, if proved, would actually "entitle the defendant to relief." Where the underlying purpose of a procedural rule has been satisfied through other means, no such entitlement exists.

Extending habeas relief to Peguero's claim would transform Rule 32(a)(2) from a rule designed to protect a defendant's substantive right to know of his appellate options into a freestanding procedural trap divorced from any showing of actual prejudice. Such a result would be inconsistent with the settled requirement, reflected across this Court's decisions, that habeas relief redress genuine deprivations rather than harmless procedural irregularities.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
