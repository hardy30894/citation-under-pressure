## Argument

The district court erred in dismissing Buckles's 28 U.S.C. § 2255 motion as untimely. The timeliness of Buckles's § 2255 motion hinges on whether his petition for certiorari was timely filed. If our order recalling the mandate restarted the clock for the 90-day period to petition for certiorari, then Buckles's petition was timely, and so was his subsequent § 2255 motion.

The Supreme Court's rules dictate that the time to file a petition for certiorari runs from the date of the denial of rehearing, unless the lower court entertains an untimely petition for rehearing. Sup.Ct. R. 13(3). However, our court's actions in this case effectively tolled the 90-day period. When Buckles filed his motion for appointment of counsel under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), our court construed it as a timely motion and subsequently denied it, recalling the mandate in the process. This court has previously held that a timely filed motion for appointment of counsel under Rule 4-1(e) can toll the certiorari period. See United States v. LaFromboise, 427 F.3d 680, 683 (9th Cir. 2005).

Moreover, our order recalling the mandate had the effect of restarting the clock for the 90-day period. See United States v. Ibarra, 502 U.S. 1, 4 n.2 (1991) (noting that a timely filed petition for rehearing tolls the time for filing a petition for certiorari until the petition is denied). By analogy, our order recalling the mandate in response to Buckles's motion for appointment of counsel effectively restarted the clock. See also Calderon v. Thompson, 523 U.S. 538, 554 (1998) (noting that a court of appeals' recall of a mandate is an "extraordinary" step that can have significant consequences).

The government's argument that the recall of the mandate was a "ministerial act" is unpersuasive. Our court's order recalling the mandate was a substantive action that had the effect of restarting the clock. See FTC v. Minneapolis-Honeywell Regulator Co., 344 U.S. 206, 211-12 (1952) (holding that a court of appeals' recall of a mandate can be a substantive action with significant consequences).

Furthermore, Buckles's reliance on the Ninth Circuit Clerk's alleged misstatement to his sister is reasonable. See Holland v. Florida, 560 U.S. 631, 653 (2010) (noting that a petitioner's reasonable reliance on a court's statement can entitle him to equitable tolling). The Clerk's statement that the timeline for filing the writ of certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the petition for appointment of counsel and a new mandate date would be issued led Buckles to believe that he had 90 days from the recalled mandate to file his petition. See also Lawrence v. Florida, 549 U.S. 327, 336-37 (2007) (noting that a petitioner's reasonable reliance on a court's statement can be a factor in determining whether equitable tolling is warranted).

In the alternative, even if Buckles's petition for certiorari was untimely, he is entitled to equitable tolling due to his attorney's misconduct and the Ninth Circuit Clerk's misstatement. See Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005) (noting that a petitioner must show that he has been pursuing his rights diligently and that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way to be entitled to equitable tolling). Buckles's repeated attempts to contact his attorney and his filing of a pro se motion for appointment of counsel demonstrate his diligence. See also Spitsyn v. Moore, 345 F.3d 796, 802 (9th Cir. 2003) (noting that a petitioner's diligence is a factor in determining whether equitable tolling is warranted).

The cases of Miranda v. Castro, 292 F.3d 1063 (9th Cir. 2002), and Harris v. Carter, 515 F.3d 1051 (9th Cir. 2008), also support the proposition that attorney misconduct can be a basis for equitable tolling. In this case, Buckles's attorney failed to assist him in filing a motion for appointment of counsel, despite Buckles's repeated requests. See also Towery v. Ryan, 673 F.3d 933, 941-42 (9th Cir. 2012) (noting that an attorney's failure to communicate with a client can be a basis for equitable tolling).

In conclusion, Buckles's § 2255 motion was timely filed, and the district court erred in dismissing it as untimely. Alternatively, Buckles is entitled to equitable tolling due to his attorney's misconduct and the Ninth Circuit Clerk's misstatement. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
