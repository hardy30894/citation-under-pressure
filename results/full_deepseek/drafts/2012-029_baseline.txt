**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Supreme Court of Texas Erred by Asserting That Federal Courts Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims Involving Embedded Patent Issues**

The question presented is whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over any case that involves patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue. The Supreme Court of Texas held that exclusive federal jurisdiction existed here, applying the test from *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005). That holding is erroneous because Minton’s malpractice claim fails three of the four *Grable* factors: the patent issue is not necessary to the claim, it is not substantial, and federal jurisdiction would disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.

**A. The Federal Patent Issue Is Not Necessary to Resolution of Minton’s State-Law Claim**

For a state-law claim to “arise under” federal patent law, the plaintiff’s “right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 809 (1988). This “necessarily depends” standard requires that the federal issue be an essential element of the claim, not merely incidental. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

Minton’s malpractice claim requires proof that Gunn’s negligence proximately caused him harm. In Texas, a legal malpractice plaintiff must prove a “case within a case,” showing that, but for the attorney’s error, the underlying lawsuit would have been successful. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). Here, the underlying lawsuit was a patent infringement action. To establish causation, Minton must show that the experimental use exception would have saved his patent from invalidation under the on-sale bar. See 35 U.S.C. § 102(b).

But causation in a malpractice case may be proved through alternative theories that do not require resolution of patent law. For example, Minton could argue that Gunn’s negligence in failing to discover the TEXCEN lease and timely plead the exception caused him to lose the opportunity to prove his case. A state court could determine that the experimental use exception was factually or legally viable without applying patent law as a controlling element. The federal issue is thus not “necessary” to the resolution of the malpractice claim; it is merely one factor in a broader causation analysis. *See New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 343 (Neb. 2005) (holding that patent issues were incidental to state malpractice claim).

**B. The Federal Patent Issue Is Not Substantial**

The “substantial” requirement under *Grable* demands that the federal issue be more than hypothetical or peripheral. The issue must have real-world significance beyond the particular case. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313. In *Grable*, the dispute over a federal tax statute’s meaning had implications for many future cases. Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies to TEXCEN—is case-specific and fact-bound. Its resolution will not affect the validity of any other patent or settle any broader patent law question.

As the dissent below noted, “[f]ederal courts will not be called upon to decide patent law questions in this case with any regularity, and the outcome of this single case will have no effect on the uniformity of patent law.” *Gunn v. Minton*, No. 10-0624, slip op. at 17 (Tex. 2011) (Willett, J., dissenting). Indeed, the Federal Circuit has recognized that “the question of whether a particular sale was primarily experimental” is a “highly factual” inquiry. *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Such fact-bound determinations do not implicate the federal interest in uniformity that Congress sought to protect through exclusive patent jurisdiction. *See Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749, 753-54 (N.D. Tex. 2010) (holding that resolution of patent issues in malpractice case would not “create or destroy any patent rights” or threaten uniformity).

**C. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities**

The *Grable* analysis requires courts to consider “whether exercising federal jurisdiction would disrupt the congressionally approved balance of state and federal judicial responsibilities.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. States have a significant interest in regulating attorney conduct and providing a forum for legal malpractice claims. This case involves the core state function of supervising the legal profession. *See Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir. 2008) (expressing skepticism about federal jurisdiction over malpractice cases involving embedded patent issues). Allowing every malpractice case involving patent law to be removed to federal court would overwhelm federal dockets with routine attorney-negligence disputes, transforming federal courts into state-law malpractice forums. *See E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 1152 (2010) (rejecting federal jurisdiction because patent issues were not substantial).

The Federal Circuit’s opinion in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), which Minton cites, fails to apply the *Grable* federalism analysis. *Air Measurement* treated any patent issue in a malpractice case as automatically substantial, ignoring the need to weigh state interests. This approach is contrary to the Supreme Court’s instruction in *Grable* and should not be followed.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed. This Court should hold that federal courts do not have exclusive jurisdiction over state legal malpractice claims where patent law is merely embedded in the causation inquiry, because the patent issue is not necessary, not substantial, and federal jurisdiction would disrupt the balance between state and federal courts.
