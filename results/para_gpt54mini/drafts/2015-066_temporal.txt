**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment does not bar Congress from using an uncounseled tribal-court conviction as one of the prior convictions that triggers liability under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The right to counsel is a federal constitutional limitation on proceedings to which the Sixth Amendment applies; it is not a rule that nullifies all convictions obtained under different sovereign systems. See **Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932)**; **Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455 (1942)**. And where Congress creates a recidivist offense, it may make the fact of prior conviction an element of the new crime without relitigating the constitutional adequacy of the earlier proceeding. **Gryger v. Burke, 334 U.S. 728 (1948)**.

First, the prior tribal convictions here were valid when entered. Tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment as such; their authority arises from retained tribal sovereignty, not Article III or the Bill of Rights. The Supreme Court has long recognized that Indian tribes possess “attributes of sovereignty” that remain until withdrawn by Congress. **United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375 (1886)**; **Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376 (1896)**. Because the Sixth Amendment does not of its own force govern tribal proceedings, a conviction entered there without counsel is not constitutionally defective on that basis. See **Talton, 163 U.S. 376**.

Second, § 117(a) punishes the new domestic assault, not the prior tribal offenses. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that recidivist laws “penalize[] only the last offense committed by the defendant.” **Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673 (1895)**; **Gryger, 334 U.S. 728**. The prior convictions function only as the factual predicate that places the defendant within the class of repeat offenders Congress chose to regulate. That structure does not transform the earlier convictions into the basis for punishment, nor does it require that every prior conviction satisfy the Sixth Amendment as if it had been entered in federal court. **Gryger, 334 U.S. 728**.

Third, petitioner’s theory would improperly extend the Sixth Amendment beyond its settled domain and would disregard the historic distinction between the validity of a conviction and the use to which Congress later puts that conviction. The Court has long permitted states to use prior convictions to define enhanced criminal responsibility. **Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554 (1967)**. The Constitution does not require Congress to ignore reliable proof of repeat offending merely because the predicate conviction was entered in a sovereign forum operating under different law. See **Kagama, 118 U.S. 375**; **Talton, 163 U.S. 376**.

Fourth, nothing in the Constitution forbids Congress from relying on a tribal conviction that was valid under the law governing the tribunal that entered it. The Due Process Clause requires fundamental fairness, but it has never been held to impose the Sixth Amendment wholesale on tribal adjudications. **Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908)**; **Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319 (1937)**. To accept petitioner’s position would be to say that a conviction valid when rendered becomes unusable later merely because Congress chose to attach consequences to it in a federal recidivist statute. That is not Sixth Amendment doctrine; it is an invitation to constitutionalize every difference among sovereign systems.

Finally, the structure of § 117(a) confirms its constitutionality. Congress defined a separate federal offense that applies only after proof of a present domestic assault and proof of two prior qualifying domestic-violence convictions. Congress was entitled to conclude that repeated domestic violence, demonstrated by prior convictions in federal, state, or tribal court, warrants enhanced federal punishment. The Constitution leaves ample room for that legislative judgment. **Gryger, 334 U.S. 728**; **Spencer, 385 U.S. 554**.

The judgment should be affirmed.
