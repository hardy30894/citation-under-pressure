**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Rule Announced in Johnson v. United States Must Apply Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment forbids the enforcement of a criminal statute that is so vague that it fails to give fair warning of the conduct it proscribes. *Connally v. Gen. Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1927). The residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague because it does not provide sufficient notice of the conduct it criminalizes. *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551, 2557 (2015). This rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review because it is a "watershed" rule of criminal procedure that implicates the fundamental fairness and accuracy of the criminal proceeding. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989) (plurality opinion).

The Supreme Court has long held that a new rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it places "certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe." *Id.* at 307 (quoting *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in the judgments)). The rule announced in *Johnson* fits squarely within this category because it invalidates a criminal statute on vagueness grounds, thereby removing a predicate offense from the scope of the ACCA. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557. The rule is also a "watershed" rule because it implicates the fundamental fairness of the criminal proceeding by ensuring that the defendant is not sentenced under an unconstitutionally vague statute. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply the rule announced in *Johnson* retroactively to Welch’s case. The court incorrectly concluded that the rule announced in *Johnson* does not apply retroactively because it is not a "watershed" rule of criminal procedure. *Welch v. United States*, 728 F.3d 1088, 1093 (11th Cir. 2013). This conclusion is contrary to the Supreme Court’s precedent, which makes clear that a rule invalidating a criminal statute on vagueness grounds applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See, e.g., Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 353-54 (1964) (holding that a judicial construction of a criminal statute that makes it unconstitutionally vague applies retroactively to cases on collateral review).

**II. The Residual Clause of the ACCA is Unconstitutionally Vague.**

The residual clause of the ACCA, which includes crimes that "otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," is unconstitutionally vague because it fails to provide sufficient notice of the conduct it criminalizes. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557. The clause is so vague that it invites arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement, thereby violating the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *Connally*, 269 U.S. at 391.

The Supreme Court has long held that a criminal statute is unconstitutionally vague if it fails to provide sufficient notice of the conduct it proscribes. *Id.* The residual clause of the ACCA fails this test because it does not provide sufficient notice of the conduct it criminalizes. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557. The clause is so vague that it invites arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement, thereby violating the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *Id.*

The Eleventh Circuit erred in concluding that Welch’s prior conviction for strong-arm robbery qualifies as a predicate offense under the ACCA. The court incorrectly applied the residual clause of the ACCA to Welch’s prior conviction, despite the fact that the clause is unconstitutionally vague. *Welch*, 728 F.3d at 1093. This conclusion is contrary to the Supreme Court’s precedent, which makes clear that the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson* must apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, and the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague. The Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply the rule announced in *Johnson* retroactively to Welch’s case and in concluding that Welch’s prior conviction for strong-arm robbery qualifies as a predicate offense under the ACCA. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed.
