## Argument

The district court erred in admitting the expert testimony of Agent Jones regarding Stanley Hill's credibility during the interrogation. The admission of this testimony was plain error, as it invaded the province of the jury to determine the credibility of witnesses.

The Supreme Court has long held that it is the exclusive province of the jury to determine the credibility of witnesses. As stated in Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Ward, 140 U.S. 76, 88 (1891), "the credibility of witnesses is a matter exclusively for the jury." Agent Jones's testimony, which opined on Stanley's credibility and guilt, usurped the jury's role and was therefore improper.

Furthermore, the courts have consistently held that expert testimony on the credibility of a witness is inadmissible. In Commonwealth v. Russ, 232 Mass. 58, 79 (1919), the court held that it was error to permit a witness to testify as to the credibility of another witness. Similarly, in People v. Sergill, 8 Cal. 2d 179, 185 (1937), the California Supreme Court held that expert testimony on the credibility of a defendant was inadmissible.

Agent Jones's testimony was particularly egregious, as he not only opined on Stanley's credibility but also provided his own interpretation of Stanley's statements and behaviors. Jones stated that Stanley's invocation of God was indicative of deception, citing his "training" and "experience" as the basis for this opinion. This testimony was tantamount to an expert opinion on Stanley's guilt, which is a matter reserved exclusively for the jury. As the Court held in Sunderland v. United States, 19 F.2d 202, 216 (8th Cir. 1927), "the jury, not the witness, is to determine the guilt or innocence of the accused."

The admission of Agent Jones's testimony was plain error, as it was not objected to at trial. However, this error was so prejudicial that it warrants reversal on appeal. The government relied heavily on Jones's testimony to establish Stanley's guilt, and it is likely that the jury gave significant weight to his expert opinion. As the Court held in Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), "a plain error...affecting substantial rights...may be noticed although they were not brought to the attention of the court." The admission of Agent Jones's testimony was a plain error that affected Stanley's substantial rights, and this Court should reverse his conviction.
