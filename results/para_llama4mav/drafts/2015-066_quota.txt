The Ninth Circuit’s decision holding that an uncounseled conviction in tribal court cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) is erroneous. The Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts because they are not subject to the constraints of the U.S. Constitution. See Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co., 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008). Therefore, the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), is misplaced. Ant predates the Supreme Court’s decision in Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), which held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction can be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines as long as the conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment. Nichols controls this case, and its holding should be applied to permit the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s distinction between the guilt and punishment phases of criminal adjudication is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has consistently held that recidivist statutes penalize only the last offense committed by the defendant. See Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008). Therefore, the use of prior convictions to establish an element of a § 117(a) offense is not fundamentally different from using them to enhance a sentence.

Furthermore, the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA) provides certain protections to defendants in tribal court, although it does not guarantee the same level of protection as the Sixth Amendment. See 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303. Congress has also taken steps to expand the right to counsel in tribal courts, particularly in cases involving domestic violence. See id. § 1302(a)-(c); id. § 1304. These developments demonstrate Congress's commitment to balancing tribal sovereignty with the need to protect defendants' rights.

The decisions of the Eighth and Tenth Circuits, which have held that uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a), are more persuasive. See United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir.2011); United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir.2011). These courts correctly applied the holding in Nichols and recognized that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts.

In Alabama v. Shelton, 535 U.S. 654 (2002), the Supreme Court held that a suspended sentence that may "end up in the actual deprivation of a person's liberty" may not be imposed unless the defendant was accorded the right to counsel. Id. at 662. However, the Court also made clear that the underlying conviction remains valid, even if the sentence is later invalidated. See id. at 661-62. This reasoning supports the conclusion that uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit's decision creates a circuit split and undermines the purpose of § 117(a), which is to provide felony-level punishment for serial domestic violence offenders. By reversing the Ninth Circuit's decision, this Court can ensure that § 117(a) is applied consistently across the country and that tribal courts are treated with the respect and comity they deserve. See Wilson v. Marchington, 127 F.3d 805, 810 (9th Cir.1997); Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987).

In Scott v. Illinois, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not apply to state or federal proceedings in which a sentence of imprisonment is not imposed. Id. at 372. This decision is relevant to the present case because it establishes that the Sixth Amendment does not prohibit the use of uncounseled convictions that do not result in imprisonment.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision is incorrect, and the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The decision should be reversed, and the conviction under § 117(a) should be upheld.
