The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson v. United States, 559 U.S. 133 (2010) (Johnson II), does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, but the Court's subsequent decision in Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120 (2016), held that Johnson v. United States, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015) (Johnson III), which invalidated the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), is a substantive rule that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. However, the issue here is whether the rule announced in Johnson II regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively. 

The government argues that the Eleventh Circuit correctly determined that Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery under Florida law was a predicate offense for the purpose of the ACCA. However, the Supreme Court's decision in Johnson II changed the legal landscape by holding that the phrase "physical force" in the ACCA's elements clause means "violent force—that is, force capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person." Johnson v. United States, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010) (Johnson II). 

At the time of Welch's conviction, Florida law allowed for a robbery conviction with a lower level of force than the federal law required, as interpreted in Johnson II. See, e.g., Goldsmith v. State, 573 So.2d 445, 445 (Fla.2d Dist.Ct.App.1991). The Eleventh Circuit's decision affirming Welch's sentence was based on a different understanding of the ACCA's elements clause, which was later clarified by Johnson II.

The rule announced in Johnson II is substantive because it narrowed the scope of the ACCA's elements clause. See Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). As a result, it applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. See Montgomery v. Louisiana, 136 S. Ct. 718, 732 (2016) (holding that a substantive rule applies retroactively on collateral review).

Therefore, the rule announced in Johnson II regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Welch's case. Under this rule, Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery under Florida law does not qualify as a predicate offense for the purpose of the ACCA because it did not involve the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another, as required by the ACCA's elements clause.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for further proceedings.
