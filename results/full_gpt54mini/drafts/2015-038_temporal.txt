Respondent should prevail because the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 does not strip the National Park Service of regulatory authority over non-federal lands and waters merely because they lie within the exterior boundaries of a park unit. The statute’s text preserves, rather than displaces, federal authority over federal lands, and longstanding precedent confirms that Congress may regulate activity affecting federal reservations even where state or private property interests are implicated.

The starting point is the familiar rule that Congress’ intent controls, and statutory terms must be read in light of their ordinary meaning and structure. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470 (1917); United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co., 278 U.S. 269 (1929). Here, petitioner relies on a single phrase in ANILCA § 103(c), but that provision must be read with the Act as a whole. Where Congress speaks expressly to particular property interests, courts do not infer a broader implied limitation absent clear language. United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358 (1805); McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819).

Under settled principles, federal power over federal reservations is broad. Congress may enact laws necessary and proper to protect federal property and federal instrumentalities, and those laws may reach conduct on nearby or intermingled non-federal lands when needed to make the federal scheme effective. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819); Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips v. Guy F. Atkinson Co., 313 U.S. 508 (1941). The federal government’s authority over commerce and navigation is especially well established. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824); Gilman v. City of Philadelphia, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 713 (1866). Because navigable waters are classic subjects of federal concern, Congress may regulate them even where state ownership of the bed or banks continues. United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121 (1967) (recognizing the federal navigational servitude); United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co., 229 U.S. 53 (1913).

That principle defeats petitioner’s claim. The mere fact that Alaska may own the riverbed or that private or Native lands may lie within a park boundary does not remove them from the reach of valid federal regulation. The Supreme Court has long held that federal authority can coexist with state ownership interests when Congress legislates within its enumerated powers. Gibson v. Chouteau, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 92 (1872); Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897). In Camfield, the Court upheld federal regulation affecting private lands because Congress may prevent conduct on non-federal property that would frustrate federal land policy. 167 U.S. at 525-526. Likewise, in United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264 (1927), the Court sustained federal restrictions designed to protect public lands from harmful activity on adjacent private land. These cases establish that Congress is not disabled from regulating non-federal land simply because title rests elsewhere.

Petitioner’s contrary reading would create an artificial jurisdictional carve-out that the statutory scheme does not compel. Congress frequently uses boundary descriptions to define the geographic scope of a federal program without surrendering regulatory authority over non-federal interests located within those boundaries. See Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897); United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264 (1927). Nothing in ANILCA requires a court to read a park boundary as a barrier to all federal regulation of conduct occurring on state or private land. To the contrary, Congress knew how to exclude specific lands when it wished, and courts should not add limitations that Congress omitted. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470 (1917); Brooks v. Zabka, 168 U.S. 229 (1897).

Petitioner also cannot invoke state sovereignty to defeat federal law. The Supremacy Clause ensures that valid federal enactments control contrary state law. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819). And although states retain title and ordinary proprietary rights, those interests remain subject to superior federal regulation when Congress acts within its constitutional powers. United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19 (1947); United States v. Louisiana, 339 U.S. 699 (1950). The same is true here. Even if the Nation River’s submerged lands are state-owned, federal regulation of hovercraft use within a national preserve is a modest and lawful incident of Congress’ authority to administer federal conservation lands. Oklahoma ex rel. Phillips v. Guy F. Atkinson Co., 313 U.S. 508 (1941).

Finally, petitioner’s position would frustrate the very purpose of national park legislation, which is to preserve federal units as coherent protected areas. Congress may make rules appropriate to secure the objects entrusted to federal custody, and courts should not construe a conservation statute to hamstring that mission absent unmistakable command. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819); Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897). Because ANILCA does not clearly withdraw NPS authority over non-federal lands and waters lying within park boundaries, respondent’s regulation remains valid as applied here.
