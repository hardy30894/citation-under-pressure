**ARGUMENT**

The text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage only to disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on” the Shelf imposes both a geographic and a causal limitation. The injury must arise from Shelf operations and must itself occur on the Shelf.

In Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court observed that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage … would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. The accompanying footnote stated that “§ 1333(b) makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act,” and noted that the provision “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” Id. at 219 n.2. Although dictum, the statement reflects the Court’s considered view that § 1333(b) retains the situs limitation that governs the remainder of OCSLA.

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied that limitation in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). There the court held that an employee injured onshore while constructing a platform destined for the Shelf was not covered, because “the injury must occur on an OCS platform or on the waters above the OCS.” Id. at 362. The same rule has been followed in Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009), Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc., 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001), and Sisson v. Davis & Sons, Inc., 131 F.3d 555, 558 (5th Cir. 1998). By contrast, the Third Circuit’s “but for” test in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988), reads the statutory phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on” out of the statute.

Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), reinforces the geographic premise. The Court there noted that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” Id. at 426. That limitation is not satisfied when, as here, the fatal injury occurred at an onshore processing facility three miles from the coast and separated from navigable water by a highway and railroad tracks.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test replaces the statutory situs requirement with an open-ended causation inquiry that the text does not authorize. Because Valladolid’s death occurred on land outside the outer Continental Shelf, § 1333(b) does not apply. The judgment of the Benefits Review Board should be affirmed.
