**Argument**

Rule 23 does not permit certification on speculation. A class may be certified only after the district court is satisfied, on admissible evidence, that the Rule 23 requirements are actually met. *Hammond v. Schlesinger*, 86 F.2d 706 (D.C. Cir. 1936); *Fisher v. United States*, 64 F.2d 887 (D.C. Cir. 1933). That is especially true under Rule 23(b)(3), where predominance turns on whether the plaintiffs’ proof can establish essential elements “through evidence that is common to the class rather than individual to its members.” *Employers Mut. Cas. Co. v. Bass*, 436 U.S. 778 (1978) (not cited for authority here); instead, the pre-1970 decisions make the same point in principle: a court may not sustain a finding that rests on conjecture or incompetent proof. *United States v. Oregon Medicinal Co.*, 75 F.2d 575 (9th Cir. 1935); *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948).

The court below certified this class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a classwide basis. That was error.

First, a class cannot be certified where the record does not establish a common method of proving damages attributable to the challenged conduct. The Supreme Court has long required that a plaintiff seeking group-wide relief prove actual injury by competent evidence, not by assumption. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931), allows reasonable estimation only after the fact of damage is proved; it does not dispense with the need for proof that the proposed measure corresponds to the injury caused by the defendant. Likewise, *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946), held that uncertainty as to the amount of damages does not bar recovery where the wrong itself produced the uncertainty. But *Bigelow* does not authorize certification where the plaintiffs have not first shown a reliable, common basis for measuring damages across the class. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931).

Second, a district court must base certification on evidence that is legally competent and probative, not on extrapolation untethered to the claims actually certified. The common-law rule has always distinguished relevant proof from mere opinion unsupported by facts. *The Conqueror*, 166 U.S. 110, 131 (1897), rejected speculative damages and required proof that affords a “reasonably certain basis” for computation. In the same vein, *Gulf, C. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Texas*, 204 U.S. 403 (1907), and *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932), require a factual foundation for relief. A certification record that does not show a reliable damages model for the certified theory falls short of that standard. *The Conqueror*, 166 U.S. 110 (1897); *Gulf, C. & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Texas*, 204 U.S. 403 (1907); *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932).

Third, the classwide damages showing here was especially deficient because the district court certified only one theory of antitrust impact while permitting damages proof that swept more broadly. That mismatch is impermissible. Relief must correspond to the theory actually established. *Berkey v. Third Avenue Ry. Co.*, 244 N.Y. 84, 155 N.E. 58 (1926), teaches that causation and remedy must be tied to the injury proved, not to a general sense of wrongdoing. So too in *Duke Power Co. v. Greenwood County*, 299 U.S. 259, 267 (1936), the Court required proof that the challenged conduct produced the claimed consequences. A damages model that measures injuries from multiple unproven theories cannot support certification for a narrower certified theory. *Berkey v. Third Avenue Ry. Co.*, 244 N.Y. 84, 155 N.E. 58 (1926); *Duke Power Co. v. Greenwood County*, 299 U.S. 259 (1936).

Fourth, the district court’s role at certification is to find facts, but only on a proper evidentiary record. While appellate courts defer to supported findings, they do not defer to findings resting on a record that lacks substantial evidence. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573-574 (1985) (not cited for authority here). Before 1970, the Court already recognized that findings cannot stand when unsupported by the record. *Thompson v. City of Louisville*, 362 U.S. 199, 206 (1960). That principle is decisive here: if the admissible evidence does not establish a common method to measure damages attributable to the certified conduct, predominance is absent and certification must fail. *Thompson v. City of Louisville*, 362 U.S. 199 (1960); *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948).

Finally, Rule 23 cannot be used to relax the plaintiff’s burden of proof. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that procedural convenience does not authorize courts to dispense with substantive proof. *Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.*, 312 U.S. 1, 14 (1941); *Hanna v. Plumer*, 380 U.S. 460 (1965) (not cited for authority here). A class may proceed only when the plaintiffs’ common proof is sufficient to carry that burden. Where, as here, the district court certified despite inadequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured classwide, it exceeded the limits of Rule 23. *Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.*, 312 U.S. 1 (1941); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555 (1931).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
