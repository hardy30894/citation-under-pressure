# ARGUMENT

## FLORIDA'S CAPITAL SENTENCING SCHEME COMPLIES WITH THE SIXTH AMENDMENT AS INTERPRETED IN *RING v. ARIZONA*, AND FLORIDA'S PROCEDURES FOR ADJUDICATING MENTAL RETARDATION CLAIMS DO NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.

### I. Florida's Sentencing Scheme Satisfies the Sixth Amendment Because a Jury Finds the Facts Necessary to Expose Hurst to the Death Penalty.

This Court held in *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), that "[c]apital defendants... are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment." *Id.* at 589. That holding rested squarely on *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466 (2000), which required that "[a]ny fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum" be submitted to a jury. *Id.* at 490. Florida's scheme satisfies this rule because a jury—not a judge—makes the critical factual determinations that expose a defendant to death.

Petitioner's jury unanimously convicted him of first-degree murder occurring during the commission of a robbery. That verdict alone establishes an aggravating circumstance under Florida law, since section 921.141(5)(d) designates murder committed during a robbery as an aggravator. The jury thus necessarily found, unanimously and beyond a reasonable doubt, at least one aggravating factor sufficient to expose Hurst to a death sentence. This is fundamentally different from the Arizona scheme struck down in *Ring*, where "the judge alone" determined the presence of aggravating circumstances "after finding the defendant guilty of first-degree murder." 536 U.S. at 588. Here, the jury—not the judge—found the operative aggravating fact through its guilt-phase verdict.

Petitioner nonetheless contends that *Ring* requires jury unanimity on the ultimate sentencing recommendation itself. But *Ring* addressed only the Sixth Amendment's requirement that a jury find the aggravating *facts* that increase the maximum punishment; it said nothing about how the jury must weigh those facts against mitigation or arrive at its ultimate recommendation. This Court has recognized that the "weighing" function differs constitutionally from fact-finding. In *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504 (1995), this Court upheld Alabama's scheme permitting a judge to override a jury's sentencing recommendation, reasoning that "the Constitution permits the trial judge, acting alone, to impose a capital sentence." *Id.* at 515. If a judge may constitutionally override a unanimous jury recommendation entirely, it follows that Florida's more protective scheme—which requires a jury recommendation before any death sentence may be imposed—does not offend the Sixth Amendment merely because that recommendation need not be unanimous.

Moreover, this Court's decision in *Apprendi* itself confirms that Sixth Amendment jury-trial protections govern the finding of facts that increase punishment, not the ultimate sentencing recommendation. The Court there emphasized that "the relevant inquiry is one not of form, but of effect—does the required finding expose the defendant to a greater punishment than that authorized by the jury's guilty verdict?" 530 U.S. at 494. In Hurst's case, the jury's guilty verdict for felony murder during a robbery *itself* authorized the death penalty under Florida law without any additional judicial fact-finding. The subsequent penalty-phase proceeding, including the jury's non-unanimous recommendation, does not implicate the *Apprendi*/*Ring* concern because no additional fact beyond what the jury already found was necessary to expose Hurst to the maximum punishment.

### II. Florida's Procedure for Adjudicating Intellectual Disability Claims Comports with the Eighth Amendment.

This Court held in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), that the Eighth Amendment "restrict[s]... the executive power to take the life of any intellectually disabled offender." *Id.* at 321. But *Atkins* expressly left the mechanics of implementation to the states: "we leave to the State[s] the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon their execution of sentences." *Id.* at 317. Florida has done precisely that, promulgating a three-part statutory test under section 921.137, Florida Statutes, requiring proof of (1) significantly subaverage intellectual functioning, (2) concurrent deficits in adaptive behavior, and (3) manifestation before age eighteen.

Petitioner claims the Eighth Amendment required a jury—rather than the trial judge—to resolve his intellectual disability claim. But *Atkins* imposed a substantive limitation on punishment, not a procedural mandate regarding the identity of the factfinder. This Court has consistently permitted judges to resolve threshold eligibility questions in capital cases. In *Ring* itself, the Court's holding was limited to aggravating circumstances that increase the *maximum* punishment; it did not extend Sixth Amendment jury-trial requirements to threshold *eligibility* determinations resolved before sentencing. Indeed, this Court in *Schriro v. Smith*, 546 U.S. 6 (2005), vacated a Ninth Circuit decision that had ordered a jury trial on intellectual disability, reaffirming that "the States retain the power to enforce" *Atkins* "in the manner in which they see fit." *Id.* at 7. Florida's assignment of this determination to the trial judge, applying a clear-and-convincing evidence standard, falls comfortably within the discretion *Atkins* and *Schriro* preserve to the states.

Here, the trial court held a full evidentiary hearing, credited conflicting expert testimony, and found that Hurst failed to establish intellectual disability under Florida's statutory test—a finding supported by evidence including full-scale IQ scores of 78 and 70, both above Florida's threshold, and expert testimony that Hurst exhibited no qualifying adaptive-functioning deficits. Nothing in the Eighth Amendment requires that this individualized, evidence-based determination be made by a jury rather than a judge.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
