The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), allows Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 372 (quoting J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, listing specific factors to consider when scheduling substances, including the substance's potential for abuse, its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). The Attorney General must also obtain a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services before adding a substance to the schedules. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b).

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is also constitutional. Although it grants the Attorney General broad authority to temporarily schedule substances, it requires consideration of specific factors, including the history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). The provision also limits the duration of temporary scheduling to one year, with a possible six-month extension. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2). These constraints provide an intelligible principle guiding the Attorney General's exercise of delegated authority.

The Attorney General's delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also authorized. The CSA grants the Attorney General the power to delegate his functions to other officials, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and the Attorney General has done so through regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). The DEA's actions in temporarily scheduling "Euphoria" were therefore lawful.

The Toubys' argument that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the Attorney General to create crimes is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has upheld delegations of authority to administrative agencies even when they involve the creation of crimes, as long as the delegation is guided by an intelligible principle. See United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506 (1911). The CSA and its temporary scheduling provision meet this standard.

The Third Circuit correctly held that the jurisdictional bar in 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6) does not preclude review of constitutional challenges to the delegation. See Califano v. Sanders, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). However, the court should uphold the delegation as constitutional, as it is guided by intelligible principles and is not an overly broad grant of authority.

The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
