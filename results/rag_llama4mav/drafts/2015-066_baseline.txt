The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Bryant, 769 F.3d 671 (9th Cir. 2014), conflicts with Supreme Court precedent and creates a circuit split. The court held that an uncounseled tribal court conviction cannot be used as a predicate offense under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) because it is not "sufficiently reliable." However, this conclusion is at odds with the Supreme Court's decision in Nichols v. United States, 511 U.S. 738 (1994).

In Nichols, the Court held that a prior uncounseled misdemeanor conviction can be used to enhance a sentence under the Sentencing Guidelines, as long as the conviction did not violate the Sixth Amendment. 511 U.S. at 746-47. The Court emphasized that the reliability concerns underlying the Sixth Amendment right to counsel are "accommodated" at sentencing because a defendant can challenge the reliability of the prior conviction, and the government need only prove the existence of the prior conviction by a preponderance of the evidence. Id. at 747-48.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in Bryant fails to apply the Nichols holding to the use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a). Instead, the court relied on United States v. Ant, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir.1989), which predates Nichols and is no longer good law. The Bryant court also ignored the fact that § 117(a) is a recidivist statute that punishes only the last offense committed by the defendant, not the prior offenses. See Nichols, 511 U.S. at 747; United States v. Rodriquez, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008).

Furthermore, the Ninth Circuit's concern about the reliability of tribal court convictions is misplaced. Tribal courts are not required to follow the same procedures as federal or state courts, but they are still subject to the Indian Civil Rights Act (ICRA), which provides certain procedural safeguards. See 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303. The ICRA ensures that tribal court convictions are reliable and trustworthy.

The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have correctly applied Nichols to hold that prior uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a). See United States v. Shavanaux, 647 F.3d 993, 997-98 (10th Cir.2011); United States v. Cavanaugh, 643 F.3d 592, 595, 604 (8th Cir.2011). This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the circuit split and affirm that Nichols applies to the use of prior uncounseled tribal court convictions under § 117(a).

The Ninth Circuit's decision also undermines the purpose of § 117(a), which is to provide felony-level punishment for serial domestic violence offenders. By excluding prior uncounseled tribal court convictions from being used as predicate offenses, the court has effectively gutted the statute, leaving many victims of domestic violence without adequate protection.

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit's decision in Bryant is contrary to Supreme Court precedent and creates a circuit split. This Court should grant certiorari to resolve the conflict and affirm that prior uncounseled tribal court convictions can be used as predicate offenses under § 117(a).
