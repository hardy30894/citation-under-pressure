**ARGUMENT**

**I. JOHNSON’S RULE ANNOUNCING THE UNCONSTITUTIONALITY OF THE ACCA’S RESIDUAL CLAUSE APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW.**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion). This principle applies to both substantive and procedural rules, provided they are “watershed” in nature. *Id.* at 311. The Court has further clarified that a rule is retroactive if it “was dictated by precedent existing at the time the defendant’s conviction became final.” *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 136 S. Ct. 718, 731 (2016) (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 301).

In *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), the Supreme Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), was unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. *Id.* at 2556-57. The Court reasoned that the residual clause failed to provide fair notice of the conduct it criminalized and invited arbitrary enforcement. *Id.* at 2557. This holding was a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure that placed certain conduct beyond the reach of the ACCA’s enhanced penalties. *See id.* at 2563.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply *Johnson* retroactively. The rule announced in *Johnson* is precisely the type of watershed rule that applies retroactively under *Teague*. *See Welch v. United States*, 755 F.3d 1279, 1283 (11th Cir. 2014) (denying relief under *Teague*). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that rules invalidating vague criminal statutes are retroactive because they implicate fundamental fairness and due process. *See Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 353-54 (1964) (holding that a judicial expansion of a criminal statute’s scope violates due process and applies retroactively). *Johnson* fits squarely within this framework, as it invalidated a statutory provision for vagueness—a defect that undermines the rule of law itself. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has explicitly recognized that *Johnson* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257 (2016) (mem.) (Ginsburg, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (arguing that *Johnson* should apply retroactively). The Court’s subsequent summary affirmance in *Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 1257 (2016), further underscores that *Johnson* is retroactive. *See Hicks v. Miranda*, 422 U.S. 332, 344-45 (1975) (holding that a summary affirmance constitutes a decision on the merits and precedential weight).

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively—was erroneous. The court improperly relied on *Chaidez v. United States*, 568 U.S. 340 (2013), which held that *Padilla v. Kentucky*, 559 U.S. 356 (2010) (requiring defense counsel to advise noncitizen defendants of deportation consequences), did not apply retroactively. *See Welch*, 755 F.3d at 1283. However, *Chaidez* is inapposite because *Johnson* announced a new rule of constitutional procedure that implicates fundamental fairness, whereas *Padilla* was a procedural rule that did not alter the understanding of the Sixth Amendment at the time of the defendant’s conviction. *Compare Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557, *with Chaidez*, 568 U.S. at 348-49.

**II. WELCH’S PRIOR CONVICTION FOR FLORIDA ROBBERY DOES NOT QUALIFY AS A “VIOLENT FELONY” UNDER THE ACCA’S ELEMENTS CLAUSE.**

Even if *Johnson* did not apply retroactively, Welch’s prior conviction for Florida robbery does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s elements clause. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as one that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). The Supreme Court has interpreted this clause to require that the predicate offense have “as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force,” meaning that the “force must be a use of force that is capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person.” *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557 (quoting *Leocal v. Ashcroft*, 543 U.S. 1, 11 (2004)).

Florida’s robbery statute, as applied to Welch’s 1996 conviction, did not require proof of force capable of causing physical pain or injury. Florida law at the time criminalized “robbery” as “the taking of money or other property from the person or custody of another, with intent to either permanently or temporarily deprive the person or the owner of the money or other property, when in the course of the taking there is the use of force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996). However, Florida courts had repeatedly held that mere snatching—such as grabbing an item from a person’s hand—could constitute robbery under this statute. *See, e.g., Andre v. State*, 431 So. 2d 1042, 1043 (Fla. 5th Dist. Ct. App. 1983) (holding that “the act of ‘snatching’ ... money from another’s hands is force and that force will support a robbery conviction”).

This interpretation of Florida law demonstrates that the statute did not require proof of force capable of causing physical pain or injury. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557. The Supreme Court has made clear that the ACCA’s elements clause requires a categorical approach—that is, the predicate offense must have as an element the use of force that is violent in nature. *See id.* Because Florida’s robbery statute could be satisfied by conduct that did not involve violent force, Welch’s conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s elements clause. *See id.* at 2557-58.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment and remand with instructions to vacate Welch’s sentence under the ACCA.
