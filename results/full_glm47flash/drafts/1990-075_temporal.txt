**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Controlled Substances Act Delegation Complies with the "Intelligible Principle" Test**

The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances to address "an imminent hazard to the public safety." 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This delegation of legislative power is constitutional because Congress provided an "intelligible principle" to guide the Attorney General’s discretion. J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Court has long held that "Congress may not delegate its legislative power to another Branch" but may "lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 409.

Here, Congress enumerated specific factors the Attorney General must consider, including a substance's actual or relative potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, the state of current scientific knowledge, the history and pattern of abuse, and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). Furthermore, the statute directs the Attorney General to act only when a substance poses an "imminent hazard to the public safety." 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). These explicit standards ensure the Attorney General’s actions are bound by legislative intent, satisfying the non-delegation doctrine.

**II. The Attorney General Properly Sub-Delegated Authority to the DEA**

The Attorney General’s sub-delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also valid. The Attorney General is a coordinate branch of government. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892). As such, he may delegate his authority to subordinates. The regulation promulgated by the Attorney General, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), explicitly vests the functions of the CSA in the DEA Administrator. This sub-delegation does not violate the separation of powers because the Attorney General retains ultimate authority and the DEA acts under his direction.

**III. The Delegation Is Distinguishable from the Unconstitutional Delegations in Panama Refining and Schechter Poultry**

The dissent’s reliance on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In Panama Refining, the Court struck down a delegation because the statute "did not declare any policy as to the transportation of the excess production" and provided no criterion "to govern the President’s course." 293 U.S. at 415, 416. Similarly, in Schechter Poultry, the Court found that Congress "failed to qualify the President’s authority to approve codes of fair competition." 295 U.S. at 521-22. In contrast, the CSA provides specific criteria and a defined purpose. The delegation here is therefore constitutional.

**IV. The Need for Flexibility in Complex Fields Supports the Delegation**

Finally, the need for flexibility in regulating complex fields supports the delegation. Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944). Congress cannot legislate in every detail of a complex field like drug control. The "intelligible principle" doctrine allows Congress to set broad goals and delegate the details to experts. The temporary scheduling provision is a necessary tool to address the rapid evolution of illicit drugs. The Attorney General’s delegation to the DEA ensures this flexibility is operationalized.
