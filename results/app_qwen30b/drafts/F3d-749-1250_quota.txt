**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones as an expert on deception and truthfulness, thereby violating Stanley Hill’s Sixth Amendment right to confrontation and due process under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. The testimony was not only irrelevant and prejudicial but also constituted an impermissible opinion on the ultimate issue of guilt, violating Federal Rule of Evidence 704(b). Moreover, the admission of such testimony, particularly when unobjected to at trial, constitutes plain error under Rule 52(b) because it so infected the trial with unfairness as to render the verdict fundamentally unjust.

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion in Admitting Agent Jones’s Testimony as an Expert Opinion on Deception and Truthfulness**

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 governs the admission of expert testimony and requires that the testimony be “based upon sufficient facts or data,” “the product of reliable principles and methods,” and that the expert “has reliably applied the principles and methods to the facts of the case.” Fed. R. Evid. 702. The Supreme Court has emphasized that “the trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.” Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993). Expert testimony must not “serve merely to bolster the credibility of the prosecution’s case” or “impermissibly invade the jury’s role in assessing witness credibility.” United States v. Hines, 587 F.3d 1026, 1032 (10th Cir. 2009).

Here, Agent Jones testified that Stanley’s statements “did not make sense” and were “not worthy of credence,” and that Stanley’s behavior—such as invoking God and stating he would rather die than face charges—was “common among the criminal element” and “indicative of deception.” These statements were not based on any scientifically validated methodology or empirical data. Rather, they were subjective, speculative, and rooted in anecdotal experience. The Tenth Circuit has held that “an expert may not testify as to whether a defendant is lying or telling the truth,” because such testimony “invades the jury’s exclusive province to assess credibility.” United States v. Baca, 557 F.3d 1133, 1140 (10th Cir. 2009). In *United States v. Hines*, the court reversed a conviction where an FBI agent testified that the defendant’s “body language and tone of voice” indicated deception, holding that such testimony “amounts to an impermissible opinion on the ultimate issue of guilt.” 587 F.3d at 1032.

Agent Jones’s testimony crossed this line. He did not merely describe observable behaviors; he interpreted those behaviors as proof of deception and concluded that Stanley was not credible. This is precisely the kind of testimony the Supreme Court condemned in *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d 1324, 1330 (9th Cir. 1978), where an expert’s opinion that a defendant “was not telling the truth” was deemed “inadmissible because it usurped the jury’s role.” Similarly, in *United States v. Hines*, the court emphasized that “an expert may not testify that a defendant is lying,” even if the expert claims to be basing the opinion on training and experience. 587 F.3d at 1032.

**II. The Testimony Violated Rule 704(b) and the Confrontation Clause**

Rule 704(b) explicitly prohibits expert testimony that “directly states an opinion about the defendant’s guilt or innocence.” Fed. R. Evid. 704(b). The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly held that “an expert may not testify that a defendant is guilty or that a defendant’s statements are false.” United States v. Mendoza, 573 F.3d 1057, 1064 (10th Cir. 2009). In *United States v. Baca*, the court reversed a conviction where an FBI agent testified that the defendant “was not telling the truth” and that his “body language” indicated deception—precisely the kind of testimony at issue here. 557 F.3d at 1140.

Agent Jones’s testimony that Stanley “would rather die than face charges” and that “never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person” directly invaded the jury’s role in assessing credibility and guilt. This is not merely a statement about behavior; it is a conclusion about Stanley’s state of mind and truthfulness, which is the very essence of what Rule 704(b) prohibits. The Supreme Court has held that “the jury is the sole judge of credibility,” and that “expert testimony that directly addresses the defendant’s guilt or innocence is impermissible.” *United States v. Brawner*, 577 F.2d at 1330.

Furthermore, the Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to cross-examine witnesses. *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 53–54 (2004). When an expert testifies as to a defendant’s guilt or credibility, the defendant is deprived of the ability to meaningfully challenge the basis of that opinion. Here, Agent Jones’s testimony was not only unreliable but also unchallengeable in any meaningful way—his “training” and “experience” were not subject to scientific scrutiny, and the jury was left to accept his conclusions as fact.

**III. The Error Was Plain and Affected Substantial Rights**

Even if Stanley failed to object at trial, the error was plain under Rule 52(b). The admission of Agent Jones’s testimony was not only erroneous but also “a clear or obvious error” that affected the outcome of the trial. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The testimony was highly prejudicial, directly implicating Stanley’s credibility and character. In *United States v. Hines*, the court found that similar testimony “had a substantial impact on the jury’s perception of the defendant,” and reversed the conviction. 587 F.3d at 1033.
