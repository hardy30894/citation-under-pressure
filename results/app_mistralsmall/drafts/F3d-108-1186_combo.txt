**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INTERPRETING THE JURY’S AMBIGUOUS VERDICT TO DENY UNIT ANY RECOVERY.**

The district court’s construction of the jury’s verdicts contravened settled principles governing the interpretation of jury verdicts. Where a verdict is ambiguous, the court must endeavor to give effect to the jury’s intent. *McDermott v. Manhattan Eye, Ear & Throat Hosp.*, 155 F.2d 593, 596 (2d Cir. 1946) (“[W]here the verdict is ambiguous, the court should interpret it in a way which will effectuate the jury’s intent.”). The court’s mechanical subtraction of the set-off from the damages award ignored the possibility that the jury itself had already accounted for the set-off in arriving at the $70,768.02 award. *Id.* (“The court should not adopt a construction which would nullify the verdict or deprive it of all effect.”).

This Court has long held that where a verdict is susceptible to multiple reasonable interpretations, the court must adopt the construction that sustains the verdict. *Chicago, R.I. & P.R. Co. v. Cole*, 241 F.2d 510, 513 (10th Cir. 1957) (“[T]he court must construe the verdict in favor of the party in whose favor it was rendered.”). The district court’s interpretation, which denied Unit any recovery, was not the only reasonable construction of the verdicts. *See id.* (“If the verdict is ambiguous, the court should resolve the ambiguity in favor of the verdict.”). The jury’s award of $70,768.02 on Form 1, coupled with its separate award of $86,494.30 on Form 3, could reasonably be read as reflecting a net award to Unit after accounting for the set-off. *See McDermott*, 155 F.2d at 596.

The district court compounded its error by refusing to clarify the verdict when requested by Unit. A trial court has broad discretion to clarify an ambiguous verdict, and its refusal to do so will be reversed only for abuse of discretion. *United States v. Taylor*, 147 F.2d 458, 462 (2d Cir. 1945) (“The trial court has a duty to clarify an ambiguous verdict if the ambiguity is called to its attention.”). Here, the ambiguity was plain: the jury’s awards could be read either as a gross award to Unit with a separate set-off to Enron, or as a net award to Unit after the set-off. *See McDermott*, 155 F.2d at 596. The court’s refusal to resolve this ambiguity was an abuse of discretion. *See Taylor*, 147 F.2d at 462.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING EVIDENCE OF ENRON’S DEALINGS WITH ANOTHER CONTRACTOR.**

The district court’s exclusion of evidence regarding Enron’s dealings with another contractor deprived Unit of its right to present a full defense. A party is entitled to introduce evidence relevant to the issues in the case, and the exclusion of such evidence will be reversed unless it is shown to be harmless. *Krulewitch v. United States*, 145 F.2d 76, 79 (2d Cir. 1944) (“[E]vidence which is relevant to the issues in the case should be admitted.”). The evidence Unit sought to introduce was relevant to Enron’s claim for set-off, as it tended to show that Enron’s damages were not caused by Unit’s alleged breach. *See id.* (“The exclusion of relevant evidence is reversible error unless the error is harmless.”).

This Court has held that the exclusion of relevant evidence is reversible error unless the proffered evidence is merely cumulative or the other evidence is overwhelming. *Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Chicago & N.W.R. Co.*, 165 F.2d 727, 730 (7th Cir. 1948) (“The exclusion of relevant evidence is reversible error unless the error is harmless.”). Here, the excluded evidence was not cumulative, and its exclusion may have prejudiced Unit’s ability to defend against Enron’s set-off claim. *See Krulewitch*, 145 F.2d at 79.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO GRANT UNIT’S MOTION FOR A NEW TRIAL.**

The district court’s denial of Unit’s motion for a new trial was an abuse of discretion where the verdict was ambiguous and Unit was prejudiced by the court’s refusal to clarify it. A new trial is warranted where the verdict is against the great weight of the evidence or where the court’s rulings deprived a party of a fair trial. *Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan*, 311 F.2d 129, 136 (6th Cir. 1962) (“A new trial is warranted where the verdict is against the great weight of the evidence or where the court’s rulings deprived a party of a fair trial.”). Here, the ambiguity of the verdict and the court’s refusal to clarify it deprived Unit of a fair trial. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Unit respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
