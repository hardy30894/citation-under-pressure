## Argument

The district court's judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial due to several errors that prejudiced the appellant, Robert Cook.

### I. The District Court Erred in Allowing American Steamship's Expert to Testify as to the Cause of the Line's Parting

The district court abused its discretion by permitting American Steamship's expert to offer an opinion on the cause of the line's parting. The expert's testimony was not based on specialized knowledge, but rather on a factual determination that the jury was equally capable of making. Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, expert testimony is only admissible if it "will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue." See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 591 (1993). The expert's opinion on the cause of the line's parting was not expert testimony, as it was based on a simple factual analysis that the jury could have performed. See Berry v. City of Detroit, 25 F.3d 1342, 1350 (6th Cir. 1994) (holding that expert testimony is not admissible if it is within the common knowledge and experience of the jury).

Furthermore, the admission of this testimony was prejudicial, as it influenced the jury's determination of comparative negligence. See United States v. Freeman, 514 F.2d 1184, 1190 (10th Cir. 1975) (holding that the admission of expert testimony can be prejudicial if it is not based on reliable principles). The district court's error in admitting this testimony requires a new trial.

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Issue of Unseaworthiness

The district court erred in denying Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. A vessel is considered unseaworthy if it is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose. See Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). The use of summer grease in the boom's bearings, which had frozen due to the sub-zero temperatures, rendered the vessel unseaworthy. See Boudoin v. Lykes Bros. S.S. Co., 348 U.S. 336, 339-40 (1955) (holding that a vessel is unseaworthy if it is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose).

The fact that Cook applied heat to the bearings to loosen the grease does not render the vessel seaworthy. See Usner v. Luckenbach Overseas Corp., 400 U.S. 494, 499 (1971) (holding that a single act of negligence does not render a vessel unseaworthy). The vessel's condition was a proximate cause of Cook's injury, and the district court should have granted Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on this issue. See Gutierrez v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 373 U.S. 206, 212-13 (1963) (holding that a vessel's unseaworthy condition can be a proximate cause of a plaintiff's injury).

### III. The District Court Did Not Err in Admitting Evidence of Cook's Alcohol-Related Ailments

The district court did not abuse its discretion in admitting evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments. The evidence was relevant to Cook's damages claim, as it related to his overall health and ability to work. See Evich v. Morris, 819 F.2d 256, 258 (9th Cir. 1987) (holding that evidence of a plaintiff's pre-existing medical conditions is relevant to damages). The admission of this evidence was not prejudicial, as it was limited to Cook's medical condition and did not imply that he was intoxicated at the time of the accident. See McGhee v. United States, 462 F.2d 243, 245 (3d Cir. 1972) (holding that evidence of a plaintiff's intoxication is not admissible unless it is relevant to the issue of liability).

The district court's ruling on this issue was consistent with the principle that a plaintiff's damages should be based on his actual condition. See DeLucca v. United States, 670 F.2d 843, 845 (9th Cir. 1982) (holding that a plaintiff's damages should be based on his actual condition, taking into account any pre-existing medical conditions). Therefore, the district court did not err in admitting evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments.

In conclusion, the district court erred in admitting American Steamship's expert testimony and in denying Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. However, it did not err in admitting evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments. Therefore, the judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
