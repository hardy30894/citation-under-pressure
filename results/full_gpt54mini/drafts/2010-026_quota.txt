The Ninth Circuit erred in expanding FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its text and the Supreme Court’s narrow construction of FOIA exemptions. FOIA “reflects a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). Because the statute’s nine exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” U.S. Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989), and “must be narrowly construed,” Rose, 425 U.S. at 361, the Navy carried the burden to show that the withheld ESQD materials fit squarely within Exemption 2’s text: matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2); U.S. Department of State v. Ray, 502 U.S. 164, 173 (1991).

They do not. The ESQD documents describe the effects of explosions and the spatial consequences of ordnance storage near Puget Sound. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, the Navy uses them “to design and construct” facilities and as a “long term planning tool.” That is operational and technical guidance, not “personnel rules and practices.” The statutory phrase reached mundane workplace matters in Rose, such as “parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave,” not substantive safety planning for explosive ordnance. 425 U.S. at 363, 369. Indeed, in Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), the court upheld withholding of a raids-and-searches manual because the material was “law enforcement” guidance whose disclosure “may risk circumvention of agency regulation.” But Hardy did not hold that any operational document with some internal use is exempt; it rested on the manual’s law-enforcement character and the risk that its disclosure would defeat enforcement procedures. Id. That rationale does not fit engineering data about blast effects.

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” test cannot be squared with the text. The D.C. Circuit created that gloss in Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051, 1056-57, 1073-75 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), but Crooker itself shows the danger of untethering Exemption 2 from Congress’s words: the court moved from “personnel rules and practices” to a free-floating inquiry into whether disclosure would “risk circumvention.” Later D.C. Circuit decisions, such as Schwaner v. Department of the Air Force, 898 F.2d 793, 794-95 (D.C. Cir. 1990), and Morley v. Central Intelligence Agency, 508 F.3d 1108, 1124 (D.C. Cir. 2007), followed that approach, but the Supreme Court has never endorsed it. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly insisted that FOIA exemptions receive a narrow reading. Rose, 425 U.S. at 361; Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. at 151.

Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service, 108 F.3d 1082, 1085-87 (9th Cir. 1997), confirms that the ESQD records are not exempt. There, the court rejected Exemption 2 for goshawk nesting-site data because it did not relate “solely,” or even “predominantly,” to internal personnel rules and practices. Id. at 1085. The court then rejected the government’s fallback “law enforcement” theory. Id. at 1086-87. If ecological location data about protected wildlife was not “internal personnel” material, blast-radius data used to plan and operate ordnance storage certainly is not.

The government’s position also conflicts with the FOIA structure recognized in Department of Justice v. Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press, 489 U.S. 749, 772-73 (1989), and John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp., 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989): exemptions protect concrete harms, but only when Congress has chosen to do so. If the Navy believes disclosure would endanger security, FOIA already provides other mechanisms. Exemption 1 applies to properly classified information, 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), and Exemption 7(F) covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” that could endanger life or safety, 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F); see NLRB v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co., 437 U.S. 214, 224 (1978); FBI v. Abramson, 456 U.S. 615, 622-24 (1982); Department of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. at 370-71. The Navy did not classify these documents, and the Ninth Circuit cannot use Exemption 2 to create a security exception Congress did not write.

This Court should reverse and hold that ESQD explosion-effect documents are not exempt under Exemption 2.
