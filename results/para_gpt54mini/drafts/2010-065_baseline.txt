**Argument**

Bond lacks standing to press her Tenth Amendment challenge. The Supreme Court has long held that private parties may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to contest a federal statute absent the state’s participation. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939), private utilities argued that a federal program intruded upon state authority, but the Court rejected the claim because “the [utilities], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment.” Id. at 144. That holding controls here. Bond is a private defendant, not a State, and she may not vindicate abstract federalism interests that belong to the States themselves.

The Third Circuit correctly treated *Tennessee Electric* as binding. The Supreme Court’s disposition there was not a stray aside; it was an express basis for rejecting the Tenth Amendment claim. As the First Circuit explained, “the Court dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits, and hence, the former holding is an alternative ground, rather than obiter dictum.” *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (citing *California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978)). A lower court may not disregard a Supreme Court holding on the theory that it is dicta. See *Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 237 (1997).

Nor does Bond gain standing merely because she asserts that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers. The federalism principles she invokes are structural limitations designed to preserve the States’ sovereign interests, not generalized grievances available to any criminal defendant. The Supreme Court has recognized that the States, not private litigants, are the usual guardians of that allocation of authority. See *Bond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 844, 856-57 (2014); *Tennessee Electric Power Co.*, 306 U.S. at 144. And other courts of appeals, faithfully applying *Tennessee Electric*, have likewise held that private parties may not maintain Tenth Amendment challenges to federal enactments. See *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525-27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 33-36; *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004).

Bond’s contrary position cannot be squared with *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95, 101-02 (1998), which requires courts to address jurisdictional defects before reaching merits questions. Standing is not a merits shortcut; it is a threshold limitation on judicial power. Because Bond does not satisfy that threshold, the Court should not entertain her claim that 18 U.S.C. § 229 is beyond Congress’s enumerated powers or inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

In any event, Bond’s case does not present the sort of state-federal conflict that would support a federalism objection. The Constitution provides that Congress may enact laws “necessary and proper” to execute the Nation’s treaty obligations. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18; art. II, § 2, cl. 2. In *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920), the Supreme Court held that if a treaty is valid, “there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute” implementing it. The Third Circuit properly recognized that § 229 implements the Chemical Weapons Convention and that Bond’s conduct fell well within that statute’s terms. Under *Holland* and *Lara*, treaty-implementing legislation is authorized even where the subject matter may also touch matters traditionally regulated by the States. See *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004).

Finally, Bond is not left without any remedy because she lacks standing to assert the Tenth Amendment in her own name. As the First Circuit explained, citizens may seek relief through their State’s political process, but they may not convert a criminal prosecution into a private federalism suit. *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35. Bond’s grievance is therefore not cognizable as a Tenth Amendment standing matter.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
