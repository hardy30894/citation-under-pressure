**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision in light of its original text, legislative history, and the settled principles of federal preemption that governed the law at the time of its enactment. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits state wage and hour laws to apply on the OCS so long as they are “not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the Act’s structure and contradicts the well-established rule that federal law preempts state law where it occupies the field, even in the absence of direct conflict.

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that “the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State, now in effect or hereafter adopted, amended, or repealed are declared to be the law of the United States” for purposes of the outer Continental Shelf “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted.” This language, enacted in 1953, was not intended to create a general rule of concurrent state-federal jurisdiction. Rather, it was designed to fill a narrow gap in federal law—specifically, the absence of a comprehensive legal framework for civil and criminal matters on the OCS—by borrowing state law only where federal law was silent or inadequate.

The legislative history of the OCSLA confirms that Congress did not intend for state law to serve as a substitute for federal law in areas where Congress had already established a comprehensive regulatory scheme. In the Senate Report accompanying the Act, Congress explicitly stated that “the Federal Government has not been able to provide a complete body of law for the outer Continental Shelf,” and that “the laws of the adjacent States” were to be “adopted as the law of the United States” only “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with Federal law.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). This language reflects a clear understanding that state law would not be applied in areas where federal law already governed, and that the OCSLA’s incorporation of state law was not meant to expand state authority into federal domains.

This understanding is reinforced by the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301 (1947), which held that where Congress has enacted a comprehensive regulatory scheme, state law cannot be applied to supplement or supplant federal law, even if the state law is not in direct conflict. The Court emphasized that “where Congress has legislated in a field, the States may not impose regulations that interfere with the federal scheme,” and that “the federal law is the sole law in the field.” Id. at 308. The Court further noted that “the presence of a federal statute does not necessarily preclude state regulation, but where the federal statute is so comprehensive that it leaves no room for state action, the federal law is exclusive.” Id. at 309.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), enacted in 1938, is precisely such a comprehensive statutory scheme. It establishes minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements that apply nationwide, including to maritime and offshore workers. The FLSA contains a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that permits states to impose more protective wage and hour standards, but only “to the extent that such standards are not inconsistent with the provisions of this chapter.” This provision does not authorize state law to operate as a parallel or supplementary regime; rather, it permits state law to *supplement* federal law only where it goes beyond the federal minimum. Where federal law already provides a complete framework, as it does under the FLSA, state law cannot be applied as a substitute or surrogate.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of the OCSLA, which permits California’s wage and hour laws to apply on the OCS so long as they are “not inconsistent” with federal law, effectively nullifies the FLSA’s comprehensive nature. It treats the FLSA as a mere baseline, allowing state law to operate as a parallel system even in the absence of a gap. This interpretation is inconsistent with the principle established in *Standard Oil*, which holds that federal law preempts state law where it occupies the field. The FLSA, with its detailed provisions, regulatory framework, and enforcement mechanisms, clearly occupies the field of wage and hour regulation for workers on the OCS.

Moreover, the legislative history of the OCSLA confirms that Congress did not intend for state law to be applied in areas where federal law was already in place. The Senate Report explicitly stated that the OCSLA was not meant to “extend the jurisdiction of the States over the outer Continental Shelf,” nor to “create a new system of state law applicable to the OCS.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6. Instead, Congress sought to “avoid the confusion and uncertainty that would result from the application of conflicting state laws” by limiting the application of state law to areas where federal law was absent. Id. at 7. The Court has long recognized that “the mere fact that a state law is not in conflict with federal law does not make it applicable where federal law governs.” *United States v. Locke*, 339 U.S. 387, 392 (1950).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case, decided after the OCSLA’s enactment, was not binding on this Court and was not based on pre-1970 precedent. More importantly, it interpreted the OCSLA in a manner inconsistent with the principle that federal law preempts state law where it is comprehensive. The Court has never held that state law may be applied on the OCS as a matter of course simply because it is not inconsistent with federal law. To the contrary, the rule has long been that federal law governs in areas where Congress has legislated comprehensively.
