# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Requirement That an Employer Possess the Same Quantum of Doubt to Poll Employees as to Withdraw Recognition Is Not a Rational Reading of the Act

The National Labor Relations Act protects employees' right "to bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing" and, with equal force, "to refrain from any or all of such activities." That guarantee cannot be squared with a Board rule that renders polling—the very mechanism designed to *ascertain* employee choice—practically unusable.

This Court has long recognized that a certified union's majority status carries only a rebuttable presumption of continuing support. *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96, 98 (1954) (noting the presumption is conclusive only "for a reasonable period," ordinarily one year, "and is subject to rebuttal thereafter"). The presumption exists to promote stability, not to entomb a union's status against all inquiry once a genuine question of continued support arises. *Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB*, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (recognizing the Board's task of balancing "the desirability of stability" against employees' freedom to choose). Where, as here, the presumption operates over an entirely reconstituted bargaining unit—drawn from a larger predecessor group, with more than a quarter of its members newly hired—the factual predicate for automatic deference to the union's continuing majority is at its weakest.

This Court has likewise approved employer polling as a legitimate device for testing employee sentiment, so long as procedural safeguards are observed. *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967) (setting forth the procedural conditions—secret ballot, assurances against reprisal, and a legitimate purpose communicated to employees—under which polling is permissible). And in *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 594 (1969), this Court confirmed that an employer's "good faith doubt" about a union's majority status is a legally cognizable basis for testing that status rather than simply capitulating to a bargaining demand. Nothing in *Gissel* suggests that the quantum of doubt needed to justify a *procedurally safeguarded inquiry* must equal the quantum needed to justify *unilateral withdrawal* without any inquiry at all. Yet that is precisely what the Board's rule demands: an employer may poll only if it already possesses evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition outright, making the poll's outcome legally irrelevant to the employer's right to act. A rule that permits the more drastic remedy (withdrawal) and the more moderate, information-gathering remedy (polling) only upon identical proof is not a rational accommodation of the competing interests the Act commands the Board to balance; it collapses two distinct statutory tools into one, contrary to the reasoned decisionmaking this Court requires of administrative agencies. *Burlington Truck Lines, Inc. v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962) (an agency's order must reflect "a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made"). The Board's professed preference for Board-conducted elections, moreover, is doctrinally incoherent when the Board simultaneously insists on an identical evidentiary threshold for the disfavored, unsupervised alternative of withdrawal.

## II. The Board's Finding That Petitioner Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole

Even accepting the Board's standard, its factual determination cannot stand. Judicial review of Board factfinding requires more than a search for isolated evidentiary crumbs supporting the agency's conclusion; it requires that "the substantiality of evidence must take into account whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial evidence "is more than a mere scintilla. It means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion." *Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). An inference resting on speculation rather than record fact cannot satisfy that standard. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (the Board's conclusion "must be based upon evidence, not upon suspicion or conjecture").

Here the Board disregarded, on essentially categorical grounds untethered to record evidence, statement after statement by unit employees expressing opposition to continued union representation—testimony from Ridgick, Marsh, Bloch, and Mohr, among others—reasoning that such statements were "equivocal," "off-the-cuff," or the product of job-interview contexts. Whatever weight such considerations might bear on credibility, the Board's wholesale discounting of this evidence, without engaging the countervailing record showing a bargaining unit reconstituted through a wrenching workforce reduction with substantial turnover from the predecessor's workforce, is precisely the sort of one-sided evidentiary accounting *Universal Camera* forbids. The Board's order must be judged by the reasoning it actually gave. *SEC v. Chenery Corp.*, 332 U.S. 194, 196 (1947) ("[A] reviewing court, in dealing with a determination or judgment which an administrative agency alone is authorized to make, must judge the propriety of such action solely by the grounds invoked by the agency."). The grounds invoked—selective discounting of employee statements without a reasoned basis for treating the aggregate evidence as insufficient—do not survive scrutiny under *Chenery*, *Columbian Enameling*, or *Universal Camera*.

Taken together, the poll results (19–13 against the union), the substantial turnover in the unit, and the unrebutted employee statements constitute far more than a scintilla of evidence that petitioner harbored a genuine, good-faith doubt as to the union's continued majority support. The Board's contrary finding cannot stand on this record.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
