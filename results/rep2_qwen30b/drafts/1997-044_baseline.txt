**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT PROPERLY DETERMINED THAT COHEN’S DEBT WAS NONDISCHARGEABLE UNDER 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) BECAUSE HE MADE A MATERIAL MISREPRESENTATION THROUGH IMPLIED CONDUCT, AND HIS RECKLESS INDIFFERENCE TO THE TRUTH SATISFIED BOTH THE KNOWLEDGE AND INTENT REQUIREMENTS.

The Third Circuit correctly applied the law in holding that Cohen’s conduct constituted “actual fraud” under § 523(a)(2)(A). That provision excepts from discharge any debt obtained by “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” The Bankruptcy Court’s findings—supported by substantial evidence—are not clearly erroneous and must be upheld. See *Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes & Co.*, 669 F.2d 98, 102 (3d Cir. 1981). More importantly, the ultimate factual determinations regarding fraud are subject to de novo review, and this Court may affirm if the legal standard was properly applied. Id.

Cohen argues he made no affirmative misrepresentation, but that argument fails. Under settled precedent, silence can constitute a misrepresentation when it is “calculated to deceive,” particularly where the debtor has a duty to disclose material facts. *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992). Here, Cohen set rents above the lawful maximum under Hoboken’s rent control ordinance, knowing full well that the Board had notified him of overcharges. He failed to inform his tenants—many of whom were non-English speakers with limited education—that the rents they paid exceeded legal limits. His failure to disclose this fact, coupled with the act of charging inflated rent, amounted to an implied representation that the charges were lawful. This is precisely the kind of conduct that constitutes a “false representation” under § 523(a)(2)(A). As the Supreme Court held in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), “the Constitution requires that we look beyond form to substance.” So too here: the substance of Cohen’s conduct—charging unlawful rent without disclosure—was fraudulent.

Cohen further contends he lacked knowledge of the falsity because he believed he could charge market rates. But § 523(a)(2)(A) does not require actual knowledge; it requires only that the debtor acted with reckless disregard for the truth. *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr.E.D.Va.1991). Recklessness is defined as “unreasonable conduct in disregard of a known or obvious risk from which it is highly probable that harm would follow.” Id. (quoting *Prosser & Keeton on Torts*). Cohen’s conduct meets that standard. He owned multiple rental properties, operated them for years, and received formal notice from the Rent Leveling Board that he had overcharged tenants. Yet he took no steps to verify the law, consulted no attorney, read no ordinance, and made no effort to correct the overcharges. His pattern of conduct—ignoring official notice, failing to seek legal advice, and continuing to collect excessive rent—demonstrates a conscious indifference to the truth. Such conduct rises far above mere negligence and satisfies the recklessness standard. *In re Van Horne*, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir. 1987).

Moreover, the intent to deceive may be inferred from the totality of circumstances, including reckless disregard. *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118–19 (3d Cir. 1995). Cohen’s failure to respond to the Board’s notice, despite being aware of the overcharges, demonstrates a deliberate refusal to rectify his conduct. This is not the behavior of an honest mistake—it is the behavior of someone who chose to profit from deception. Thus, the Bankruptcy Court properly concluded that Cohen intended to deceive and knew his actions were wrong.

II. THE TREBLE DAMAGES AWARDED UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE NONDISCHARGEABLE BECAUSE THEY ARE NOT PUNITIVE IN NATURE AND WERE FORESEEABLE AS A DIRECT RESULT OF COHEN’S FRAUDULENT CONDUCT.

Cohen argues that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act (*N.J.S.A. 56:8-1 et seq.*) are punitive and therefore should be discharged. This argument is foreclosed by *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which held that statutory damages imposed under consumer protection laws are not dischargeable when they flow directly from fraud. The Eleventh Circuit reasoned that such damages are not discretionary punishments but rather a legislatively mandated consequence of violating the law. Id. at 679. Because the penalty was foreseeable and codified, it is part of the debt incurred through fraud—not a separate punishment.

This reasoning is sound and consistent with federal bankruptcy policy. The purpose of § 523(a)(2)(A) is to prevent debtors from profiting from their fraud. *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286, 111 S.Ct. 654, 659 (1991). If a debtor can commit fraud, then discharge the resulting compensatory damages, and still avoid the statutorily mandated penalty, the system rewards wrongdoing. The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act explicitly provides for treble damages upon proof of fraud. *N.J.S.A. 56:8-1*. Cohen’s conduct triggered this statutory remedy. The award is not punitive in the traditional sense—it is a direct, proportional response to his violation of law. Unlike jury-discretionary punitive damages, these damages were certain and predictable. Therefore, they are not “punitive” within the meaning of federal bankruptcy law.
