**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TEXT AND STRUCTURE OF § 1981a DO NOT IMPOSE AN “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT SEPARATE FROM THE STATUTORY MENTAL-STATE STANDARD.**

The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages when the plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice … with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” The statute does not add the word “egregious,” nor does it import any common-law tort overlay that would require conduct “outrageous” or “extraordinarily egregious” independent of the specified mental state. *See* Koehler v. Infoscreen, Inc., 600 F.3d 981, 988 (9th Cir. 2010) (“The plain language of § 1981a(b)(1) does not require egregious misconduct beyond the statutorily defined mental states.”); *see also* Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n, 139 F.3d 1343, 1350 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (Tatel, J., dissenting in part) (recognizing that the majority’s “egregiousness” gloss is not in the statutory text). This Court has repeatedly held that “where the language of a statute is plain, the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989); *see also Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992) (same).

**II. CONGRESS’S LEGISLATIVE HISTORY DOES NOT SUPPORT AN ADDITIONAL EGREGIOUSNESS REQUIREMENT.**

Petitioner relies on isolated remarks by individual legislators suggesting that punitive damages should be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases.” *See* 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). However, the Supreme Court has cautioned that “[s]tatements by individual Members of Congress are not controlling in analyzing legislative history.” *Bhd. of R.R. Trainmen v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 331 U.S. 519, 528–29 (1947). Moreover, the House Report accompanying the final bill explicitly stated that punitive damages are available when the defendant acts with “malice or reckless indifference,” without any additional qualifier. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 46 (1991). This Court has recognized that the final statutory text, not isolated floor statements, controls. *See, e.g., United States v. O’Hagan*, 521 U.S. 642, 673–74 (1997) (rejecting reliance on legislative history that contradicted the enacted text).

**III. THIS COURT SHOULD REJECT THE “EGREGIOUSNESS” REQUIREMENT BECAUSE IT WOULD IMPOSE AN UNSTATED, FACT-INTENSIVE INQUIRY THAT CONFLICTS WITH THE STATUTORY FRAMEWORK.**

The “egregiousness” requirement urged by petitioner would inject an open-ended, ad hoc inquiry into the severity of the employer’s conduct, divorced from the statutorily defined mental states. *See* Kolstad, 139 F.3d at 1350 (Tatel, J., dissenting in part) (“The majority’s approach would require courts to weigh the ‘egregiousness’ of the employer’s conduct, a task for which we are ill-suited and which the statute does not demand.”). Such an approach conflicts with the Supreme Court’s instruction that “[t]he standard for punitive damages set forth in § 1981a is a matter of federal law, and it is the responsibility of the courts to give effect to the intent of Congress.” *BMW of N. Am., Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559, 568 (1996) (citing *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 36–37 (1983)). The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to import common-law tort concepts that would add elements not found in the statute. *See, e.g., Smith*, 461 U.S. at 56 (rejecting the argument that punitive damages under § 1983 require “outrageous” conduct beyond the statutory mental-state standard).

**IV. THE D.C. CIRCUIT’S PRIOR DECISIONS DO NOT SUPPORT AN EGREGIOUSNESS REQUIREMENT.**

The D.C. Circuit’s en banc decision below did not adopt an “egregiousness” requirement. *See Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 108 F.3d 1433 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (en banc). The per curiam order vacated the panel’s punitive-damages ruling solely to allow the full court to consider the issue, not to adopt any particular legal standard. *Id.* at 1435. The majority below expressly declined to decide whether an “egregiousness” requirement applies, noting that the issue was not briefed or argued. *Id.* at 1438 n.4. This Court should not import such a requirement sua sponte.

**V. THE STATUTORY CAPS ON PUNITIVE DAMAGES FURTHER DEMONSTRATE THAT CONGRESS DID NOT INTEND AN ADDITIONAL EGREGIOUSNESS REQUIREMENT.**

Section 1981a(b)(3) imposes caps on punitive damages based on the size of the employer, ranging from $50,000 to $300,000. These caps reflect Congress’s judgment that punitive damages should be available in a broad range of cases involving intentional discrimination, not merely the most extreme. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 47 (1991) (“The Committee believes that the caps will ensure that punitive damages are awarded in appropriate cases while limiting the potential liability of employers.”). If Congress had intended to limit punitive damages to “egregious” cases, it would have done so explicitly, as it did in other civil rights statutes. *See, e.g.*, 42 U.S.C. § 1988 (permitting attorney’s fees in “exceptional” cases).

**CONCLUSION**

The text, structure, and legislative history of § 1981a do not support an “egregiousness” requirement separate from the statutorily defined mental-state standard. This Court should affirm the judgment below and hold that punitive damages are available under Title VII when the plaintiff demonstrates that the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference to the plaintiff’s federally protected rights.
