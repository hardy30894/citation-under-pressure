**Argument**

A district court’s failure to give the appeal advisement required by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32 does not automatically entitle a defendant to habeas relief where the defendant already knew of the right to appeal and chose not to pursue it. Federal courts do not grant collateral relief for every procedural misstep; they grant it only for errors that produced prejudice or a miscarriage of justice. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428-29 (1962); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947); *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 511 (1954).

Rule 32’s advisement provision is designed to protect a defendant’s opportunity to take a direct appeal. But where that opportunity was already known, the omission caused no cognizable injury. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that collateral relief is not available for technical departures from criminal procedure absent substantial prejudice. In *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947), the Court explained that habeas is not a substitute for direct review and is reserved for fundamental defects. Likewise, *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), held that a procedural error in sentencing does not justify collateral relief unless it amounts to “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice.” See also *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205, 210-19 (1952) (collateral relief is not intended to replace ordinary appellate review); *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 511 (1954) (coram nobis lies only to correct errors “of the most fundamental character”).

That principle controls here. The District Court found as a fact that Peguero knew of his appeal right at sentencing and elected not to appeal. On those findings, the Rule 32 omission did not deprive him of any meaningful opportunity to seek review. Where the defendant already possessed actual knowledge of the right, the purpose of the rule has been satisfied notwithstanding the court’s failure to recite the advisement. Habeas relief is therefore unavailable because there is no prejudice and no miscarriage of justice. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. at 428-29; *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. at 178-79.

The Court’s cases on waiver and forfeiture reinforce that result. A litigant who knowingly elects not to pursue an available remedy ordinarily cannot later obtain collateral relief from the consequences of that choice. See *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464-65 (1938) (waiver is the “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963) (describing the role of deliberate bypass in limiting collateral review); *Walker v. Johnston*, 312 U.S. 275, 286-87 (1941) (habeas requires a showing that the prisoner is in custody in violation of the Constitution or laws of the United States). If Peguero knew of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, he cannot convert the sentencing court’s omission into a basis for vacatur after the fact.

The lower courts correctly treated the claim as subject to harmless-error principles. The Supreme Court has long recognized that not every violation of a criminal rule requires reversal or postconviction relief. See *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760-65 (1946) (errors that do not affect substantial rights do not warrant reversal); *Lisansky v. United States*, 348 U.S. 424, 427-28 (1955) (technical errors not affecting substantial rights do not justify relief); *Bollenbach v. United States*, 326 U.S. 607, 615-16 (1946) (reversal depends on whether error undermined the fairness of the proceeding). Because the District Court’s omission did not affect Peguero’s substantial rights, any error was harmless.

Nor does *McCarthy v. United States*, 394 U.S. 459 (1969), compel a different result. *McCarthy* involved a direct-appeal challenge to a plea colloquy, and the Court held that Rule 11 errors warranted vacatur to assure a voluntary plea. Id. at 465-72. That decision rests on the supervisory power over direct review and does not establish that every violation of a criminal rule is cognizable on habeas. To the contrary, *Hill* reaffirmed that collateral relief is narrower than direct appellate correction. 368 U.S. at 428-29. Here, unlike in *McCarthy*, the defendant seeks postconviction relief despite undisputed actual knowledge of the omitted right.

Finally, habeas relief is especially inappropriate where, as here, the record establishes no denial of appellate access. The Court has stressed that collateral remedies exist to address unlawful custody, not to redress abstract procedural imperfections. See *Anderson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 350, 353-54 (1943); *Walker v. Johnston*, 312 U.S. at 286-87. Because Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to appeal, the Rule 32 omission caused no cognizable harm. The judgment below should be affirmed.
