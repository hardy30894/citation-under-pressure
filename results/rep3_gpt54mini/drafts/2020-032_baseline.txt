**ARGUMENT**

This case is moot because the College revised the challenged policies and Plaintiffs seek only prospective relief plus nominal damages. A claim for nominal damages does not keep a case alive after the underlying constitutional policy has been repealed. That is the rule this Court applied in *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), and the Eleventh Circuit correctly followed it here.

I. **The repeal of the challenged policies mooted Plaintiffs’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief.**

A case becomes moot when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Already, LLC v. Nike, Inc.*, 568 U.S. 85, 91 (2013) (citation omitted). Voluntary cessation does not necessarily moot a case, but it does when the defendant shows that “subsequent events make it absolutely clear that the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.” *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000) (citation omitted). Here, Georgia Gwinnett College replaced the challenged speech-zone policy and removed the challenged conduct-code provision while the case was pending. The revised policies permit speech across campus, subject only to limited circumstances. Plaintiffs do not challenge the revised policies. On these facts, the controversy over the old rules is over. See *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91-92; *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. at 189.

II. **Plaintiffs’ request for nominal damages does not save an otherwise moot case.**

The Eleventh Circuit held in *Flanigan’s* that a claim for nominal damages does not prevent mootness where the challenged ordinance has been repealed and the plaintiff seeks no live prospective relief. 868 F.3d at 1265-66. That decision is binding in the Eleventh Circuit and correctly controls here. The court below properly concluded that Plaintiffs’ nominal-damages request did not preserve a live Article III controversy. See *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1264-66.

Plaintiffs rely on *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), but *Carey* concerned the availability of nominal damages after a completed constitutional violation on the merits; it did not hold that nominal damages alone defeat mootness after the challenged policy has been withdrawn. *Carey* recognized nominal damages as “the appropriate means of ‘vindicating’ rights” when no actual injury is proved. 435 U.S. at 266. That is not the same as providing Article III jurisdiction where the dispute is otherwise moot. The Supreme Court has long required a “real and substantial controversy admitting of specific relief.” *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240-41 (1937). Once the College abandoned the policy and Plaintiffs sought only nominal damages for past conduct, there was no such controversy over prospective relief. See *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1265-66.

III. **Plaintiffs’ merits arguments cannot revive this moot case.**

Plaintiffs’ claims for facial and as-applied invalidity both depend on the superseded rules. But “[f]ederal courts may not decide questions that cannot affect the rights of litigants in the case before them.” *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312, 316 (1974) (per curiam). A court may not issue what would amount to an advisory opinion about a policy no longer in force. *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91.

Nor can Plaintiffs manufacture jurisdiction by characterizing their suit as one for damages without alleging a concrete injury. In *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 (1986), the Court held that damages under § 1983 must be grounded in actual injury, not the “abstract value” of constitutional rights. Plaintiffs’ complaint sought nominal damages and prospective relief based on past enforcement of the old policy; it did not keep a live controversy open after the College changed its rules. See *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1265-66.

IV. **The judgment should be affirmed.**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision accords with settled mootness principles and this Court’s cases limiting the federal judicial power to actual cases and controversies. See U.S. Const. art. III; *Already, LLC*, 568 U.S. at 91; *Laidlaw*, 528 U.S. at 189; *DeFunis*, 416 U.S. at 316. Because the challenged policy was repealed and Plaintiffs sought no surviving form of relief that could redress a live injury, the case is moot. The judgment should be affirmed.
