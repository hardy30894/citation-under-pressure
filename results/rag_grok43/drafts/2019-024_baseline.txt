**ARGUMENT**

The three-year limitations period of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars respondent’s claims. That provision precludes commencement of an action under 29 U.S.C. § 1104 more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” Respondent filed suit on October 29, 2015. The record establishes without genuine dispute that he possessed actual knowledge of the challenged investments before October 29, 2012.

ERISA fiduciaries must act “solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries” and exercise “the care, skill, prudence, and diligence … that a prudent man acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use.” 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1); see Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996). A claim that a fiduciary breached this duty by selecting particular investments therefore rests on knowledge of the investments themselves and the process by which they were chosen. When petitioners’ disclosures placed that information in respondent’s possession, the limitations period began to run.

This Court has recognized that ERISA limitations periods serve the same policies of repose that govern other federal statutes. See Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co., 571 U.S. 99, 105-06 (2013). The statute’s text distinguishes “actual knowledge” from the constructive-knowledge standard Congress eliminated in 1987. Once the facts constituting the alleged imprudence are known, the plaintiff cannot avoid the bar by asserting that he did not read the disclosures or cannot recall having done so. See Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101, 115 (1989) (participant status turns on eligibility, not subjective awareness).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that a plaintiff must also have known the investments were imprudent—finds no support in the statutory language or in this Court’s ERISA decisions. LaRue v. DeWolff, Boberg & Associates, Inc., 552 U.S. 248, 256 (2008), and Hughes Aircraft Co. v. Jacobson, 525 U.S. 432, 445 (1999), confirm that fiduciary liability turns on the objective character of the challenged acts, not on the plaintiff’s contemporaneous legal characterization of them. Requiring proof that the plaintiff appreciated the imprudence would effectively revive the repealed constructive-knowledge provision and render the three-year period a nullity in disclosure cases.

Because the undisputed facts show respondent had actual knowledge of the investments more than three years before suit, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
