## ARGUMENT

**I. THE BOARD ERRED BY EXTENDING THIS COURT'S NARROW HOLDING IN *HARARI I* TO APPLICATIONS THAT NEVER CORRECTED THE DEFECTIVE INCORPORATION LANGUAGE**

In *Harari v. Hollmer*, 602 F.3d 1348 (Fed. Cir. 2010) ("*Harari I*"), this Court articulated a deliberately confined rule: where an applicant files a preliminary amendment at the "initial filing stage" to correct an ambiguous incorporation-by-reference statement, "[t]he proper standard by which to evaluate the sufficiency of incorporation by reference language, at this stage of the proceedings, is whether the identity of the incorporated reference is clear to a reasonable examiner in light of the documents presented." *Id.* at 1352–53 (emphasis added). That standard was crafted for one circumstance and one circumstance only: the '880 application, where a transmittal sheet and a preliminary amendment—filed contemporaneously with the application itself—corrected the ambiguity in the copied incorporation language and identified the '579 application by serial number and filing date. *Id.* at 1353–54.

The Board's error on remand was treating *Harari I* as announcing a general, permissive standard applicable to every application in the priority chain, including the '838 and '768 applications, neither of which contains a transmittal sheet, a preliminary amendment, or any other corrective filing. Those applications simply copied forward the same erroneous statement that the '579 application was "filed on the same day" as the host application—a statement that, for the '838 and '768 applications, is not ambiguous; it is false. The '579 application was filed on April 13, 1989. The '838 application was not filed until 1992, and the '768 application not until 1993. There is no "reasonable examiner" gloss available to rescue a statement that is factually incorrect on its face and unaccompanied by any contemporaneous document resolving the discrepancy. This Court's holding was expressly limited "at this stage of the proceedings"—the initial filing stage, where correction is possible under 35 U.S.C. § 132 without introducing new matter. *Harari I*, 602 F.3d at 1352–53. Nothing in *Harari I* purports to relax the ordinary rules governing incorporation by reference for continuation applications that make no attempt to correct the defect they perpetuate.

**II. UNDER THE CORRECT, ORDINARY STANDARD, THE '838 AND '768 APPLICATIONS FAILED TO INCORPORATE THE '579 APPLICATION BY REFERENCE**

Outside the narrow context addressed in *Harari I*, this Court has consistently required that incorporation by reference satisfy an objective, particularity-based test. "To incorporate material by reference, the host document must identify with detailed particularity what specific material it incorporates and clearly indicate where that material is found in the various documents." *Advanced Display Sys., Inc. v. Kent State Univ.*, 212 F.3d 1272, 1282 (Fed. Cir. 2000). This standard exists because incorporation by reference "provides a method for integrating material from various documents into a host document ... by citing such material in a manner that makes clear that the material is effectively part of the host document as if it were explicitly contained therein." *Cook Biotech Inc. v. Acell, Inc.*, 460 F.3d 1365, 1376 (Fed. Cir. 2006). This Court reaffirmed that same particularity requirement in *Zenon Environmental, Inc. v. U.S. Filter Corp.*, 506 F.3d 1370, 1378–79 (Fed. Cir. 2007), holding that a host document's reference to outside material must be sufficiently definite that a skilled artisan can locate and identify the incorporated subject matter without resort to speculation.

The '838 and '768 applications cannot satisfy this standard. Each copied, verbatim, the '566 application's statement referring to an application "entitled 'Multi-State EEprom Read and Write Circuits and Techniques,' filed on the same day as the present application." J.A. 1204. For the '838 and '768 applications, this description is not merely imprecise—it identifies a temporal relationship that does not exist. A reasonable examiner reviewing the '838 or '768 application in isolation, without the benefit of any transmittal sheet or preliminary amendment (because none was filed), would have no basis to conclude that the referenced "same day" filing was, in fact, the '579 application filed years earlier. That is precisely the failure of "detailed particularity" this Court condemned in *Advanced Display Systems*, 212 F.3d at 1282, and precisely the kind of indefinite cross-reference disapproved in *Zenon*, 506 F.3d at 1378–79.

**III. BECAUSE THE '838 AND '768 APPLICATIONS DO NOT INCORPORATE THE '579 APPLICATION, THE PRIORITY CHAIN UNDER 35 U.S.C. § 120 IS BROKEN**

Section 120 requires an unbroken chain of copending applications, each containing an adequate written description of the claimed invention, in order for a later application to claim the benefit of an earlier filing date. 35 U.S.C. § 120. Where an intervening application in the chain lacks the necessary disclosure—here, the '579 application's disclosure of "Multi-State EEprom Read and Write Circuits and Techniques," improperly incorporated only by an inaccurate cross-reference—the chain of priority is severed at that link, and no later-filed application in the series may reach back past the break. Because neither the '838 nor the '768 application properly incorporated the '579 application, the '880 application cannot claim the benefit of the '566 application's April 13, 1989 filing date. At most, the '880 application is entitled to the December 20, 1996 filing date of the '708 application—the first application in the chain that undisputedly discloses the '579 application's subject matter.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board's judgment should be vacated and the case remanded with instructions to hold that the '880 application is entitled only to the December 20, 1996 priority date, requiring judgment in favor of Hollmer.
