**ARGUMENT**

**The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act Extends Longshore Act Coverage Only to Employees Injured on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself.**

**I. The Plain Language of Section 1333(b) Imposes a Geographic Situs-of-Injury Requirement.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”), 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), provides workers’ compensation coverage “with respect to disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The statutory text commands that the injury itself must bear a direct and immediate relationship to operations physically conducted on the outer continental shelf. This Court has long recognized that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). The Act’s focus on “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” establishes a geographic anchor; an injury occurring miles inland at an onshore processing facility cannot satisfy this requirement.

The word “resulting” in § 1333(b) does not eliminate the need for a geographic nexus. Rather, it requires a causal connection between the injury and offshore operations—a connection that is necessarily broken when the injury occurs at a location wholly removed from the Shelf. This Court has consistently interpreted similar “arising out of” language in workers’ compensation statutes as requiring both a causal and geographic relationship. *See, e.g., Cardillo v. Liberty Mut. Ins. Co.*, 330 U.S. 469, 479-80 (1947) (holding that under the Longshore Act, an injury must “arise out of” and “in the course of” employment, with the latter encompassing a temporal and spatial nexus).

**II. The Statutory Purpose Confirms That Congress Intended OCSLA to Cover Only Injuries on the Outer Continental Shelf.**

OCSLA was enacted to extend federal jurisdiction and workers’ compensation protection to the unique hazards of offshore mineral extraction. The legislative history demonstrates that Congress was concerned with injuries occurring on the Shelf itself—on platforms, artificial islands, and fixed structures. *See S. Rep. No. 411, 83d Cong., 1st Sess. 1-2 (1953)*. The Act’s primary purpose was to fill a gap in state workers’ compensation coverage for employees working on the Shelf, *not* to displace state law for onshore injuries. *See United States v. Maine*, 420 U.S. 515, 524 (1975) (recognizing that OCSLA was designed to govern “the submerged lands seaward of the three-mile limit”). Allowing coverage for onshore injuries would expand OCSLA far beyond its intended scope and conflict with the established principle that state workers’ compensation law governs onshore industrial accidents. *See Bradford Elec. Co. v. Clapper*, 286 U.S. 145, 152 (1932) (holding that states retain exclusive jurisdiction over injuries occurring within their borders absent a clear congressional intent to the contrary).

This Court has consistently declined to expand OCSLA’s geographic reach absent explicit statutory language. In *Tallentire*, the Court refused to apply OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision to an accident occurring on the waters *above* the Shelf, explaining that the Act’s “general scope” is “determined principally by locale.” 477 U.S. at 219. The same reasoning applies with even greater force here: if a helicopter crash on the waters is insufficient to trigger OCSLA’s substantive provisions, then an onshore forklift accident is plainly beyond the Act’s reach.

**III. The Cases Relied Upon by the Court of Appeals Are Inapposite.**

The court of below relied heavily on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), which adopted a “but for” test requiring only a causal connection between the injury and offshore operations. But *Curtis* is an outlier decision that conflicts with the plain text and purpose of OCSLA. Moreover, the *Curtis* court’s reliance on *Tallentire* is misplaced; the Court in *Tallentire* expressly noted that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement,” 477 U.S. at 219 n.2, indicating that both status *and* situs are required.

Petitioner also invokes *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), but that case addressed whether a worker on a platform in state waters was engaged in “maritime employment” under the Longshore Act, not whether OCSLA covers onshore injuries. The Court’s observation that OCSLA “draws a clear geographic line” at the three-mile boundary, *id.* at 423, actually supports respondent’s position—the same geographic limitation should apply to the injury’s location.

**IV. Policy Considerations Confirm the Situs-of-Injury Rule.**

A rule requiring that the injury occur on the outer continental shelf produces predictable, administrable results. Employees on platforms know that OCSLA applies; employees working onshore at processing facilities know that state workers’ compensation law governs. The alternative—a “substantial nexus” test—would invite litigation over every onshore accident involving employees who occasionally work offshore. The Longshore Act itself requires both a maritime situs *and* maritime status for coverage. *See* 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); *Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson*, 396 U.S. 212, 218 (1969) (requiring that the injury occur on “navigable waters” or an “adjoining area”). There is no reason to interpret OCSLA, which incorporates the Longshore Act by reference, any differently.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Juan Valladolid’s injury occurred at an onshore facility, not on the outer continental shelf. Section 1333(b) therefore does not extend Longshore Act coverage, and Mrs. Valladolid’s claim for benefits under OCSLA must be denied.
