**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power, and the Attorney General’s temporary-scheduling authority was properly subdelegated to the DEA Administrator.

I. **Congress supplied an intelligible principle for temporary scheduling.**

Article I permits Congress to seek assistance from another branch so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Court has upheld delegations that are far broader than the one here, explaining that in a complex society Congress must often legislate “under broad general directives.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372; see also *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944); *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

Section 811(h) comfortably satisfies that standard. Congress authorized temporary scheduling only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and it required the decisionmaker to consider specified factors concerning the drug’s abuse history, scope, duration, significance, and public-health risk. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). Congress also required notice in the Federal Register and consideration of the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ comments. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). These are meaningful constraints, not a blank check. The delegation is narrower than those sustained in *Mistretta*, where Congress identified the goals to be pursued, factors to be considered, and limits to the commission’s power. 488 U.S. at 374-79.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved standards so open-ended that the executive was left to decide for itself what policy to adopt. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415-16; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h) is different: Congress itself made the policy judgment that temporary control is warranted only where there is an imminent hazard to public safety, and it cabined that judgment by enumerated criteria. That is precisely the sort of legislative guidance approved in *J.W. Hampton*, 276 U.S. at 409, and repeatedly reaffirmed since. See *Whitman v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 531 U.S. 457, 474-76 (2001).

Nor does the fact that scheduling carries criminal consequences change the analysis. The Court has never adopted a special nondelegation rule simply because delegated authority may affect criminal liability. In *Yakus*, the Court sustained a delegation that fixed criminal penalties for violation of price regulations. 321 U.S. at 420-21, 427-28. And in *Mistretta*, the Court reiterated that Congress may delegate even where the consequences are serious, so long as it provides an intelligible principle. 488 U.S. at 372-73. The CSA meets that test.

II. **The Attorney General was authorized to delegate temporary-scheduling power to the DEA Administrator.**

Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General promulgated a regulation assigning CSA functions to the DEA Administrator. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). That is a straightforward exercise of the very power Congress conferred.

Subdelegation within the Executive Branch is permissible where Congress has authorized it. See *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 512-14 (1974); *Morrison v. Olson*, 487 U.S. 654, 696-97 (1988). Nothing in § 811(h) withdraws the broader delegation authority contained in § 871(a). To the contrary, Congress enacted the temporary-scheduling provision against the backdrop of an existing statutory scheme that permitted delegation within the Department of Justice. See *Touby v. United States*, 500 U.S. 160, 165-66 (1991). The DEA Administrator therefore acted pursuant to validly delegated authority.

III. **The constitutional challenge to judicial review does not alter the validity of the delegation.**

The Court presumes judicial review of constitutional claims absent a clear contrary statement. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). But even where Congress restricts review of a particular administrative order, that does not transform an otherwise valid delegation into an unconstitutional one. The relevant question remains whether Congress supplied an intelligible principle and retained sufficient legislative control. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-73; *Touby*, 500 U.S. at 165-67. It did both.

Because Congress permissibly delegated temporary-scheduling authority to the Attorney General and authorized intra-departmental subdelegation to the DEA Administrator, the convictions rest on a valid scheduling order and should be affirmed.
