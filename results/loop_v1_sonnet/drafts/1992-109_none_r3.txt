**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals held that due process demands a heightened "reasoned choice" standard—more exacting than the standard for competency to stand trial—before a court may accept a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. That holding cannot be squared with this Court's competency jurisprudence, misreads this Court's waiver decisions, and finds no support in the single case the Ninth Circuit invoked to create it. The judgment below should be reversed.

**I. Due Process Requires Only the Competency Standard Set Forth in *Dusky v. United States*.**

This Court has defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether [the defendant] has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* announced the constitutional measure of competency itself; it did not create a floor applicable only to the decision to stand trial while leaving other, more consequential decisions to a stricter test. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirmed as much, holding that a State's failure to provide adequate procedures for resolving doubts about a defendant's mental capacity denies him due process—without suggesting that the capacity at issue varies depending on which right the defendant seeks to exercise. *Id.* at 385–86.

Nothing in this framework changes when a defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, elects to plead guilty or to represent himself. The "reasoned choice" standard rests on the premise that the *decision* to waive rights demands greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether a defendant can understand and participate in the proceedings against him—not whether his particular choice is the wisest one available to him. Once a defendant satisfies *Dusky*, due process asks whether his choice was voluntary and intelligent, not whether it was strategically sound.

**II. This Court's Waiver Decisions Confirm That the Relevant Inquiry Is Voluntariness and Knowledge, Not an Elevated Capacity Threshold.**

This Court's waiver decisions confirm that the constitutional inquiry concerns the character of the waiver, not a heightened capacity standard layered atop *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court explained that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and required courts to presume against such abandonment absent a clear showing to the contrary. That standard asks whether a defendant knew of and intended to relinquish a right—a question distinct from his baseline mental capacity.

*Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required a searching inquiry before a court accepts a waiver of counsel, so that the record demonstrates the defendant understood the charges, the range of punishment, and the risks of proceeding alone. *Id.* at 723–24. That inquiry protects against uninformed waivers; it does not impose a separate mental-capacity threshold. And in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court held that "[t]he Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant. He may waive his Constitutional right to assistance of counsel if he knows what he is doing and his choice is made with eyes open." *Id.* at 279. The Court treated that election as a matter of intelligent waiver, not as one demanding some elevated quantum of rationality. *Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms the same focus: the touchstone is whether the record affirmatively shows the plea was "entered understandingly," *id.* at 174–75, not whether the defendant possessed capacity beyond that required to stand trial.

*Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), addressed the guilty-plea context directly. Quoting *Kercheval v. United States*, 274 U.S. 220, 223 (1927), the Court observed that "[a] plea of guilty is more than a confession which admits that the accused did various acts; it is itself a conviction; nothing remains but to give judgment and determine punishment." *Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 242. Because such a plea works so significant a waiver, *Boykin* required an affirmative showing on the record that the plea was voluntary and intelligent, *id.* at 242–44—but nowhere suggested that this required a standard of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s "reasonable degree of rational understanding." 362 U.S. at 402. Each of these decisions polices the *voluntariness and knowledge* of a waiver already made by a competent defendant; none purports to raise the threshold of competence itself.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Support a Heightened Competency Standard.**

The court of appeals traced its "reasoned choice" language to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), but that decision cannot bear the weight placed on it. *Westbrook* remanded because the record failed to show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel." *Id.* at 150. The Court did not articulate a standard distinct from that governing competency generally, nor did it hold that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s formulation. *Westbrook* establishes only that a trial court must separately determine whether a valid waiver occurred; it supplies no basis for a freestanding, elevated mental-capacity threshold applicable solely to waiver decisions.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard governing whether a defendant may stand trial—articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*—likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional protections required by *Zerbst*, *Von Moltke*, *Adams*, *Carter*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold, and *Westbrook* supplies no authority for one. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
