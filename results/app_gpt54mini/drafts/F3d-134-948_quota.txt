## Argument

### I. Selam was denied compulsory process because the tribal court failed to secure witnesses material to his defense.

The Indian Civil Rights Act guarantees an accused “the right to a speedy and public trial, to be confronted with the witnesses against him, and to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). That guarantee is not satisfied by a merely formal invitation to request subpoenas; where the court knows the accused lacks the ability to secure witnesses and those witnesses are material, the court must ensure the right is real, not illusory. The Sixth Amendment cases construing compulsory process confirm that the right is fundamental to a fair adversary trial. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967); Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 408-09 (1988).

Here, Selam identified five potential character witnesses to appointed counsel. Counsel sought his help, but he did not assist; two witnesses were located and indicated their testimony would be harmful. The record also shows that the tribal judge and counsel advised Selam that he could compel witnesses, but no one took steps to subpoena any defense witness. That sequence does not defeat Selam’s claim. In Washington, the Supreme Court held that compulsory process protects the accused’s “right to present his own witnesses to establish a defense.” 388 U.S. at 19. And in Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973), the Court explained that the right to present defense evidence may not be frustrated by rigid procedure where it “denied” the accused a fair opportunity to defend.

The denial here mattered. Selam faced two bench trials based largely on the testimony of child complainants and hearsay testimony from investigators and other adults. In the second case, the five-year-old did not testify at all, and the prosecution relied on multiple witnesses repeating her out-of-court statements. The absence of defense witnesses deprived Selam of the only available means to contest credibility and context. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the Constitution protects meaningful defense presentation, not merely the abstract possibility of it. Chambers, 410 U.S. at 294; Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986); Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S. 44, 55-56 (1987).

The district court’s conclusion that Selam “waived” compulsory process because he did not ask the judge to subpoena anyone improperly treats the right as forfeited by inaction, notwithstanding the circumstances. While Taylor v. Illinois upheld exclusion where the defense violated discovery rules, it emphasized that preclusion of defense evidence is an extreme sanction reserved for willful misconduct. 484 U.S. at 414-15. Nothing in this record shows that Selam deliberately sandbagged the court; at most, he was unable or unwilling to assist counsel in locating witnesses. That is not a valid basis to convert a core defense right into a nullity.

### II. Selam’s confrontation claim was preserved for habeas review, and in any event the tribal proceedings did not satisfy the right of confrontation.

The district court also erred in holding that Selam waived his confrontation claim by failing to raise it in the tribal court of appeals. Exhaustion under the ICRA requires presentation to tribal courts, but it does not authorize a federal court to refuse habeas review where the claim concerns the fundamental legality of custody and the issue arises from the same trial record. Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez, 436 U.S. 49, 65 (1978); Dry Creek Lodge, Inc. v. Arapahoe & Shoshone Tribes, 623 F.2d 682, 685-86 (10th Cir. 1980). Federal habeas courts may review claims that the petitioner has been detained in violation of rights secured by 25 U.S.C. § 1302. See Poodry v. Tonawanda Band of Seneca Indians, 85 F.3d 874, 890-91 (2d Cir. 1996).

Moreover, Selam did not forfeit the confrontation issue in any meaningful sense. His federal petition was filed soon after tribal appellate dismissal, and the confrontation violation flows directly from the way the second trial was conducted: the five-year-old did not testify, and the prosecution introduced her statements through parents, a physician, and an investigator. The Confrontation Clause protects the accused’s right to face adverse witnesses and to test reliability through cross-examination. Coy v. Iowa, 487 U.S. 1012, 1016-17 (1988); Delaware v. Van Arsdall, 475 U.S. 673, 678 (1986). Although hearsay may be admissible in some circumstances, the Court has repeatedly held that reliability is not a substitute for confrontation where testimonial evidence is offered against the accused. Ohio v. Roberts, 448 U.S. 56, 66 (1980), overruled on other grounds by Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36 (2004).

The State cannot avoid confrontation by simply presenting the child’s statements through adult intermediaries. In Idaho v. Wright, 497 U.S. 805, 813-14 (1990), the Court held that admission of hearsay from a non-testifying child requires careful scrutiny, and “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness” are necessary when confrontation is absent. The record here contains no such constitutionally adequate showing. The five-year-old never appeared, and Selam had no opportunity to cross-examine her. That is precisely the sort of substitution the Confrontation Clause forbids. Coy, 487 U.S. at 1019-20; Van Arsdall, 475 U.S. at 678.

### III. Because the violations undermined the fairness of the convictions, the writ should issue.

Habeas relief is appropriate where custody rests on proceedings that violate fundamental rights secured by the ICRA. 25 U.S.C. § 1303. The combined effect of the denial of compulsory process and the confrontation violation undermined the reliability of both convictions. The Supreme Court has long held that when a defendant is denied a meaningful opportunity to present a defense and to confront adverse witnesses, the resulting conviction cannot stand. Washington, 388 U.S. at 19; Chambers, 410 U.S. at 294; Crane, 476 U.S. at 690. This Court should reverse and direct that the petition be granted.
