# ARGUMENT

The Sixth Amendment does not bar the government from using Bryant's prior tribal-court convictions to satisfy the predicate-offense element of 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). This conclusion follows from two independent and mutually reinforcing propositions established by this Court's precedent: (1) a valid prior conviction, even if uncounseled, may be used to establish an element of a subsequent offense without offending the Sixth Amendment; and (2) Bryant's tribal-court convictions were valid because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel never attached to those proceedings in the first place.

## I. A Valid Uncounseled Conviction May Be Used to Establish the Element of a Recidivist Offense.

This Court held in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid when entered under *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367 (1979), because no term of imprisonment was imposed, may be used to enhance a defendant's sentence for a subsequent offense. The Court explained that recidivism statutes "penaliz[e] only the last offense committed by the defendant." *Nichols*, 511 U.S. at 747. That principle applies with equal force whether the recidivist fact functions as a sentencing enhancement or as an element of the substantive offense. *See United States v. Rodriquez*, 553 U.S. 377, 386 (2008) (recidivism statutes impose punishment where "100% of the punishment is for the offense of conviction. None is for the prior convictions."). Under § 117(a), the "last offense" is Bryant's 2011 domestic assault; the prior tribal-court convictions merely establish his status as a recidivist meriting felony-level treatment for that offense.

This Court's decision in *Lewis v. United States*, 445 U.S. 55 (1980), forecloses any argument that the guilt/sentencing distinction matters. In *Lewis*, the Court held that an uncounseled felony conviction—one that indisputably could not itself have supported a term of imprisonment consistent with the Sixth Amendment—could nonetheless serve as the predicate element of a federal felon-in-possession offense. *Id.* at 65-67. The Court reasoned that Congress could rely on the "fact of conviction" for a wholly separate regulatory purpose without reopening the validity of that conviction. *Id.* at 67. *Lewis* thus establishes that the location of the recidivist fact—element versus enhancement—does not control the Sixth Amendment analysis; what matters is the validity of the predicate conviction when rendered.

*Parke v. Raley*, 506 U.S. 20 (1992), confirms that once a conviction is final, a "presumption of regularity" attaches to it, *id.* at 29, and a defendant seeking to use a subsequent recidivist prosecution as a vehicle to relitigate the fairness of the prior proceeding bears a heavy burden that Bryant cannot meet here, where no constitutional infirmity attached to the tribal proceedings at all.

## II. Bryant's Tribal-Court Convictions Are Valid Because the Sixth Amendment Does Not Apply to Tribal Court Proceedings.

The Ninth Circuit's holding rests on the premise that Bryant's tribal convictions were themselves "constitutionally infirm." *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014). That premise cannot be reconciled with this Court's longstanding recognition that the Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, simply does not govern the internal proceedings of Indian tribes. As this Court held in *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896), tribal governmental powers are "not delegated powers granted by express words" of the Constitution but survive as attributes of retained sovereignty existing prior to and independent of it. More recently, this Court reaffirmed that it is "beyond dispute" that the "Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes." *Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008).

Congress, exercising its plenary authority over relations with Indian tribes, *see United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004), has instead calibrated the procedural protections owed to tribal defendants through the Indian Civil Rights Act, 25 U.S.C. §§ 1301-1303, which affords protections "not . . . coextensive with the Constitution." Tribal courts exercise "the continued exercise of retained tribal sovereignty," *United States v. Wheeler*, 435 U.S. 313, 323-24 (1978), and this Court has cautioned against "unnecessarily intrud[ing] on tribal self-government" by importing federal constitutional requirements into the tribal forum. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) (crafting the appropriate remedy for ICRA violations without a private federal cause of action, in order to avoid interference with tribal self-governance).

Because the Sixth Amendment right to counsel never applied to Bryant's tribal-court proceedings, those proceedings were not deficient in the constitutional sense that animates *Scott* and *Nichols*. There is, in short, no Sixth Amendment violation to "import" into the subsequent § 117(a) prosecution, because there was never a Sixth Amendment violation to begin with. Treating tribal convictions that fully complied with ICRA as if they were void federal or state convictions collapses the distinct constitutional structures Congress has painstakingly built for tribal versus non-tribal proceedings, and disregards this Court's repeated instruction that comity, not equivalence, governs the relationship between federal courts and tribal adjudications. *See Iowa Mut. Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 15 (1987).

Because Bryant's predicate tribal convictions were valid when rendered, and because *Nichols*, *Lewis*, and *Rodriquez* permit reliance on valid prior convictions to establish recidivist liability regardless of whether that liability arises through an element or an enhancement, the use of those convictions to satisfy § 117(a)'s predicate-offense requirement violates no right secured by the Sixth Amendment.
