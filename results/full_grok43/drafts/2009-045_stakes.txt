ARGUMENT

The lodestar figure may be enhanced under a federal fee-shifting statute when the fee applicant offers specific evidence that the quality of service rendered was superior to that reasonably expected in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984). The district court’s detailed findings, grounded in its observation of counsel’s performance throughout the litigation, satisfied that standard.

In Blum the Court explained that “where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee,” and that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” Id. at 901 (quoting Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)). The Court reversed the enhancement in Blum only because the plaintiffs had offered no such specific evidence. Id. at 899-900. Here the record contains precisely the evidence Blum requires.

Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), reaffirmed the same principle. The Court stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible … in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” Id. at 565 (quoting Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Although the Court cautioned that the lodestar ordinarily subsumes most relevant factors, it left open the possibility of enhancement upon the requisite showing. Id. at 566.

Every circuit that has addressed the question has read Blum and Delaware Valley I to permit enhancement on this narrow ground. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Hyatt v. Apfel, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc., 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); Shipes v. Trinity Indus., 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with that uniform authority.

The district court’s findings rested on its direct observation of counsel’s work from the filing of the complaint through entry of the comprehensive injunction. Those findings supplied the “specific evidence” and “detailed findings” that Blum and Delaware Valley I demand. Because the enhancement rested on that record-supported determination rather than on quality and results alone, it was a permissible exercise of the discretion the governing precedents afford.
