**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATE HAS NO CONSTITUTIONAL BURDEN TO DISPROVE THE DEFENDANT’S MENTAL STATE OR CRIMINAL INTENT.**

The State argues that the trial court’s ruling did not violate Clark’s due process rights because the State has no constitutional obligation to disprove the defendant’s mental state or criminal intent. Under *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389 (1993), the State bears no burden of proof regarding the defendant’s mental state. The Court held that "the State has no burden to disprove the defendant's mental state." *Id.* at 395. This principle was reaffirmed in *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735 (2006), where the Supreme Court held that the State has no burden to disprove the defendant's mental state. *Id.* at 749.

Because the State has no burden to disprove the defendant’s mental state, the trial court was not required to consider evidence regarding Clark’s intent to kill. The State’s case-in-chief was complete once Clark admitted to the actus reus of shooting the officer. The trial court’s restriction of the expert evidence to the insanity defense was therefore a proper application of the law, as the evidence was not needed to establish the State’s case. The State was not required to prove that Clark formed the specific intent to kill, nor was it required to rebut Clark’s claims of insanity.

**II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY LIMITED THE EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE BECAUSE IT WAS NOT RELEVANT TO THE STATE’S CASE-IN-CHIEF.**

The State further contends that the trial court’s exclusion of the evidence was proper because it was not relevant to the State’s case-in-chief. Under *Davis v. United States*, 512 U.S. 454 (1994), the State is not required to prove the defendant's mental state. The Court noted that "the State is not required to prove the defendant's mental state." *Id.* at 461. Consequently, the State has no obligation to present evidence to counter the defendant’s claims regarding his mental state.

The trial court correctly determined that the expert evidence was relevant only to the insanity defense, which Clark bore the burden of proving. The court did not err in limiting the evidence to that specific defense. The State was not required to present evidence to disprove Clark’s claims of alien abduction or paranoid schizophrenia, nor was it required to address the issue of specific intent. The trial court’s ruling ensured that the State was not forced to prove a negative or expend resources to rebut evidence that was not necessary to establish the elements of the crime.

**III. THE DEFENDANT HAS NO SEPARATE DUE PROCESS RIGHT TO INTRODUCE EVIDENCE TO NEGATE THE STATE’S BURDEN OF PROOF.**

Finally, the State argues that Clark has no separate due process right to introduce evidence to negate the State’s burden of proof. The right to present evidence is not absolute. As the Supreme Court stated in *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43 (1906), "the right to be heard would be of little avail if it could be exercised only when appealing to the bounty of the court, and not upon the record." *Id.* at 75. This right is limited to evidence that is relevant to the State’s case-in-chief or the defense.

Because the State has no burden to disprove the defendant’s mental state, Clark’s proposed use of the expert evidence to negate criminal intent was legally irrelevant to the State’s case. The trial court’s ruling was a valid exercise of its discretion to exclude irrelevant evidence. Clark was not denied a fair trial; rather, the trial court ensured that the State was not forced to prove a mental state that the law does not require the State to prove. The Arizona courts correctly applied the law, and the judgment of the trial court should be affirmed.
