**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Refusing to Clarify the Ambiguous Verdict**

When a jury returns a verdict that is facially ambiguous or inconsistent, the trial court has a nondiscretionary duty to seek clarification before discharging the jury. *See Skidmore v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co.*, 167 F.2d 54, 60 (2d Cir. 1948) (“if the verdict is ambiguous or inconsistent, it is the duty of the court to call the jury’s attention to the defect and to send them back for a correction”); *Phoenix Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Kidd*, 250 F.2d 575, 577 (10th Cir. 1957) (holding that court must “reconcile the findings or submit the matter to the jury for clarification” when verdict forms are inconsistent). Here, the jury returned a Form 1 award of $70,768.02 to Unit, and a Form 3 set-off award of $86,494.30 to Enron. The district court interpreted these awards as canceling each other, yielding zero damages for Unit. But Unit’s unpaid invoices totaled $157,262.36. Subtracting the set-off from that figure yields $70,768.02—the exact amount the jury awarded on Form 1. This mathematical coherence strongly suggests the jury already accounted for the set-off in its Form 1 damages, and that the Form 3 set-off was intended to be a nullity or an alternative finding. The district court’s refusal to ask the jury a single clarifying question, despite Unit’s timely request, was an abuse of discretion. *Crow v. Wyoming Timber Prods. Co.*, 424 F.2d 93, 97 (10th Cir. 1970) (“When a verdict is ambiguous, the court should not speculate as to its meaning, but should require the jury to clarify its intent.”).

**II. The District Court’s Interpretation of the Verdict Was Plainly Erroneous**

A verdict must be interpreted in light of the instructions and the evidence to give effect to the jury’s intention. *Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd.*, 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962). The district court’s mechanical subtraction of the set-off from the Form 1 award ignored the jury’s likely net calculation. The jury was instructed that if it found only substantial performance, it could reduce Unit’s damages by the cost of correcting defects. It was also instructed that Enron could recover a set-off for the same repair costs. The jury’s Form 1 award of $70,768.02 equals the unpaid invoices ($157,262.36) minus Enron’s repair costs ($86,494.30). The jury then erroneously also awarded the same repair costs as a set-off on Form 3. The only rational construction is that the jury intended Unit to recover $70,768.02, not zero. *See Morrison-Knudsen Co. v. Fireman’s Fund Ins. Co.*, 175 F.2d 927, 929 (10th Cir. 1949) (verdicts should be construed to avoid absurd results); *United States v. 3,064.66 Acres of Land*, 299 F.2d 281, 284 (10th Cir. 1962) (court must adopt “the interpretation that will make the verdict consistent and reasonable”). The district court’s interpretation produced a double reduction of Enron’s repair costs—once as a deduction from Unit’s damages, and again as an affirmative set-off—which violates the instructions and common sense.

**III. The District Court’s Refusal to Clarify Rendered the Trial Unfair**

When a verdict is “so inconsistent or ambiguous that it cannot stand,” the court must either submit it to the jury for clarification or order a new trial. *Belen v. United States*, 47 F.2d 299, 300 (10th Cir. 1931). Here, the district court did neither. Instead, it engaged in “speculation as to the meaning of the verdict,” which is precisely what the Tenth Circuit condemned in *Crow*, 424 F.2d at 97. The court’s failure to clarify also deprived Unit of its right to a jury trial on the amount of damages. *See Dimick v. Schiedt*, 293 U.S. 474, 486 (1935) (noting the “inviolability” of the jury’s role in assessing damages). At a minimum, the court should have granted Unit’s motion for a new trial. *See City of Lincoln v. Power*, 151 U.S. 436, 440 (1894) (when verdict is “uncertain and ambiguous,” a new trial is proper). Finally, the district court misapplied the law of set-off. Even if the jury intended both awards to stand, a set-off does not automatically reduce a judgment to zero; the court should have entered judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02 and then offset Enron’s larger award, resulting in a net judgment for Enron—not zero. *See St. Louis & S.F. Ry. Co. v. Johnston*, 133 F. 193, 196 (8th Cir. 1904). The district court’s sua sponte decision to award “no damages” was without legal support.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment and remand with instructions to enter judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02, or, in the alternative, to hold a new trial on damages.
