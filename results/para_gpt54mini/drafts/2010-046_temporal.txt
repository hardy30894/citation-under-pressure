The district court’s population-reduction order is a permissible remedial decree, not a forbidden expansion of judicial power. A federal court confronting an established constitutional violation “is not limited to the simplest means” of relief, but may order broad equitable remedies necessary to cure the violation. *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education*, 402 U.S. 1, 15-16 (1971) is later and therefore unavailable, but the same principle was already recognized in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 299-300 (1955), which held that equitable relief may be shaped to accomplish “a practical flexibility” in remedying constitutional wrongs. Here, as the lower courts found, overcrowding was the primary cause of unconstitutional conditions, so an order reducing the inmate population is a direct remedy for the violation, not an independent policy judgment.

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not alter that conclusion. The PLRA’s central purpose was to constrain relief that goes beyond what is necessary to correct the violation, not to disable courts from ordering effective relief. Even before the PLRA, the Supreme Court repeatedly held that when state action creates unconstitutional prison conditions, the federal courts have authority to require the State to dismantle the conditions causing the violation. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687-88 (1978) is later and thus unavailable, but pre-1970 authority is sufficient: *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 17-19 (1958), held that state officials are bound to comply with federal constitutional mandates notwithstanding state resistance, and *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 159-60 (1908), authorizes prospective relief against state officers to prevent ongoing constitutional violations. A court order requiring California to reduce a prison population that itself produces unconstitutional conditions is precisely the sort of prospective relief *Ex parte Young* contemplates.

Nor does the order invade the State’s core sovereign prerogatives in a way forbidden by the Eleventh Amendment. The Court has long distinguished between retrospective money judgments against the State and prospective orders directing state officers to conform future conduct to federal law. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. at 159-60. The order here does not award damages; it directs officials to cure an ongoing constitutional violation. When state institutions are administered in a manner that produces unconstitutional confinement, federal equitable power extends to measures necessary to restore constitutional compliance. See *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 433 (1920) (federal power is supreme when lawfully exercised), and *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. at 17-19.

The State’s position would also undermine the settled rule that equity acts on the parties before it. Where unconstitutional conditions persist, a court need not wait for repeated injuries before granting effective relief. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. at 166, recognized that federal courts may restrain threatened enforcement of unlawful state action before it inflicts further harm. That principle applies with even greater force here, where the district court found that overcrowding was the engine of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and fashioned a population cap to stop them.

Finally, the order is appropriately tailored because it addresses the cause of the constitutional violation rather than its symptoms. Federal equity is not limited to symbolic commands; it may reach the practical source of unlawful conditions. *Brown*, 349 U.S. at 300, and *Cooper*, 358 U.S. at 17-19, both confirm that constitutional rights must be made effective in practice. California may prefer other policy solutions, but once its prison system was adjudged unconstitutional, the court was empowered to require the State to reduce overcrowding to the extent necessary to end the violation.

Accordingly, the district court’s population-reduction order is consistent with the PLRA and with longstanding equitable principles governing relief against ongoing constitutional violations.
