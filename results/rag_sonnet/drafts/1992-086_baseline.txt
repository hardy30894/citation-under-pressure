# ARGUMENT

## THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL'S FILM SERIES SOLELY BECAUSE OF ITS RELIGIOUS VIEWPOINT VIOLATED THE FREE SPEECH CLAUSE OF THE FIRST AMENDMENT

### A. The District Created a Limited Public Forum, and Once It Did So, It Could Not Exclude Speech Based on Viewpoint

This Court has long recognized that when government property is opened for use by the public for expressive activity, even on a limited basis, the government's power to regulate speech within that forum is constrained by the First Amendment. *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37 (1983). Although a State need not open its property to expressive activity at all, once it does so for a class of speakers or subjects, it may not thereafter discriminate against speech on the basis of viewpoint. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981).

The Center Moriches Union Free School District, by policy and by repeated practice, opened its facilities after hours to a wide range of community groups for social, civic, and educational purposes. Having created this forum, the District could not then exclude Lamb's Chapel's film series simply because the group wished to discuss family values from a religious perspective while other groups were permitted to discuss the same secular subject matter from a secular perspective. The Second Circuit did not dispute that the property was a forum of this kind; it conceded that the School District's facilities constituted a "limited public forum." The question is whether the District's flat exclusion of *all* religious speech, and its specific application of that exclusion to Lamb's Chapel's film series, comports with the First Amendment. It does not.

### B. Exclusion of Speech Because It Is Religious in Character Is Viewpoint Discrimination, Not a Permissible Subject-Matter Limitation

The District attempts to characterize its rule — barring use of school premises "for religious purposes" — as a content-neutral, subject-matter limitation rather than viewpoint discrimination. That characterization cannot withstand scrutiny. Lamb's Chapel sought to discuss the topics of family values and child-rearing — subjects that the District otherwise permitted community groups to address in after-hours programming. The District did not exclude discussion of family life; it excluded the *religious viewpoint* on family life. This is the paradigm of viewpoint discrimination that *Widmar* forbids.

In *Widmar*, this Court confronted a nearly identical problem: a state university had created a forum generally open to student groups but sought to exclude a religious student organization from using facilities "for purposes of religious worship or religious teaching." *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981). The University argued that its policy was a reasonable, content-based limitation necessary to avoid Establishment Clause difficulties. This Court rejected that argument, holding that the University's discrimination against religious speech, once it had opened a forum "generally open for use by student groups," could not survive First Amendment scrutiny absent a compelling interest — and the Court found no such interest, expressly rejecting the notion that the Establishment Clause required exclusion of religious speech from an otherwise open forum. *Id.*

The same reasoning controls here. The District, like the University in *Widmar*, permitted an array of groups to use its facilities for discussion of social and civic topics after hours. Once that door was opened, the exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series — addressing a topic (family values) that other, secular speakers were permitted to address — constituted precisely the kind of viewpoint discrimination this Court condemned in *Widmar*.

### C. The Establishment Clause Does Not Justify — Indeed, Does Not Require — the District's Exclusion of Religious Speech

The Second Circuit and the District have suggested that permitting Lamb's Chapel access to school facilities would run afoul of the Establishment Clause. This concern is misplaced and was squarely addressed and rejected in *Widmar*. There, this Court held that granting religious groups the same access afforded to other community groups does not offend the Establishment Clause, because such access is properly characterized as an "incidental" benefit flowing from a neutral policy of equal access, not an impermissible endorsement of religion. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981).

This Court's decision in *Board of Ed. of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), reinforces this conclusion. In *Mergens*, the Court considered whether the Equal Access Act required a public high school to permit a student religious club equal access to meet on school premises during noninstructional time, "prohibit[ing] Westside High School from denying a student religious group permission to meet on school premises." The Court held that equal access for religious student groups does not offend the Establishment Clause. *Id.* Although *Mergens* was resolved on statutory grounds, its logic — that equal, neutral access to a forum does not constitute governmental endorsement of religion — applies with full force to the constitutional question presented here.

The District cannot invoke the Establishment Clause as a shield for viewpoint discrimination when this Court has already made clear that equal access to a public forum, extended without regard to whether the speech is religious or secular, poses no realistic danger of establishing religion. Permitting Lamb's Chapel to show its film series alongside other community programming would not signal District endorsement of the Chapel's religious message any more than allowing the "Southern Harmonizers" performance or the "Psychology and The Unknown" lecture series signaled endorsement of those programs' content.

### D. The District's Justifications Cannot Sustain a Content-Based Exclusion in a Limited Public Forum

Even accepting that the District's forum was limited rather than fully public, an exclusion of speech within that forum must, at minimum, be reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. See *Perry*, 460 U.S. 37. The District's rule fails both requirements. It is not reasonable to treat a film series about family values and child-rearing differently depending solely on whether the speaker's perspective is secular or religious, and it is not viewpoint-neutral to exclude the religious perspective on a subject while welcoming the secular one.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
