**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial of Prior Conviction Allegations in Noncapital Sentencing Proceedings**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This constitutional protection extends beyond the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution to sentencing proceedings where the state seeks to enhance a defendant’s punishment based on prior convictions. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438 (1981) ("[T]he Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second attempt by the State to put a defendant in jeopardy for the same offense after an acquittal."); *Rumsey v. Maryland*, 467 U.S. 203, 209 (1984) (applying *Bullington* to sentencing proceedings).

The Supreme Court has recognized that sentencing proceedings, particularly those involving prior conviction allegations, possess the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439 ("The capital sentencing proceeding at issue here clearly resembles a trial on guilt or innocence."). In *Bullington*, the Court held that a jury’s decision not to impose the death penalty after a sentencing trial constituted an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes, precluding a second sentencing trial. *Id.* at 446. The Court reasoned that the procedural safeguards—such as proof beyond a reasonable doubt, the right to counsel, and the opportunity to present and challenge evidence—rendered the sentencing proceeding "like the trial on guilt or innocence." *Id.* at 443.

This Court’s analysis in *Bullington* was not limited to capital cases. The Court explicitly stated that its holding was based on the "nature of the proceeding" and the "procedural protections" afforded the defendant, not the potential punishment. *Id.* at 443-44. Subsequent decisions have reinforced this principle. In *Rumsey*, the Court applied *Bullington* to a noncapital sentencing proceeding, holding that a jury’s decision to impose a life sentence rather than death constituted an acquittal barring retrial. 467 U.S. at 210. The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding "involve[d] the same concerns that drove our decision in *Bullington*." *Id.*

The California Supreme Court’s attempt to distinguish *Bullington* and *Rumsey* is unavailing. The court erroneously suggested that these cases were limited to capital sentencing proceedings, citing *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), for the proposition that the Supreme Court had "suggested" such a limitation. *Monge v. California*, 16 Cal. 4th 826, 836 (1997). However, *Caspari* did not hold that *Bullington* was limited to capital cases; it merely noted that the Supreme Court had not yet extended *Bullington* to noncapital sentencing proceedings. 510 U.S. at 392. The absence of a holding does not constitute a holding to the contrary. Moreover, the Supreme Court has never suggested that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* are inapplicable to noncapital sentencing proceedings. To the contrary, the Court’s reasoning in those cases was based on the procedural protections and the nature of the proceeding, not the severity of the potential punishment. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 443; *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that the Due Process Clause does not require a formal sentencing hearing, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court has since clarified that *Williams* does not apply when the state affords procedural protections akin to those at trial. *Gardner v. Florida*, 430 U.S. 349, 360 (1977) (distinguishing *Williams* in capital sentencing contexts). Here, California statutorily affords defendants a jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses in prior conviction allegations. Cal. Penal Code § 1025; *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). These protections transform the sentencing proceeding into a trial-like adversarial process, triggering double jeopardy protections.

**II. The Double Jeopardy Clause Protects Against Multiple Punishments for the Same Offense**

The Double Jeopardy Clause also prohibits multiple punishments for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969) ("[T]he constitutional guarantee against multiple punishments for the same offense absolutely prohibits a second punishment for the same offense after a valid acquittal."). In *Monge*, the state sought to retry the prior conviction allegation after the trial court found the evidence insufficient to support the enhancement. This retrial would subject Monge to a second punishment for the same prior conviction, in violation of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The California Supreme Court’s conclusion that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple punishments for the same offense, regardless of whether the proceeding is capital or noncapital. *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 396 (1995) ("The Double Jeopardy Clause ... protects against ... multiple punishments for the same offense."). The fact that California statutorily affords procedural protections in sentencing proceedings does not diminish the constitutional protection against double jeopardy.

**Conclusion**

The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of prior conviction allegations in noncapital sentencing proceedings where the state has afforded the defendant procedural protections akin to those at trial. The California Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand for further proceedings consistent with the Double Jeopardy Clause.
