**ARGUMENT**

**I. SELAM WAS DENIED HIS RIGHT TO COMPULSORY PROCESS UNDER THE INDIAN CIVIL RIGHTS ACT.**

The Sixth Amendment’s compulsory process clause, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment in *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), guarantees an accused the right “to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” This right is equally applicable to tribal courts under the Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”), 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). *See Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376, 382 (1896) (holding that the Bill of Rights, including the Sixth Amendment, does not apply to tribal courts unless Congress so provides). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the compulsory process right is fundamental to a fair trial. *See, e.g., In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948) (“A person’s right to reasonable notice of a charge against him, and an opportunity to be heard in his defense—a right to his day in court—are basic in our system of jurisprudence.”).

Here, Selam was denied this right when his appointed counsel, Patricia Leno-Baker, failed to subpoena witnesses he identified as favorable to his defense. The tribal judge and counsel both advised Selam of his right to compel witnesses, yet he did not request subpoenas. This failure does not waive the right itself. *See Ex parte Hull*, 312 U.S. 546, 549 (1941) (“The right to the assistance of counsel is not nullified by a prisoner’s failure to insist upon it.”). The Sixth Amendment’s compulsory process guarantee is not contingent on a defendant’s proactivity; it is the state’s obligation to ensure its enforcement. *See Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (“The right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel.”).

The tribal court’s rejection of Selam’s compulsory process claim was erroneous. The right to compulsory process is not limited to securing the attendance of witnesses but includes the right to present a defense. *See United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858, 867 (1982) (citing *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. at 16) (“The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to present a defense.”). Selam’s inability to present witnesses due to the tribal court’s failure to enforce his compulsory process right deprived him of a fair trial.

**II. SELAM DID NOT WAIVE HIS CONFRONTATION CLAIM UNDER THE ICRA.**

The district court erred in holding that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to raise it in tribal court. The exhaustion requirement under the ICRA is not absolute. *See Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 419 (1963) (“[T]he deliberate bypass rule should be applied only where it would serve a legitimate state interest.”). The Supreme Court has recognized that procedural default rules must yield where fundamental rights are at stake. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (“A waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege.”).

Here, Selam’s confrontation claim was not raised in tribal court because he was unaware of the right or its applicability under the ICRA. The tribal court’s failure to inform him of this right does not constitute a waiver. *See Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962) (“Presuming waiver of constitutional rights from a silent record is impermissible.”). The district court’s application of a rigid exhaustion rule in this context undermines the remedial purpose of the ICRA.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Selam’s convictions should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with his rights under the ICRA.
