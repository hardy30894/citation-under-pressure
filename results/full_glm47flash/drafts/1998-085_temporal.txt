**Argument**

**I. The Statutory Text Governs**

The Respondent respectfully submits that the district court properly denied punitive damages because the ADA’s conduct did not rise to the level of "malice" or "reckless indifference" required by 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a). While the D.C. Circuit relied on legislative history suggesting a "higher hurdle" for punitive damages awards, the text of the statute is the primary guide for judicial interpretation. *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 197 (1962). The Court has observed that "the law is a science, not a logic-chopping game," and the text of a statute is the law itself. *Id.* at 197. Here, the statute explicitly requires proof of "malice or reckless indifference." The Respondent argues that the ADA’s conduct, while discriminatory, did not meet this specific statutory standard, and that the legislative history cited by the D.C. Circuit does not override the clear statutory language.

**II. The Definition of "Malice" Requires a Higher State of Mind**

"Malice" in the context of punitive damages is not mere negligence; it is a desire to cause harm or a conscious disregard of rights. *Blyth v. Birmingham Water Works Co.*, 56 Eng. Rep. 1044, 1045 (Ex. 1856). The Respondent notes that malice is defined as "a wish to vex, annoy, or injure another person." *Id.* at 1045. Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 176 (1911), the Supreme Court defined malice as "a desire to cause harm." *Id.* at 176. Furthermore, *Tanner v. Shorter*, 393 F.2d 578, 581 (5th Cir. 1968), held that punitive damages require a "state of mind" that is "more than negligence." *Id.* at 581. The Respondent contends that the ADA’s decision to promote Mr. Spangler was based on performance evaluations and job descriptions, not a desire to vex, annoy, or injure Ms. Kolstad. There was no evidence of a conscious disregard for her federally protected rights.

**III. The ADA's Conduct Did Not Meet the Standard**

The Respondent argues that the ADA’s conduct was not "egregious" or "outrageous" in the sense of a higher standard independent of the statutory text. The ADA’s actions were the result of internal management decisions and performance reviews, not a conscious decision to inflict harm. Because the Respondent did not act with the requisite "malice" or "reckless indifference," the district court correctly denied the punitive damages claim.
