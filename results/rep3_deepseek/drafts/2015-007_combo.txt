**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida’s Sentencing Scheme Does Not Violate the Sixth Amendment Because the Jury’s Role Is Advisory, and Aggravating Factors Are Not Elements of the Offense.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to trial by jury for “all crimes.” *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2, 123 (1866). But this right extends only to “facts necessary to constitute the crime.” *Id.* at 126. In *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 538 (1884), this Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate the grand-jury requirement, underscoring that the states retain flexibility in defining criminal procedures. Florida’s scheme, which leaves the ultimate sentencing decision to the judge, comports with this principle.

Under Florida law, the jury’s role is advisory. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141 (2012). The jury recommends a sentence, but the judge makes the final determination. This Court has long held that “the jury’s function is to find the facts, and the court’s function is to apply the law.” *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 102 (1895). Because aggravating factors in Florida are not elements of a separate offense but rather sentencing considerations, the Sixth Amendment does not require jury unanimity on their existence. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 250 (1949) (holding that due process does not require jury findings on sentencing facts).

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Unanimity in Capital Sentencing.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments,” but it does not mandate specific procedural requirements for capital sentencing. *See Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958) (plurality opinion). This Court has consistently upheld state discretion in capital procedures. *See McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 196 (1971) (holding that the Constitution does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing). Indeed, in *McGautha*, the Court rejected the argument that the Eighth Amendment demands “a unitary, all-or-nothing verdict” from the jury. *Id.* at 207.

Florida’s scheme, which permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation, is not “cruel and unusual” simply because it differs from other states’ practices. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910) (defining cruel and unusual punishment as “something more than the mere extinguishment of life”). The Eighth Amendment’s “evolving standards of decency” test, *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101, does not require uniformity among the states. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535 (1968) (plurality opinion) (noting that states may experiment with different procedures).

**III. The Jury Need Not Determine Mental Retardation as a Bar to Execution.**

In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), this Court left to the states the “task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction” on executing the mentally retarded. *Id.* at 317. Florida has done so by requiring the defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence to the sentencing judge. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4). The jury’s role in this determination is not constitutionally required. *See Williams*, 337 U.S. at 250 (holding that the judge may consider evidence not presented to the jury).

**IV. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Violate the Prohibition Against Cruel and Unusual Punishment.**

The Eighth Amendment does not require that every capital defendant receive a unanimous jury verdict. *See McGautha*, 402 U.S. at 196. Florida’s scheme, which requires the judge to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors, ensures the “heightened reliability” demanded by the Eighth Amendment. *See Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976) (plurality opinion) (requiring individualized sentencing). The judge’s independent review of the evidence, including mental retardation claims, satisfies this standard.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
