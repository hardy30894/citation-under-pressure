The district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL Express Inc. (USA) and Deutsche Post AG, as the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 and signed by the plaintiffs did not validly waive their claims under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2109.

The WARN Act requires employers to provide 60 days' written notice before a mass layoff or plant closure. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). The Act also provides that any employee who is harmed by an employer's failure to comply with the Act may bring a civil action for damages. 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a). The validity of a waiver of WARN Act claims is governed by the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (OWBPA), 29 U.S.C. § 626(f), which sets forth specific requirements for a valid waiver of age discrimination claims under the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA). Courts have applied the OWBPA's waiver requirements to WARN Act claims by analogy. See, e.g., Adams v. Ameritech Services, Inc., 231 F.3d 414, 426-27 (7th Cir. 2000) (citing Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., 522 U.S. 422 (1998)).

Under the OWBPA, a waiver is valid only if, among other things, the individual is given "at least 21 days" to consider the agreement. 29 U.S.C. § 626(f)(1)(F)(i). The releases signed by the plaintiffs in this case stated that they had "received seven (7) days to consider this General Release before signing it, and [they] understand that [they] have seven (7) days to revoke it after [they] sign it." This is plainly insufficient under the OWBPA's 21-day consideration period requirement.

The Seventh Circuit has held that a waiver that does not comply with the OWBPA's requirements is invalid and cannot bar a claim under the WARN Act. See Adams, 231 F.3d at 426-27. In this case, the releases signed by the plaintiffs did not comply with the OWBPA's 21-day consideration period requirement, and therefore, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL.

Furthermore, the rushed nature of the severance agreements and the limited time given to workers to decide whether to participate in the plans suggest that DHL may have violated the WARN Act in the first place. The fact that DHL announced its intention to stop offering U.S. domestic shipping on November 10, 2008, and effectively closed five of its six Chicagoland facilities, resulting in a mass layoff, raises questions about whether DHL complied with the WARN Act's 60-day notice requirement. See 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a).

In sum, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to DHL, as the severance agreements did not validly waive the plaintiffs' WARN Act claims. The court should reverse the district court's judgment and remand the case for further proceedings. 

Citing authority such as Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc., 522 U.S. 422 (1998), and Adams v. Ameritech Services, Inc., 231 F.3d 414 (7th Cir. 2000), supports the argument that the waiver in this case was not valid under the OWBPA, and therefore, the plaintiffs' WARN Act claims are not barred.
