# ARGUMENT

**Section 1231(a)(6) Must Be Construed Not to Authorize Indefinite, Potentially Permanent Detention of a Removable Alien Once Removal Is No Longer Reasonably Foreseeable**

## I. Indefinite Civil Detention Without a Foreseeable Endpoint Raises Serious Constitutional Concerns

"Freedom from bodily restraint has always been at the core of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause from arbitrary governmental action." *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). That liberty interest is not diminished merely because detention is labeled civil rather than criminal. This Court has permitted civil confinement only where accompanied by strong procedural protections and a special justification tightly linked to a legitimate, non-punitive purpose—for example, the connection between dangerousness and a diagnosed mental abnormality that itself distinguishes the confined individual and provides a rational endpoint to the confinement. *Kansas v. Hendricks*, 521 U.S. 346, 358 (1997). Where such a justification is absent, or where the confinement lacks any limiting principle, due process forbids it. *Foucha*, 504 U.S. at 82-83 (State could not confine an insanity acquittee indefinitely once he was no longer mentally ill, without more).

Even in the criminal pretrial context, where the government's interest in preventing flight and danger to the community is at its zenith, this Court has emphasized that "[i]n our society liberty is the norm, and detention prior to trial or without trial is the carefully limited exception." *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 755 (1987). The Bail Reform Act passed constitutional muster in *Salerno* only because detention was strictly time-limited, subject to stringent procedural protections, and imposed on the basis of individualized findings by a judicial officer. *Id.* at 747, 751-52. Indefinite detention of a removable alien with no reasonably foreseeable endpoint shares none of these safeguards: it rests on an executive determination, is potentially permanent, and bears no relationship to any judicially cognizable limiting principle.

## II. Zadvydas, a Lawfully Admitted Resident Alien, Is Entitled to the Full Protection of the Due Process Clause

Aliens who have entered the United States—even unlawfully—"have long been recognized as 'persons' guaranteed due process of law by the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments." *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 210 (1982). This principle traces to *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896), where this Court held that once an alien has "passed through the port of entry," the Constitution's protections against deprivation of liberty without due process apply with full force, regardless of alienage. Nor is the constitutional calculus altered by the fact that Zadvydas has been ordered removed. As this Court has recognized, resident aliens facing deportation retain a due process interest cognizable by the courts, and the government may not treat them, for purposes of physical liberty, as though they possessed no protected interest at all. *Mathews v. Diaz*, 426 U.S. 67, 77 (1976) (aliens who have become part of our population are entitled to the protection of the Due Process Clause).

## III. Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei Does Not Control Because Zadvydas Effected Entry

The government's reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. Mezei was an alien who had never gained entry into the United States; he was detained at the border after being denied admission upon return from abroad, and his detention was analyzed under the "entry fiction," whereby an alien stopped at the threshold is treated, for constitutional purposes, as if he had never entered the country. *Id.* at 212-13. Zadvydas, by contrast, lawfully entered and resided in the United States for decades. That distinction is constitutionally decisive: an alien who has effected entry, unlike one merely paroled or excluded at the border, is entitled to the substantive liberty protections that flow from presence within the national community. *Plyler*, 457 U.S. at 210; *Wong Wing*, 163 U.S. at 238.

## IV. The Canon of Constitutional Avoidance Requires Reading an Implicit Reasonable-Time Limit into Section 1231(a)(6)

Nothing in the text of § 1231(a)(6) expressly authorizes detention beyond any temporal limit whatsoever. Where, as here, "an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress." *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988). This Court applies that canon with particular vigilance where a broad reading of a statute would place personal liberty indefinitely within executive discretion, unmoored from any judicially enforceable endpoint. *Jones v. United States*, 529 U.S. 848, 857 (2000) (construing a federal statute narrowly to avoid grave constitutional doubt). Section 1231(a)(6) should likewise be read to permit detention only for the period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal—not to authorize potentially permanent detention whenever no receiving country can be found.

## Conclusion

Because indefinite civil detention untethered from any reasonably foreseeable removal offends the liberty interest this Court has repeatedly protected, and because the statute is fairly susceptible to a narrower construction that avoids that constitutional infirmity, § 1231(a)(6) must be read to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure an alien's removal. The judgment below should be reversed.
