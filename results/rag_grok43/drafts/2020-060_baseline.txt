**ARGUMENT**

The 2004 consent decree did not resolve Guam’s liability “for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action” within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Guam therefore retains its independent right to recover response costs under § 107(a), id. § 9607(a), and the six-year limitations period of § 113(g)(2)(B) governs. The decision below, which treated the decree as triggering the three-year bar of § 113(g)(3), rests on a misreading of the statute and controlling precedent.

Section 113(f)(3)(B) authorizes contribution only after a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action.” The phrase “response action” is defined by reference to CERCLA’s own remedial scheme. See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25). The 2004 decree arose exclusively from alleged violations of the Clean Water Act; it imposed obligations to close the Ordot Dump and install a cover system to abate ongoing pollutant discharges, not to perform a CERCLA response action. As the Court explained in Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 166 (2004), the contribution right created by § 113(f) is “expressly conditioned” on the existence of a qualifying CERCLA proceeding or settlement. No such proceeding occurred here.

The decree’s disclaimer of liability reinforces the point. It states that the agreement was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3. This Court has long recognized that a settlement does not resolve liability when the parties expressly disclaim any adjudication of fault. See, e.g., Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc., 516 U.S. 479, 485 (1996) (distinguishing RCRA citizen suits from CERCLA cost-recovery actions on the ground that only the latter permit recovery of past cleanup costs). The decree’s substantive terms—payment of a civil penalty and construction of a cover—address CWA violations, not CERCLA liability for hazardous-substance releases.

Even if the decree could be read to address some response costs, it did not resolve liability “to the United States or a State” for purposes of CERCLA. The United States appeared solely as EPA enforcer under the Clean Water Act; it did not act in its capacity as a CERCLA settling party. Cf. Ngiraingas v. Sanchez, 495 U.S. 182, 190 (1990) (distinguishing Guam’s sovereign status from federal instrumentalities). Treating every CWA consent decree as a CERCLA settlement would nullify the distinct limitations periods Congress enacted and would permit the government to extinguish § 107 claims through non-CERCLA litigation—an outcome the statute does not countenance.

Because Guam’s § 113(f)(3)(B) right never accrued, the six-year period of § 113(g)(2)(B) applies to its § 107(a) claim. The complaint, filed in 2017, is timely. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
