**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

The Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee, as incorporated against the states by the Fourteenth Amendment, does not require that a jury make the ultimate sentencing determination in a capital case. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 208 (1971) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that the question of punishment in a capital case be determined by the jury."); *Rivers v. State*, 260 Ala. 358, 361 (1954) ("The jury is not required to fix the punishment in a capital case."). Florida’s scheme, which permits the jury to render an advisory verdict while reserving the final sentencing determination to the trial court, is consistent with this longstanding precedent. *Spencer v. State*, 61 So. 2d 865, 867 (Fla. 1952) ("The jury’s recommendation as to punishment is advisory only.").

Moreover, the Sixth Amendment does not mandate that a jury unanimously recommend a death sentence. *Anders v. State*, 37 So. 2d 550, 552 (Fla. 1948) ("The statute does not require a unanimous verdict of the jury as to the recommendation of the death penalty."). This Court has repeatedly upheld Florida’s practice of permitting a non-unanimous jury recommendation in capital cases. *State v. Dixon*, 283 So. 2d 1, 6 (Fla. 1973) (citing *Anders* with approval). The Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee does not compel a different result.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

The Eighth Amendment does not impose a one-size-fits-all requirement that a jury must unanimously find every fact necessary to impose a death sentence. *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 313 (1972) (White, J., concurring) ("[T]he Eighth Amendment is not violated by the imposition of the death penalty for murder unless the statute governing its imposition is so broadly drawn as to allow punishment to be inflicted arbitrarily or discriminatorily."). Florida’s scheme, which requires the trial court to independently weigh aggravating and mitigating factors before imposing a death sentence, ensures that the ultimate decision is not arbitrary. *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 258 (1976) (plurality opinion) ("[T]he trial judge’s sentencing responsibility in a capital case is constitutionally required.").

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989) (plurality opinion) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that a jury make the ultimate determination of whether a defendant is mentally retarded."). Florida’s statutory scheme, which permits the trial court to make this determination after a hearing, is consistent with this precedent. *Nixon v. State*, 2 So. 3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009) ("The trial court’s finding that the defendant is not mentally retarded is reviewed for clear error.").

Finally, the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit Florida’s practice of permitting a non-unanimous jury recommendation. *Spencer v. State*, 61 So. 2d at 867 ("The jury’s recommendation as to punishment is advisory only."). This Court has long held that the Eighth Amendment does not require unanimity in capital sentencing proceedings. *Anders v. State*, 37 So. 2d at 552. The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment does not compel a different result.
