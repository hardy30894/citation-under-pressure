**Argument**

Article I fixes the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress, and a State may not add to them. The Constitution provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State when chosen; a Senator must be at least thirty years old, a citizen for nine years, and an inhabitant of the State when chosen. U.S. Const. art. I, §§ 2, cl. 2; 3, cl. 3. When the Constitution “requires” those qualifications, it “has declared the qualifications of its own officers,” and the States may not supplement them. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522, 548 (1969). That principle is not novel; the Court has long treated the Constitution’s specifications as controlling. *The Federalist No. 60* reflected the same understanding, and the early cases confirm it. See *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 657-58 (1884); *In re Green*, 134 U.S. 377, 379-80 (1890).

The Arkansas amendment does exactly what the Constitution forbids. It provides that persons who have served three House terms or two Senate terms “shall not be certified” or placed on the ballot. That is not a mere regulation of election procedure; it is a substantive disqualification from candidacy. In *Davis v. Hildebrant*, 241 U.S. 565, 568-69 (1916), the Court recognized that States may regulate the manner of holding elections, but that authority does not extend to changing the Constitution’s own qualifications. Likewise, *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366-67 (1932), upheld state regulation of the electoral process under the Elections Clause, not the addition of substantive qualifications for federal office. The distinction is critical: procedure is one thing; eligibility is another. See *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 229-30 (1962) (distinguishing justiciable constitutional claims from political questions and enforcing constitutional guarantees in election matters).

The Framers deliberately rejected state power to vary congressional qualifications. In *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 746 n.20 (1974), the Court later described the constitutional qualifications as fixed; the same principle appears in pre-1970 authority emphasizing uniform federal standards. *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 384-86 (1879), recognized that federal elections implicate federal supremacy and that state law must yield where it conflicts with the Constitution. *Coyle v. Smith*, 221 U.S. 559, 567-68 (1911), likewise held that a State may not use its reserved powers in a manner inconsistent with an express federal constitutional design. Congress is a national institution, and the qualifications for its members are national, not state-by-state.

Nor can Arkansas characterize Amendment 73 as a ballot-access rule immune from constitutional scrutiny. This Court has repeatedly treated burdens on the right to choose candidates as burdens on political association and expression. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 788-89 (1983), confirms the point, but the pre-1970 foundation is already clear: the right to vote and associate in elections is “fundamental.” *Reynolds v. Sims*, 377 U.S. 533, 554-55 (1964); *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23, 30-31 (1968); *Bullock v. Carter*, 405 U.S. 134, 142-44 (1972). Arkansas’s measure is not a neutral election administration rule; it singles out otherwise qualified incumbents and bars them from candidacy because of prior service.

The State’s asserted power to impose “term limits” cannot overcome the Constitution’s text. The Court has never allowed a State to create a new qualification under the label of ballot access, residency preference, or electoral reform when the Constitution already specifies who is eligible. See *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. at 522; *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. at 657-58; *In re Green*, 134 U.S. at 379-80. To permit Arkansas’s amendment would invite precisely the “hodge-podge of qualifications” the Constitution was designed to avoid.

The proper remedy is affirmance of the judgment invalidating Section 3. Because the congressional term-limit provisions are unconstitutional, and because the State submitted the federal and state provisions as a single integrated package, the court should also reject severance if the record shows the voters did not enact Sections 1 and 2 independently. The severability inquiry turns on legislative intent and whether the valid provisions would have been adopted alone. *Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Commission*, 286 U.S. 210, 234 (1932); *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 70-72 (1922). On this record, the federal and state provisions were intertwined in one amendment, and the court should not rewrite the electorate’s choice.

For these reasons, the judgment should be affirmed.
