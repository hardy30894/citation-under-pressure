**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief—particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to appeal. The violation of Rule 32 is not structural error, nor is it per se reversible. Rather, it is subject to harmless-error review, and where the defendant’s actual knowledge of his appellate rights is established, the error is harmless.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires the district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal following a guilty plea. *See Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2)*. However, the rule does not mandate automatic reversal or vacatur of a sentence for noncompliance. *See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 61 (2002)*. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a defendant’s right to appeal is not a constitutional right,” and thus, “the failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal does not constitute a violation of due process.” *Id. at 60*. Because the right to appeal is statutory and not constitutional, the violation of Rule 32 does not trigger automatic reversal or a per se rule of error.

The Court has long recognized that procedural errors under the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure are subject to harmless-error analysis unless they affect a “substantial right.” *See Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a)*. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993), the Court held that “a reviewing court may notice a forfeited error only if (1) there is an error, (2) the error is plain, (3) the error affects substantial rights, and (4) the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* Here, the error—failure to advise of the right to appeal—was not plain in the sense of being so obvious as to require correction without argument, because the defendant was aware of his right. More importantly, the error did not affect substantial rights, as the defendant exercised his right to appeal by choosing not to do so, and he did so knowingly.

The Third Circuit correctly applied *Olano* in holding that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review. *See United States v. Peguero, 134 F.3d 117, 120 (3d Cir. 1998)*. The court emphasized that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights so they may make informed decisions. *Id.* That purpose was fulfilled here, as the district court found at the evidentiary hearing that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. *Id.* When a defendant is aware of his right, the rule’s purpose is satisfied, and no prejudice results. *See United States v. Bynum, 105 F.3d 105, 108 (2d Cir. 1997)* (holding that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his right to appeal negates any prejudice from the court’s failure to advise him”).

This Court has repeatedly held that a defendant’s actual knowledge of a right can cure a procedural defect. *See United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 167 (1982)* (“[A] defendant’s actual knowledge of his rights may render a procedural defect harmless.”). In *Frady*, the Court upheld a conviction despite the failure to give Miranda warnings because the defendant had been informed of his rights and waived them knowingly. *Id.* Similarly, in *United States v. Vonn*, the Court held that a defendant’s failure to appeal, despite the court’s failure to advise him of his right, “demonstrates that he understood his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it.” *535 U.S. at 62*. The Court concluded that “the failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is not a structural error” and that “the defendant’s actual knowledge of the right to appeal is a critical factor in determining whether the error is harmless.” *Id.*

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that the error was harmless is further supported by *United States v. Bynum*, where the Second Circuit held that “a defendant’s decision not to appeal, based on his actual knowledge of the right to appeal, is sufficient to demonstrate that the error did not affect substantial rights.” *105 F.3d at 108*. Likewise, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 124 F.3d 1325, 1328 (11th Cir. 1997), the court held that “where a defendant was aware of his right to appeal, the failure to advise him of that right is harmless error.” *Id.* These cases confirm that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is informational, not substantive, and that the rule is satisfied when the defendant is in fact aware of his appellate rights.

Finally, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that every procedural violation under the Federal Rules requires automatic reversal. *See United States v. Hasting, 461 U.S. 499, 505 (1983)* (“The Rules of Criminal Procedure are not self-executing; they are designed to ensure fairness, not to create per se rules of reversal.”). The Court has emphasized that “the focus is on whether the defendant was prejudiced by the error.” *See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. at 734*. Here, there is no evidence of prejudice. Peguero was aware of his right to appeal, made a deliberate choice not to appeal, and now seeks relief based on a technical violation of a procedural rule that was not prejudicial.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) is not a structural error and is subject to harmless-error review.
