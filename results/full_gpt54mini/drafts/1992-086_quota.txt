The District’s refusal to let Lamb’s Chapel use school premises to show religious-oriented films violated the First Amendment because it discriminated against speech based on viewpoint in a forum the District had opened to community use. When the government creates a forum for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech because of the perspective from which the speaker addresses an otherwise permitted subject. Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 44-46 (1983); Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 800-06 (1985).

The school facilities here were at least a limited public forum. The District authorized after-hours use by community groups and regularly permitted outside organizations to use school property for presentations, meetings, and other expressive activities. Once the State opens property for “certain groups” or “certain subjects,” it may not engage in content discrimination that is not necessary to preserve the forum’s purpose. Perry, 460 U.S. at 46. In a limited public forum, restrictions must be reasonable and viewpoint neutral. Cornelius, 473 U.S. at 806; Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260, 267-70 (1988).

This Court’s cases make clear that denying access because the program is religious in perspective is viewpoint discrimination, not a permissible subject-matter limitation. In Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269-70 (1981), the Court held that when a university generally opens its facilities, it may not exclude religious use simply because the speech is religious. The Court emphasized that the policy there burdened “religious speech” and that exclusion based on the “religious nature” of the speech was unconstitutional. Id. at 269-70. Similarly, Board of Education of the Westside Community Schools v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 236-47 (1990), held that once a school opens a limited forum to noncurricular student groups, it may not discriminate “based on the content of the students’ speech” against a Christian club. Id. at 247. Although Mergens arose under the Equal Access Act, the Court explained that the school could not single out religious speech for exclusion once the forum was opened. Id. at 240-47.

The District’s asserted distinction—that Lamb’s Chapel wanted to discuss family values from a Christian perspective—cannot save the denial. The school permitted community use for civic, educational, and social presentations; it then excluded Lamb’s Chapel because the same subject matter would be presented from a religious viewpoint. That is precisely the sort of discrimination forbidden by Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 829-31 (1995), which held that the government may not “regulate speech when the specific motivating ideology or the opinion or perspective of the speaker is the rationale for the restriction.” Id. at 829. See also Good News Club v. Milford Central School, 533 U.S. 98, 107-12 (2001) (excluding a club because it sought to address permitted subjects from a Christian viewpoint was impermissible viewpoint discrimination).

Nor can the District justify its policy as a neutral refusal to allow “religious purposes.” The Court has repeatedly held that speech does not lose First Amendment protection because it is religious. See Widmar, 454 U.S. at 269-70; Mergens, 496 U.S. at 248-53; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 828-31. The District’s rule operated not as a general limit on meetings, but as a prohibition on one viewpoint within otherwise allowed subject matter. That is unconstitutional in any forum other than a nonpublic forum, and the District’s own practice prevents it from claiming that status. Perry, 460 U.S. at 46; Cornelius, 473 U.S. at 802-06.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on prior restrictions cannot withstand this Court’s First Amendment decisions. A forum’s character depends on the government’s policy and practice, but once the government permits expressive use by community groups, it must administer access on neutral terms. Cornelius, 473 U.S. at 802-06; Widmar, 454 U.S. at 267-70. The District could exclude all outside groups, or all discussions of a certain subject, if done evenhandedly. It could not permit family-oriented films and then deny one because the film presents the same ideas “from a Christian perspective.” Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 829-31; Good News Club, 533 U.S. at 107-12.

Finally, the Establishment Clause provides no defense. In Widmar, the Court held that equal access to religious speech does not amount to an establishment of religion. 454 U.S. at 274-75. The same principle governs here: allowing Lamb’s Chapel to use the premises on the same terms as other groups would reflect neutrality, not endorsement. See Mergens, 496 U.S. at 250-53; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 839-46.

The judgment should be reversed.
