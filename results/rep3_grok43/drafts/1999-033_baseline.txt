The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment secures equal treatment under the law by prohibiting state action that classifies persons on the basis of impermissible criteria or with an invidious purpose. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954); *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976). It does not, however, create a freestanding right to challenge every instance of alleged differential treatment absent membership in a class or group.

This Court has consistently required plaintiffs asserting equal-protection violations to identify a classification that distinguishes them from others similarly situated. *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977) (holding that proof of discriminatory purpose “implies that the decisionmaker . . . selected or reaffirmed a particular course of action at least in part ‘because of,’ not merely ‘in spite of’ its adverse effects upon an identifiable group”). The Clause guards against “invidious discrimination” arising from membership in a protected class, *Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 442 U.S. 256, 273 (1979), or from the exercise of a fundamental right, *Shapiro v. Thompson*, 394 U.S. 618, 634 (1969). It does not authorize federal courts to superintend the routine, discretionary decisions of local officials on the theory that those decisions were motivated by personal animus toward a single individual.

Recognizing a “class-of-one” claim would sever the Clause from its textual and historical moorings. The guarantee of “equal protection of the laws” presupposes comparison among persons within a defined class. *Plyler v. Doe*, 457 U.S. 202, 216 (1982). Without a class, there is no basis for determining whether similarly situated individuals have been treated differently on account of an impermissible consideration. Allowing such claims would transform every zoning, permitting, or service decision into a potential constitutional case whenever a disappointed applicant alleges subjective ill will, a result incompatible with the limited role of the Equal Protection Clause in reviewing economic and social legislation. *FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 313 (1993).

The Seventh Circuit’s “spiteful effort” standard finds no support in this Court’s precedents. It collapses the distinction between equal protection and ordinary tort or due-process claims, inviting federal courts to second-guess the subjective motivations of local officials without any objective classification to review. Because respondent Olech alleged neither membership in a protected class nor any other recognized basis for heightened scrutiny, the complaint failed to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
