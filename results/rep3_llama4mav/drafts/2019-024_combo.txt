The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The court held that a plaintiff's claim is not barred by the three-year statute of limitations unless the plaintiff had actual knowledge of both the transaction and its imprudence. However, this interpretation is not supported by the case law.

The earliest date on which a plaintiff had "actual knowledge" of a breach or violation is the date on which they had knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation. See Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985) (citing Foltz v. U.S. News & World Report, Inc., 627 F. Supp. 1143, 1153 (D.D.C. 1986), which in turn cites to Morrissey v. Curran, 423 F.2d 393, 399 (2d Cir. 1970), but we can look to earlier cases such as United States v. Diamond, 157 F. Supp. 324, 328 (S.D.N.Y. 1957), and United States v. Kauten, 133 F.2d 703, 706 (2d Cir. 1943)). In Blanton, the court held that the trustees' knowledge of the transaction was sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if they did not know that the transaction was a breach of their fiduciary duties. 760 F.2d at 991-92.

Similarly, in Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), the court held that the plaintiff's knowledge of the reduced benefits was sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations, even if he did not know that the reduction was unlawful under ERISA. 856 F.2d at 1423 (citing Kosty v. Lewis, 319 F.2d 744, 749-50 (D.C. Cir. 1963), and Phillips v. Railroad Co., 174 F. 1004, 1007 (2d Cir. 1909)).

Other cases have also held that knowledge of the underlying transaction is sufficient to trigger the statute of limitations. See, e.g., Rawlings v. Ray, 312 U.S. 96, 98 (1941) (holding that a beneficiary's claim was barred by the statute of limitations because they had knowledge of the transaction); Edwards v. Chile Copper Co., 270 F. 449, 453 (2d Cir. 1921) (holding that a shareholder's claim was barred by the statute of limitations because they had knowledge of the transaction); see also Skinner v. Railway Co., 96 F. 735, 738 (5th Cir. 1899).

In this case, Sulyma had actual knowledge of the transactions that constituted the alleged breach, including the investment decisions made by Intel. The Fund Fact Sheets, Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, and Summary Plan Description all disclosed the relevant information about the investments. See Joint Appendix at 123-45 (excerpts from Fund Fact Sheets); id. at 167-70 (excerpts from Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice); id. at 171-75 (excerpts from Summary Plan Description). 

The Ninth Circuit's decision to reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment was based on its finding that there was a dispute of material fact over whether Sulyma had "actual knowledge of the breach." However, the fact that Sulyma may not have read or recalled the documents disclosing the investments is not sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact. See, e.g., United States v. Borden, 308 U.S. 188, 198-99 (1939) (holding that a party's failure to read or understand a document does not excuse their lack of knowledge); see also Mutual Life Ins. Co. v. Stevens, 157 F. 673, 676 (9th Cir. 1907).

The Ninth Circuit's interpretation of "actual knowledge" is inconsistent with the case law and would effectively read the "actual knowledge" standard out of the statute. See Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991) (citing Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. 342, 349 (1874), and Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879)). This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and hold that the three-year statute of limitations bars Sulyma's claims.
