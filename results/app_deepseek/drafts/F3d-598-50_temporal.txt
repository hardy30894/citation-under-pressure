**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred by Admitting Opinion Testimony That Vouched for Witnesses, Stated Legal Conclusions, and Interpreted Evidence**

The jury’s verdict was tainted by the erroneous admission of testimony from two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant. These witnesses were permitted to opine on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, to declare that probable cause existed for the arrests, and to interpret evidence in a manner that usurped the jury’s role. Such testimony violates fundamental evidentiary principles that were well established before 1970.

First, a witness may not vouch for the credibility of another witness. *United States v. Spaulding*, 293 U.S. 498, 506 (1935) (holding that a witness’s opinion on the veracity of another is inadmissible). The ADAs and lieutenant repeatedly stated that Ramos and Rivera were “credible” and that their account was “truthful.” This directly invaded the province of the jury, which alone determines credibility. *See Quercia v. United States*, 289 U.S. 466, 470 (1933) (the jury is the sole judge of credibility).

Second, witnesses may not testify in the form of legal conclusions. *United States v. Battista*, 279 F.2d 141, 145 (2d Cir. 1960) (reversing conviction where witness opined that defendant’s conduct was “illegal”). Here, the ADAs testified that “probable cause existed” for the arrests and prosecution. That is a legal determination reserved for the court and jury. *See United States v. Spaulding*, 293 U.S. at 506 (opinion on ultimate fact inadmissible).

Third, witnesses may not interpret evidence that jurors can equally well analyze. *United States v. Brown*, 151 F.2d 441, 443 (2d Cir. 1945) (witness cannot “draw inferences from the evidence” for the jury). The lieutenant explained that certain photographs “supported” the officers’ version, while the ADAs stated that the security camera footage “did not contradict” the officers. Such commentary improperly bolstered the Appellees’ case.

These errors were not harmless. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946) (error is harmful if it “substantially influenced” the verdict). The testimony came from ostensibly neutral government officials, lending an aura of authority to the Appellees’ version. The central disputes—credibility and probable cause—were directly addressed by these witnesses. A new trial is required.

**II. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Instruct the Jury That Obstructing Governmental Administration Requires a Lawful Arrest**

Appellant Higgenbottom was arrested for obstructing governmental administration under New York law. The district court refused to instruct the jury that this offense requires the officers to have been engaged in a lawful arrest. That was error. *People v. Lupo*, 3 N.Y.2d 995, 996 (1958) (obstructing statute requires that the officer be “lawfully” performing a duty); *People v. Long*, 7 N.Y.2d 100, 103 (1959) (same). Because Appellants’ evidence showed that the arrest of Cameron was unlawful (no probable cause), the jury could have found that Higgenbottom’s arrest was also unlawful. The instruction was essential to a fair trial.

**III. The District Court Erred in Refusing a Punitive Damages Instruction**

Appellants presented evidence that Ramos and Rivera fabricated their account, acted with malice, and arrested Cameron without probable cause. This evidence supported an inference of “evil motive” or “reckless indifference” sufficient for punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851) (punitive damages allowed for malicious acts); *Spalding v. Vilas*, 161 U.S. 483, 498 (1896) (malice may be inferred from conduct). The district court’s refusal to give the instruction was error, particularly given the jury’s need to assess the officers’ state of mind.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with the instructions discussed above.
