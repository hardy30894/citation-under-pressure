## ARGUMENT

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to introduce expert evidence of mental disease or defect to negate the *mens rea* element of a crime when the State has provided an alternative avenue—the insanity defense—for considering such evidence. Arizona’s evidentiary rule is a constitutionally permissible exercise of its sovereign authority to define criminal responsibility and to structure the trial process.

### I. States Have Broad Discretion to Define the Role of Mental Illness in Criminal Cases

The Constitution leaves to the States the task of defining the elements of criminal offenses and the defenses available to a defendant. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–202 (1977). This includes the authority to determine whether and how evidence of mental illness may be used to negate criminal intent. In *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 56 (1996), this Court upheld a state law that excluded evidence of voluntary intoxication to negate *mens rea*, reasoning that the State could rationally conclude that such evidence is unreliable or confusing. Similarly, Arizona has determined that expert testimony about mental disease or defect is best confined to the insanity defense, where the defendant bears the burden of proof. That choice does not offend due process.

Arizona’s rule does not bar all evidence of mental illness. It simply channels such evidence to the insanity proceeding, where the defendant may present it fully. The defendant remains free to introduce lay testimony or other non-expert evidence to challenge *mens rea*. The restriction applies only to expert opinion evidence, which the State may regulate to ensure reliability and avoid jury confusion. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993) (trial courts serve as gatekeepers for expert testimony).

### II. Due Process Does Not Require States to Allow Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea

The right to present a defense is fundamental but not absolute. *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973). States may impose reasonable restrictions on the admissibility of evidence so long as they are not arbitrary or disproportionate. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 55–56 (1987). Arizona’s rule is neither.

First, the rule serves a legitimate state interest in preserving the distinction between *mens rea* and insanity. *Mens rea* concerns whether the defendant intended the act; insanity concerns whether the defendant should be held criminally responsible despite that intent. Allowing expert testimony on mental illness to negate *mens rea* would blur this line and invite juries to acquit based on diminished capacity, a defense Arizona has chosen not to recognize. *See State v. Mott*, 931 P.2d 1046, 1050 (Ariz. 1997) (en banc) (Arizona rejects diminished capacity defense). This Court has long recognized that States may define the insanity defense narrowly. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 800–801 (1952) (upholding Oregon’s requirement that defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt).

Second, the rule avoids the risk of misleading or confusing the jury. Expert testimony about a defendant’s mental state at the time of the crime is inherently complex and often speculative. Arizona could rationally conclude that such evidence is more probative on the issue of insanity—where the defendant bears the burden—than on *mens rea*, where the prosecution must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 51 (State may exclude evidence that “could easily mislead the jury”).

Third, the rule does not deprive the defendant of a meaningful opportunity to present a defense. Clark was permitted to introduce all of his expert evidence in support of his insanity claim. He simply could not use that same evidence to argue that he lacked *mens rea*. Because the insanity defense provides a full and fair forum for considering mental illness, the defendant’s due process rights are satisfied. *See Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 535–536 (1968) (plurality opinion) (State may choose to address chronic alcoholism through insanity defense rather than as a defense to *actus reus*).

### III. Arizona’s Rule Is Consistent with Historical Practice and the Common Law

At common law, evidence of mental disease was admissible only on the issue of insanity, not to negate *mens rea*. *See, e.g., Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946) (noting that “the defense of insanity is separate and distinct from the defense of lack of intent”). The modern trend toward allowing such evidence for *mens rea* is a policy choice, not a constitutional command. Arizona has adhered to the traditional rule, and nothing in the Due Process Clause requires it to adopt a more permissive approach.

### IV. The Defendant’s Proposed Rule Would Undermine the State’s Insanity Scheme

If defendants could present expert evidence of mental illness to negate *mens rea*, the insanity defense would become superfluous in many cases. A defendant could avoid both conviction and the consequences of an insanity commitment by simply arguing that he lacked intent. Arizona has a legitimate interest in ensuring that defendants who are mentally ill but criminally responsible are held accountable, and that those who are not responsible are subject to civil commitment. *See Addington v. Texas*, 441 U.S. 418, 426–427 (1979) (State has parens patriae interest in committing mentally ill persons). The rule at issue preserves that balance.

### Conclusion

Arizona’s evidentiary rule is a rational and constitutional exercise of state authority. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
