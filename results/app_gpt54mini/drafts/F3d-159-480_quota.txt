### Argument

#### A. Congress lacked constitutional authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 1951 as applied here.

Mr. Pearson first contends that the Hobbs Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, exceeds Congress’s commerce power when applied to a local robbery of a neighborhood restaurant. That argument is foreclosed by controlling precedent. In *Stirone v. United States*, 361 U.S. 212 (1960), the Supreme Court recognized that the Hobbs Act reaches conduct affecting commerce “in any way or degree.” The Court has repeatedly upheld Congress’s power to regulate even intrastate conduct that has a substantial relation to interstate commerce. *Wickard v. Filburn*, 317 U.S. 111 (1942); *Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States*, 379 U.S. 241 (1964).

This Court has specifically applied that principle to Hobbs Act prosecutions involving local businesses. In *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598 (2000), the Supreme Court explained that Congress may not regulate noneconomic, criminal conduct simply because of an attenuated aggregate effect on commerce; but *Morrison* distinguished statutes targeting economic activity. A robbery is quintessential economic conduct. See *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995). The Hobbs Act therefore remains within Congress’s power when the robbery depletes the assets of a business engaged in interstate commerce. See *Taylor v. United States*, 579 U.S. 301 (2016); *United States v. Culbert*, 435 U.S. 371 (1978).

The evidence here easily satisfied that standard. Mr. Peterson testified that Mr. Goodcents was part of a national chain, purchased goods from Kansas and out-of-state vendors, and used the stolen proceeds to buy goods produced outside Kansas. That evidence is more than sufficient to establish the minimal effect on commerce the Hobbs Act requires. See *Taylor*, 579 U.S. at 312-13; *United States v. Nguyen*, 155 F.3d 1219 (10th Cir. 1998) (recognizing Hobbs Act jurisdiction where robbery affected business engaged in interstate commerce); *United States v. Zeigler*, 19 F.3d 486 (10th Cir. 1994) (same). Accordingly, Mr. Pearson’s constitutional challenge fails.

#### B. The district court abused its discretion in refusing Mr. Pearson’s tardy alibi defense.

The late disclosure of an alibi defense may be sanctioned, but exclusion is a severe remedy. Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12.1, the trial court has discretion to fashion an appropriate response, yet the sanction must be proportionate. See *Taylor v. Illinois*, 484 U.S. 400 (1988); *Michigan v. Lucas*, 500 U.S. 145 (1991). Exclusion is improper where it needlessly impairs the truth-seeking function and there is no showing of deliberate gamesmanship. *Taylor*, 484 U.S. at 414-15.

Here, the record showed that Mr. Pearson produced a receipt and a witness to support his claim that he was at a jewelry store near the time of the robbery. The defense was directly relevant to identity, the central issue in the case. Under *Taylor* and *Lucas*, the court should have considered less drastic remedies, such as a brief continuance. See also *United States v. Collins*, 7 F.3d 1076 (10th Cir. 1993); *United States v. Wicker*, 848 F.2d 1059 (10th Cir. 1988). The refusal to permit the defense deprived Mr. Pearson of a meaningful opportunity to present exculpatory evidence.

#### C. The evidence was insufficient to support conviction.

A conviction must rest on evidence from which any rational juror could find guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979). Although appellate review is deferential, it is not a rubber stamp. *United States v. Hooks*, 780 F.2d 1526 (10th Cir. 1986).

The government’s proof depended heavily on accomplice testimony from witnesses who had powerful incentives to shift blame. See *United States v. Youngpeter*, 986 F.2d 349 (10th Cir. 1993). Ms. Starks could not identify Mr. Pearson from a photo array and had seen his picture on television before trial. The physical evidence was absent. The defense evidence placed him elsewhere. On this record, the government failed to prove identity beyond a reasonable doubt. See *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319; *United States v. McVeigh*, 153 F.3d 1166 (10th Cir. 1998).

#### D. The felony-murder instruction misstated the law.

A jury instruction is erroneous if, viewed as a whole, it fails to state the law correctly or misleads the jury. *United States v. Lin Lyn Trading, Ltd.*, 149 F.3d 1112 (10th Cir. 1998); *United States v. Pack*, 773 F.2d 261 (10th Cir. 1985). Felony murder requires proof that the killing occurred during and in furtherance of a qualifying felony. See *United States v. Pino*, 606 F.2d 908 (10th Cir. 1979).

If the instruction allowed conviction without requiring the jury to find the necessary nexus between the robbery and the homicide, reversal is required. See *United States v. Brown*, 200 F.3d 700 (10th Cir. 1999).

#### E. The court erred in refusing lesser-included offense instructions.

A defendant is entitled to a lesser-included offense instruction when the evidence would permit a rational jury to convict on the lesser offense and acquit on the greater. *Keeble v. United States*, 412 U.S. 205 (1973); *Sansone v. United States*, 380 U.S. 343 (1965); *United States v. Joe*, 831 F.2d 218 (10th Cir. 1987). That standard was met here. At minimum, the evidence supported a finding that Mr. Pearson was not a participant in the homicide or the robbery, and the jury should have been given the option to convict on lesser offenses consistent with the defense theory. See *United States v. Schmuck*, 489 U.S. 705 (1989); *United States v. Medina*, 755 F.2d 1269 (7th Cir. 1985).

For these reasons, Mr. Pearson’s convictions should be reversed, or at minimum the case remanded for a new trial.
