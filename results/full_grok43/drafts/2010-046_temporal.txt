The district court's order does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act. Federal courts possess inherent equitable authority to remedy constitutional violations through orders that restructure state institutions when necessary to eliminate unconstitutional conditions. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954). This authority includes directing state officials to take affirmative steps to cure ongoing violations. Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 7 (1958).

Such remedial orders are consistent with the Supremacy Clause and do not improperly invade state sovereignty when federal rights are at stake. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908); Sterling v. Constantin, 287 U.S. 378, 398 (1932). Equity courts retain flexibility to fashion relief that addresses the root causes of violations, including measures that alter institutional operations or populations. Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 321 U.S. 321, 329 (1944).

The findings here establish that overcrowding is the primary cause of Eighth Amendment violations in the facilities. The resulting population reduction is therefore a permissible exercise of equitable power to enforce constitutional guarantees rather than an unauthorized intrusion. These principles, long recognized before enactment of the Act, confirm that the order complies with its requirements.
