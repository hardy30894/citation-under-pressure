**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Acted Within the Settled Equitable Power of Federal Courts to Fashion Remedies Adequate to Cure Proven Constitutional Violations.**

Federal courts sitting in equity have always possessed broad discretion to shape relief to the exigencies of the case before them. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321, 329 (1944), "[t]he essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." That discretion is not diminished merely because the necessary remedy is extensive. In *In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593–94 (1895), the Court recognized that courts of equity may issue orders of significant breadth where necessary to prevent continuing injury that legal remedies cannot adequately redress. And in *Swift & Co. v. United States*, 286 U.S. 106, 114–15 (1932), the Court observed that a decree addressing an ongoing wrong is not static, for "life is never static," and a court retains authority to adjust its remedy as changed conditions require. The three-judge court's population order rests squarely within this tradition: it followed years of failed piecemeal remedies and was calibrated, not to punish the State, but to eliminate the root cause—overcrowding—of documented, ongoing constitutional injury.

**II. The Population Cap Was a Necessary Response to an Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment's Prohibition on Cruel and Unusual Punishment.**

The Eighth Amendment's protections are not static; they must be measured against "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long recognized that the constitutional inquiry into punishment focuses on substance, not labels—whether the treatment imposed is consistent with "the dignity of man." *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 373 (1910) (holding that the Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause "may be therefore progressive"). The Amendment applies with full force to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), and its command extends to the conditions under which punishment is administered, not merely the sentence itself. *See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (examining whether the method and circumstances of punishment offend "the concept of the dignity of man"). Here, the uncontested trial record established that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of the deprivation of adequate medical and mental health care to tens of thousands of prisoners—precisely the kind of systemic, ongoing injury that these precedents instruct courts must be remedied at its source, not merely at its symptoms.

**III. Structural, System-Wide Relief Is an Established and Sometimes Indispensable Tool Where Lesser Measures Have Failed to Cure Constitutional Violations.**

This Court has repeatedly approved broad structural decrees when narrower remedies proved inadequate to the constitutional wrong. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), the Court directed the district courts to fashion decrees with "practical flexibility" and to retain jurisdiction to ensure that constitutional rights were vindicated in fact, not merely on paper. When school boards resisted more modest remedies, this Court sanctioned comprehensive relief that went to the structure of the system itself. *See Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430, 437–38 (1968) (requiring a remedy that works "now" and eliminates the constitutional violation "root and branch"). Similarly, in *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218, 233 (1964), the Court held that district courts possess the equitable power to order relief—including directives with significant fiscal and administrative consequences for the State—"whatever manner is necessary" to secure the constitutional right, up to and including ordering local officials to levy taxes to fund the remedy. And in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 16 (1958), this Court made clear that constitutional rights "are not to be sacrificed or yielded to" administrative inconvenience or the resistance of state officials, however genuine the practical burdens created by compliance. These cases establish the principle at the heart of respondents' defense of the order below: where, as here, the district court found after years of litigation that piecemeal remedies had failed and that overcrowding itself was the disease rather than a symptom, a remedy addressing population directly is not overbroad—it is the *narrowest* remedy capable of curing the violation actually found.

**IV. The District Court's Extensive Factual Findings, Made After Trial, Are Entitled to Substantial Deference.**

The three-judge court's order followed extensive evidentiary proceedings and rested on detailed findings regarding the causal relationship between overcrowding and the constitutional violations. Consistent with the equitable principles discussed above, appellate courts do not lightly disturb a trial court's considered judgment as to the scope of relief necessary to remedy a violation it has itself found and observed over years of supervision. *See Hecht Co.*, 321 U.S. at 329–30. The State's objection that the remedy is too broad cannot be evaluated in the abstract; it must be measured against the violation the district court actually found, on a record the State does not seriously dispute. Under the equitable principles this Court has applied since *Debs*, *Swift*, and *Brown II*, that finding-driven, remedy-tailored approach is exactly what the law requires—and exactly what the district court provided.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the order below should be affirmed.
