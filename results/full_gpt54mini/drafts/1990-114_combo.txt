Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision does not violate the ADEA or the Equal Protection Clause. The ADEA applies only to an “employee,” and Congress did not include all state judges within that term. Courts construe federal statutes to avoid needless intrusion into state sovereignty absent a clear statement. United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). That principle is especially apt where the State’s structure of government is at issue. See Gregory v. Ashcroft, 501 U.S. 452, 460–61 (1991) (requiring clear expression before reading the ADEA to displace Missouri’s constitutional rule governing judicial tenure).

On the merits, state judges are not covered by the ADEA because they are “appointee[s] on the policymaking level” within 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Judicial office is not ministerial. Judges decide disputed questions of law, fill gaps in legal authority, and render decisions that create governing rules for future conduct. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 467–69. That understanding accords with the traditional conception of the judicial function. Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803); American Insurance Co. v. Canter, 26 U.S. (1 Pet.) 511, 546 (1828). State judges also shape the content and administration of the law through rulemaking and supervision of the courts. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 468–69. Because the ADEA’s exception is broad enough to encompass those who exercise such authority, Missouri’s judges fall outside the Act. See Snell v. Bell Telephone Co., 658 F. Supp. 556, 559–60 (E.D. Pa. 1987), aff’d, 800 F.2d 1136 (3d Cir. 1986) (recognizing that policymaking status depends on actual authority and responsibilities).

Even if the ADEA could be read to reach state judges, the statute should not be construed to upset a State’s chosen method of selecting and regulating its judiciary absent unmistakable congressional direction. Federal statutes ordinarily are not read to displace state prerogatives in matters of fundamental state concern without clear language. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 460–61; see also Helvering v. Gerhardt, 304 U.S. 405, 410–11 (1938) (recognizing the importance of preserving state functions). Missouri’s constitutional retirement rule reflects a legislative judgment about the judiciary’s effectiveness, accountability, and administration. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 472–73.

The Equal Protection Clause fares no better. Age is not a suspect classification, and laws classifying by age are sustained if rationally related to a legitimate state purpose. Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam); see also Morey v. Doud, 354 U.S. 457, 463–64 (1957) (rational basis governs ordinary economic and social classifications). Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule plainly satisfies that standard. States may rationally conclude that age seventy is a sensible point at which to ensure a vigorous judiciary, avoid costly individualized competency hearings, promote public confidence, and facilitate regular turnover. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 473–74. Similar judgments about public service have long been upheld where the State reasonably seeks administrative efficiency and effective performance. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420, 425–26 (1961); Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc., 348 U.S. 483, 487–88 (1955).

Respondents therefore should prevail. Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is consistent with the ADEA properly construed and does not offend the Fourteenth Amendment. Gregory, 501 U.S. at 476–77.
