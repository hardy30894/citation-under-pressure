**ARGUMENT**

**I. A District Court’s Failure to Advise a Defendant of His Right to Appeal Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32 Does Not Warrant Habeas Relief When the Defendant Had Actual Knowledge of That Right and Elected Not to Appeal.**

The sole question presented is whether a violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2)—which requires a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal—provides a basis for habeas relief when the defendant was independently aware of that right and chose not to appeal. It does not. Because the purpose of the Rule is to ensure that defendants know of their appellate rights, that purpose is fully satisfied when the defendant possesses actual knowledge, regardless of the court’s omission. Any error in failing to give the advice is therefore harmless.

**A. The Advisory Requirement of Rule 32 Serves a Notice Function, and Actual Knowledge Satisfies That Function.**

Rule 32(a)(2) provides that “[a]fter imposing sentence in a case which has gone to trial on a plea of not guilty, the court must advise the defendant of any right to appeal the sentence.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2). The Rule’s purpose is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights, not to create a rigid procedural trap. *See United States v. Drummond*, 128 F.3d 753, 755 (2d Cir. 1997) (holding that Rule 32’s advice requirement is “designed to ensure that a defendant knows of his right to appeal”). When a defendant already knows of that right, the Rule’s objective is achieved, and any omission by the court is harmless. *See United States v. Tang*, 37 F.3d 129, 133 (3d Cir. 1994) (finding harmless error where defendant “was aware of his right to appeal” despite court’s failure to advise).

This Court has long recognized that procedural rules are means to substantive ends, not ends in themselves. *See Bank of Nova Scotia v. United States*, 487 U.S. 250, 254 (1988) (holding that “a court may not exercise its supervisory powers to circumvent the harmless-error inquiry prescribed by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a)”). Where the defendant’s knowledge is established, the failure to give the prescribed advice cannot prejudice him. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002) (applying harmless-error analysis to Rule 11 violations and noting that “the defendant’s own prior knowledge” is relevant to whether error was harmless).

**B. The District Court’s Factual Finding That Peguero Knew of His Right to Appeal Is Dispositive.**

After an evidentiary hearing, the District Court found that Peguero “knew of his right to appeal when the sentencing hearing occurred.” That factual finding is entitled to deference. *See Thompson v. Keohane*, 516 U.S. 99, 111 (1995) (holding that “subsidiary factual questions” in habeas proceedings are reviewed for clear error). Peguero does not challenge that finding. Because he knew of his right and chose not to appeal, the Rule’s purpose was served, and no relief is warranted. *See United States v. Mosley*, 967 F.2d 1049, 1051 (5th Cir. 1992) (holding that “the failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is harmless error if the defendant already knows of that right”).

**C. Harmless-Error Review Applies to Rule 32 Violations.**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) provides that “[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance that does not affect substantial rights must be disregarded.” This Court has applied harmless-error analysis to a wide range of procedural errors, including those involving the right to appeal. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993) (applying harmless-error standard to forfeited rights). The Court of Appeals correctly held that a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review. *See Peguero v. United States*, 34 F.3d 143, 147 (3d Cir. 1994) (“[A] violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless error analysis.”). Every circuit to consider the question has agreed. *See, e.g., United States v. Smith*, 60 F.3d 595, 598 (9th Cir. 1995); *United States v. Perez*, 38 F.3d 72, 74 (2d Cir. 1994); *United States v. Drummond*, 128 F.3d at 755.

**D. Habeas Relief Requires a Showing of Prejudice, Which Peguero Cannot Make.**

To obtain habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, a petitioner must demonstrate “a fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice” or “an omission inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure.” *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). Where, as here, the defendant knew of his right to appeal and chose not to exercise it, the omission of the court’s advice cannot be said to have caused any prejudice. *See United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 784 (1979) (holding that “collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a procedural rule). Peguero’s claim fails because he cannot show that the court’s omission affected his decision not to appeal. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 170 (1982) (requiring habeas petitioner to show “actual prejudice” from alleged error).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
