**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to workers injured on land. The text of OCSLA, its structure, and this Court’s precedents compel the conclusion that the situs of the injury must be on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Plain Language of OCSLA Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision states that LHWCA benefits extend to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” defines not merely the source of the injury but the geographical locus of the covered event. This Court has long held that “the language of the statute is the starting point for its interpretation,” and when the text is clear, judicial inquiry ends. *Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924); *see also Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (where the language of an act is clear and unambiguous, it is the duty of the court to give it effect).

The statutory phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” cannot bear the Ninth Circuit’s construction. The word “occurring” modifies “injury,” establishing a direct link between the physical location of the injury-producing event and the shelf. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). If Congress had intended to cover all injuries with any connection to shelf operations, it would have said so—as it did in other provisions of OCSLA. *Compare* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (extending state law to artificial islands and fixed structures), *with* § 1333(b) (limiting coverage to injuries occurring as the result of operations conducted on the shelf). The deliberate distinction in phrasing demonstrates congressional intent to confine compensation coverage to the shelf itself. *Cf. United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 83 (1932) (where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another, it is generally presumed that Congress acted intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion).

**II. This Court’s Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Coverage Turns on Situs**

In *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947), this Court held that submerged lands are subject to the exclusive jurisdiction and control of the United States, confirming that OCSLA’s coverage is confined to the geographic area of the outer Continental Shelf. The reasoning of *California* is dispositive: OCSLA’s compensation provision is tied to the physical place of injury, not merely the nature of the employment.

Similarly, in *United States v. Ray*, 423 F.2d 16, 19 (5th Cir. 1970), the court recognized that OCSLA’s purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf’s natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers’ compensation program. The Act’s general scope is determined principally by locale. *Id.* at 19. That geographic limitation bars compensation for injuries occurring onshore.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. *Curtis* conflicts with this Court’s instruction that OCSLA’s clear geographic limitation governs. *See Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 423 (1985). In *Herb’s Welding*, this Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* at 423. That geographic limitation bars compensation for injuries occurring onshore.

**III. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Is Consistent with the Statutory Scheme**

OCSLA was enacted to govern activities on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act’s purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf’s natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers’ compensation program for offshore workers. *See United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 318 (1936) (statutory construction must respect jurisdictional boundaries). Extending OCSLA to onshore injuries would disregard the Act’s limited territorial reach.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test—requiring only a connection between the injury and shelf operations—improperly expands OCSLA beyond its text. This Court has rejected such open-ended standards when Congress has spoken clearly. *See United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 485 (1917) (penal statutes are to be construed strictly, and the power of punishment is vested in the legislative, not the judicial department). Workers’ compensation statutes, while remedial, are still subject to the plain meaning of their terms. *See Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946).

**IV. Applying the Correct Situs Standard, Valladolid’s Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility located on the California coast. Because his injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s remand for application of its “substantial nexus” test was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
