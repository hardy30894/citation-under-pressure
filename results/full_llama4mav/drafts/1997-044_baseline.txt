## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to those who commit fraud. The question presented is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The plain language of this provision indicates that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable. The Court should interpret this provision to achieve its purpose of preventing debtors from escaping liability for fraudulent conduct.

The elements required to establish nondischargeability under § 523(a)(2)(A) are well-established. A creditor must show that the debtor obtained money, property, or services through a material misrepresentation, that the debtor knew the representation was false or made with gross recklessness as to its truth, that the debtor intended to deceive the creditor, that the creditor reasonably relied on the debtor's false representations, and that the creditor sustained a loss and damages as a proximate result of the debtor's materially false representations (In re Poskanzer, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992)).

In this case, the debtor, Edward S. Cohen, committed actual fraud by charging his tenants excessive rents in violation of the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance. The bankruptcy court found that Cohen made an implicit representation regarding the rent he charged, and that his silence coupled with the rental amount fixed constituted a representation that he was charging lawful rent. Cohen's reckless disregard for the truth of his representation is evident from his failure to familiarize himself with the rent control ordinance, his failure to consult with an attorney or the Rent Leveling Board, and his decision to surcharge his tenants for increases in taxes and water charges without ensuring that the base rent was lawful.

The Court should affirm the lower court's holding that Cohen's debt to his tenants is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are also nondischargeable, as they are a direct result of Cohen's fraudulent conduct. The Eleventh Circuit's decision in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir.1993), supports this conclusion, holding that punitive damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are a debt incurred as a result of the debtor's fraudulent conduct.

Furthermore, the "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code is not intended to protect debtors who engage in fraudulent conduct (Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991)). Allowing Cohen to discharge his debt would undermine the purpose of § 523(a)(2)(A) and create an unjust result.

In conclusion, the Court should hold that individuals who commit fraud cannot discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The plain language of § 523(a)(2)(A), the elements required to establish nondischargeability, and the "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code all support this conclusion.
