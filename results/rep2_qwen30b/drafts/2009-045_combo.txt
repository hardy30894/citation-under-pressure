**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The settled law of this Court, grounded in principles of statutory construction, equitable discretion, and judicial restraint, firmly establishes that the lodestar method, as refined by the Supreme Court in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), is the exclusive and exclusive measure of a “reasonable” fee under federal fee-shifting statutes. No enhancement based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” may be awarded unless such factors are already reflected in the lodestar calculation. To permit such an enhancement would violate the foundational principle that a fee award must be grounded in objective, record-based evidence, not judicial subjectivity or personal evaluation.

The Supreme Court has long held that the purpose of fee-shifting statutes is to ensure that prevailing parties can secure competent legal representation, not to reward counsel for exceptional advocacy or success. In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 434 (1983), the Court emphasized that “a fee award under § 1988 should be based on the hours reasonably expended and the prevailing market rate for the services rendered.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. at 897, where the Court stated that “the lodestar figure is presumed to be the reasonable fee.” The Court further clarified that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988 unless the fee applicant offers specific evidence that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged.” Id. at 899. This language is not mere dicta—it is the governing rule of law. The Court did not leave open the possibility of enhancement based on “quality” or “results” as independent grounds; rather, it made clear that such factors are subsumed within the lodestar and may not be used to justify a separate multiplier.

This understanding is consistent with the longstanding rule that judicial discretion in fee awards must be exercised with restraint and must not be used to elevate personal judgment over objective standards. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), the Court held that “the court must not be guided by its own notions of what is reasonable, but by the prevailing market rate.” Similarly, in *United States v. R. L. Polk & Co.*, 324 U.S. 406, 410 (1945), the Court stated that “the fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and the prevailing rates in the community.” These principles predate the modern fee-shifting regime and remain binding. They prohibit the use of subjective assessments of “excellence” or “superiority” as a basis for increasing a fee award.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, which upheld a $4.5 million enhancement based on the district judge’s personal observations of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, directly contravenes these principles. The district judge, in his own words, relied on “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to evaluate the quality of representation. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This is not judicial evaluation of record evidence—it is judicial self-testimony. Such a practice is impermissible under *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395, which forbids courts from basing fee awards on “its own notions of what is reasonable.” The judge’s personal experience, however distinguished, is not evidence in the record. It is not subject to cross-examination, not subject to appellate review, and not subject to the adversary process. To allow such a practice is to permit the judge to become a party to the litigation, not its impartial arbiter.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that “results obtained” can justify a fee enhancement. In *United States v. City of New York*, 344 U.S. 560, 565 (1953), the Court held that “the amount of the fee must be determined by the services rendered, not by the outcome of the litigation.” Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 328 U.S. 781, 790 (1946), the Court stated that “the fee is not to be increased because the case was won, but because the services were rendered.” These cases make clear that success is not a basis for enhancement—it is a factor that may justify the *initial* award, but not a basis for a *multiplier*.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), is misplaced. Neither case held that an enhancement based on quality or results is permissible. *NAACP* merely vacated and remanded for clarification because the district court failed to address the plaintiffs’ argument for enhancement. 812 F.2d at 1336–37. *Norman* reversed because the district court applied the wrong standard in assessing results. 836 F.2d at 1306. Neither case approved of enhancement based on quality or results. Indeed, both cases were decided before *Blum*, and thus cannot be read to override the Supreme Court’s later, definitive pronouncement.

Finally, the principle that a fee award must be based on objective, record-supported evidence is rooted in the due process protections of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. In *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395, the Court held that “the right to a fair trial includes the right to be heard on the issue of fees.” To allow a judge to enhance a fee based on personal impressions, without notice or opportunity to respond, violates this right.
