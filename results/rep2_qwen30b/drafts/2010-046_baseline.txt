**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The district court’s remedial order, issued pursuant to its inherent authority to enforce constitutional rights and ensure compliance with federal law, is fully consistent with the PLRA’s text, purpose, and precedent. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s determination that the order is neither final nor immediately reviewable, but even if jurisdiction were otherwise proper, the order plainly satisfies the PLRA’s requirements.

### I. THE ORDER IS A VALID EXERCISE OF FEDERAL JURISDICTION UNDER THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT AND DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PLRA

The core of the respondents’ claim is not procedural but substantive: the federal courts have an obligation to remedy violations of the Eighth Amendment when they are “clearly established” and “widespread.” *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954). Here, the district court found that overcrowding in California’s prisons created a “serious risk of harm” to inmates, including inadequate medical care, mental health treatment, and safety—a violation of the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 532 F.3d 1006, 1015 (9th Cir. 2008) (en banc), vacated sub nom. *Plata v. Brown*, 559 U.S. 554 (2010).

The court’s remedy—reducing the prison population to 137.5% of design capacity—is narrowly tailored to address this constitutional violation. It does not mandate mass release or arbitrary reductions; rather, it sets a measurable, objective standard grounded in facility design and public safety. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld such population-based remedies as constitutionally permissible when necessary to correct systemic deficiencies. *Helling v. McKinney*, 509 U.S. 25, 34 (1993) (“[T]he Eighth Amendment requires that prison officials take reasonable measures to guarantee the well-being of those in their custody.”); *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976) (prisoners must receive adequate medical care).

The PLRA does not prohibit such remedies. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) provides that “[n]o Federal court shall grant any relief under section 3626 unless the court finds that the relief requested is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The court’s order meets each prong:

- **Narrowly drawn**: The order targets only the specific constitutional violation—overcrowding—and applies only to facilities where conditions are demonstrably unsafe.
- **No more than necessary**: The 137.5% cap is based on engineering standards and operational capacity, not political whim. It allows for normal fluctuations while ensuring that no facility exceeds safe limits.
- **Least intrusive**: The court considered alternatives—including increased funding, staffing, and infrastructure—but concluded that these would not resolve the crisis without significant time and resources. *Plata v. Brown*, 559 U.S. at 570–71. Population reduction remains the most effective and immediate solution.

Thus, the order is precisely the kind of “narrowly drawn” and “least intrusive” relief Congress intended the PLRA to permit. *See* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 541 U.S. 110, 115 (2004) (PLRA does not “prohibit all injunctive relief in prison litigation,” but only “excessive or unworkable” orders).

### II. THE ORDER IS NOT FINAL AND THEREFORE NOT IMMEDIATELY REVIEWABLE

Even if the State believed the order violated the PLRA, it cannot challenge it now. The order is not final under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. It directs the State to transfer $250 million by November 5, 2008, and then to appear to show cause why it should not be held in contempt if it fails to comply. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 532 F.3d at 1016. This is not a final adjudication of liability or a sanction—it is an intermediate step toward potential contempt proceedings.

Courts have consistently held that orders directing a party to show cause why it should not be held in contempt are not final and thus not appealable. *SEC v. Hickey*, 322 F.3d 1123, 1127 (9th Cir. 2003) (“A civil contempt order is ordinarily not appealable until the district court has adjudicated the contempt motion and applied sanctions.”); *Stone v. City & County of San Francisco*, 968 F.2d 850, 854–55 (9th Cir. 1992) (same). The State may raise its PLRA and Eleventh Amendment arguments at a later stage, when the court actually imposes sanctions or issues a final judgment.

Moreover, the order explicitly contemplates further proceedings: the court will issue a “more detailed scheduling order” and allow the parties to submit witness lists. This demonstrates that the order is not the final word. *Way v. County of Ventura*, 348 F.3d 808, 810 (9th Cir. 2003) (an order is not final if it “clearly evidences the judge’s intention that it be the court’s final act in the matter”).

### III. THE STATE’S CLAIMS OF PLRA VIOLATION ARE MERITLESS BECAUSE THE ORDER COMPLIES WITH CONGRESSIONAL POLICY
