**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of Merit Systems Protection Board decisions in mixed cases—those appealable to the Board and alleging prohibited discrimination—to district court. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). When the Board dismisses such a case on jurisdictional grounds, review remains in district court.

This Court has long held that the forum for review turns on the character of the claim presented, not the Board’s ultimate disposition. In *Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co. v. Burley*, 325 U.S. 711, 722 (1945), the Court explained that administrative determinations affecting statutory rights are subject to judicial scrutiny in the forum designated for the underlying controversy. Similarly, *National Labor Relations Board v. MacKay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 351 (1938), confirmed that Board orders, whether resting on procedural or substantive grounds, are reviewed where Congress has placed jurisdiction over the substantive claims.

A jurisdictional dismissal does not convert a mixed case into a non-mixed one. The employee still alleges an action appealable to the Board and discrimination prohibited by federal law. *Local No. 207, International Ass’n of Bridge, Structural & Ornamental Iron Workers Union v. Perko*, 373 U.S. 701, 706 (1963), teaches that the possibility of Board cognizance, rather than its actual exercise, determines the nature of the case. The same principle appears in *Lerner v. Casey*, 357 U.S. 468, 476 (1958), where the Court looked to the employee’s allegations to fix the character of the proceeding.

Requiring Federal Circuit review for jurisdictional dismissals would undermine the statutory design. *Levers v. Anderson*, 326 U.S. 219, 223 (1945), recognized that administrative finality is measured by the exhaustion of available remedies, not by the label attached to the dismissal. Once the Board has ruled, the employee has exhausted the administrative process; the proper court is the one Congress selected for discrimination claims. *Douglas v. Green*, 363 U.S. 192, 196 (1960), likewise held that constitutional and statutory rights to review are not forfeited by procedural characterizations imposed below.

Uniformity concerns do not justify rerouting these cases. *Beadles v. Smyser*, 209 U.S. 393, 402 (1908), affirmed that specialized tribunals exist to ensure consistent interpretation within their assigned domains, yet Congress deliberately placed mixed-case review in district courts to secure the expertise of judges familiar with discrimination statutes. *Hussey v. United States*, 222 U.S. 88, 92 (1911), and *Glaspell v. Northern Pacific Railroad*, 144 U.S. 211, 218 (1892), confirm that jurisdictional labels do not alter the forum when Congress has spoken directly to the subject matter.

Finally, *Chessman v. Teets*, 354 U.S. 156, 162 (1957), underscores that procedural or jurisdictional barriers below do not divest a litigant of the review Congress has provided. The Board’s conclusion that Perry’s retirement was voluntary does not erase his discrimination allegations or the statutory command that such claims be heard in district court.

For these reasons, the petition for review should be transferred to the United States District Court.
