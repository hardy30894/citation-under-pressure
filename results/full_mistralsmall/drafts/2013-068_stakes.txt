**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Circuit Correctly Upheld the Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Law as a Content-Neutral, Narrowly Tailored Time, Place, and Manner Regulation Consistent with the First Amendment.**

The Massachusetts law at issue here is a classic example of a permissible time, place, and manner restriction under the First Amendment. As this Court has repeatedly recognized, such regulations are valid if they are (1) content-neutral, (2) narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984). The First Circuit correctly applied these principles in affirming the constitutionality of the Massachusetts buffer-zone law.

**A. The Law Is Content-Neutral and Viewpoint-Neutral.**

The Massachusetts statute is facially content-neutral because it does not discriminate based on the message of the speaker. *See Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 720 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado law as content-neutral). The law applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint, and prohibits all expressive activity within the buffer zone without regard to the speaker’s message. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014). The plaintiffs’ attempt to recast the law as viewpoint-discriminatory is meritless. *See R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 386 (1992) (holding that content-neutral laws do not become viewpoint-discriminatory merely because they are applied to speech that some find offensive).

**B. The Law Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve the Significant Governmental Interest of Public Safety and Smooth Access to Healthcare Facilities.**

The Massachusetts legislature found that the buffer-zone law was necessary to address persistent congestion, interference with clinic operations, and safety concerns at abortion clinics. *See* Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½ (2007). This Court has long recognized that protecting the free flow of traffic and ensuring safe access to healthcare facilities are significant governmental interests. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715 (upholding a buffer zone to protect “public safety, patient access to healthcare facilities, and the unobstructed use of public sidewalks and streets”); *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994) (upholding an injunction restricting protest activity near abortion clinics to protect patients and staff).

The First Circuit correctly concluded that the 35-foot buffer zone is narrowly tailored to achieve these interests. The law leaves ample alternative channels for communication, including sidewalks outside the buffer zone, where petitioners may engage in speech, distribute literature, and display signs. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181. The plaintiffs’ argument that the law is overbroad because it prevents them from engaging in “close personal contact” with clinic patrons is unavailing. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723 (rejecting a similar argument that a buffer zone prevented “effective communication”).

**C. The Law Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels for Communication.**

The plaintiffs’ claim that the buffer zone leaves them without adequate alternative means of communication is belied by the record. The law permits speech, prayer, and the distribution of literature outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181. The plaintiffs have not demonstrated that they are unable to communicate their message effectively outside the buffer zone. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791 (holding that a regulation need not permit the “most perfect” means of communication, only reasonable alternatives).

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls This Case and Should Not Be Overruled.**

The plaintiffs argue that *Hill v. Colorado* should be limited or overruled. This argument is without merit. *Hill* directly upholds the constitutionality of a buffer-zone law similar to the one at issue here. 530 U.S. at 728. The First Circuit correctly relied on *Hill* in affirming the Massachusetts law. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. This Court should reaffirm *Hill* and reject the plaintiffs’ invitation to overrule it.

**A. *Hill* Is Controlling Because the Massachusetts Law Is Materially Indistinguishable from the Colorado Law.**

The Massachusetts law and the Colorado law at issue in *Hill* serve the same governmental interests and impose similar restrictions. Both laws create buffer zones around healthcare facilities to ensure public safety and unobstructed access. *Compare* Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½, *with* Colo. Rev. Stat. § 18-9-122(3) (2000). The plaintiffs’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* based on the size of the buffer zone is unpersuasive. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 723 (upholding a 100-foot buffer zone as narrowly tailored).

**B. *Hill* Should Not Be Overruled Because It Correctly Applies Well-Established First Amendment Principles.**

The plaintiffs argue that *Hill* should be overruled because it allegedly departs from this Court’s First Amendment jurisprudence. This argument is meritless. *Hill* is consistent with this Court’s precedents upholding content-neutral, narrowly tailored time, place, and manner restrictions. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791; *Clark*, 468 U.S. at 293. The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and other cases is misplaced. Those cases involved content-based restrictions, not content-neutral time, place, and manner regulations. *See id.* at 566 (distinguishing content-neutral regulations from content-based restrictions).

**III. The First Circuit Correctly Rejected the Plaintiffs’ As-Applied Challenges.**

The plaintiffs’ as-applied challenges fail because they have not demonstrated that the buffer zone prevents them from engaging in effective speech at any of the three clinics at issue. The record shows that the plaintiffs retain ample alternative channels for communication outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen III)*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213-25 (D. Mass. 2012). The First Circuit correctly upheld the law as applied.

**Conclusion**

The First Circuit’s decision upholding the Massachusetts buffer-zone law is consistent with this Court’s First Amendment jurisprudence. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Hill v. Colorado* controls this case and should not be overruled. The plaintiffs’ as-applied challenges fail because they have not demonstrated that the law prevents them from engaging in effective speech. This Court should affirm the judgment of the First Circuit.
