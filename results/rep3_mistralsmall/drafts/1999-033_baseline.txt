**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Seventh Circuit Correctly Recognized a "Class of One" Equal Protection Claim.**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from denying any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). While equal protection claims typically arise in the context of classifications disadvantaging members of a suspect class or burdening a fundamental right, this Court has recognized that the Clause also prohibits arbitrary and irrational state action directed at a single individual. *See City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985) ("The Fourteenth Amendment requires that all persons similarly situated should be treated alike."); *see also Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923) ("The inhibition of the amendment against state action denying equal protection of the laws is not confined to discrimination against persons as members of a particular class.").

The Seventh Circuit correctly applied this principle in holding that a plaintiff may bring a "class of one" equal protection claim where the plaintiff alleges that state action was motivated by "a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 363, 366 (7th Cir. 1998) (quoting *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metro. Hous. Dev. Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265 (1977)). This Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits invidious discrimination, even when directed at a single individual. *See Sioux City Bridge*, 260 U.S. at 445 ("The Fourteenth Amendment protects persons, not groups."); *see also Allee v. Medrano*, 416 U.S. 802, 814 (1974) ("The Fourteenth Amendment reaches and forbids discriminatory state action whether directed at groups or at individuals.").

The Village’s contention that a "class of one" claim requires membership in a class or group is unsupported by precedent. The Village cites *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562 (2000), for the proposition that such a claim is unavailable, but the decision below was affirmed on other grounds, and this Court did not address the "class of one" theory. *Id.* at 564. Moreover, the Village’s reliance on *United States v. Carolene Prods. Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), is misplaced, as that case involved a facial challenge to a statute, not an as-applied claim of arbitrary treatment. *Id.* at 152-53.

**II. The Seventh Circuit’s Standard for a "Class of One" Claim Is Consistent with This Court’s Precedent.**

The Seventh Circuit’s standard—requiring proof that the plaintiff was treated differently from others similarly situated and that the disparity was motivated by irrational or illegitimate animus—is consistent with this Court’s equal protection jurisprudence. *See Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 439 ("The Equal Protection Clause directs that 'all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike.'") (quoting *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920)). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational state action, regardless of whether it targets a group or an individual. *See Sioux City Bridge*, 260 U.S. at 445; *see also Allee*, 416 U.S. at 814.

The Village’s argument that the Seventh Circuit’s standard is overly broad ignores the narrow scope of the "class of one" theory. The theory applies only where the plaintiff demonstrates that the state action was motivated by "spite, ill will, or an improper purpose," *Olech*, 160 F.3d at 366, and not merely a good-faith policy choice. *See Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 448 (rejecting a claim where the state action was rationally related to a legitimate objective). The Seventh Circuit’s standard thus properly cabins the "class of one" theory to cases involving arbitrary and invidious discrimination.

**III. The Seventh Circuit Correctly Applied the "Class of One" Theory to the Facts of This Case.**

The Village conditioned Olech’s water connection on an easement that was substantially larger than those required of similarly situated property owners. This differential treatment, coupled with Olech’s allegation that the Village acted out of spite to "get" her, states a cognizable equal protection claim under the "class of one" theory. *See Olech*, 160 F.3d at 366. The Village’s reliance on *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 528 U.S. 562, is unavailing, as this Court affirmed the Seventh Circuit’s decision on other grounds and did not address the "class of one" theory. *Id.* at 564.

**Conclusion**

The Seventh Circuit correctly recognized a "class of one" equal protection claim and properly applied it to the facts of this case. The Village’s contrary arguments are unsupported by precedent and should be rejected.
