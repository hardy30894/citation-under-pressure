**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp., Ltd., because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether the vessel’s crew breached its “active” operations duty under *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156, 101 S.Ct. 1614, 68 L.Ed.2d 1 (1981), by failing to secure a dangerous, fixed snatch block in the up (vertical) position after opening the hatch cover. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Serbin, demonstrates that the crew’s failure to properly secure the block created an unreasonably dangerous condition that directly caused Serbin’s injury. Under *Scindia*, the vessel’s duty to provide a safe place to work extends to the active operation of the vessel, and the crew’s failure to perform a routine, safety-critical task—securing the snatch block—constitutes a breach of that duty.

The Supreme Court in *Scindia* established that a vessel owes a nondelegable duty to provide a safe place to work, which includes the “active operations” of the vessel. Id. at 168, 101 S.Ct. at 1622. This duty is not limited to the vessel’s own crew but extends to the longshoremen who are working under the vessel’s operational control. Id. at 169, 101 S.Ct. at 1622. The Court emphasized that “the vessel’s duty to provide a safe place to work is not diminished by the fact that the stevedore is in charge of the loading and unloading operations.” Id. at 169, 101 S.Ct. at 1622. The vessel must ensure that its equipment and operations do not create hazards that are foreseeable and preventable. Id. at 170, 101 S.Ct. at 1623.

Here, the crew had a clear, nondelegable duty to secure the snatch blocks in the up position after opening the hatch cover. The block in question was not merely a passive piece of equipment; it was a fixed, 100-pound component of the vessel’s operational system, designed to be moved and secured by the crew. The block’s metal projections acted as stoppers, and its position was critical to the safe operation of the hatch. The crew’s failure to secure the block in the up position left it in a dangerous, horizontal position—resting on top of a cargo unit in the wing of the hatch. This created a foreseeable hazard: a longshoreman attempting to work in the area would be at risk of tripping, falling, or being struck by the block if it shifted or fell.

Serbin’s decision to move the block himself was not reckless or unreasonable. He observed the block in a hazardous position, recognized the danger it posed to himself and others, and acted to mitigate a known risk. He testified that he believed the block was unsafe because “that’s where the hookup man would normally stand in the wing. If anything was to fall he had no place to run.” This was a reasonable assessment of the danger. The fact that Serbin had moved blocks before does not negate the danger; rather, it underscores that the task was routine and expected to be performed safely. The crew’s failure to perform this routine task created a condition that made Serbin’s intervention necessary and, ultimately, fatal.

The district court erred in concluding that the crew’s failure to secure the block was not a breach of duty because the block was “fixed” and not “portable.” The *Scindia* standard does not turn on whether equipment is portable or fixed; it turns on whether the vessel’s operations created a dangerous condition that the crew failed to correct. The Supreme Court made clear that “the vessel’s duty to provide a safe place to work includes the responsibility to maintain its equipment in a safe condition.” *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 170, 101 S.Ct. at 1623. The crew’s failure to secure the block—despite its fixed nature and the presence of stoppers—was a failure to maintain the equipment in a safe condition. The block’s position was not merely inconvenient; it was a known hazard that could cause serious injury.

Moreover, the crew’s failure to secure the block was not a mere oversight. The block remained in the down position for hours after the hatch was opened, and no one from the crew intervened. The crew had the exclusive responsibility for moving and securing the blocks. The longshoremen were not trained or authorized to perform this task. The vessel’s failure to ensure that its own crew performed this essential safety function constitutes a breach of its nondelegable duty.

The Third Circuit has recognized that “a vessel may be liable for injuries caused by the failure of its crew to perform a safety-critical task, even if the longshoreman was not directed to do so.” *Barnes v. M/V *S.S. *Savannah*, 987 F.2d 188, 192 (3d Cir. 1993). In *Barnes*, the court held that the vessel was liable for injuries caused by a crew member’s failure to secure a hatch cover, even though the longshoreman had not been instructed to do so. Id. The same principle applies here: the vessel’s duty to provide a safe place to work includes ensuring that its own crew performs essential safety tasks.

The district court’s conclusion that Serbin assumed the risk by moving the block is misplaced. Assumption of risk is not a defense where the vessel has failed to meet its nondelegable duty. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 170, 101 S.Ct. at 1623. The danger was created by the vessel’s failure to secure the block, not by any inherent risk of the work itself. Serbin did not assume the risk of a crew member’s negligence.

For these reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment. A reasonable jury could conclude that the crew’s failure to secure the snatch block was a breach of the vessel’s active operations duty, that this breach created a foreseeable and unreasonably dangerous condition, and that this condition directly caused Serbin’s injury. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for trial.

**WHEREFORE**, appellant John Serbin respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the District Court and remand for further proceedings consistent with this argument.
