**ARGUMENT**

I. **The injunction should be reversed because the Fair Housing Amendments Act requires only reasonable accommodation, not the creation of a preferred benefit.**

The Fair Housing Amendments Act makes it unlawful to “discriminate” by refusing “reasonable accommodations” when such accommodations are “necessary to afford” a disabled person “equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling.” 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). But the statute does not require a housing provider to confer a special advantage or to displace a facially neutral allocation system absent a showing that the requested accommodation is necessary and reasonable. *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 410-12 (1979), held that antidiscrimination law does not require an entity to “substantially modify” its program to meet an individual’s needs. Likewise, *Alexander v. Choate*, 469 U.S. 287, 300-01 (1985), recognized that “reasonable accommodations” are required to assure meaningful access, but not measures that transform the benefit itself.

Shapiro’s request was not for access to parking on equal terms; it was for immediate priority over every other resident on the waiting list. Cadman Towers had a neutral first-come, first-served system, with limited exceptions historically recognized for grandfathered units, employees, and a resident’s family member. The FHAA does not compel a cooperative to abolish a uniform waiting-list policy whenever a resident’s disability makes waiting difficult. *Davis*, 442 U.S. at 412; *Choate*, 469 U.S. at 301. The requested relief would not merely adjust the manner of access; it would alter the substantive order of allocation.

II. **The district court erred in treating the requested space as “necessary” without adequate findings and without considering less intrusive alternatives.**

An accommodation is “necessary” only if, absent the accommodation, the disabled person lacks an equal opportunity to use and enjoy the dwelling. *Smith & Lee Associates, Inc. v. City of Taylor*, 102 F.3d 781, 795-96 (6th Cir. 1996), explains that necessity requires a showing of a direct link between the accommodation and equal housing opportunity. *Howard v. HMK Holdings, LLC*, 988 F.3d 1185, 1190-91 (9th Cir. 2021), likewise requires proof that the accommodation is essential to afford meaningful access. The record here showed that Shapiro had lived in the building for two years using street parking, public transportation, and car services. Her difficulty parking is serious, but difficulty is not the same as denial of equal opportunity.

Nor did the district court adequately address alternatives short of displacing the waiting list. Reasonableness under the FHAA is contextual and fact-sensitive. *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Management Co.*, 29 F.3d 1413, 1418 (9th Cir. 1994), held that a housing provider must make accommodations that are reasonable under the circumstances, not whatever accommodation the tenant prefers. *Auburn Woods I Homeowners Ass’n v. Fair Employment and Housing Commission*, 121 Cal. App. 4th 1578, 1601-02, 18 Cal. Rptr. 3d 520 (2004), similarly recognized that reasonable accommodation does not require the provider to accept an undue burden or abandon neutral rules when other adjustments may suffice. Here, the cooperative suggested that once Shapiro reached the top of the list, it would attempt to place her near her apartment. That approach preserved both disability accommodation and equitable administration.

III. **Granting Shapiro priority parking imposes an undue burden and fundamentally alters the cooperative’s allocation system.**

Courts applying disability-accommodation statutes have repeatedly held that providers need not incur undue burdens or fundamental alterations. *School Board of Nassau County v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 287 n.17 (1987), recognized that accommodation is bounded by burden and practicability. *Staron v. McDonald’s Corp.*, 51 F.3d 353, 356-57 (2d Cir. 1995), likewise explained that an accommodation is not reasonable if it imposes undue hardship or would be excessively costly or disruptive. Requiring Cadman Towers to carve out a coveted parking space immediately for one resident would burden the cooperative’s existing allocation system and invite similar demands from other residents whose medical conditions make waiting inconvenient.

This is especially true in a setting where parking is scarce and heavily valued. The ground-floor spaces at 101 Clark Street are not fungible with street parking; they are a limited amenity allocated by a neutral queue. *Bonner v. Lewis*, 857 F.2d 559, 566 (9th Cir. 1988), cautions that accommodation law does not require a provider to create a new service or preference that others do not receive. The injunction here effectively created such a preference.

IV. **The preliminary injunction should be vacated because the record does not support the extraordinary equitable relief awarded.**

A preliminary injunction requires a clear showing of irreparable harm and a likelihood of success on the merits. *Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 555 U.S. 7, 20-22 (2008). Although pretrial relief can issue in housing cases, the movant still must establish the statutory entitlement to accommodation. *Disability Advocates, Inc. v. New York Coalition for Quality Assisted Living, Inc.*, 675 F.3d 149, 158-59 (2d Cir. 2012). On this record, Shapiro showed a desire for more convenient parking, not a right to priority over other residents. The order should therefore be reversed.
