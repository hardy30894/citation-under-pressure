ARGUMENT

The Massachusetts buffer zone is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. It is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. This Court has long recognized that even in public forums, the state may impose reasonable restrictions on speech to protect other legitimate interests.

I. The Act Is a Content-Neutral Regulation.

The statute applies to all persons equally, regardless of viewpoint. It prohibits any person from knowingly entering or remaining within a 35-foot buffer zone adjacent to reproductive health care facilities, subject to specific exemptions for those entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and persons using the sidewalk to reach another destination. This Court has consistently upheld content-neutral restrictions on speech in public forums. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576 (1941), the Court upheld a parade licensing statute because it regulated “the time, place, and manner of the use of the streets” without reference to the content of the speech. Similarly, in *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 87 (1949), the Court upheld a ban on sound trucks that “restricts the volume of sound” but does not “restrict the communication of ideas.” The Massachusetts Act does not target any particular message; it merely creates a spatial buffer to ensure safe access to medical facilities.

II. The Act Serves Significant Governmental Interests.

The Commonwealth has a compelling interest in protecting the safety of patients, staff, and the public, and in ensuring unimpeded access to healthcare facilities. This Court has repeatedly recognized that the state may regulate conduct that threatens public safety or obstructs lawful activities. In *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 559, 562 (1965), the Court upheld a statute prohibiting picketing near a courthouse because the state’s interest in “protecting the administration of justice” justified the restriction. Here, the buffer zone protects patients from harassment, physical obstruction, and intimidation as they enter medical facilities. The record shows that prior to the Act, petitioners engaged in conduct that blocked entrances, shouted at patients, and created chaotic conditions. The state’s interest in preserving “peace and good order” is well established. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 574 (1942).

III. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored.

The 35-foot zone is the minimum necessary to achieve the state’s objectives. It is far smaller than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill v. Colorado*, but even without that precedent, this Court has approved similar restrictions. In *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611, 615 (1968), the Court upheld a Mississippi statute prohibiting picketing “in such a manner as to obstruct or unreasonably interfere with” ingress and egress, finding it “narrowly drawn” to serve the state’s interest. The Massachusetts Act is even more precise: it applies only to the area immediately adjacent to entrances, exits, and driveways, and it exempts persons with legitimate business at the facility. The zone does not ban speech; it merely moves it 35 feet away. This Court has never required that regulations be the *least* restrictive means, only that they be “reasonable” and not “substantially broader than necessary.” *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. at 576.

IV. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels.

Petitioners remain free to communicate with patients from outside the buffer zone. They may speak, pray, display signs, distribute literature, and engage in sidewalk counseling. The record shows that petitioners have successfully persuaded women to forgo abortions even after the Act’s enactment. This Court has held that a regulation does not violate the First Amendment so long as “the means of communication are not completely foreclosed.” *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. at 88. In *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 162 (1939), the Court struck down a ban on leafleting because it “completely denied” that method of communication. Here, petitioners retain all traditional methods of expression; they simply cannot stand within 35 feet of the clinic entrance. That is a reasonable spatial restriction, not a prohibition on speech.

V. The Act Is Not Overbroad or Vague.

The statute clearly defines the prohibited area and lists specific exemptions. This Court has upheld statutes with similar clarity. In *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 408 (1953), the Court upheld a parade permit law that gave officials “no discretion” and was “narrowly drawn.” The Massachusetts Act leaves no room for arbitrary enforcement; the buffer zones are marked with painted arcs and signs. The Attorney General’s guidance letter does not alter the statute’s plain meaning. Petitioners’ vagueness claim fails because the Act “gives fair notice of the conduct prohibited.” *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. at 616.

VI. The Act Does Not Discriminate Based on Viewpoint.

Petitioners argue that the exemptions favor clinic employees and patients, but those exemptions are based on status, not viewpoint. The Act applies equally to pro-life and pro-choice advocates. This Court has consistently upheld regulations that treat all speakers alike. In *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966), the Court upheld a trespass conviction for protesters on jail grounds, noting that the state may “control the use of its own property” without regard to the speaker’s message. The buffer zone is a neutral safety measure, not a viewpoint-based restriction.

Conclusion

The Massachusetts buffer zone is a constitutional exercise of the state’s police power. It is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and leaves ample alternative channels for speech. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
