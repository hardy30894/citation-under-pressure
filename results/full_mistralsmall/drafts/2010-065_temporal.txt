**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE FEDERAL STATUTE**

A criminal defendant may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge the validity of a federal statute under which she has been prosecuted. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) ("The [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment."); *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485 (1923) (taxpayer lacks standing to challenge federal spending as violating the Tenth Amendment because "[t]he administration of any federal statute, valid because within the scope of the national power, must be entrusted to some officer or agent of the United States"). The Tenth Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It does not confer rights upon individuals but merely reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government. *See id.* at 124–25; *Ashwander v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 297 U.S. 288, 341 (1936) (Brandeis, J., concurring) ("The Tenth Amendment was intended to confirm the understanding that the federal government could exercise no powers except those expressly granted, and those properly incident to the expressly granted powers.").

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. That case permitted a private party to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal statute, but it did so without acknowledging the Supreme Court’s clear instruction in *Tennessee Electric Power* that private parties lack standing to assert such claims. *See id.* at 703–04. This Court is bound by controlling Supreme Court precedent, which forecloses Bond’s standing argument. *See, e.g., Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 404 (1821) ("It is a maxim not to be disregarded, that general expressions in every opinion are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used.").

**II. THE FEDERAL STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER**

Congress acted within its enumerated powers in enacting § 229 to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention. The Constitution grants Congress the power "[t]o regulate Commerce with foreign Nations," U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3, and "[t]o make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States, or in any Department or Officer thereof," *id.* art. I, § 8, cl. 18. The Treaty Power permits the President, "by and with the Advice and Consent of the Senate," to make treaties, provided two-thirds of the Senators present concur. *Id.* art. II, § 2, cl. 2. Congress may then enact legislation to enforce those treaties. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) ("If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government."); *see also United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 320 (1936) ("The investment of the federal government with the powers of external sovereignty did not depend upon the affirmative grants of the Constitution.").

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing it. *See Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33. The statute does not intrude upon state sovereignty because the federal government retains exclusive authority over foreign affairs, and treaties are the supreme law of the land. *See id.* at 432–33; *Ware v. Hylton*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 199, 281 (1796) ("The treaty is the law of the land, and as such, is binding on every individual in the United States."). The Tenth Amendment does not limit Congress’s power to enact laws enforcing valid treaties. *See Darby*, 312 U.S. at 124–25; *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33.

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE**

A statute is void for vagueness if it fails to give "fair warning" of the conduct it prohibits. *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). Section 229 provides clear notice of its prohibitions. It criminalizes the knowing use or possession of a "toxic chemical" or "chemical weapon," defined with specificity in the statute. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229F (1994). The statute’s terms are sufficiently definite to apprise ordinary citizens of the prohibited conduct. *See Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) ("No one may be required at peril of life, liberty or property to speculate as to the meaning of penal statutes."); *United States v. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) ("[A] criminal statute must be sufficiently definite to give notice of the required conduct to one who would avoid its penalties.").

Bond’s argument that § 229 is overbroad or vague in its application to her conduct fails. The statute targets the knowing use or possession of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes, conduct that is plainly proscribed. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 229(a)(1). Bond’s repeated attempts to poison her husband’s mistress with toxic chemicals fall squarely within the statute’s prohibitions. The statute provides fair notice of its reach, and Bond cannot credibly claim otherwise.
