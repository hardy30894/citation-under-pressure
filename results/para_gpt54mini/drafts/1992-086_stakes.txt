**ARGUMENT**

The School District violated the First Amendment by excluding Lamb’s Chapel from a facility it had opened for expressive activity solely because the proposed speech was religious. When the government creates a forum for speech, it may not engage in content-based discrimination unless the exclusion is narrowly drawn to serve a compelling interest. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 44-45 (1983). If the forum is limited to certain subjects or speakers, the State may confine access consistent with that limitation, but it may not discriminate among viewpoints within the subject matter the forum allows. *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829-30 (1995); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

The school premises here were, at minimum, a limited public forum for community use after hours. The District had authorized non-school use by outside groups for civic and cultural purposes. Once it opened the facilities for expressive activity, it was bound by the First Amendment’s prohibition on viewpoint discrimination. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267-70 (1981); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 390-91 (1993). The District’s refusal to permit Lamb’s Chapel to show films on family values and child rearing was not a permissible restriction on subject matter; it was discrimination because the presentation would discuss that subject from a religious perspective. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94. The Court has made clear that “the first amendment forbids the government to regulate speech in ways that favor some viewpoints or ideas at the expense of others.” *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 828-29.

The District’s asserted justification cannot survive scrutiny. The proposed films addressed a topic the District had allowed others to discuss: family life. What the District excluded was not the subject, but the religious viewpoint from which that subject would be addressed. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, this Court held that excluding a church’s film series on family issues “would be viewpoint discrimination” because “the films would not have been shown because of their religious content.” *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393. That is exactly what happened here. The District’s repeated statements that the film “appears to be church related” confirm that the exclusion turned on viewpoint, not on any neutral limitation of the forum. *Id.* at 393-94.

Nor can the District defend its conduct as necessary to avoid an Establishment Clause violation. The Supreme Court has rejected the notion that accommodating religious speech in a neutral forum violates the Establishment Clause. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271-75; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 845-46. “The guarantee of neutrality is respected, not offended, when the government, following neutral criteria and evenhanded policies, extends benefits to recipients whose ideologies and viewpoints, including religious ones, are broad and diverse.” *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 839. Allowing Lamb’s Chapel equal access would have treated religious and secular speakers alike; it would not have amounted to endorsement. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75; *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 113-19 (2001).

The Second Circuit’s reliance on the District’s prior practice cannot change the result. A forum’s limits are defined by the speech it permits, but once the government has opened the forum to some speakers on a topic, it may not exclude others because their view is religious. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829-30. The District’s policy did not preserve a neutral subject-matter restriction; it singled out religion for disfavored treatment. That is precisely what the First Amendment forbids. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394; *Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 107-08.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
