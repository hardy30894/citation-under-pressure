The federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the resolution of a substantial federal question, as seen in Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738, 822 (1824).

Minton's malpractice claim against his attorneys turns on the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar, a critical component of federal patent law. The validity of Minton's patent, and thus his malpractice claim, depends on the resolution of this federal issue. The Court has consistently held that cases "arising under" federal law involve a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912); Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99, 102 (1850). Minton's claim meets this standard, as the outcome depends on the interpretation of federal patent law.

The federal patent laws are intended to provide a uniform system of patent regulation, as noted in Wheaton v. Peters, 33 U.S. 591, 658 (1834). Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity by permitting multiple interpretations of federal patent law. The Court has recognized the importance of uniformity in Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897), where it held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents.

The Court has also cautioned against broadly defining "arising under" jurisdiction. In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court adopted a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Id. at 117-18. Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law necessary to its resolution, meeting this standard.

The supremacy of federal law in matters involving patent law is well-established. Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852), and Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850), demonstrate the Court's consistent recognition of federal jurisdiction over patent law cases. Given the critical role of federal patent law in Minton's malpractice claim, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case.

In conclusion, the experimental use exception is a critical aspect of federal patent law, and its interpretation is essential to resolving Minton's claim. The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case, and the state court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction to consider Minton's appeal.
