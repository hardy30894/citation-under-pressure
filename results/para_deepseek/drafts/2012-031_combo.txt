## ARGUMENT

The district court properly certified the class under Rule 23(b)(3) after conducting a four-day evidentiary hearing, reviewing 32 expert reports, and issuing an 81-page memorandum opinion. The court’s factual findings are entitled to substantial deference. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (clear error standard requires “definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed”). The court’s account of the evidence is plausible, and reversal is unwarranted.

**I. The District Court Had Adequate Admissible Evidence That Damages Could Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis**

The court found that Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, presented a common methodology to quantify damages across the class. This methodology was based on a benchmark regression comparing actual Comcast prices to prices in competitive markets. The court properly concluded that such a common approach satisfies Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement. *Green v. Wolf Corp.*, 406 F.2d 291, 301 (2d Cir. 1968) (class certification appropriate where common questions predominate, even if some individual issues remain). Antitrust damages need not be proven with mathematical precision; a reasonable estimate suffices. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (jury may approximate damages where defendant’s wrong makes precise calculation impossible); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562–563 (1931) (damages need not be proved with certainty, only with reasonable certainty). The district court’s acceptance of Dr. McClave’s methodology was not an abuse of discretion.

**II. The District Court’s Findings on Antitrust Impact Are Supported by Common Evidence**

The court limited the class’s theory of impact to a single, common theory: that Comcast’s anticompetitive clustering deterred overbuilder entry, leading to supra-competitive prices for all class members. This theory is capable of proof through common evidence—such as market concentration data and evidence of deterred entry—rather than individual testimony. *See Klor’s, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc.*, 359 U.S. 207, 212 (1959) (per se violation eliminates need for individualized proof of anticompetitive effect); *Northern Pacific Railway Co. v. United States*, 356 U.S. 1, 5 (1958) (certain agreements are so “plainly anticompetitive” that they are per se illegal). The district court correctly held that class-wide impact could be established through common evidence, satisfying Rule 23(b)(3).

**III. The Geographic Market Definition Was Proper**

The district court defined the relevant geographic market as the Philadelphia Designated Market Area. This definition comports with *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 337 (1962) (geographic market “may be as small as a single metropolitan area”). Comcast’s suggestion that each household is its own geographic market has no support in law or economics. The court’s market definition is a factual finding subject to clear error review, and it is not clearly erroneous.

**IV. The Court’s “Rigorous Analysis” Satisfied Rule 23**

The district court properly engaged in the “rigorous analysis” required by Rule 23, including a preliminary inquiry into the merits where necessary. *Esplin v. Hirschi*, 402 F.2d 94, 99 (10th Cir. 1968) (court must conduct “a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations” at certification). The court heard live testimony, examined expert reports, and issued detailed findings. This is precisely the kind of analysis that sound discretion requires. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 44–45 (1940) (due process demands that class representatives adequately protect absent members). The district court’s certification order rests on more than adequate evidentiary support.

**Conclusion**

The district court did not abuse its discretion. It had ample admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
