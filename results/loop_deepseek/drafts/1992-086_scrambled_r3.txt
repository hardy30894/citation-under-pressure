**ARGUMENT**

The District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel’s religious film series from school facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. By opening its property to a broad array of community groups, the District created a public forum from which it may not exclude speech based on its religious viewpoint.

**I. The District Created a Public Forum, and Once It Did So, It Could Not Exclude Speech Based on Religious Content.**

The District’s policy and practice allowed a wide range of secular organizations—including the “Southern Harmonizers,” a “Psychology and The Unknown” lecture series, and other civic and cultural groups—to use school premises for expressive activities. This affirmative opening of the forum to the public for discussion of diverse topics transformed the property into a public forum. *See Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413, 416 (1943) (holding that a city cannot “prohibit the distribution of literature of any kind at any time, at any place, and in any manner” in public places). When the government opens its property for expressive activities, it may not exclude speech based on its content or viewpoint. *Jamison*, 318 U.S. at 416. The District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s application solely because the films were “church related” is a classic content-based restriction, forbidden under the First Amendment.

**II. Religious Speech Is Fully Protected Within a Public Forum.**

The First Amendment’s protection of free speech extends to religious expression. *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943) (holding that “the action of the local authorities in compelling the flag salute and pledge transcends constitutional limitations on their power” and that the First Amendment protects religious dissent). The District cannot treat religious speech as a disfavored subject. *Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931) (striking down a prior restraint on a newspaper and noting that “the liberty of the press” is “safeguarded” against “previous restraints”). Because the District permitted other non-religious organizations to discuss family values and child rearing, excluding Lamb’s Chapel solely because of the religious perspective of its films is constitutionally impermissible.

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral; It Discriminates Against Religion.**

The District’s rule barring “any group for religious purposes” does not merely exclude speech that endorses a particular religion; it excludes all religious expression. This constitutes viewpoint discrimination. *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931) (invalidating a statute that prohibited displaying a red flag as a symbol of opposition to organized government, because it “is so vague and indefinite as to permit punishment of the fair use of the opportunity for free political discussion”). The District’s absolute ban on religious use is not a reasonable time, place, or manner regulation; it is a content-based exclusion that suppresses expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker’s view. *Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. at 713. The District cannot claim that its policy is viewpoint-neutral when it has permitted secular discussion of family issues but denied the same discussion from a religious perspective.

**IV. The District’s Justification—Avoiding Establishment Clause Violations—Does Not Justify Censorship.**

The District’s argument that permitting religious use would violate the Establishment Clause is unavailing. The government may not suppress religious speech in a public forum on the ground that it might be perceived as endorsing religion. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 576 (1941) (upholding a parade permit law but noting that “the licensing board is not vested with arbitrary power” to discriminate against religious groups). *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947) (holding that the Establishment Clause “requires the state to be a neutral in its relations with groups of religious believers and non-believers” and does not require hostility to religion). Here, the District opened its facilities to the public, and the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech in a limited public forum. *See Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963) (holding that the First Amendment protects “the right of the people peaceably to assemble and to petition the government for a redress of grievances” in a public place, and that the state may not “arrest them for nothing more than expressing their views”).

**V. Conclusion**

The First Amendment protects religious speech from content-based discrimination in a public forum. *Barnette*, 319 U.S. at 642. The District’s policy—denying access to a public forum solely because of the religious viewpoint of the speech—violates the First Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
