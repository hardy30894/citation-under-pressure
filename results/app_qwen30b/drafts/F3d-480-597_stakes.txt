**ARGUMENT**

I. THE GOVERNMENT FAILED TO PROVE THE MENS REA REQUIRED UNDER STAPLES V. UNITED STATES, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), FOR THE MACHINE GUN CONVICTION, RENDERING THE CONVICTION VOID FOR SUFFICIENCY OF THE EVIDENCE.

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause guarantees that no person shall be deprived of liberty without proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of the offense, including the requisite mental state. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This principle is especially critical in criminal cases involving strict liability, where the government must prove not only the physical act (*actus reus*) but also the defendant’s culpable mental state (*mens rea*). In *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600 (1994), the Supreme Court held that the government must prove that the defendant knew the nature of the weapon she possessed—specifically, that it was a machine gun—before she could be convicted under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o). Id. at 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793. The Court emphasized that “the statute does not make it a crime to possess a machinegun without knowledge of its character,” and that “the Government must prove that the defendant knew the weapon was a machinegun.” Id. at 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793.

Here, the government’s evidence fell far short of meeting this burden. The sole evidence of Nieves-Castaño’s knowledge came from a post-arrest statement to a federal agent: that she had once opened the golf bag, seen a rifle inside, and recognized it as an AK-47. This statement, however, does not establish that she knew the weapon was a *machine gun*. The AK-47 is a semi-automatic rifle, not a machine gun, under federal law. 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(33)(A). A machine gun is defined as “any weapon which shoots, is designed to shoot, or can be readily restored to shoot, automatically more than one shot, without manual reloading, by a single function of the trigger.” Id. § 921(a)(33)(A). The government presented no evidence—no testimony, no forensic report, no expert analysis—that the rifle in question was capable of automatic fire, or that Nieves-Castaño knew it was capable of such fire.

The government’s theory at trial was that Nieves-Castaño’s knowledge of the weapon’s identity as an AK-47 was sufficient to satisfy the *mens rea* requirement for a machine gun conviction. But *Staples* explicitly rejected this argument. The Court held that “the fact that a weapon is a machinegun is not self-evident,” and that “a defendant may not be convicted of possessing a machinegun unless the Government proves that he knew the weapon was a machinegun.” 511 U.S. at 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793. The Court further noted that “the possession of a machinegun is not a crime unless the person knows it is a machinegun.” Id. at 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793. Thus, knowledge of the weapon’s general type—such as “AK-47”—is not equivalent to knowledge of its specific classification under the statute.

The government’s failure to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was a machine gun renders the conviction legally insufficient. *See United States v. Baez*, 477 F.3d 1, 5 (1st Cir. 2007) (holding that “a conviction cannot stand if the government fails to prove the required mens rea”); *United States v. Maldonado*, 453 F.3d 1, 5 (1st Cir. 2006) (reversing conviction where “the evidence did not support the jury’s finding of knowledge” under *Staples*). The jury was not instructed on the *mens rea* requirement for the machine gun charge, and the government did not present any evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon could fire automatically. The only evidence of her knowledge was that she knew it was an AK-47—a fact that, under *Staples*, is legally insufficient to sustain a conviction under § 922(o).

The district court’s denial of Nieves-Castaño’s Rule 29 motion for judgment of acquittal was error. *See United States v. Maldonado*, 453 F.3d at 5. The motion was properly preserved and should have been granted because no rational trier of fact could have found beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon was a machine gun. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the government, fails to establish the essential element of *mens rea* required by *Staples*. Therefore, the conviction must be reversed.

Moreover, the government’s reliance on the defendant’s statement that she “knew it was an AK-47” is legally inadequate. The Supreme Court has made clear that “knowledge of the weapon’s general type does not equate to knowledge of its statutory classification.” *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793. The defendant’s statement, even if credited, only establishes that she knew the weapon was a rifle, not that it was a machine gun. Without evidence of her awareness of the weapon’s automatic-fire capability, the government cannot meet its burden under *Staples*.

For these reasons, the conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) must be reversed. The judgment of the district court is contrary to settled precedent and violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.
