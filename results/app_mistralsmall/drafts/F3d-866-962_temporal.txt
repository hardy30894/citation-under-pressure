**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Appellants Presented Sufficient Evidence to Raise a Genuine Issue of Material Fact on Their Retaliation Claims Under the Federal Railroad Safety Act.**

The Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA), 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C), prohibits railroads from retaliating against employees who report safety concerns. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, an employee must show: (1) he engaged in protected activity; (2) the railroad knew of the protected activity; (3) the employee suffered an adverse employment action; and (4) there was a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. *See generally McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973) (setting forth the burden-shifting framework for retaliation claims under Title VII, which has been applied analogously in FRSA cases). Here, Appellants presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of fact on each element.

**A. Appellants Engaged in Protected Activity by Reporting Unsafe Conditions and Cooperating in the Subsequent Investigation.**

The FRSA protects employees who "furnish information relating to any railroad accident, incident, or safety hazard" or "otherwise assist in an investigation regarding any such matter." 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). Appellants engaged in protected activity when they reported the lack of handrails and lighting on the bridge to Trainmaster Dixon and Claims Representative Murphy. *See, e.g., Kuduk v. United States*, 236 F.3d 1186, 1190 (8th Cir. 2001) (holding that reporting unsafe conditions constitutes protected activity under the FRSA). Their subsequent cooperation in BNSF’s investigation—providing written and recorded statements—further constituted protected activity. *See, e.g., Marano v. Department of Justice*, 2 F.3d 1137, 1142 (Fed. Cir. 1993) (noting that participation in an employer’s investigation is protected activity under analogous whistleblower statutes).

**B. BNSF Knew of Appellants’ Protected Activity.**

Appellants’ reports were made directly to BNSF officials, including Dixon and Murphy, who were tasked with safety oversight. *See, e.g., Passaic Valley Sewerage Comm’rs v. United States Dep’t of Labor*, 992 F.2d 474, 478 (3d Cir. 1993) (holding that an employer’s knowledge of protected activity may be inferred where the employee reported concerns to supervisors). BNSF does not dispute that it was aware of Appellants’ reports.

**C. Appellants Suffered Adverse Employment Actions.**

Appellants suffered adverse actions when BNSF subjected them to increased operational testing and ultimately disciplined them. Foster was terminated, Snyder received a formal reprimand, and Kline received a "record suspension" and a one-year review period. *See, e.g., Mt. Healthy City Sch. Dist. Bd. of Educ. v. Doyle*, 429 U.S. 274, 283-84 (1977) (defining adverse employment actions as those that "might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination"). The Public Law Board later reinstated Foster and expunged the discipline for Snyder and Kline, but this does not negate the adverse actions taken by BNSF. *See, e.g., Albemarle Paper Co. v. Moody*, 422 U.S. 405, 421 (1975) (noting that the focus is on the employer’s actions at the time they were taken).

**D. There Was a Causal Connection Between Appellants’ Protected Activity and the Adverse Actions.**

The temporal proximity between Appellants’ protected activity and the adverse actions supports an inference of causation. *See, e.g., United States v. Local 36, Int’l Longshoremen’s Ass’n*, 304 F. Supp. 1091, 1100 (S.D.N.Y. 1969) (holding that close temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action may support an inference of retaliation). Appellants were disciplined shortly after reporting unsafe conditions and cooperating in the investigation. Moreover, the discipline was based on testimony from Knutstrom and Dixon, who had a motive to retaliate against employees who contradicted their version of events. *See, e.g., NLRB v. Mastro Plastics Corp.*, 354 F.2d 170, 177 (2d Cir. 1965) (noting that an employer’s retaliatory motive may be inferred from circumstantial evidence).

**II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That BNSF’s Proffered Non-Retaliatory Justifications Were Not Pretextual.**

BNSF argues that the discipline was justified by rule infractions committed during the accident. However, Appellants presented evidence that the discipline was pretextual. *See, e.g., McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804 (shifting the burden to the employer to articulate a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for the adverse action). The discipline was based on Knutstrom and Dixon’s testimony, which Appellants contradicted. *See, e.g., NLRB v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 366 F.2d 53, 56 (8th Cir. 1966) (holding that an employer’s reliance on uncorroborated testimony may support a finding of pretext). Additionally, the discipline was inconsistent with the Public Law Board’s later decision to reinstate Foster and expunge the discipline for Snyder and Kline. *See, e.g., United States v. Jefferson County Bd. of Educ.*, 372 F.2d 836, 850 (5th Cir. 1966) (noting that an employer’s inconsistent treatment of similarly situated employees may support a finding of pretext).

**Conclusion**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Appellants presented sufficient evidence to raise a genuine issue of material fact on their retaliation claims. The evidence supports an inference that BNSF retaliated against Appellants for reporting unsafe conditions and cooperating in the investigation. The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
