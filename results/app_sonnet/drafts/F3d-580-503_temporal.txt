ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Violated Winbush's Right to a Fair Opportunity to Present a Defense When It Denied His Motion for a Fingerprint Expert and a Continuance.

A criminal defendant's request for a continuance is committed to the trial court's discretion, but that discretion is not unlimited. As the Supreme Court explained in Ungar v. Sarafite, 376 U.S. 575 (1964), "the answer must be found in the circumstances present in every case, particularly in the reasons presented to the trial judge at the time the request is denied." Id. at 589. A blanket denial that ignores the defendant's actual need for time to prepare a defense offends due process. See Avery v. Alabama, 308 U.S. 444, 446 (1940) (recognizing that "a necessary ingredient of substantial fairness [is] a reasonable opportunity to prepare"). And the right at stake here was not merely procedural. The Supreme Court has held that "the right to offer the testimony of witnesses, and to compel their attendance, if necessary, is in plain terms the right to present a defense," and that this right "is a fundamental element of due process of law." Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967).

The government's own expert notice told Winbush that no latent fingerprints of value were found on the jacket, gun, or drug baggies recovered from the snow. That absence of evidence was the centerpiece of the government's forensic case linking Winbush to items he never had in his physical possession when arrested. An independent expert could have explained—consistent with Deputy Commander Reilly's own anticipated testimony about "the difficulties in obtaining latent fingerprints from different materials and surfaces"—that the absence of prints on cold, wet outdoor items proves nothing about who touched them. Winbush was entitled to test that inference through his own expert rather than relying solely on the government's characterization of it.

The magistrate judge denied the motion because it came "woefully late," filed five days before a trial that had "been pending for over two years." That reasoning addresses docket management, not whether Winbush had a fair opportunity to prepare his defense on an issue the government itself had flagged only weeks earlier. As Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), makes clear, the right to defend includes "a fair opportunity to present" one's case, and a court's insistence on proceeding without regard to that opportunity "would be a denial of a substantial right." Id. at 59. Because the fingerprint issue went to the heart of the identity question at trial—whether Winbush, rather than someone else, discarded the jacket, gun, and crack—the denial of both the expert and the continuance was not harmless, and the conviction should be reversed.

II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove That Winbush Possessed the Firearm and the Crack Cocaine Recovered Away from His Person.

On review of the sufficiency of the evidence, this Court must view the record in the light most favorable to the government, Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942), but that deferential standard does not permit conviction on mere speculation. Where the government relies on circumstantial evidence, the jury may convict only if the evidence, taken as a whole, permits a finding of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Holland v. United States, 348 U.S. 121, 139-40 (1954). A conviction cannot rest on evidence that is "equally consistent with innocence as with guilt." Curley v. United States, 160 F.2d 229, 232 (D.C. Cir. 1947).

Here, no witness saw Winbush carry the leather jacket—containing the Ruger handgun and the 20.2 grams of Kentucky crack—to the spot on Kentucky Street where it was found, nor did any witness see him deposit the 9.3 grams of Tennessee crack a block away. The government's proof consisted entirely of the facts that Winbush fled on foot through a snowy neighborhood wearing that jacket, that footprints led generally in that direction, and that the jacket and gun were found somewhere along the path police retraced after the fact. No fingerprint evidence tied Winbush to any of these items. Nothing excludes the reasonable possibility that Winbush shed the jacket at some point, that another person moved through the same area, or that the items were dropped by someone other than Winbush during the chaotic foot chase and struggle. Under Holland, the jury's verdict must rest on more than the coincidence of general direction and time; it must exclude, on the whole record, the reasonable hypothesis of innocence. 348 U.S. at 139-40.

The government's own investigative choices underscore the weakness of this proof: it sought a fingerprint expert precisely because, absent such prints, physical proximity alone was the only evidence connecting Winbush to the jacket, gun, and Tennessee crack. Proximity in space, without more, is not "possession" under the settled principle that guilt may not be inferred from circumstances "equally consistent" with an innocent explanation. Curley, 160 F.2d at 232. Because no rational jury could find beyond a reasonable doubt, on this record, that Winbush possessed the firearm or the crack recovered away from his person, the convictions on Counts charging possession of the Tennessee and Kentucky crack and the § 924(c) firearm offense must be reversed for insufficient evidence. Glasser, 315 U.S. at 80.

For these reasons, Winbush's convictions should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial or, in the alternative, for entry of judgment of acquittal on the possession counts.
