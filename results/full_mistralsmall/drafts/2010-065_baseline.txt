**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE VALIDITY OF § 229.**

A. The Tenth Amendment does not confer a private right of action on individuals to challenge the validity of federal legislation.

The Supreme Court has long held that the Tenth Amendment is a structural provision that “secure[s] the boundary between federal and state authority” and does not confer individual rights enforceable by private litigants. *Bond v. United States*, 564 U.S. 211, 220 (2011) (quoting *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144, 156 (1992)). In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), the Court rejected a Tenth Amendment challenge brought by private utility companies against a federal agency’s competition in the local power market, explaining that the companies “ha[d] no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [A]mendment.” *Id.* at 144. The Court reasoned that the Tenth Amendment “is not a bar to the exertion of powers conferred upon the federal government by other provisions of the Constitution.” *Id.* at 143-44. This holding remains controlling. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 220 (citing *Tennessee Electric Power* with approval).

B. Bond’s reliance on dicta from *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced.

The Seventh Circuit’s observation in *Gillespie* that private parties may assert Tenth Amendment claims is an outlier and has been widely criticized. *See, e.g., Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005) (rejecting *Gillespie* and holding that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims). Moreover, the Supreme Court has since clarified that lower courts must treat alternative holdings as binding precedent. *See California v. United States*, 438 U.S. 645, 689 n.10 (1978) (treating alternative holdings as precedential). *Tennessee Electric Power* is thus controlling, and Bond lacks standing to challenge § 229 under the Tenth Amendment.

**II. § 229 IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER UNDER THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE.**

A. Congress acted within its enumerated powers in enacting § 229 to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention.

The Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress broad authority to enact legislation that is “conducive to the beneficial exercise” of its enumerated powers. *United States v. Comstock*, 560 U.S. 126, 133 (2010) (quoting *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 413-14 (1819)). The Treaty Power, in particular, permits Congress to enact laws necessary to effectuate valid treaties. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) (“If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.”). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a permissible means of implementing it. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 223 (recognizing that § 229 was enacted to implement the Convention).

B. The Tenth Amendment does not limit Congress’s authority to enact treaty-implementing legislation.

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the states powers not delegated to the federal government, but it does not constrain Congress’s authority to legislate pursuant to its enumerated powers. *New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. at 156. Because § 229 was enacted under the Treaty Power—a valid enumerated power—it does not violate the Tenth Amendment. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 223 (rejecting the argument that § 229 violates the Tenth Amendment).

**III. § 229 IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE.**

A. The statute provides fair notice of the conduct it prohibits.

A criminal statute is void for vagueness if it “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited.” *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 304 (2008) (quoting *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983)). Section 229 defines “chemical weapon” and “toxic chemical” with sufficient clarity to apprise individuals of the prohibited conduct. *See Bond*, 564 U.S. at 224 (noting that § 229’s definitions are sufficiently precise).

B. Bond’s conduct falls squarely within the statute’s prohibitions.

Bond’s repeated use of toxic chemicals to harm another person—conduct that caused physical injury—clearly violates § 229(a)(1). The statute’s scienter requirement ensures that only knowing violators are prosecuted. *See id.* at 224. Thus, § 229 is not unconstitutionally vague as applied to Bond.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
