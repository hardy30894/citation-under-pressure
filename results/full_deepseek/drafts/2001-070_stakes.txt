ARGUMENT

The Eighth Amendment prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments.” This Court has long recognized that the Amendment “must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). In determining whether a particular punishment comports with those standards, this Court looks to “objective indicia that reflect the public attitude toward the sanction,” including state legislation, sentencing practices, and the actions of sentencing juries. Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 331 (1989). Applying that framework, the Court in Penry squarely held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically bar the execution of mentally retarded persons. Id. at 340. That holding remains binding precedent and compels affirmance here.

I. There Is No National Consensus Against Executing Mentally Retarded Offenders.

In Penry, this Court examined state legislation and found that only two states and the federal government had enacted statutes barring execution of the mentally retarded. 492 U.S. at 334. The Court concluded that “the two state statutes prohibiting execution of the mentally retarded, even when added to the 14 States that have rejected capital punishment completely, do not provide sufficient evidence of a national consensus.” Id. at 335. Since Penry, the landscape has not changed materially. At the time of Atkins’s sentencing, only a handful of additional states had adopted such prohibitions. The continued existence of capital punishment in the vast majority of states, and the absence of any uniform legislative movement against executing the mentally retarded, demonstrates that “there is insufficient evidence of a national consensus” to overrule Penry. Id. at 340.

Moreover, sentencing juries have consistently rejected the notion that mental retardation automatically precludes a death sentence. In Penry, the Court noted that “many juries have sentenced mentally retarded defendants to death.” Id. at 334. That pattern persists. The very fact that Atkins’s own jury, after hearing expert testimony regarding his mental retardation, twice returned a death sentence underscores that the community does not view such offenders as categorically exempt from capital punishment. See Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 373 (1989) (plurality opinion) (looking to jury determinations as evidence of contemporary values).

II. Mental Retardation Is a Mitigating Factor, Not a Categorical Bar.

The Eighth Amendment requires that sentencing authorities consider “any relevant mitigating evidence” offered by a capital defendant. Penry, 492 U.S. at 328. Mental retardation is precisely such evidence—a powerful mitigating factor that may lead a jury to impose a life sentence rather than death. But the Constitution does not require that this factor be given dispositive weight. As Penry explained, “a sentence of death may still be appropriate” for a mentally retarded offender if the jury, after considering all mitigating and aggravating circumstances, concludes that death is warranted. Id. at 340. The individualized sentencing process already ensures that mental retardation is fully considered. A categorical ban would improperly remove that discretion from the jury and replace it with a rigid rule that “would be inconsistent with the principle that the punishment should be directly related to the personal culpability of the defendant.” Id. at 322.

III. Executing Mentally Retarded Offenders Does Not Offend Evolving Standards of Decency.

Petitioner argues that executing the mentally retarded is disproportionate because such offenders have diminished culpability. But this Court has never held that diminished culpability alone renders a punishment unconstitutional. In Penry, the Court rejected the argument that mental retardation “necessarily” reduces culpability to a level that precludes capital punishment. Id. at 338. Indeed, the Court recognized that “some mentally retarded persons” may possess sufficient cognitive capacity to be “fully responsible for their actions.” Id. at 339. The Eighth Amendment does not require a one-size-fits-all rule; it demands that each defendant be judged on his own individual characteristics. See Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280, 304 (1976) (plurality opinion). Atkins’s own case illustrates the point: the jury heard evidence that he was mildly retarded, yet also heard evidence of his violent conduct and lack of remorse. The jury was entitled to weigh those factors and conclude that death was appropriate.

Finally, the fact that some professional organizations and foreign nations oppose executing the mentally retarded does not establish a national consensus. This Court has consistently looked to “the actions of legislatures and juries” as the primary indicia of evolving standards, not the views of advocacy groups or international bodies. Stanford, 492 U.S. at 370 (plurality opinion). The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
