The Railroad Retirement Act provides for judicial review of final decisions of the Board. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A decision refusing to reopen a prior claim constitutes such a final decision because it terminates the administrative process and affects substantive rights. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 54 (1932) (holding that administrative orders “affecting private rights” are subject to judicial scrutiny to ensure compliance with statutory authority).

The statute does not distinguish between initial denials and refusals to reopen; both resolve the claim with finality. St. Joseph Stock Yards Co. v. United States, 298 U.S. 38, 51 (1936) (administrative action is final when it “puts an end to the administrative proceeding”). Refusal to reopen therefore falls within the review provision.

This conclusion follows from the principle that courts must examine whether an agency has exceeded its powers or failed to afford due process. Morgan v. United States, 298 U.S. 468, 477 (1936) (“The right to a hearing embraces not only the right to present evidence, but also a reasonable opportunity to know the claims of the opposing party and to meet them.”). A denial of reopening without consideration of new evidence or error denies that opportunity.

The same rule applies when an agency declines to exercise discretion conferred by its own regulations. United States v. Morgan, 313 U.S. 409, 422 (1941) (courts review whether administrative action is “in accordance with law”). Here the Board’s refusal to reopen under 20 C.F.R. § 261.2 ends the matter and is therefore reviewable.

Estep v. United States, 327 U.S. 114, 122 (1946), confirms that statutory review extends to “any final order” that determines rights, without artificial limitations on the form of the order. Chicago & Southern Air Lines, Inc. v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 333 U.S. 103, 113 (1948), likewise holds that review lies once administrative remedies are exhausted and the agency has spoken its last word.

Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Committee v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 149 (1951), teaches that an agency decision is final when it “has a direct and immediate effect” on the party. Service v. Dulles, 354 U.S. 363, 388 (1957), adds that courts enforce statutory and regulatory limits on agency discretion precisely to prevent arbitrary termination of claims. Vitarelli v. Seaton, 359 U.S. 535, 545 (1959), reiterates that an agency must follow its own procedures or face judicial correction.

These authorities establish that the Board’s refusal to reopen is a final decision subject to review in the court of appeals. The petition should be reinstated for consideration on the merits.
