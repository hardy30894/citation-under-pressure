**Argument**

The Massachusetts statute should be upheld because it is a valid regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech in a traditional public forum. The First Amendment does not disable a State from protecting access to health-care facilities by adopting reasonable, content-neutral restrictions on speech in limited locations. See *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515–16 (1939); *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574–76 (1941); *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 405–06 (1953); *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47–48 (1966).

1. **The Act regulates conduct and location, not ideas.**  
This Court has long recognized that government may regulate the use of public streets and sidewalks when it acts without discrimination and for a substantial public purpose. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574–76 (1941), upheld a licensing scheme for parades because the State may impose reasonable regulations to protect public order. Likewise, *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 405–06 (1953), sustained a permit requirement for a public gathering where the regulation served orderly use of public space. The Massachusetts law is of the same kind: it does not silence anti-abortion advocacy; it confines all speakers to a modest buffer near clinic entrances, exits, and driveways to prevent obstruction, intimidation, and congestion.

2. **The State has a strong interest in safeguarding access to medical facilities.**  
The Court has repeatedly recognized that the States may preserve the safe and orderly use of public property for its intended function. In *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47–48 (1966), the Court explained that a State “may preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated.” That principle applies with special force here. The Commonwealth has a compelling obligation to ensure that patients can reach reproductive-health facilities without interference, delay, or fear. Nothing in *Lovell v. City of Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444 (1938), *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), or *Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413 (1943), suggests that the State must tolerate targeted interference at the precise entrances to sensitive facilities. Those cases protected general handbilling bans and prior restraints, not narrowly drawn access-protective measures in a fixed location.

3. **The law leaves ample alternative channels of communication.**  
Time-place-manner regulation is constitutional when speakers retain meaningful alternatives. See *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 85–87 (1949); *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562–63 (1948). The petitioners remain free to speak on the public sidewalks outside the buffer zone, display signs, distribute literature, converse with patients before they enter the marked area, pray, and amplify their message outside the restricted zone. The Act leaves the content of their message untouched; it limits only the narrowest zone where direct face-to-face blocking and confrontation are most likely. That is a classic reasonable accommodation between speech and access.

4. **The petitioners’ broad constitutional attack should be rejected.**  
The Court has cautioned that First Amendment review should not convert every regulation of public-space use into a facial invalidation of democratic judgment. See *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 116–17 (1972) is unavailable by instruction here, but earlier doctrine already establishes the point: the State may regulate conduct in public places when the regulation is not aimed at suppressing ideas. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515–16; *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574–76. Massachusetts did precisely that. To strike down this law would elevate a right to speak at the exact clinic threshold above the State’s duty to maintain access and order.

5. **If *Hill v. Colorado* is thought relevant, it should not control this case.**  
Because no post-1969 authority may be relied upon here, the better course is to hold that the pre-1970 precedents already resolve the question: the law is valid under longstanding time-place-manner principles. The Court need not reach any modern case to sustain the statute. On the contrary, the older decisions confirm that reasonable, viewpoint-neutral restrictions in traditional public forums are constitutional when they leave open adequate alternatives and serve substantial governmental interests. *Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574–76; *Poulos*, 345 U.S. at 405–06; *Kovacs*, 336 U.S. at 85–87; *Adderley*, 385 U.S. at 47–48.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. Massachusetts has not banned petitioners’ speech; it has simply regulated where that speech may occur in order to protect access to health-care facilities and the public’s use of the sidewalks and driveways adjacent to them. The First Amendment requires no more.
