Section 1407(a) does not permit a transferee district court to assign a remanded case to itself for trial. Congress provided that multidistrict cases may be transferred only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That text leaves no room for a transferee judge to defeat remand by recharacterizing the case under § 1404(a). As this Court has repeatedly held, venue statutes must be applied according to their terms. See Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949); Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960); Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964).

This Court has already recognized that § 1404(a) is a limited transfer power exercised by “a district court” in the forum where suit was initiated, and that it authorizes transfer only to a district “where it might have been brought.” Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 343-44 (1960). In Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), the Court explained that § 1404(a) authorizes transfer for convenience and justice, but only within the statutory confines Congress imposed. And in Van Dusen, 376 U.S. at 636-37, the Court treated § 1404(a) as a change of forum, not a device for altering the statutory allocation of venue authority.

Those decisions do not support what occurred here. The transferee court in an MDL proceeding is not the original forum in which the action was filed; it is a court to which the JPML has sent the case for a limited purpose. Section 1407(a) makes that limitation explicit. The statute’s command that the Panel “shall” remand at or before the end of pretrial proceedings is mandatory, not precatory. Compare Kansas City Southern Railway Co. v. Guardian Trust Co., 281 U.S. 1, 8 (1930) (courts must give effect to statutory text), with Missouri ex rel. Southern Railway Co. v. Mayfield, 340 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1950) (recognizing that Congress may alter venue consequences by statute).

The lower court’s approach also conflicts with the structure of § 1407. Congress enacted multidistrict transfer to promote “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” not to authorize a self-assignment for trial. If Congress had intended transferee courts to retain cases for trial, it would not have required remand by the Panel. The better reading is the one urged by Judge Kozinski below: § 1407(a) “doesn’t grant proprietary rights” in transferred cases. The statute instead preserves the original forum for trial once pretrial coordination is complete, just as this Court preserved the plaintiff’s statutory rights in Herbert v. Pitcairn, 325 U.S. 77, 78-79 (1945), and Kilpatrick v. Texas & Pacific Railway Co., 337 U.S. 75 (1949), by insisting that procedural devices not defeat federal statutory choices.

Nor can § 1404(a) supply an end run around § 1407(a). The two provisions serve different functions. Section 1404(a) allows a district court to transfer a case “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. at 58. Section 1407(a) instead authorizes transfer only for pretrial coordination and then mandates remand. Reading § 1404(a) to let the transferee court keep the case would nullify § 1407(a)’s remand command, contrary to basic principles of statutory interpretation. See Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad, 345 U.S. 379, 382-83 (1953) (a statute should not be expanded beyond its terms by implication). As Justice Harlan observed in Hoffmann, the transferee court cannot assume a power Congress did not grant. Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. at 344-45.

The decisions below relied on convenience and efficiency, but this Court has rejected similar arguments when they conflict with venue statutes. In Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co., 375 U.S. 71 (1963), the Court emphasized that § 1404(a) discretion is bounded by statute. In Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19, 26-27 (1960), the Court approved transfer only because the case fit within § 1404(a)’s text and purpose. Here, by contrast, the district court’s self-transfer violated the express remand mandate of § 1407(a). Efficiency cannot authorize what Congress forbade.

This Court should therefore reverse and hold that a transferee district court conducting pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a) may not invoke § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial. The proper course is remand to the transferor court under § 1407(a), where any further venue motions may be made in accordance with the governing statutes. See Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960); Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612 (1964); Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Railway Co., 375 U.S. 71 (1963).
