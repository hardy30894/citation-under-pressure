**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal issue: whether Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered its right to seek contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The D.C. Circuit correctly concluded that it did. The district court erred in holding otherwise, and its decision conflicts with the plain language of CERCLA, the settled interpretation of the statute by this Court, and the principle that “where a statute is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe the statute other than according to its terms.” *Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia*, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958).

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree “Resolved” Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from any person not party to the settlement. The statute defines “response action” to include “remedial action,” which, in turn, is defined as “any action consistent with permanent remedy to clean up the release or threatened release of hazardous substances.” *42 U.S.C. § 101(24)*. The Consent Decree in this case required Guam to “design and install a ‘dump cover system’” to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate into the Lonfit River, a response action that is “consistent with permanent remedy” under CERCLA’s own definition. *Id.* The Decree also required Guam to pay a civil penalty and close the Ordot Dump—actions that constitute “costs of response” under § 107(a)(4)(B). Thus, the Decree indisputably addressed “some or all of a response action” and “some or all of the costs of such action.”

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that the Decree “resolved” Guam’s liability for these response actions. *Guam v. United States*, 947 F.3d 1, 16 (D.C. Cir. 2020). The Supreme Court has long recognized that a settlement “resolves” liability when it “involves a party’s assumption of obligations that are consistent with a finding of liability,” even if the party disclaims liability in the agreement. *Asarco Inc. v. United States*, 866 F.3d 1118, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (citing *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004)). The mere presence of a disclaimer of liability—such as the one in the Consent Decree stating that the agreement was “without any finding or admission of liability”—does not defeat the statutory trigger. *Id.* at 1123. As this Court has observed, “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” *Id.* The substantive terms of the Decree—requiring Guam to pay a penalty, close the landfill, and install a cover system—demonstrate that Guam assumed responsibility for the cleanup, which is the very essence of “resolving” liability under § 113(f)(3)(B).

**II. Section 113(f)(3)(B) Is the Exclusive Remedy for Costs Incurred Under a Settlement**

The D.C. Circuit correctly rejected Guam’s argument that it may proceed under § 107(a) because the Decree did not “resolve” liability. The Court of Appeals held that “section 113(f)(3)(B) is the exclusive CERCLA remedy for the costs a liable party is compelled to incur pursuant to a judicially-approved settlement with the United States.” *Guam*, 947 F.3d at 17. This conclusion is consistent with the structure and purpose of CERCLA. Congress created two distinct causes of action: cost recovery under § 107(a) and contribution under § 113(f). But § 113(f)(3)(B) explicitly limits the availability of contribution to those who have settled with the government. *Id.* at 16. To allow a party to bypass this provision and proceed under § 107(a) would undermine the statutory scheme, which incentivizes settlements by immunizing settling parties from further contribution liability. *Id.* at 16–17.

The district court’s contrary conclusion would render § 113(f)(3)(B) meaningless. If a party could always bypass the contribution provision by claiming the settlement did not “resolve” liability, then the three-year statute of limitations in § 113(g)(3) would be a nullity. Congress could not have intended such a result. *Id.* at 22. The D.C. Circuit correctly recognized that “most PRPs would find themselves barred by the statute of limitations by the time they gained the ability to sue under section 113(f)(3)(B)” if the statute were interpreted as the district court did. *Id.* at 22. That would be “a result Congress could not have intended.” *Id.*

**III. Guam’s Due Process and Property Interest Arguments Are Foreclosed**

Guam argues that denying it the right to sue under § 107(a) violates due process because it extinguishes a property interest in contribution without notice. But this argument is forfeited. Guam failed to raise it in the district court, and “it is forfeited.” *Keepseagle v. Vilsack*, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016). Even if preserved, the argument fails on the merits.
