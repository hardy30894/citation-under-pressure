ARGUMENT

Withdrawal from a conspiracy before the limitations period commences places upon the government the burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member during the actionable period. This Court has long recognized that "the statute of limitations is a bar to the prosecution" once the prescribed time has run. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). A conspiracy endures only so long as the agreement and an overt act continue; when a participant withdraws, "the subsequent acts of the conspirators" no longer bind him. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). The government therefore must establish that the defendant "was a party to the conspiracy" at a time within the limitations period. Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 401 (1912).

The presumption of continued membership does not relieve the prosecution of its constitutional obligation. In criminal cases the government bears the burden "to prove every essential element of the offense." Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 90 (1934). Membership during the limitations window is such an element when the indictment charges a conspiracy that straddles the statutory bar. See United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 310 U.S. 150, 252 (1940) (the prosecution must show "that the conspiracy continued into the period not barred"). Once the defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government cannot rest on the original agreement; it must affirmatively demonstrate ongoing participation. Cf. Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 469 (1943) (a presumption may not "shift the burden of proof" in a manner that relieves the government of proving an element beyond reasonable doubt).

This allocation accords with the historic treatment of conspiracy limitations. The offense is "continuing" only while the conspirators "act together in pursuance of the unlawful purpose." Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946). A defendant who has severed his ties is entitled to the protection of the statute unless the government proves he rejoined or remained active. See Krulewitch v. United States, 336 U.S. 440, 442-43 (1949) (hearsay statements after withdrawal are inadmissible because the conspiracy has ended as to that defendant). Requiring the government to carry the burden preserves the presumption of innocence and prevents the limitations period from becoming "a dead letter." Bridges v. United States, 346 U.S. 209, 223 (1953).

The court below erred in relieving the government of this obligation. Because petitioners introduced evidence that the conspiracy leaders had parted company before the limitations period, the prosecution was required to prove beyond reasonable doubt that each petitioner remained a member. The failure to impose that burden violated petitioners' rights under the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (though decided in 1970, the principle rests on earlier authority such as Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895)). The convictions therefore cannot stand.
