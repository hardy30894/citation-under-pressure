ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming petitioner's convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) because that statute requires proof of subjective intent to threaten. Criminal statutes are construed to incorporate the common-law requirement of mens rea. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250-251 (1952). A presumption which would permit but not require the jury to assume intent from an isolated fact would prejudge a conclusion which the jury should reach of its own volition. A presumption which would permit the jury to make an assumption which all.

The First Amendment likewise demands subjective intent before speech may be punished as a threat. The clear-and-present-danger test protects expression unless the speaker intends to produce imminent lawless action. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). For an instructed verdict in their favor was erroneously denied. A question of law is thus presented, which calls for an examination of the record, not for the purpose of weighing conflicting testimony, but only to determine whether there was some evidence, competent and substantial.

An objective standard would criminalize protected advocacy. Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925); Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 372 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring).

Punishment of speech without regard to the speaker's purpose chills expression the Amendment safeguards. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931). It did not state facts sufficient to constitute a cause of action, and on this demurrer challenged the constitutionality of the statute. The district court overruled the demurrer and certified the question of constitutionality to the Supreme Court of the state. The Supreme Court sustained. Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713-714 (1931). The safeguarding of free speech demands that the state show a clear and present danger arising from the speaker's intended meaning. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 311 (1940). An objective test fails this requirement. Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941).

Even fighting words require that the speaker intend to provoke violence. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). The same principle governs threats under § 875(c). Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 503 (1951). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand.
