**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in the coverage of federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that state law may apply whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law leaves a void. That reading ignores the plain text, structure, and purpose of OCSLA, and would allow state law to supplant comprehensive federal schemes like the Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”).

**I. OCSLA’s Text and Structure Require State Law to Fill Gaps in Federal Law**

OCSLA provides that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is inherently limiting. It directs courts to adopt state law only insofar as federal law does not already govern the subject. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365–66 (1969) (holding that state law applies under OCSLA when federal law “does not cover” the claim). The term “applicable” reinforces this gap-filling function: state law is “applicable” only when it can be applied without displacing existing federal law. *See Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (state law applies only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent—renders the phrase “to the extent that” superfluous. If state law could apply automatically absent inconsistency, the limiting language would have no work to do.

**II. The Purpose and History of OCSLA Confirm a Gap-Filling Approach**

Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The Act was meant to “define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures” on the Outer Continental Shelf. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. Congress recognized that federal law would be inadequate to govern the full range of activities on the Shelf, so it borrowed state law to fill the interstices. *See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 27 n.8 (1988). But where federal law is comprehensive—as with the FLSA—there is no void to fill. The gap-filling principle thus respects Congress’s intent that federal law remain the primary rule, with state law serving only as a surrogate where federal law is deficient.

**III. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Scheme That Leaves No Gaps for State Wage and Hour Law**

The FLSA establishes a detailed federal regime governing minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping. 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219. It includes a savings clause that preserves more protective state laws, *id.* § 218(a), but that clause operates only within the states’ own territorial jurisdiction. On the OCS—an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction—the FLSA’s comprehensive provisions leave no room for state supplementation. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1) (declaring the OCS “an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction”). The savings clause does not create a gap; it merely confirms that states may apply their own laws within their own borders. *See* *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365–66 (federal law that “does not cover” the claim creates a gap). Because the FLSA covers the same subject matter as California’s wage and hour laws, there is no void for state law to fill. *Cf. Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036 (no gap where federal law “comprehensively” addresses the issue).

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Undermine Federal Supremacy**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule—that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—would allow state law to override federal policy even where federal law is complete. That result conflicts with OCSLA’s command that federal law governs “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). It also ignores the Supremacy Clause, which requires that state law yield to federal law when the latter occupies the field. *See* *Arizona v. United States*, 567 U.S. 387, 399 (2012). The FLSA occupies the field of wage and hour regulation on the OCS, leaving no room for state law to operate. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding would permit fifty different state regimes to govern the same federal enclave, creating the very inconsistency Congress sought to avoid.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
