**ARGUMENT**

**A State Prisoner’s Procedurally Defaulted Claim of Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Cannot Excuse the Procedural Default of Another Habeas Claim Unless the Ineffective Assistance Claim Itself Was Properly Presented in State Court.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that Carpenter’s procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. This Court has long held that a habeas petitioner must demonstrate “cause and prejudice” to overcome a procedural default. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Cause requires “some objective factor external to the defense” that impeded compliance with state procedural rules. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). Ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute such cause. *Id.* at 488-89. But this Court has also made clear that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” *Id.* at 489.

This principle flows from the fundamental nature of habeas review. A federal court may not grant habeas relief unless the petitioner has exhausted state remedies. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b)(1)(A). Exhaustion requires “giving the State the opportunity to pass upon and correct alleged violations of its prisoners’ federal rights.” *Baldwin v. Reese*, 541 U.S. 27, 29 (2004) (internal quotation omitted). When a petitioner fails to present a claim to the state courts in a timely manner, that claim is procedurally defaulted. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). And when a petitioner uses an ineffective-assistance claim as cause for a default, he must have “exhausted state remedies with respect to the ineffective-assistance claim” itself. *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446, 452 (2000). Otherwise, “the cause question would be moot” because the ineffective-assistance claim “would itself be subject to an independent and adequate state procedural bar.” *Id.* at 453.

Here, Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance-of-appellate-counsel claim was itself procedurally defaulted. He filed a motion to reopen his direct appeal in the Ohio Court of Appeals, but that court dismissed the motion as untimely under Ohio Rule of Appellate Procedure 26(B). The Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. That dismissal rests on an independent and adequate state procedural ground. *See Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 729-30. Consequently, Carpenter cannot rely on that defaulted claim to excuse the default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. *Edwards*, 529 U.S. at 453.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary conclusion conflicts with this Court’s precedent. By allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to serve as cause, the court below eviscerated the exhaustion requirement and circumvented the comity principles that underpin federal habeas review. *See Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 88. The State of Ohio never had a full and fair opportunity to adjudicate Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim on the merits. The federal courts therefore should not have considered it as a gateway to review a second, otherwise-barred claim. *See Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489.

Moreover, the Sixth Circuit’s rule invites petitioners to manufacture cause by raising defaulted ineffective-assistance claims for the first time in federal habeas proceedings. This undermines finality and encourages piecemeal litigation. As this Court has warned, “the cause and prejudice standard is not a perfect safeguard against fundamental miscarriages of justice,” but it strikes the appropriate balance between federal review and state sovereignty. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750.

Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was procedurally defaulted and not properly exhausted in state court, it cannot excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
