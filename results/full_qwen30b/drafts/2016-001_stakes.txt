**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—arises with profound significance in the modern economy, where products are increasingly complex and composed of numerous interchangeable parts. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the award of Samsung’s entire profits on the basis of a design patent covering only the ornamental appearance of a smartphone’s front face—specifically, the arrangement of icons and the bezel—contravenes settled principles of patent damages and the statutory framework governing design patent remedies. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Statutory Text of 35 U.S.C. § 289 Mandates That Damages Be Limited to the Infringer’s Profit Attributable to the Infringed Design

The starting point for any analysis of design patent damages is 35 U.S.C. § 289, which provides:

> “Whoever, without authority, makes, uses, offers to sell, or sells any patented invention, within the United States or imports into the United States any patented invention during the term of the patent therefor, infringing the patent, shall be liable to the patentee for the recovery of damages adequate to compensate for the infringement, but in no event less than a reasonable royalty for the use made of the invention by the infringer, together with interest and costs as fixed by the court. In the case of a design patent, the damages shall be the total profit made by the infringer from the article bearing the design, or the total profit made by the infringer from the sale of the article, whichever is greater.”

35 U.S.C. § 289 (emphasis added). The statute is unambiguous: in the case of a design patent, damages are “the total profit made by the infringer from the article bearing the design.” This language does not authorize the recovery of the entire profit from a complex, multi-component product simply because one component bears an infringing design. Rather, it limits recovery to the profit attributable to the article *bearing* the design—i.e., the component that embodies the infringing ornamental features.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary interpretation—endorsed in this case—ignores the plain meaning of “the article bearing the design.” The term “article” is not synonymous with “product.” In the context of design patents, “article” refers to the specific item whose appearance is protected. See *Bonito Boats, Inc. v. Thunder Craft Boats, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 141, 158 (1989) (noting that design patents protect “the appearance of a particular article of manufacture”). The Supreme Court has long recognized that a design patent may cover only a part of a larger product. See *Ward v. United States*, 155 U.S. 576, 580 (1895) (holding that a design patent may cover “a part of a machine”); *In re Breslow*, 175 F.3d 1372, 1375 (Fed. Cir. 1999) (affirming that a design patent may cover “a component of a larger product”).

Thus, when a design patent covers only a component—such as the front face of a smartphone—the “article bearing the design” is that component, not the entire smartphone. The damages must therefore be limited to the profit attributable to that component. To hold otherwise would transform a design patent into a de facto monopoly over the entire product, which is contrary to the statutory scheme and the constitutional purpose of patent law.

### II. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Contradicts This Court’s Precedent on Patent Damages

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that patent damages must be measured with precision and fairness, avoiding windfalls that exceed the actual harm caused by infringement. In *Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 85, 98 (1993), the Court held that “the measure of damages in a patent case is the patentee’s actual loss or the infringer’s unjust enrichment,” and that “the patentee is entitled to recover only the amount of damages that fairly compensate for the infringement.” Similarly, in *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.*, 547 U.S. 388, 394 (2006), the Court reaffirmed that “a patentee is not entitled to a windfall” and that “the remedy must be tailored to the harm caused.”

The Federal Circuit’s award of Samsung’s entire smartphone profits—despite the fact that the infringing design covered only the front face—constitutes precisely such a windfall. The smartphone is a complex product with numerous components, each contributing to its overall value. The front face, while important, is only one part of the whole. To award Apple the entire profit from the sale of a smartphone based on the infringement of a design covering only the front face is to award damages far exceeding the actual value of the infringing design.

This Court has previously recognized the importance of limiting damages to the value of the infringing feature. In *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co.*, 376 U.S. 225, 230 (1964), the Court held that “where a design patent covers only a part of a product, the damages should be limited to the profit attributable to that part.” The Court explained that “the patentee is entitled to recover the profit made by the infringer from the article bearing the design,” and that “the profit from the entire product cannot be recovered unless the design is the dominant feature of the product.” Id. at 231. This principle remains sound and binding.

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to apply *Sears* in this case—despite the fact that the infringing design covered only a component of a larger product—represents a significant departure from established precedent. The Court should correct this error.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Decision Creates a Dangerous Precedent That Will Undermine Innovation

The Federal Circuit’s interpretation of § 289 opens the door to massive, disproportionate damages awards in cases involving multi-component products. If a design patent covering a single component of a smartphone, car, or appliance can justify recovery of the entire product’s profit, then the risk of liability becomes enormous—even for minor design elements. This will discourage innovation, particularly among smaller firms and startups, who cannot afford the risk of being held liable for the entire profit of a complex product based on a single design feature
