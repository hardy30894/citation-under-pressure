## Argument

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for a potentially responsible party (PRP) to seek recovery for environmental cleanup costs: a cost-recovery action under section 107 and a contribution action under section 113(f). The distinction between these two provisions is crucial, as the statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, whereas the statute of limitations for a remedial cost-recovery action is six years. 42 U.S.C. §§ 9613(g)(2)(B), 9613(g)(3).

The Government of Guam (Guam) seeks to recover costs associated with the Ordot Dump, a landfill contaminated with hazardous waste, including munitions and chemicals allegedly deposited by the United States Navy. Guam's claim is governed by CERCLA, and the question presented is whether Guam's 2004 consent decree with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), thereby precluding it from pursuing a cost-recovery claim under section 107.

The court's analysis should begin with the language of section 113(f)(3)(B), which provides that a party that "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement" may seek contribution from any person who is not a party to the settlement. The 2004 consent decree between Guam and the EPA constitutes a settlement that resolves Guam's liability for the response action.

In United States v. Atlantic Refining Co., 360 U.S. 19 (1959), the Supreme Court held that a consent decree can be a final settlement of a dispute, even if it does not explicitly state that it is a final settlement. Similarly, in Swift & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 311 (1928), the Court held that a consent decree can be binding on the parties, even if it is not a judicial determination of the merits. These precedents support the conclusion that the 2004 consent decree is a settlement that triggers Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B).

Guam's argument that the consent decree does not resolve its liability because it includes a disclaimer of liability is unpersuasive. As the Court held in United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673 (1971) is not available, we look to earlier cases. In United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co., 131 F.2d 545 (2d Cir. 1942), the court held that a consent decree can be binding on the parties, even if it includes a disclaimer of liability. The court should follow this reasoning and conclude that the disclaimer of liability in the 2004 consent decree does not negate its effect as a settlement that triggers Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim.

The consequence of Guam's consent decree with the EPA is that it is now barred from pursuing a cost-recovery claim under section 107. As the Court held in Erie R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), a party is bound by the terms of a settlement agreement. Guam's failure to pursue a contribution claim within the three-year statute of limitations period means that its claim is now time-barred.

In conclusion, the 2004 consent decree between Guam and the EPA triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), and Guam's failure to pursue that claim within the three-year statute of limitations period means that its claim is now time-barred. The court should reverse the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss and remand with instructions to dismiss the complaint.
