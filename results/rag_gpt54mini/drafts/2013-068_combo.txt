**Argument**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts statute as a permissible time, place, and manner regulation. The First Amendment, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, does not disable a State from protecting the safe ingress and egress of patients and staff at reproductive-health facilities. See *Patterson v. Colorado ex rel. Attorney General of Colo.*, 205 U.S. 454 (1907); *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958). Nor does it require Massachusetts to tolerate targeted obstruction at clinic entrances, exits, and driveways when ample alternative channels of communication remain. *Hill v. Smith*, 260 U.S. 592 (1923); *Bi-Metallic Inv. Co. v. State Bd. of Equalization*, 239 U.S. 441 (1915).

1. **The statute is content neutral and directed to conduct in a traditional public forum.**  
The sidewalks at issue are public forums, but that does not mean all regulation is forbidden. The Court has long recognized that even on public property, speech may be regulated “so as to prevent interference with the use to which the property is ordinarily put by the State.” *Amalgamated Food Employees Union Local 590 v. Logan Valley Plaza, Inc.*, 391 U.S. 308, 320 (1968). Massachusetts did exactly that. Its law applies to any person who knowingly enters or remains within a clearly marked buffer zone around clinic entrances, exits, and driveways, regardless of viewpoint. That kind of generally applicable access regulation is materially different from a prohibition keyed to agreement or disagreement with the speaker’s message. See *Patterson*, 205 U.S. at 463–64; *Speiser*, 357 U.S. at 518–19.

2. **The law is narrowly tailored to significant governmental interests.**  
The Commonwealth’s interests in protecting access, safety, and order at medical facilities are substantial. The Court has repeatedly approved restrictions necessary to prevent interference with ingress and egress. *Logan Valley*, 391 U.S. at 320; *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611, 616 (1968). Massachusetts chose a modest 35-foot zone, marked by signs and arcs, and exempted patients, employees, emergency personnel, and persons merely passing through to another destination. That legislative line-drawing is a classic exercise of state authority. *Selover, Bates & Co. v. Walsh*, 226 U.S. 112 (1912). The First Circuit thus correctly held the Act to be a content-neutral time, place, and manner restriction.

Petitioners argue that because they wish to engage in “sidewalk counseling,” the State must adopt narrower, speaker-specific enforcement tools. But the Constitution does not require a State to wait until confrontation turns to obstruction before acting. The Court has sustained prophylactic measures where the State acts to preserve orderly use of public ways and prevent disruption of ordinary traffic. See *Cameron*, 390 U.S. at 616; *Bi-Metallic*, 239 U.S. at 445–46.

3. **Alternative channels remain available.**  
The statute leaves open ample alternative means of communication. Petitioners may speak, pray, distribute literature, display signs, wear expressive clothing, and communicate outside the buffer zone. That is enough. The First Amendment guarantees a right to communicate, not a right to stand at a particular point inches from a clinic doorway. *Logan Valley*, 391 U.S. at 320; *Hill v. Smith*, 260 U.S. at 594–95. On this record, the First Circuit correctly found that petitioners could still reach their audience effectively through direct speech, literature, and other expressive conduct.

4. **Hill v. Colorado should not be overruled.**  
If *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), applies, it should be followed, not limited or overruled. *Hill* recognized that States may adopt reasonable buffer-zone protections around health-care facilities while leaving speakers with meaningful avenues of expression. The decision is consistent with the long-settled principle that the State may regulate access to public ways to prevent interference with their ordinary use. *Logan Valley*, 391 U.S. at 320; *Cameron*, 390 U.S. at 616. Petitioners’ request to overrule *Hill* would not vindicate a prior constitutional baseline; it would unsettle a settled accommodation between speech and safe access. Stare decisis is especially weighty where a rule has structured state legislation and lower-court decisions for years. See *Patterson*, 205 U.S. at 463–64; *Speiser*, 357 U.S. at 518–19.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
