**Argument**

The court of appeals erred in affirming class certification because Rule 23(b)(3) does not permit certification on the basis of conjecture about classwide damages. A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” to determine whether the Rule 23 requirements are actually satisfied, and that inquiry may require resolving factual disputes bearing on predominance. *General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982); *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350-51 (2011); *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 320-21 (3d Cir. 2008). Predominance is not established by a damages theory that simply exists in the abstract; the proponent must show that damages can be measured “on a classwide basis” through common proof. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 655 F.3d 182, 190-91 (3d Cir. 2011), vacated and remanded, 569 U.S. 27 (2013).

The Third Circuit’s approach conflicts with this Court’s class-certification cases. Rule 23 is not a pleading standard. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350. A district court may not certify a class by accepting an unsupported damages model and postponing scrutiny until later proceedings. In *East Texas Motor Freight System, Inc. v. Rodriguez*, the Court held that class certification requires more than allegations that a proposed class shares a common injury; the named plaintiffs must demonstrate that the Rule’s requirements are in fact met. 431 U.S. 395, 403-05 (1977). And in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, the Court explained that Rule 23(b)(3) predominance “tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation.” 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997).

That cohesiveness was missing here. Respondents’ expert offered multiple theories of antitrust impact, but the district court certified the class after narrowing the merits inquiry to a single theory while leaving in place a damages model that did not measure only that theory’s harm. The Third Circuit accepted a model that swept in damages from excluded theories of injury, even though class certification must be tied to the particular liability theory the class will actually prove. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 655 F.3d at 190-91. That is legal error. A model “purporting to serve as evidence of damages in this class action” must “measure only those damages attributable to” the classwide theory of injury. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 35 (2013).

The court below also relieved respondents of their burden to prove admissible, reliable classwide proof. Rule 23 requires a preponderance showing that the certification prerequisites are satisfied. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d at 320. The court may not assume a methodology will work at trial; it must determine whether it can presently be shown, with evidence, that damages are capable of common proof. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350-51; *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d at 323-24. Here, the certification record did not establish that all class members suffered injury in the same way or that any admissible model could quantify their damages without individualized inquiries. That defeats predominance. *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. at 623-24; *Chem. Weapons Working Grp., Inc. v. United States Dep’t of the Army*, 113 F.3d 1142, 1147-48 (10th Cir. 1997).

Finally, the Third Circuit’s deferential approach cannot be squared with the need for meaningful appellate review. Although certification decisions are reviewed for abuse of discretion, that deference does not insulate legal error. *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463, 469 (1978); *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312. Where the district court certifies a class without reliable, classwide evidence of a method to quantify damages attributable to the certified theory, it has not merely made a discretionary judgment; it has applied the wrong Rule 23 standard. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350-51.

This Court should reverse and hold that class certification is improper absent adequate admissible evidence that damages can be measured and quantified on a classwide basis.
