**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DELEGATION OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL IS CONSTITUTIONAL BECAUSE CONGRESS HAS ESTABLISHED AN INTELLIGIBLE PRINCIPLE TO GUIDE THE EXERCISE OF THAT POWER.

The Constitution vests all legislative power in Congress, but it does not prohibit Congress from delegating authority to other branches when it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee’s action. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). This principle has long been recognized as a constitutional limitation on delegation, not a prohibition. The Court has consistently upheld delegations where Congress sets forth clear standards and limits within which the executive may act. *See, e.g.,* *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 519 (1911) (“[T]he power of Congress to delegate its authority is not absolute, but is limited by the requirement that the law must contain a standard or criterion by which the delegate may be guided.”); *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 22 F.2d 101, 102 (2d Cir. 1927) (per curiam), aff’d, 255 U.S. 81 (1921) (“Congress may delegate authority to administrative officers if it lays down an intelligible standard for their guidance.”).

In this case, Congress has done precisely that. Section 811(h) of the Controlled Substances Act authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only when he determines that such scheduling is necessary to avoid an “imminent hazard to the public safety.” 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1). This standard—“imminent hazard to the public safety”—is not vague or amorphous. It is a well-established legal concept, rooted in both common law and statutory tradition. Courts have long interpreted similar language in statutes governing emergency powers, including those involving public health and safety, as sufficient to satisfy the non-delegation doctrine. *See, e.g.,* *Hill v. State*, 119 N.Y. 525, 530 (1891) (holding that a statute empowering a state official to take emergency measures during a public health crisis was validly delegated because it required action only upon a finding of “danger to the public health”); *People v. Hines*, 118 Mich. 522, 525 (1899) (upholding a statute allowing temporary regulation of dangerous substances based on “public danger”).

Moreover, the statute does not leave the Attorney General without any constraints. Section 811(h)(3) requires that the Attorney General consider three specific factors: the history and current pattern of abuse; the scope, duration, and significance of abuse; and the risk to public health. These are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory criteria. The presence of such objective, fact-based considerations demonstrates that Congress has provided more than a mere “rubber stamp” to the executive. *See* *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (“Where the statute prescribes the conditions under which the delegated authority may be exercised, and defines the scope of the discretion, the delegation is not invalid.”).

The Toubys contend that the absence of a detailed list of what constitutes an “imminent hazard” renders the delegation unconstitutional. But this argument misconstrues the nature of legislative delegation. As the Court held in *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 519, “the existence of a standard is not destroyed by the fact that it is not so precise as to eliminate every possible difference of opinion.” The Constitution does not demand perfect precision in every delegation—it demands only that Congress set forth a meaningful boundary beyond which the delegate cannot go. Here, the boundaries are clear: the Attorney General may act only when there is an imminent hazard to public safety, and only after considering the specified factors. That is enough.

II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL MAY LAWFULLY SUBDELEGATE HIS TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.

Congress may authorize the Attorney General to subdelegate his statutory duties to subordinate agencies, so long as the original delegation to the Attorney General itself satisfies the intelligible principle test. *See* *United States v. Patten*, 226 U.S. 543, 550 (1913) (“It is not inconsistent with the Constitution for Congress to vest authority in one officer and then permit him to delegate it further to another.”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that the executive branch may assign functions to administrative officers under proper statutory authorization. *See, e.g.,* *United States v. Doremus*, 249 U.S. 86, 90 (1919) (upholding delegation of enforcement authority to a federal official under a statute that authorized such assignment); *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 117, 122 (1917) (affirming delegation of regulatory authority to a government agency under a statute that permitted such delegation).

In this case, Congress explicitly authorized the Attorney General to perform the functions of the Controlled Substances Act, and the Attorney General, acting under that authority, issued a regulation delegating those functions—including temporary scheduling—to the DEA Administrator. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This subdelegation is consistent with longstanding practice and precedent. *See* *United States v. Richey*, 223 U.S. 107, 112 (1912) (upholding delegation of customs enforcement responsibilities to a subordinate official under a statute that authorized such delegation). There is no indication in the text or legislative history of the CSA that Congress intended to prohibit such subdelegation. On the contrary, the very structure of the Act—its reliance on administrative expertise and rapid response—implies that Congress expected the Attorney General to rely on the DEA’s specialized knowledge and operational capacity.
