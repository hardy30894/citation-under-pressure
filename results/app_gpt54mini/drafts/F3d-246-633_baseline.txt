**ARGUMENT**

I. **The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s RLA conspiracy claim.**

Summary judgment is appropriate only where there is no genuine dispute of material fact. Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. On this record, a reasonable factfinder could conclude that UTU and CSX acted in concert to deprive Smoot of the benefit of the collective bargaining arrangement governing distribution of CSX stock. The Railway Labor Act imposes a duty on bargaining representatives to carry out their representational obligations without arbitrary or bad-faith conduct. See *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 202-04 (1944); *Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 177-78 (1967). The duty extends to union conduct in grievance and arbitration proceedings and forbids arbitrary discrimination or perfunctory handling of an employee’s claim. *Air Line Pilots Ass’n, Int’l v. O’Neill*, 499 U.S. 65, 67, 78 (1991); *Hines v. Anchor Motor Freight, Inc.*, 424 U.S. 554, 563-67 (1976).

Smoot presented evidence that UTU’s initial representative, Elswick, rejected his claim; that the UTU Board of Appeals reversed that decision and directed the union to proceed; and that, at the arbitration hearing, UTU’s designated representative, Earley, was allegedly aligned against Smoot’s interest. A union breaches its duty where its conduct is “arbitrary, discriminatory, or in bad faith.” *O’Neill*, 499 U.S. at 67. Evidence that the union’s representative undermined the claimant in the very forum designed to vindicate his rights is sufficient to create a triable issue. *Hines*, 424 U.S. at 567; *Vaca*, 386 U.S. at 177-78.

The district court also erred in treating CSX as insulated from the claim. The RLA contemplates that carriers and unions may not collude to deprive employees of rights secured by collective bargaining. See *Steele*, 323 U.S. at 202-04. Where the evidence permits an inference that the carrier and the union collaborated to defeat the employee’s claim, summary judgment is improper. See *Hines*, 424 U.S. at 567; *DelCostello v. Int’l Bhd. of Teamsters*, 462 U.S. 151, 164-65 (1983) (recognizing hybrid claims where union breach and employer wrongdoing intertwine). Smoot’s evidence regarding the arbitration process, the union’s shifting positions, and the carrier’s participation in a proceeding that allegedly denied him contractual stock benefits was enough to require trial.

II. **The district court’s damages award under the Federal Wiretap Act is legally and factually unsustainable.**

The Wiretap Act creates civil liability for intentional interception, disclosure, or use of wire, oral, or electronic communications. 18 U.S.C. § 2511; 18 U.S.C. § 2520. But the Act’s remedial scheme is not a vehicle for punitive sanctions untethered to proven statutory harm. A plaintiff may recover only the damages authorized by statute, and punitive damages require proof warranting such an extraordinary remedy. See 18 U.S.C. § 2520; *Goodman v. Lukens Steel Co.*, 482 U.S. 656, 661-62 (1987) (fees and damages depend on established liability and proof); *Browder v. City of Moore*, 522 U.S. 921 (1997) (denying certiorari).

Here, the award of $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and $86,308.74 in fees and costs is excessive on the record described and reflects legal error. Statutory damages under the Act are not automatic windfalls; they must be tied to the statutory measure and the circumstances proven. See *DIRECTV, Inc. v. Huynh*, 318 F. Supp. 2d 1122, 1130-31 (M.D. Ala. 2004) (collecting authorities on discretion in wiretap damages), aff’d, 503 F.3d 847 (9th Cir. 2007). Punitive damages likewise require a showing of conduct that justifies punishment beyond compensation. See *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). The district court’s figures, especially in the absence of any showing that defendants suffered cognizable statutory injury commensurate with such amounts, cannot stand.

III. **The fees and costs award must be reversed or, at minimum, reduced.**

Even where fees are authorized, the prevailing party bears the burden of proving reasonableness. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433-37 (1983). A court must exclude excessive, redundant, or otherwise unnecessary hours and ensure that the resulting fee is reasonable in relation to the results obtained. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 434, 436-37. The same principle governs costs. *Perdue v. Kenny A. ex rel. Winn*, 559 U.S. 542, 551-54 (2010).

Because the compensatory and punitive awards here rest on an overbroad view of the Wiretap Act and because the record does not justify the scale of the fee request, the award of $86,308.74 should be vacated or substantially reduced. See *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 434-37; *Perdue*, 559 U.S. at 551-54.

**CONCLUSION**

The Court should reverse the summary judgment on Smoot’s RLA claim, vacate the damages and fee awards on the Wiretap Act counterclaims, and remand for trial or further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
