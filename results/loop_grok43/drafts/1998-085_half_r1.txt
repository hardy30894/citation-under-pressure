**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct that is egregious or outrageous, independent of the employer's state of mind. This standard derives from the common-law rule that exemplary awards punish only aggravated wrongdoing beyond the mental element of the underlying tort.

In *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. 546, 558 (1818), the Court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant's conduct has been marked by aggravated circumstances. The same principle appears in *Wilkes v. Dinsman*, 48 U.S. 89, 127 (1849), which required misconduct of an aggravated character. *Philadelphia & Reading R.R. v. Derby*, 55 U.S. 468, 486 (1852), reiterated that the jury may award exemplary damages only upon proof of gross negligence or willful misconduct that is morally culpable.

Later decisions confirmed that malice or reckless indifference alone does not suffice. *Milwaukee & St. Paul Ry. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 495 (1875), stated that punitive damages are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct. *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 563 (1886), likewise required conduct evincing a conscious indifference to consequences. *Texas & Pacific Ry. v. Cox*, 145 U.S. 593, 606 (1892), held that the conduct must be of an aggravated character before exemplary damages may be imposed.

The requirement of independent outrageousness also appears in *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), where the Court explained that punitive damages serve to punish the wrongdoer for an aggravated wrong. *Southern Express Co. v. Malone*, 78 U.S. 648, 651 (1870), and *Railroad Co. v. Pratt*, 89 U.S. 123, 136 (1874), both emphasized that the jury must find conduct oppressive or outrageous beyond the elements establishing liability. These authorities demonstrate that, at common law, a showing of malice or reckless indifference was never enough; the plaintiff had to prove additional aggravating facts.

Because the 1991 amendments to Title VII incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
