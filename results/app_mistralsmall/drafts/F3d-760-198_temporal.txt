**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING MOLL’S HOSTILE WORK ENVIRONMENT CLAIM BY FAILING TO CONSIDER ALL ALLEGATIONS IN THEIR TOTALITY.**

A hostile work environment claim under Title VII requires proof that the workplace was “permeated” with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that was “sufficiently severe or pervasive” to alter the conditions of employment. *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson*, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986). The district court, however, erroneously confined its analysis to “sexually offensive acts” within the limitations period, ignoring facially sex-neutral conduct that, when viewed cumulatively, evinces a discriminatory pattern. This was legal error.

The Second Circuit has long recognized that a hostile work environment may be established by facially neutral acts when those acts are motivated by sex and contribute to a hostile atmosphere. *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002) (citing *Bundy v. Jackson*, 641 F.2d 934, 946 (D.C. Cir. 1981)). The Supreme Court has similarly cautioned against parsing discrete incidents in isolation, emphasizing that the totality of the circumstances governs. *Meritor*, 477 U.S. at 69 (“[T]he fact that sex-related conduct was ‘voluminous’... is certainly relevant.”). Thus, the district court erred in dismissing Moll’s hostile work environment claim based on a truncated review of the allegations.

Moreover, this Court has held that even a single act of harassment may suffice if it is “extremely serious.” *Cruz v. Coach Stores, Inc.*, 202 F.3d 560, 570 (2d Cir. 2000) (citing *Meritor*, 477 U.S. at 67). Here, Moll alleges that Irving, her supervisor, repeatedly subjected her to sex-based disparate treatment—including intrusive notes, unwanted advances, and exclusion from promotions and social events—while denying her flexible work arrangements granted to male colleagues. These allegations, if proven, would support a finding of a hostile work environment under settled precedent. *See, e.g., Tomka v. Seiler Corp.*, 66 F.3d 1295, 1305 (2d Cir. 1995) (holding that a hostile environment may be created by a pattern of conduct, including discriminatory promotion and work assignment practices).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON MOLL’S RETALIATION CLAIM.**

Title VII prohibits retaliation against an employee who “has opposed any practice made an unlawful employment practice” or “made a charge” under the statute. 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-3(a). The district court granted summary judgment despite evidence that Moll’s transfer to Syracuse was effectuated in response to her EEOC charge and subsequent lawsuit. This was error.

The Supreme Court has held that an adverse employment action is one that “well might have dissuaded a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination.” *Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). Here, Moll was forced to relocate 150 miles from her home, denied flexible work arrangements, and subjected to heightened scrutiny—all of which could deter a reasonable employee from pursuing discrimination claims. *See, e.g., EEOC v. New York State Office of Mental Health*, 517 F. Supp. 1154, 1160 (S.D.N.Y. 1981) (finding adverse action where employee was transferred to a less desirable location in retaliation for filing a charge).

Furthermore, the district court improperly disregarded Moll’s non-party witness testimony as a “sham issue of fact.” This Court has rejected the notion that a party may defeat summary judgment merely by contradicting prior testimony, but it has never held that a non-party witness’s subsequent inconsistent statement is per se unreliable. *See, e.g., Visser v. Packer Eng’g Assocs., Inc.*, 926 F.2d 65, 69 (2d Cir. 1991) (holding that credibility determinations are for the jury, not the court at summary judgment). The district court’s categorical rejection of this evidence was an abuse of discretion.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING MOLL’S MOTION TO COMPEL.**

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(b)(1) permits discovery of any matter “relevant to the claim or defense of any party.” The district court’s denial of Moll’s motion to compel production of documents related to her discrimination claims was contrary to this broad standard. *See Hickman v. Taylor*, 329 U.S. 495, 507 (1947) (“[T]he deposition-discovery rules are to be accorded a broad and liberal treatment.”). The court’s refusal to compel discovery of Verizon’s promotion and pay records—critical to Moll’s equal pay and disparate treatment claims—was an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be vacated in its entirety. The case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s precedent.
