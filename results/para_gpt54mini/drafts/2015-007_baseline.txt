**Argument**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme is constitutional. The Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find any fact that increases the maximum punishment, but it does not require the jury itself to impose the ultimate sentence or to return a unanimous death recommendation. Nor does the Eighth Amendment require a jury determination of intellectual disability in every case. This Court should therefore reject Hurst’s facial and as-applied challenges.

I. **Ring requires jury factfinding of aggravation, not jury sentencing or unanimity.**

In **Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002)**, the Court held only that “a sentencing judge, sitting without a jury, cannot find an aggravating circumstance necessary for imposition of the death penalty.” **536 U.S. at 609.** Ring rested on the Apprendi principle that any fact increasing the penalty beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. **Id. at 602-09; Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000).**

Florida’s scheme satisfies that rule because the jury’s verdict at guilt phase establishes death eligibility for first-degree murder, and the sentencing phase permits the jury to hear and evaluate aggravation and mitigation. The Constitution does not require juror unanimity in the recommendation itself. In **Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984)**, the Court upheld Florida’s capital sentencing procedure and explained that “the Constitution permits the trial judge, acting alone, to impose a sentence of death.” **468 U.S. at 464.** Although Ring overruled Spaziano on judge-found aggravation, it did not disturb Spaziano’s rejection of a constitutional right to jury sentencing. See **Ring, 536 U.S. at 589, 609.**

Likewise, **Harris v. Alabama, 513 U.S. 504 (1995)** held that the Eighth Amendment does not require the State to assign binding weight to a jury’s death recommendation. **513 U.S. at 515.** And in **Caldwell v. Mississippi, 472 U.S. 320 (1985)**, the Court’s concern was misleading the jury about appellate review, not mandating unanimity. Nothing in Ring or Apprendi converts a constitutionally permissible advisory recommendation into a required unanimous verdict.

This Court’s post-Ring decisions likewise recognize that Florida may constitutionally employ an advisory jury and judicial sentencing, so long as the jury determines the aggravating facts that make death legally available. See **State v. Steele, 921 So. 2d 538, 547-49 (Fla. 2005)**; **Coday v. State, 946 So. 2d 988, 1004-05 (Fla. 2006).** The Florida Supreme Court therefore correctly rejected Hurst’s unanimity argument.

II. **The Eighth Amendment does not require the jury, rather than the court, to determine intellectual disability.**

The Eighth Amendment forbids execution of the intellectually disabled. **Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 321 (2002).** But Atkins expressly left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction upon their execution of sentences.” **Id. at 317.** Florida has done so by creating a judicially enforceable procedure for determining intellectual disability. See **Floyd v. State, 808 So. 2d 175, 191-92 (Fla. 2002)**; **Cherry v. State, 959 So. 2d 702, 710-12 (Fla. 2007).**

Nothing in Atkins, Ring, or the Eighth Amendment requires that the jury determine intellectual disability. The inquiry is a constitutional eligibility determination, but Atkins did not specify the factfinder. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly recognized that States may channel capital determinations through judges and courts. See **Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 416-17 (1986)**; **Panetti v. Quarterman, 551 U.S. 930, 949-50 (2007)**. Florida’s procedure, which allowed Hurst to present intellectual-disability evidence in mitigation while reserving the bar-to-execution question for the court, comports with Atkins.

III. **Any aggravator-finding error was harmless, and the sentence remains valid under Florida law.**

Even if Ring error occurred, this Court has recognized harmless-error review in capital sentencing. See **State v. DiGuilio, 491 So. 2d 1129, 1135 (Fla. 1986)**. The record here establishes overwhelming aggravation: the murder was committed during a robbery and was especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel. Those findings were supported by the evidence recounted by the Florida Supreme Court, including the bound victim, the repeated stabbing, and the theft of restaurant money. Hurst v. State, 147 So. 3d 435, 441-44 (Fla. 2014). No reasonable possibility exists that a different sentencer would have rejected death eligibility on this record.

IV. **The Court should not extend Ring into a constitutional mandate of unanimity or jury sentencing.**

Hurst asks this Court to create a new constitutional rule requiring unanimity for a death recommendation and jury determination of intellectual disability. But the Court has repeatedly declined to constitutionalize specific capital-sentencing forms absent clear Sixth or Eighth Amendment command. See **Kansas v. Carr, 577 U.S. 108, 119-20 (2016)**; **Buchanan v. Angelone, 522 U.S. 269, 275-77 (1998)**. Florida’s scheme, while different from some other States’, is not unconstitutional merely because other States have chosen a different allocation of responsibility.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
