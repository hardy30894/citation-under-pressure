## Argument

### I. Design-patent damages under § 289 must be limited to the infringing component, not the infringer’s entire product profits, when the patented design is only a component of a larger product.

Section 289 authorizes a “total profit” remedy only for the “article of manufacture to which the design or colorable imitation has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. The statute does not say “the accused product,” much less the defendant’s entire profits from a multi-component device. In *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), this Court held that a design-patent owner could recover only the profit attributable to the patented design and not the profit from the whole carpet when the design covered only a part of the product. Congress later enacted § 289 to override *Dobson*’s insistence on apportionment in the sense of requiring proof of the precise value added by the design. But Congress did not eliminate the statutory limitation to the “article of manufacture.” See *Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc.*, 580 U.S. 53, 61-62 (2016) (explaining that § 289 “limits a design-patent owner’s recovery to the profits on the article of manufacture to which the patented design has been applied” and that “the relevant ‘article of manufacture’ may be a component of a product”).  

This Court in *Samsung Electronics* rejected the Federal Circuit’s rule that the “article of manufacture” must always be the end product sold to consumers. Id. at 57-58. The Court held that a design patent may cover only “a component of a product” and that the proper damages inquiry must account for that fact. Id. at 62. That holding necessarily forecloses awarding all profits from a complex device whenever the patented design covers only a component. If the “article of manufacture” can be less than the entire smartphone, then the recoverable profits must be tied to that smaller article. See *id.* at 62; *City of Elizabeth v. American Nicholson Pavement Co.*, 97 U.S. 126, 137 (1878) (recognizing that patent damages turn on the proper patented subject, not on unrelated value in the defendant’s larger enterprise).

The Federal Circuit’s decision below effectively reinstated the very rule this Court rejected. By allowing recovery of Samsung’s entire profits on the accused phones even though the asserted design patents covered only specific ornamental features of the phones, the judgment untethers § 289 from its text. That result conflicts with the settled principle that “statutory interpretation begins with the language of the statute itself.” *Consumer Prod. Safety Comm’n v. GTE Sylvania, Inc.*, 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980). Here, the statute’s reference to the “article of manufacture” is the operative limitation, and the Court should give it effect. See *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 172 (2001).

### II. The historical purpose of § 289 confirms that total-product profits are improper where the patent covers only part of the product.

Section 289 was enacted against the backdrop of *Dobson*, which had made recovery difficult by requiring proof of the amount of profit attributable to the design. See *Samsung Elecs.*, 580 U.S. at 61. But Congress responded by permitting recovery of the infringer’s “total profit” from the relevant article of manufacture—not from every product in which the design appears. 35 U.S.C. § 289. The phrase “total profit” modifies the profits from the “article of manufacture,” not the profits from the whole commercial product when the design is only an incidental component. See *Samsung Elecs.*, 580 U.S. at 62.  

That reading accords with the ordinary meaning of the statute and avoids converting a design patent into a windfall right over an entire multi-feature device. This Court has repeatedly warned against interpretations that allow patent remedies to exceed the scope of the patented invention. See *Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476, 507 (1964) (patent law does not permit recovery beyond the patented contribution); *Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912) (damages must correspond to the value of the patented feature). The same principle applies here. The remedy must be confined to the article to which the design is applied.

### III. Limiting profits to the component is necessary to prevent disproportionate and inequitable damages.

Awarding entire-product profits for a component design patent produces the very distortion § 289 was not meant to create. Design patents protect ornamental appearance, not technological functionality. *Egyptian Goddess, Inc. v. Swisa, Inc.*, 543 F.3d 665, 679 (Fed. Cir. 2008) (en banc). When the asserted design is only one aspect of a smartphone containing many distinct, valuable features, allowing recovery of all profits from the phone ignores the economic reality that consumers purchase the device for its aggregate functionality, software, brand, and hardware integration—not solely for the ornamental design at issue. See *Samsung Elecs.*, 580 U.S. at 63 (rejecting an approach that would treat the entire smartphone as the article of manufacture regardless of what the design actually covers).  

This Court should not interpret § 289 to grant a patentee the entire value of a complex product where the patented design is merely a component. Such an interpretation would confer a penalty, not compensation, contrary to the remedial structure of patent law. See *Georgia-Pacific Corp. v. U.S. Plywood Corp.*, 318 F. Supp. 1116, 1120 (S.D.N.Y. 1970), aff’d, 446 F.2d 295 (2d Cir. 1971) (reasonable royalties are designed to compensate, not punish); *Aro Mfg.*, 377 U.S. at 507.

### IV. The Court should reverse and hold that damages under § 289 are limited to the profits attributable to the relevant article of manufacture.

The question presented is straightforward: when the patented design covers only a component, must damages be limited to profits attributable to that component? *Samsung Elecs.* already answered the first half of that question by holding that the “article of manufacture” may be a component. 580 U.S. at 62. The second half follows from the statute’s text and structure: profits cannot be awarded from the entire device when the relevant article is smaller.  

The judgment below should therefore be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of the proper article of manufacture and the profits, if any, attributable to that article. See *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. 370, 388-89 (1996) (courts must carefully preserve the legal scope of patent rights); *Samsung Elecs.*, 580 U.S. at 62.
