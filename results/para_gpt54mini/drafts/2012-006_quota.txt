**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals correctly rejected petitioner’s effort to shift the burden of persuasion to the Government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. Withdrawal is not an element of the conspiracy offense; it is an affirmative defense that the defendant must prove. The Government’s burden is to prove the conspiracy existed, that the defendant knowingly joined it, and that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period as to at least one conspirator. Once membership is shown, the defendant bears the burden of establishing that he withdrew before the limitations period. That allocation is consistent with this Court’s conspiracy precedents and with the general rule that the party asserting an affirmative defense bears the burden of proof. See *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106 (2013); *United States v. Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422 (1978); *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946).

This Court has long treated withdrawal as something distinct from the Government’s prima facie proof of conspiracy. In *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court explained that a conspirator remains liable absent a showing that he “withdrew” from the agreement. Likewise, in *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601 (1910), the Court recognized conspiracy as a continuing offense whose duration depends on the life of the unlawful agreement. Because conspiracy continues until accomplished or abandoned, the statute of limitations is ordinarily satisfied if the agreement persisted into the relevant period. *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946); *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391 (1957).

The statute-of-limitations issue does not alter the burden. The Government must prove the offense occurred within the limitations period, but when the defendant claims he severed himself earlier, he raises a separate, affirmative matter. This Court’s decisions have repeatedly placed the burden of proving affirmative defenses on defendants unless Congress has said otherwise. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977); *Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1 (2006). Withdrawal fits that model. It does not negate the existence of the conspiracy or the defendant’s initial participation; it excuses continued liability after a point in time. *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106 (2013).

The Court of Appeals’ approach also accords with the general principle that, once the prosecution proves membership in a continuing offense, the defendant bears the burden of showing he ended his own participation. In *United States v. Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422 (1978), the Court emphasized that conspiracy liability turns on agreement and participation, not on a defendant’s ability to later escape responsibility by silence or inactivity. In *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640 (1946), the Court explained that a conspirator is liable for the acts of coconspirators so long as the conspiracy endures and he remains part of it. That continuing-liability principle would be undermined if the Government had to disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt every time limitations became an issue.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would be especially unworkable. Withdrawal is peculiarly within the defendant’s knowledge. Evidence of disassociation, notification to coconspirators, or notice to law enforcement is ordinarily available to the defendant, not the Government. Requiring the Government to prove the negative—that the defendant did not withdraw—would conflict with the practical and doctrinal structure of conspiracy law. See *Smith v. United States*, 568 U.S. 106 (2013); *Dixon v. United States*, 548 U.S. 1 (2006). And the rule would invert the ordinary burden just because the defense is tied to limitations, even though this Court has never treated the statute of limitations as converting affirmative defenses into prosecution elements. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391 (1957); *Fiswick v. United States*, 329 U.S. 211 (1946).

The petition also cannot be reconciled with the law governing conspiracy duration. A conspiracy continues so long as its purposes have not been achieved or abandoned. *Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601; *Grunewald*, 353 U.S. 391. Thus, if the Government proves that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period, that satisfies its burden as to the offense. Whether a particular defendant earlier withdrew is a separate factual question. The defendant, not the Government, must prove the withdrawal. *Hyde*, 225 U.S. 347; *Smith*, 568 U.S. 106.

Finally, petitioner’s position would blur the distinction between proof of guilt and proof of an affirmative defense, contrary to settled due-process doctrine. This Court has held that States and the Federal Government may allocate to the defendant the burden of proving affirmative defenses so long as the prosecution still proves the elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Patterson*, 432 U.S. 197; *Dixon*, 548 U.S. 1. That is precisely the rule the court of appeals applied. It should be affirmed.
