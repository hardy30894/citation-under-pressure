**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) adopts adjacent state law only as surrogate federal law when federal law is actually deficient on the point at issue. The statute does not invite courts to apply all state rules that are merely “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law in the abstract. Rather, as the Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized, OCSLA’s purpose is to furnish a body of law for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf by borrowing state law only where federal law leaves a gap. See **Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355, 366 (1969)**; **Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97, 101-02 (1971)**; **Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473, 480-81 (1981)**.

1. **OCSLA’s text requires more than mere noninconsistency.**  
Section 1333(a)(2)(A) provides that adjacent state law is declared to be federal law only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” **43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A)**. That language does not say state law applies whenever it concerns the subject matter of the dispute. It says state law is assimilated only to the extent it is both applicable and not inconsistent. The Supreme Court’s OCSLA decisions read that clause as a borrowing rule, not a free-standing state-law incorporation command. **Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355, 366** (explaining that fixed platforms are treated like federal enclaves and that state law is incorporated by “application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]” when federal law does not supply the answer); **Gulf Offshore, 453 U.S. at 480-81** (stating that OCSLA “mandates that state laws apply as federal laws” to the extent they are applicable and not inconsistent).

2. **The Supreme Court has treated OCSLA as gap-filling legislation.**  
In **Rodrigue**, the Court held that Louisiana wrongful-death law applied because maritime law did not govern fixed platforms and therefore there was no federal rule occupying the field. **395 U.S. at 355, 366**. The Court’s reasoning was not that state law is always available whenever it is not inconsistent with some federal law; it was that the absence of an applicable federal rule left room for surrogate state law. **Id.** Likewise, in **Chevron Oil**, the Court described OCSLA as making adjacent state law federal law only for the offshore area that would be within the state if its boundaries were extended seaward, and only for laws “applicable” and “not inconsistent” with federal law. **404 U.S. at 101-02**. And in **Gulf Offshore**, the Court emphasized that application of state law under OCSLA requires a court to “consider the content of both potentially applicable federal and state law.” **453 U.S. at 480**. That formulation presupposes a federal-law baseline against which state law is borrowed where federal law is incomplete.

3. **Petitioner’s rule is confirmed by the OCSLA’s structure and history.**  
Congress enacted OCSLA to establish a body of law for the seabed, subsoil, and fixed structures on the Shelf. **Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355**. The Act extends federal law to those structures “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” **43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1)**. That enclave-like design is inconsistent with respondent’s theory that state law applies on its own force whenever it is not preempted. The better view is the one reflected in **Rodrigue** and **Huson**: adjacent state law is assimilated as federal law only where federal law is silent or incomplete. **395 U.S. at 366**; **404 U.S. at 101-02**.

4. **The FLSA forecloses California’s wage-and-hour rules here.**  
The Fair Labor Standards Act already addresses minimum wage, overtime, and related compensation questions. See **29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207**. Congress also included a savings provision preserving more protective state wage laws in ordinary domestic application. **29 U.S.C. § 218(a)**. But that savings clause does not rewrite OCSLA. On the Outer Continental Shelf, the question is not whether California law could coexist with federal law in the abstract; it is whether OCSLA incorporates California law as surrogate federal law where federal law leaves a gap. Because the FLSA supplies the governing federal rules for minimum wage and overtime, there is no gap for California to fill. See **Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc., 450 U.S. 728, 740 (1981)** (recognizing the FLSA as an independent statutory wage regime); **Christensen v. Harris County, 529 U.S. 576, 579-80 (2000)** (describing the FLSA’s overtime scheme).

5. **The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with Supreme Court precedent.**  
The decision below effectively converts OCSLA’s borrowing clause into an anti-preemption clause. But the Supreme Court has never adopted that approach. Instead, it has treated the statute as assimilating state law only when federal law is deficient, because OCSLA is designed to fill offshore legal voids, not to duplicate every potentially relevant state rule. **Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355, 366; Gulf Offshore, 453 U.S. at 480-81**. That is the rule this Court should reaffirm.

For those reasons, the Court should hold that OCSLA permits application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, and it should reverse the judgment below.
