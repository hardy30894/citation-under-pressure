The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed because the Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a valid, content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies the requirements of *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989), and is squarely controlled by *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). *Hill* remains good law and should not be overruled.

**I. The Act Is a Content-Neutral Time, Place, and Manner Regulation**

Public sidewalks adjacent to healthcare facilities are “quintessential public forums.” *Perry Educ. Assn. v. Perry Local Educators’ Assn.*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). In such forums, the government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, or manner of protected speech if they are content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791; *Clark v. Cmty. for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984).

The Act is content-neutral on its face. It prohibits *all* persons from knowingly entering or remaining within the buffer zone, without regard to viewpoint or subject matter. The four exemptions—people entering or leaving the facility, employees acting within the scope of employment, government agents, and persons passing through to another destination—do not single out any speaker or message. As *Hill* recognized, “the fact that the statute covers all speakers” and “is not a discrimination on the basis of content” is a hallmark of a neutral regulation. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719–20. Petitioners’ desire to communicate a particular message does not transform a neutral rule into a content-based one. *See Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 763 (1994) (finding injunction content-neutral where it applied to all persons).

**II. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored to Serve Significant Government Interests**

The Commonwealth has compelling interests in protecting public safety, preserving access to healthcare facilities, and preventing obstruction and harassment of patients. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 728–29; *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W. N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 372–73 (1997). The buffer zone is a “narrowly tailored” means of serving those interests. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799.

The zone is limited to 35 feet, smaller than the 100-foot floating zone upheld in *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 707, and comparable to the 36-foot fixed buffer upheld in *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 770–72, and the 15-foot fixed buffer upheld in *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 375–77. The Act does not ban speech; it merely relocates it. Because it leaves petitioners free to communicate from the boundary line, it burdens no more speech than necessary. *See United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968) (incidental restriction is valid if no greater than essential).

**III. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels of Communication**

Petitioners retain abundant means to convey their message: they may speak, use sound amplification, display signs, distribute literature, and pray—both within their cars and on the public sidewalk just beyond the buffer zone. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726–27; *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88–89 (1949) (upholding ban on sound trucks because other communication channels remained available). The First Circuit correctly found that “adequate communicative channels remain available to the plaintiffs.” The Act does not foreclose access to the intended audience; it merely prevents petitioners from standing directly at the doorway. That is a permissible time, place, and manner restriction. *See Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness, Inc.*, 452 U.S. 640, 654–55 (1981) (upholding rule limiting distribution to a booth because other means of communication existed).

**IV. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Was Properly Applied**

The First Circuit relied on *Hill*, and that reliance was correct. In *Hill*, the Supreme Court upheld a Colorado statute that made it unlawful to “knowingly approach” within eight feet of any person entering a healthcare facility for purposes of “protest, education, or counseling.” 530 U.S. at 707. The Massachusetts Act is far less restrictive of speech because it prohibits *all* entry into a fixed 35-foot zone but does not regulate speech occurring beyond that boundary, nor does it require speakers to withdraw upon a patient’s approach. *Hill* is directly on point and dispositive. Petitioners’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* on the ground that the Massachusetts Act has no “knowing approach” requirement actually demonstrates why the Act is *narrower*: it imposes a clear, ministerial distance rule that avoids the discretionary and potentially viewpoint-driven enforcement that *Hill* found permissible. *Id.* at 719–20.

**V. *Hill* Should Not Be Limited or Overruled**

Petitioners ask this Court to overrule *Hill*. There is no basis to do so. Stare decisis is “the preferred course” because it “promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles.” *Hilton v. S.C. Pub. Railways Comm’n*, 502 U.S. 197, 202 (1991). Since *Hill* was decided, this Court has repeatedly reaffirmed its framework in the context of abortion-clinic buffer zones. *See Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 770–72; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 375–77. Those decisions, along with the traditional time-place-manner analysis of *Ward* and *Clark*, demonstrate that *Hill* fits comfortably within the mainstream of First Amendment jurisprudence. Overruling it would require the Court to repudiate the settled principle that government may regulate the *place* of speech on public property in order to protect health and safety. *See Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 116 (1972) (upholding anti-noise ordinance near schools because it served significant interests). Petitioners’ disagreement with the outcome in this case is not a special justification for overruling precedent. *See Hilton*, 502 U.S. at 202.

In sum, the Act is a narrow, content-neutral regulation that leaves open ample alternative communication channels and serves compelling state interests. *Hill* controls and should be followed. The First Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
