**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OSH ACT’S PLAIN TEXT AND CONGRESSIONAL DESIGN EXTEND COVERAGE TO ALL WORKPLACES, INCLUDING UNINSPECTED VESSELS LIKE MALLARD BAY DRILLING RIG 52.

The Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 applies to “employment performed in a workplace” without geographic or vessel-type limitation. 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The Act defines “workplace” to mean “any establishment, site, or location where work is performed by an employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 652(10). Nothing in the statute carves out uninspected vessels from the definition of “workplace,” and this Court has repeatedly refused to read such exclusions into remedial statutes. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 356 (1969) (“[W]e are not at liberty to create exceptions to the operation of a statute which are not written into it.”). The explosion aboard Rig 52 occurred while employees were performing drilling operations in Louisiana’s territorial waters—a quintessential workplace under the Act.

II. SECTION 4(b)(1) DOES NOT DEPRIVE OSHA OF JURISDICTION BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED “STATUTORY AUTHORITY TO PRESCRIBE OR ENFORCE STANDARDS OR REGULATIONS AFFECTING OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY OR HEALTH” ON UNINSPECTED VESSELS.

Section 4(b)(1) withholds OSHA coverage only where “other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to discrete safety equipment requirements—such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems—set forth in 46 U.S.C. § 4102 and implemented at 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. These regulations do not address the full spectrum of occupational hazards present on drilling barges, including blowout prevention, gas detection, and emergency evacuation procedures. *Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit*, 362 U.S. 440, 448 (1960) (federal safety certification does not preempt local regulation where federal standards are “minimum” and do not occupy the field).

Crucially, the Coast Guard’s investigative role after the explosion did not constitute the exercise of “authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” within the meaning of § 4(b)(1). *Maul v. United States*, 274 U.S. 501, 508 (1927) (Coast Guard’s enforcement of navigation laws does not equate to comprehensive occupational safety regulation). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), misreads § 4(b)(1) by conflating the Coast Guard’s general maritime safety authority with the specific statutory mandate to regulate occupational hazards.

III. THE COAST GUARD’S REGULATORY SCHEME FOR UNINSPECTED VESSELS IS NOT EXCLUSIVE OR COMPREHENSIVE, AND DOES NOT PREEMPT OSHA’S APPLICATION.

The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels are fragmentary and do not address the full range of occupational dangers faced by drilling rig workers. *Sherlock v. Alling*, 93 U.S. 99, 103 (1876) (state safety laws may supplement federal maritime regulations where Congress has not occupied the field). The Court has long recognized that federal maritime safety laws do not preempt state or federal occupational safety laws unless Congress has expressly so provided. *United States v. Louisiana*, 394 U.S. 11, 23 (1969) (federal navigation laws do not displace state authority absent clear congressional intent). The OSH Act’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(4), explicitly preserves state occupational safety laws, underscoring that Congress did not intend to leave workers on uninspected vessels without federal protection.

IV. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION CONFLICTS WITH THE SUPREME COURT’S REPEATED HOLDINGS THAT FEDERAL AGENCIES MUST EXERCISE “STATUTORY AUTHORITY” TO TRIGGER § 4(b)(1) PREEMPTION.

The Fifth Circuit erred by assuming that the Coast Guard’s general maritime authority over vessels automatically preempts OSHA. *Northern Belle v. Robson*, 154 U.S. 571, 576 (1870) (federal regulation of vessels does not displace state tort remedies unless Congress so provides). Section 4(b)(1) requires an agency to have “statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health”—a standard the Coast Guard has not met for uninspected vessels. *Blamberg Bros. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 452, 458 (1923) (federal authority must be “expressly conferred” to preempt state law).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment below should be reversed. The OSH Act covers all workplaces, including uninspected vessels like Rig 52, and § 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction because the Coast Guard has not exercised the requisite statutory authority to regulate occupational safety on such vessels.
