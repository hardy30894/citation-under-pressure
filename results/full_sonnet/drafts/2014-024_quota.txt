# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 875(c) REQUIRES PROOF THAT THE DEFENDANT SUBJECTIVELY INTENDED HIS STATEMENT TO BE UNDERSTOOD AS A THREAT

### A. The Presumption in Favor of Scienter Requires Reading a Subjective Intent Element Into §875(c)

This Court has "long recognized that determining the mental state required for commission of a federal crime requires 'construction of the statute and . . . inference of the intent of Congress.'" *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994) (quoting *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 253 (1922)). The "presumption in favor of a scienter requirement should apply to each of the statutory elements that criminalize otherwise innocent conduct." *Id.* at 619. This presumption "is a rule based on a legislative purpose that courts should ordinarily construe criminal statutes to include broadly applicable scienter requirements, even where the statute by its terms does not contain them." *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 70 (1994). Indeed, this Court has stated it is "not inclined to construe the statute" so as to "criminalize a broad range of apparently innocent conduct" absent a clear indication of contrary congressional intent. *Id.* at 78–79.

Section 875(c) criminalizes "any communication containing any threat," but nowhere specifies the required mental state. Following *X-Citement Video* and *Staples*, this silence should be construed to require that the defendant know the threatening nature of his communication—that is, that he subjectively intend the recipient to understand the statement as a threat. Without such a requirement, §875(c) sweeps in defendants who make statements that a reasonable person might consider threatening, even though the speaker had no such intent—the very type of "otherwise innocent conduct" this Court has refused to criminalize absent clear congressional direction. *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 72.

The Third Circuit's rule, drawn from *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), asks only whether "a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat." That negligence-like standard collapses the scienter analysis into an objective inquiry focused on the listener's perception, not the speaker's intent, and cannot be squared with the "general rule" that criminal statutes "require some mental element." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (explaining crime "generally consists" not "merely" of forbidden conduct "but also of an evil intent").

### B. This Court's True-Threats Jurisprudence Confirms That Subjective Intent Is Constitutionally Required

The First Amendment poses independent difficulties for a purely objective standard. In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), this Court defined "true threats" as statements where "the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." *Id.* at 359 (emphasis added). The Court's formulation is unmistakably speaker-focused: what matters is what the speaker "means to communicate," not merely how a reasonable listener might receive the statement. The Court further explained that a "prohibition on true threats" is permissible precisely because "the speaker" has engaged in intentional conduct that falls outside protected expression. *Id.*

This emphasis on the speaker's mental state reflects the Court's broader teaching that incidental restrictions on protected speech demand careful attention to intent. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), the Court reversed a conviction under a threat statute, holding that the statute "requires the Government to prove a true 'threat'" and cautioning against literalistic readings that would sweep in "political hyperbole." *Id.* at 708. The Court's concern in *Watts* was whether the speaker's statement, taken "in context," constituted a genuine expression of intent to harm—an inquiry that necessarily implicates the speaker's own state of mind, not merely the impression created in a hypothetical reasonable listener.

An objective standard risks punishing protected speech because it "creates an unacceptable risk of the suppression of ideas." *Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 535 U.S. 234, 246 (2002) (internal quotation marks omitted). Speakers of hyperbole, satire, or artistic expression—forms of speech that lie close to the heart of First Amendment protection, *see Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46, 55 (1988)—may reasonably fear that jurors will find their words threatening even though the speaker intended no threat at all. Requiring proof of subjective intent guards against this chilling effect and ensures that only genuine threats, not merely careless or artistic expression, are punished. *Cf. Elonis v. United States*, No. 12-3798, slip op. at 2 (3d Cir. Sept. 19, 2013) (acknowledging that the question presented is "whether the true threats exception to speech protection under the First Amendment requires a jury to find the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats").

### C. An Objective Standard Improperly Reduces §875(c) to a Negligence Offense

This Court has repeatedly rejected the criminalization of speech or conduct based on mere negligence where more demanding culpability standards are constitutionally or statutorily required. In *Elonis*'s case, the jury was instructed that a statement qualifies as a "true threat" merely if "a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted . . . as a serious expression of an intention to inflict bodily injury." This objective, "reasonable person" test permits conviction based solely on how the statement was likely to be perceived, without regard to what the defendant actually intended.

Such an approach is inconsistent with the "general" and "familiar" principle that "criminal statutes are to be construed so as to require" that a defendant "know the facts that make his conduct illegal." *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 605, 619 (quoting *United States v. Dotterweich*, 320 U.S. 277, 281 (1943)). It also risks constitutional infirmity by permitting the conviction of individuals who lacked any intent to threaten, resting culpability instead on the fortuity of a listener's subjective reaction. As this Court has cautioned, a rule of law "requiring proof of a defendant's state of mind" serves to protect against the danger that "the jury will convict for reasons wholly unrelated to the legitimate interest" the statute seeks to vindicate. *X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. at 73 n.3.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that §875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten.
