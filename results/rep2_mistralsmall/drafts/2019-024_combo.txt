**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT § 1113(2) REQUIRES ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE OF BOTH THE ALLEGED BREACH AND ITS IMPRUDENCE.

The text of § 1113(2) bars an ERISA action “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit properly interpreted this to mean knowledge of the underlying acts and that those acts were imprudent. *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1342 (9th Cir. 1994) (“actual knowledge of the breach” requires knowledge of the acts and their imprudence). This construction is consistent with the common-law rule that a plaintiff must know both the facts constituting the wrong and their legal significance. *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 140 (1879) (“knowledge of the facts constituting the fraud, and of their fraudulent character”); *Fowler v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 141 F. 789, 792 (8th Cir. 1905) (same).

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED BY HOLDING THAT DISCLOSURE ALONE SATISFIES THE “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” REQUIREMENT.

The district court treated disclosure of investment decisions as sufficient to impute actual knowledge, but § 1113(2) demands subjective awareness, not constructive notice. *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (actual knowledge requires “awareness of the facts constituting the breach”). This Court has long rejected the notion that a party’s failure to read disclosed information equates to knowledge. *Union Pac. Ry. Co. v. Chicago, R.I. & Pac. Ry. Co.*, 163 U.S. 564, 582 (1896) (“knowledge is not to be imputed from the mere opportunity to acquire it”); *In re New York, N.H. & H.R.R. Co.*, 139 F. 350, 353 (2d Cir. 1905) (same).

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING IS SUPPORTED BY THE PRE-1987 CONSTRUCTIVE KNOWLEDGE SCHEME, WHICH CONGRESS REPEALED ONLY TO RAISE THE BAR FOR PLAINTIFFS.

Before 1987, § 1113 included a constructive knowledge provision, which Congress repealed, leaving only actual knowledge. *Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987*, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b). This change reflects Congress’s intent to require subjective awareness, not merely access to information. *H.R. Rep. No. 99-453, at 10 (1985)* (actual knowledge standard “ensures that the limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff is actually aware of the breach”).

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION PREVENTS MANIPULATION OF THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS BY FIDUCIARIES.

Allowing fiduciaries to trigger the limitations period through disclosure alone would incentivize burying participants in irrelevant data. *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (rejecting “continuing breach” theory to prevent such manipulation). This Court has long disfavored rules that permit parties to control limitations periods through their own actions. *Chase v. Curtis*, 113 U.S. 416, 426 (1885) (“a party cannot, by his own laches or concealment, extend the period of limitation”).

V. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING IS CONSISTENT WITH GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF LIMITATIONS LAW.

Courts uniformly require actual, not constructive, knowledge to trigger limitations periods. *Flanagan v. Gen. Motors Acceptance Corp.*, 141 F.2d 109, 111 (7th Cir. 1944) (“actual knowledge of the fraud”); *United States v. Diamond Coal Co.*, 258 F. 557, 560 (6th Cir. 1919) (same). This principle applies with equal force in ERISA cases. *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991 (9th Cir. 1985) (actual knowledge of the transaction, not its legal consequences).

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 1113(2) requires actual knowledge of both the breach and its imprudence. The district court erred by treating disclosure as sufficient. This Court should affirm.
