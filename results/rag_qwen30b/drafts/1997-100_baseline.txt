**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *U.S. Const., amend. V*. This protection is not limited to the guilt-innocence phase of a criminal trial; it extends to any proceeding that, in substance and effect, constitutes a second prosecution for the same offense. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The question before this Court is whether the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding—specifically, a determination under California’s Three Strikes law that a defendant has suffered a prior serious felony conviction.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings—contradicts settled precedent and undermines the constitutional protection against multiple prosecutions. The Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies not only to trials on the underlying offense but also to sentencing determinations that are “in all relevant respects” like a trial on guilt or innocence. *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438 (1981). In *Bullington*, the Court held that a defendant who had received a life sentence in a capital sentencing proceeding could not be retried for the death penalty after the conviction was reversed, because the sentencing proceeding “in all relevant respects was like the immediately preceding trial on the issue of guilt or innocence.” Id. The Court emphasized that the sentencing phase in a capital case “is not a mere formality” but a “critical stage” of the criminal process, where the defendant is entitled to the same protections as at trial. Id. at 439.

The reasoning of *Bullington* is not confined to capital cases. The Court has repeatedly affirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a sentencing proceeding involves the same essential characteristics as a trial on guilt or innocence. In *Freddie Davis v. Georgia*, 459 U.S. 891 (1982), the Court acknowledged that a determination that an aggravating circumstance does not apply is “analogous to a determination that the accused is not guilty of an offense,” and thus, the Double Jeopardy Clause should protect a defendant from being resentence to death on the basis of an aggravating circumstance previously found inapplicable. Id. The Court has also held that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial when a defendant is acquitted of a sentencing enhancement, even in a noncapital case. *Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33, 39 (1988). In *Lockhart*, the Court rejected the argument that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to sentencing proceedings, stating that “the Double Jeopardy Clause does not preclude retrial” only when the evidence at the first trial would have been sufficient to sustain a guilty verdict. Id. at 39. The Court emphasized that the Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), and that the “same offense” analysis must be applied with fidelity to the substance of the proceeding.

Here, the prior conviction allegation was tried before a jury, the prosecution bore the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the trial court made a factual determination that the prior conviction was a “serious felony” under the Three Strikes law. The Court of Appeal reversed that finding due to insufficient evidence. The California Supreme Court, however, held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial. This conclusion is irreconcilable with *Bullington* and *Lockhart*. The sentencing proceeding in this case was not a mere administrative function; it was a full adversarial hearing, complete with jury trial, burden of proof, and evidentiary rules. The outcome—whether the defendant receives a doubled sentence—has profound consequences, including a significant increase in prison time. To hold that such a proceeding is not subject to double jeopardy protection is to treat it as a mere formality, contrary to the Court’s repeated insistence that sentencing is a “critical stage” of the criminal process.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection is misplaced. *Williams* upheld a sentencing judge’s reliance on untested, out-of-court information, but it did not address the issue of retrial after an acquittal on a sentencing enhancement. The Court in *Williams* recognized that sentencing is not a trial on guilt or innocence, but it did not suggest that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable when a defendant is subjected to a full trial on a sentencing issue. Indeed, the Court has since reaffirmed that the Clause applies to sentencing determinations when they are “in all relevant respects” like a trial. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438.

The dissent’s argument that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply because the proceeding is “noncapital” is unpersuasive. The Clause does not turn on the severity of the penalty; it turns on the nature of the proceeding. As the Court stated in *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), the reasoning in *Bullington* and *Rumsey* was based on the “unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding,” but the Court did not hold that the Clause is inapplicable in noncapital cases. The Court has never drawn such a rigid distinction. In *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985), the Court rejected the argument that double jeopardy bars resentencing after an appeal by the Commonwealth, but it did so on the ground that the sentencing proceeding in a noncapital case does not have the “constitutional finality” of a conviction. Id. at 34. That reasoning does not support the conclusion that the Clause is inapplicable at all—it only means that the finality of a sentencing determination is not absolute. But when a defendant is acquitted of a sentencing enhancement, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial, regardless of the penalty involved.
