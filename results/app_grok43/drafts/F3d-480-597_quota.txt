I. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish the Requisite Mens Rea That the Weapon Was a Machinegun.

The government was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm “had the characteristics that brought it within the scope of” the machinegun prohibition. Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994). “A felony conviction imposes a stigma” that demands this scienter element even when the statute is silent. Id. at 614. The same principle applies to regulatory offenses carrying substantial penalties. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 256 (1952); United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc., 513 U.S. 64, 72 (1994). Because the prosecution introduced no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle was capable of automatic fire, the conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) cannot stand. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979); In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970).

II. The School-Zone Statute Is Unconstitutionally Vague.

Section 922(q)(2)(A) fails to specify the method for measuring the 1,000-foot radius, leaving ordinary citizens without fair notice of the conduct prohibited. Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). A criminal statute is void for vagueness if it “fails to provide a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited.” Grayned v. City of Rockford, 408 U.S. 104, 108 (1972). The statute’s silence on measurement—whether straight-line, street route, or from property line to property line—renders it unconstitutional on its face. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 561 (1995) (recognizing the need for clear geographic boundaries in federal gun laws).

III. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove a School Zone.

Even if the statute were valid, the government failed to establish that the firearm was possessed within 1,000 feet of a school. Jackson, 443 U.S. at 319. No witness testified to the precise measurement methodology or the location of the nearest school boundary. The same due-process requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt applies to every element. Winship, 397 U.S. at 364.

IV. The District Court Erred in Instructing the Jury on the Machinegun Charge.

The court’s instruction omitted the knowledge element required by Staples. Because the error was preserved, reversal is required. The instruction permitted conviction without proof that Nieves-Castaño knew the weapon’s automatic-fire capability, contrary to the mens rea mandated for this felony offense. X-Citement Video, 513 U.S. at 72; Morissette, 342 U.S. at 263.
