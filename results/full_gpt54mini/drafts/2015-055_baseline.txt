## Argument

### I. Johnson announced a new substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review.

The question here is controlled by the framework for retroactivity announced in **Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989)**, and refined in **Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348 (2004)**. Under **Teague**, a new rule applies retroactively on collateral review if it is substantive rather than procedural. **Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 351-52 (2004)**. Substantive rules “alter the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” and they apply retroactively because “they necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal” or is subjected to a punishment the law cannot impose. **Id. at 351-52**; **Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 198-99 (2016)**.

**Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)** is substantive. Johnson held that the ACCA residual clause is unconstitutionally vague and therefore cannot be used to increase a defendant’s mandatory minimum sentence. **Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591, 597, 606 (2015)**. That decision did not regulate the manner of determining a sentence; it “changed the substantive reach” of the ACCA by “alter[ing] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” **Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353**. After Johnson, a defendant’s prior conviction counts as a violent felony only if it satisfies the elements clause or another valid ACCA provision. **Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606**. Thus, Johnson narrowed the universe of defendants subject to the ACCA’s 15-year minimum and removed a category of prior convictions from the statute’s reach. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. **Schriro, 542 U.S. at 351-52**; **Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 198-99**.

This Court’s retroactivity precedents confirm the point. In **Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614 (1998)**, the Court held that a decision narrowing the scope of a federal criminal statute applies retroactively because it interprets the statute’s meaning and therefore establishes that certain conduct was never covered. **Id. at 620-21**. Likewise, in **Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120 (2016)**, the Court held that Johnson is retroactive because “by striking down the residual clause as void for vagueness, Johnson changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act.” **Id. at 129**. The Court explained that Johnson “affected the reach of the underlying statute rather than the judicial procedures by which the statute is applied.” **Id. at 131**. That reasoning is dispositive here.

### II. The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary ruling cannot stand after Johnson and Welch.

The Eleventh Circuit’s affirmance rested on the assumption that Welch’s Florida robbery conviction qualified under the ACCA’s residual clause or otherwise remained valid even if Johnson were applied. But Johnson invalidated the residual clause itself. **Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606**. Once that clause is gone, only convictions falling within the ACCA’s remaining definitions may support the enhancement. **Id.** Because Welch’s sentence was imposed under the ACCA’s now-invalid residual clause, his enhanced sentence exceeds the lawful statutory range unless another ACCA provision independently applies. **Welch, 578 U.S. at 129-30**.

The government cannot avoid retroactivity by recasting Johnson as procedural. A rule is procedural only when it “regulate[s] only the manner of determining the defendant’s culpability.” **Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353**. Johnson did the opposite: it removed an entire clause from the ACCA’s substantive definition of “violent felony.” **Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606**. When a rule “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” it is substantive and retroactive. **Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353**.

Nor does the fact that Welch seeks collateral relief change the analysis. Retroactivity doctrine exists precisely to decide whether a new rule may be invoked on habeas or § 2255 review. **Teague, 489 U.S. at 310**; **Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 198-99**. Because Johnson announced a substantive rule, Welch may rely on it in this proceeding. **Welch, 578 U.S. at 130-31**.

### III. Relief is required.

Welch’s ACCA sentence depends on a legal basis Johnson invalidated. A defendant “convicted under an unconstitutional law” is entitled to collateral relief when the rule invalidating the law is substantive and retroactive. **Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 200-01**. That is this case. Johnson struck down the residual clause; Welch was sentenced as an armed career criminal under that clause’s reach; and under **Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120 (2016)**, the rule applies retroactively. The judgment should therefore be reversed and the case remanded for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.
