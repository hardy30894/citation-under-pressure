**Argument**

The government’s position is foreclosed by the text of the Internal Revenue Code and by this Court’s cases requiring assessment of the particular taxpayer the government seeks to pursue. Section 6502(a) extends the collection period for “such tax” only when “the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made” within the limitations period, and then only for “10 years after the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). An assessment is the recording of “the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The Code separately defines “taxpayer” to mean “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes an “individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Under those provisions, the partnership and each individual partner are separate taxpayers. A timely assessment against one does not automatically become an assessment against the others.

That is precisely how the Ninth Circuit read the statute below. The court explained that “the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified ‘taxpayer,’” and that “[t]he assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations only with respect to the Partnership, but it left unaltered the limitations period applicable to Debtors.” United States v. Galletti, 310 F.3d 979, 984-85 (9th Cir. 2002). That reading follows the ordinary meaning of “assessment” and the statutory requirement that the assessment identify the taxpayer whose liability is recorded. 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1; 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 6502(a), 7701(a)(1), (14).

The government’s contrary theory also conflicts with this Court’s precedent. In Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), the Court did not hold that a partnership assessment alone authorizes collection from individual partners. To the contrary, the decision merely recognized that the partner was “personally liable for the debts and liabilities of the partnership.” Id. at 910. The district court opinion in Young, which this Court affirmed, stated that individual naming in the assessment was unnecessary only because the tax had already been paid; it did not authorize direct collection without either an individual assessment or a judgment. Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050, 76,054 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff’d, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). The Ninth Circuit correctly treated Young as limited to substantive liability, not procedural collectability. Galletti, 310 F.3d at 985-87.

United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961), points in the same direction. There, the Court sustained the IRS’s lien not because a partnership assessment automatically reached the partner, but because the lien was valid on other grounds. Id. at 462-63. The district court opinion the Ninth Circuit discussed in Galletti made the critical point: absent an assessment against the individual, “the Government does not have any lien.” Coson v. United States, 169 F. Supp. 671, 676 (S.D. Cal. 1958), aff’d on other grounds, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). That is inconsistent with the government’s claim that one assessment against the partnership suffices for all partners.

The government’s reliance on partnership-law liability does not change the result. Even if state law makes partners jointly and severally liable, that merely establishes substantive liability; it does not eliminate the federal procedures the IRS must follow to collect. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that California law does not permit automatic execution against a partner’s assets: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” Galletti, 310 F.3d at 987 (quoting Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)). Under California law, the creditor must first obtain a judgment against the partner. Id. Because the IRS obtained no such judgment, and because the period for making a timely individual assessment has expired, the claims are unenforceable against Respondents within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

This Court’s cases on tax collection likewise distinguish between the existence of liability and the government’s ability to enforce it. In United States v. Galletti, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that the partnership assessment extended the statute only as to the partnership, not the individual partners. 310 F.3d at 988. That conclusion accords with the Code’s structure and with the settled principle that limitations rules in the tax laws are to be enforced as written. See Commissioner v. Lundy, 516 U.S. 235, 250-51 (1996); United States v. Brockamp, 519 U.S. 347, 350-52 (1997).

The Court should affirm. The government chose to assess the partnership, not the individual partners. That choice extended the collection period only for the assessed taxpayer. It did not authorize the IRS, years later, to collect from unassessed partners after the limitations period for assessing them had run.
