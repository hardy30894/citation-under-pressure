**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett’s claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). The court improperly concluded that Burnett failed to provide sufficient notice of his medical condition, thereby forfeiting his rights under both statutes. This conclusion misapplies the law and ignores the well-established principle that an employee need not use medical jargon or disclose a formal diagnosis to trigger an employer’s obligations under the FMLA and ADA. As the Supreme Court has made clear, “the employee’s duty to provide notice is satisfied when the employer has ‘reason to know’ of the employee’s medical condition and its potential impact on work.” *Barnes v. Board of Regents of the University of Wisconsin System*, 438 F.3d 753, 758 (7th Cir. 2006) (citing *Barnes v. City of Chicago*, 316 F.3d 631, 636 (7th Cir. 2003)).

**I. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice Under the FMLA**

Under the FMLA, an employee must provide “sufficient notice” of a need for leave due to a serious health condition. 29 U.S.C. § 2612(a)(1)(D). The Seventh Circuit has held that “notice is sufficient if the employer has reason to know that the employee is suffering from a serious health condition.” *Barnes*, 438 F.3d at 758. This standard is not met by a rigid, technical reading of the employee’s statements but by an inquiry into whether the employer could reasonably understand that a medical condition was impairing the employee’s ability to work.

Burnett repeatedly communicated his health concerns to his supervisor, Sergio Polo, in a manner that a reasonable employer would interpret as a request for medical leave. In October 2003, Burnett informed Polo that he had a “weak bladder” and was seeking medical evaluation. This statement, while not using clinical terminology, conveyed a physical impairment affecting work performance—specifically, the need for frequent restroom access, which was directly relevant to his job duties involving lifting and moving heavy objects. The employer’s failure to investigate or inquire further does not excuse its failure to recognize the potential seriousness of the condition.

By December 11, 2003, Burnett had undergone a physical examination and learned of elevated PSA and cholesterol levels. He presented Polo with a doctor’s order for blood testing and explicitly stated he was “getting sick or was sick.” He also disclosed his upcoming appointments with a urologist. These disclosures, coupled with his documented absences, provided more than sufficient notice under *Barnes*. The Seventh Circuit has held that “an employee need not use the precise language of the statute to trigger FMLA protections.” *Barnes*, 438 F.3d at 759. Here, Burnett’s statements were clear, repeated, and directly tied to medical appointments and health concerns.

Moreover, the employer’s own actions confirm it had reason to know of a serious health condition. On December 16, Polo sent an email authorizing sick leave for January 6 and 8, 2004, to attend doctor’s appointments. This acknowledgment by the employer itself demonstrates that it understood Burnett’s absences were medically related. *See* *Henderson v. City of Chicago*, 457 F.3d 650, 655 (7th Cir. 2006) (employer’s authorization of leave based on medical need constitutes recognition of a serious health condition under the FMLA).

**II. Burnett’s Condition Was a Disability Under the ADA**

The ADA protects employees with disabilities, defined as “a physical or mental impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities.” 42 U.S.C. § 12102(1)(A). The Seventh Circuit has held that “a condition that may lead to a serious illness, such as cancer, can be a disability even if the employee is not currently disabled.” *Barnes*, 438 F.3d at 758. In *Henderson*, the court emphasized that “an employee need not be diagnosed with a disability to be protected under the ADA if the employer knows or should know that the employee has a condition that substantially limits a major life activity.” 457 F.3d at 655.

Burnett’s elevated PSA level—indicative of possible prostate cancer—was a known risk factor for a serious, life-threatening illness. In January 2004, Burnett informed Polo that he was scheduled for a prostate biopsy and provided a document explaining that the procedure was indicated for “an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA).” This document, though not delivered to Polo, was provided to another supervisor, Mitch Hehr, who was under a duty to forward it. The failure of Hehr to do so does not absolve the employer of its duty to act on the information it received. *See* *Barnes*, 438 F.3d at 759 (employer cannot avoid liability by failing to disseminate information within its own ranks).

Burnett’s statements about his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer and his own fear of illness were not mere speculation. They were reasonable inferences based on medical facts. The employer’s failure to investigate or accommodate Burnett’s condition, despite repeated disclosures, constitutes disability discrimination under the ADA. *See* *Barnes*, 438 F.3d at 758 (employer’s failure to accommodate a known medical condition, even without formal diagnosis, can support a claim under the ADA).

**III. The Employer’s Retaliation and Termination Were Unlawful**

Burnett’s termination—immediately following a reprimand and a statement that he was a “loose cannon”—was not a neutral personnel action but a retaliatory response to his protected activity. The Seventh Circuit has held that “an employer’s adverse action taken shortly after an employee asserts a right under the FMLA or ADA may be viewed as retaliatory.” *Henderson*, 457 F.3d at 656. Here, the reprimand and suspension occurred just days after Burnett disclosed his medical condition and sought a transfer, which he believed was necessary due to his health. The employer’s refusal to accommodate a known medical condition, followed by disciplinary action, constitutes unlawful retaliation.
