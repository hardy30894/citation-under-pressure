ARGUMENT

The Railroad Retirement Act provides for judicial review of final Board decisions. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A Board order denying reopening constitutes such a final decision because it terminates the administrative process and fixes rights with respect to the prior claim. Dismuke v. United States, 297 U.S. 167, 172 (1936) (Tucker Act jurisdiction extends to claims “founded upon . . . any” right created by federal statute). 

This Court has long recognized that administrative finality encompasses refusals to reopen prior determinations. Cheng Fan Kwok v. Immigration & Naturalization Service, 392 U.S. 206, 210 (1968) (review procedures apply to “all determinations made during and incident to the administrative proceeding”); SEC v. Louisiana Pub. Serv. Comm’n, 353 U.S. 368, 372 (1957) (order denying petition to reopen is itself reviewable). The same principle governs here. Robertson v. Chambers, 341 U.S. 37, 39 (1951) (scope of review includes all materials considered by the agency in reaching its decision).

Statutory schemes for railroad benefits have consistently been treated as subject to judicial oversight once administrative remedies are exhausted. Pennsylvania Railroad v. Day, 360 U.S. 548, 552 (1959) (Railway Labor Act creates “broad framework” for adjustment of claims); Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad, 295 U.S. 330, 344 (1935) (Act establishes compulsory retirement and annuity system enforceable through ordinary judicial processes). Denials of reopening fall within this framework because they conclusively resolve whether new evidence or error warrants further consideration. Wragg v. Federal Land Bank of New Orleans, 317 U.S. 325, 328 (1943) (dismissal of petition for composition is final and appealable).

The Board’s regulations fixing a four-year reopening period do not strip courts of jurisdiction to review whether that period was lawfully applied. Dodge v. Board of Education of Chicago, 302 U.S. 74, 78 (1937) (legislative changes to annuity rights remain subject to judicial construction); Joint Industry Board of Electrical Industry v. United States, 391 U.S. 224, 228 (1968) (priority claims under federal statutes are adjudicated by courts applying statutory text). 

Finally, the rule of orderliness cannot insulate an erroneous jurisdictional holding from correction when the underlying statute confers review. International Union of Mine, Mill & Smelter Workers v. Eagle-Picher Mining & Smelting Co., 325 U.S. 335, 340 (1945) (courts retain authority to enforce and interpret administrative decrees). The Fifth Circuit therefore possessed jurisdiction to review the Board’s refusal to reopen.
