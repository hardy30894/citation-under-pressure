# ARGUMENT

The Massachusetts buffer zone law is a valid content-neutral time-place-manner regulation that satisfies the First Amendment. The decision below should be affirmed, and this Court’s holding in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), should remain undisturbed.

## I. The Act Is a Content-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation

The Act restricts speech only within a 35-foot radius of clinic entrances, exits, and driveways. It does not regulate based on the message, viewpoint, or subject matter of the speech. The statute applies equally to all persons—pro-life, pro-choice, or neutral. Content-neutrality is the touchstone for intermediate scrutiny under the time-place-manner framework. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). As this Court held in *Hill*, a statute that “places no restrictions on—and does not in any way bar—any person from communicating messages” is content-neutral. 530 U.S. at 720. The Massachusetts Act, like Colorado’s, regulates only the *location* of speech, not its content.

The First Circuit correctly found that the Act is “content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral, and a valid time-place-manner regulation.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176 (1st Cir. 2009). This Court has consistently upheld such restrictions on public sidewalks when they are justified by significant governmental interests and are narrowly tailored. *See, e.g., Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994) (upholding 36-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances).

## II. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored To Serve Significant Governmental Interests

Massachusetts has compelling interests in protecting public safety, ensuring access to healthcare facilities, and preserving the free flow of pedestrian traffic. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. The Act specifically targets the problem of “congestion, obstruction, and harassment” outside clinics. *Id.* at 727–28. The 35-foot buffer zone is a measured response, far less restrictive than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill*.

The Act is narrowly tailored because it does not burden substantially more speech than necessary. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. It contains four express exemptions for persons entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and pedestrians using the sidewalk to reach other destinations. *See* Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(b). These exemptions ensure that the Act does not freeze all speech or activity on the sidewalk. Moreover, the Act leaves petitioners free to engage in “oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, displays of signage and symbols, wearing of evocative garments and costumes, and prayer alone or in groups.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F. Supp. 2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010).

The First Circuit properly rejected petitioners’ claim that the Act is underinclusive because it exempts clinic employees. Exemptions for those acting within the scope of their employment do not render a law viewpoint-based. *See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 375 (1997) (upholding similar exemption for clinic employees). And the Act’s failure to regulate all offensive speech does not undermine its validity. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 727.

## III. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels of Communication

Petitioners retain ample means to communicate their message. They may stand anywhere outside the 35-foot zone, approach patients on the sidewalk beyond that distance, distribute literature, display signs, and engage in prayer or counseling. The First Circuit found that “adequate communicative channels remain available to the plaintiffs, including oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d at 181. This Court has repeatedly held that a regulation does not fail the ample-alternatives test simply because it “reduces the audience” for a message. *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 295 (1984); *see also Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 484 (1988) (upholding residential picketing ban because protesters could march on sidewalks and distribute literature).

Petitioners’ claim that they cannot achieve “close personal contact” or “normal conversational distance” is insufficient to invalidate the Act. The First Amendment does not guarantee a right to a particular level of communicative effectiveness. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 803. Indeed, the Act still permits petitioners to speak to patients who are willing to stop and converse outside the zone. The record shows that petitioners have, in fact, continued to engage in successful counseling, convincing “approximately eighty women” to refrain from abortion. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213 (D. Mass. 2012).

## IV. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled

This Court’s decision in *Hill* is directly on point. *Hill* upheld a Colorado statute that created a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facilities, prohibiting “knowingly approaches” within eight feet of another person without consent. 530 U.S. at 707. The Court applied intermediate scrutiny and found that the statute advanced the “significant and legitimate” interests of protecting patients and ensuring access. *Id.* at 715. The Massachusetts Act is even less restrictive: it creates a smaller 35-foot zone, does not require a “knowing approach” trigger, and exempts more categories of speakers.

Petitioners invite this Court to overrule *Hill*, but they offer no basis for doing so. The principles underlying *Hill*—that states may regulate conduct on public sidewalks around healthcare facilities in a content-neutral manner—remain sound. *Hill* has been cited with approval in subsequent cases. *See, e.g., Virginia v. Hicks*, 539 U.S. 113, 123 (2003) (citing *Hill* for the proposition that “a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation” may restrict access to public forums). Overruling *Hill* would destabilize the settled expectations of states that have relied on its holding to protect public safety and access to healthcare.

The Act is a modest, content-neutral regulation that leaves ample room for protected speech. The First Circuit correctly upheld it. The judgment should be affirmed.
