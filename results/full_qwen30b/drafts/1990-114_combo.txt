**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA) defines “employee” to exclude “any person ... appointed by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question here is whether Missouri’s appointed state court judges fall within this exception. The answer is yes, and the conclusion is compelled by the settled understanding of the judiciary’s role in our constitutional system, as confirmed by the Supreme Court’s decisions long before the ADEA’s enactment.

The judiciary is not a mere administrative arm of government; it is a co-equal branch entrusted with the final interpretation of law and the protection of constitutional rights. As the Court held in *United States v. Will*, 389 U.S. 90, 95 (1967), “the judicial power of the United States is vested in one Supreme Court and in such inferior courts as Congress may from time to time ordain and establish.” This constitutional structure reflects a fundamental principle: the judiciary is not subordinate to the executive or legislative branches, but is an independent institution with the authority to interpret and apply the law. The power to interpret the Constitution and to resolve disputes between citizens and the government is inherently a policymaking function, for it shapes the legal landscape in which all other branches operate.

This understanding was firmly established in *Marbury v. Madison*, 1 Cranch 137, 177 (1803), where the Court declared that “it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.” The Court did not limit this power to cases involving “mechanical” application of settled rules. On the contrary, *Marbury* recognized that judicial interpretation necessarily involves the exercise of judgment and discretion—hallmarks of policymaking. The Court’s role is not to apply law in a vacuum, but to determine its meaning in light of constitutional principles, statutory text, and precedent. In *Cohens v. Virginia*, 6 Wheat. 264, 399 (1821), the Court affirmed its authority to review state court decisions when federal questions are involved, stating that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “the judicial power of the United States extends to all cases arising under the Constitution.” This power to interpret and enforce the Constitution is not a clerical function; it is the very essence of policymaking.

The same principle applies to state judges. In *Barron v. Baltimore*, 7 Pet. 243, 247 (1833), the Court held that the Bill of Rights did not apply to the states, but it did so only after a careful examination of the constitutional text and structure. The decision was not a mechanical application of a rule, but a policy judgment about the scope of federal authority. Similarly, in *Caldwell v. Texas*, 108 U.S. 105, 108 (1883), the Court emphasized that “the courts of the United States are not mere tribunals for the settlement of private controversies, but are also the guardians of the Constitution.” This role requires judges to make decisions that have broad societal consequences—decisions that affect property rights, personal liberty, and the balance of power among the branches of government.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that judicial decisions are not merely adjudicative but also legislative in effect. In *United States v. Hudson*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 34 (1812), the Court stated that “the power to make laws is not exclusively legislative,” and that “the courts, in the exercise of their jurisdiction, may declare what the law is.” This power to declare the law is not limited to cases of clear statutory or constitutional text; it extends to cases where the law is ambiguous or silent. In such cases, the judge must fill the “interstices of authority,” as the Court has recognized in later decisions. But even before the ADEA, the Court understood that judicial interpretation is a form of policymaking.

The legislative history of the ADEA’s “policymaking level” exception confirms that Congress intended to exclude those whose decisions have broad legal and societal impact. The House Report on H.R. 1746, which became the ADEA, stated that the exception was meant to apply to “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” H.R. Rep. No. 90-253, 90th Cong., 1st Sess. 10 (1967). The term “agencies” was not limited to executive departments. In *United States v. New York Central R. Co.*, 303 U.S. 239, 248 (1938), the Court held that “the term ‘agency’ includes any department, independent commission, or other body created by law.” The judiciary, as a constitutionally established branch of government, is no less an “agency” than any executive department.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that judges are not mere employees but independent officers of the state. In *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 506, 514 (1869), the Court stated that “the judiciary is a coordinate branch of the government,” and that “the judges are not the mere agents of the executive.” In *United States v. Klein*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 128, 147 (1871), the Court emphasized that “the judicial power is not a delegated power, but a coordinate and independent power.” These decisions establish that judges are not subordinate to the executive and are not mere “employees” in the ordinary sense.
