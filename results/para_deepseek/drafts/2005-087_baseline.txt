**ARGUMENT**

**A Defendant Has No Fourteenth Amendment Due Process Right to Introduce Expert Mental-State Evidence Outside the Framework of an Insanity Defense When State Law Properly Channels Such Evidence to That Defense.**

The Due Process Clause guarantees a criminal defendant a fair opportunity to present a defense. *See Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 294 (1973). But that right is not absolute; it yields to legitimate state evidentiary rules that serve important interests. *See Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42 (1996) (plurality opinion). Arizona’s rule confining expert evidence of mental disease or defect to the insanity defense is precisely such a legitimate rule.

**I. Arizona’s Evidentiary Rule Is a Permissible Exercise of State Authority to Define the Elements of Crimes and the Scope of Defenses.**

States have broad latitude to define the mental states required for criminal liability. *See Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977). Arizona has chosen to treat evidence of mental disease or defect as relevant only to the insanity defense, not to negate *mens rea*. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-502(A). This choice reflects a considered judgment that mental illness, while potentially excusing culpability entirely, should not fragment the *mens rea* inquiry. This Court has upheld similar restrictions. *See Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946) (holding that Congress may limit the use of mental-condition evidence to the insanity defense). Arizona’s rule is no different.

**II. The Rule Does Not Violate Due Process Because It Does Not Eliminate the State’s Burden to Prove *Mens Rea* Beyond a Reasonable Doubt.**

Clark argues that the rule prevented him from rebutting the prosecution’s evidence of intent. But the prosecution still bore the burden of proving every element, including intent, beyond a reasonable doubt. *See In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Clark was free to introduce non-expert evidence—such as testimony about his bizarre behavior—to argue that he lacked intent. What he could not do was introduce expert testimony about his schizophrenia to negate *mens rea* directly. That limitation does not shift the burden of proof or relieve the State of its obligation. *See Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 206-07 (upholding a state’s decision to place the burden of proving an affirmative defense on the defendant).

**III. The Rule Serves Important State Interests in Avoiding Confusion and Preserving the Integrity of the Insanity Defense.**

Allowing expert mental-state evidence outside the insanity framework would invite jury confusion and undermine the coherence of the insanity defense. *See Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 770-71 (2006) (upholding Arizona’s rule). The insanity defense already provides a full and fair opportunity to present such evidence. Clark availed himself of that opportunity; the jury simply rejected his claim. Due process does not require a second bite at the apple.

**IV. Clark’s Reliance on *Ake v. Oklahoma* Is Misplaced.**

In *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 83 (1985), this Court held that a defendant is entitled to psychiatric assistance when his mental state is a significant factor at trial. But *Ake* addressed access to an expert, not the admissibility of expert testimony. Arizona provided Clark with expert assistance; it merely limited how that evidence could be used. That limitation does not offend due process.

**Conclusion**

Arizona’s evidentiary rule is a rational and constitutional means of channeling mental-condition evidence to the insanity defense. The judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
