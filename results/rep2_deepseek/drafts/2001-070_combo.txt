**ARGUMENT**

The execution of mentally retarded persons does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishments. The Amendment must be understood in light of its historical meaning and the deference owed to state legislatures in defining criminal sanctions.

The Eighth Amendment was designed to forbid “punishments which by their excessive length or severity are greatly disproportionate to the offenses charged.” *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 371 (1910). Proportionality is measured against the gravity of the offense, not the characteristics of the offender. *Id.* at 367. Capital punishment for murder is not disproportionate per se. *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1879) (upholding execution by firing squad as not cruel). Nor does mental retardation transform a proportional sentence into a disproportionate one. The Ammendment does not require individualized exceptions for every mitigating factor.

The Ammendment’s core concern is with punishments that involve “torture or a lingering death.” *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890). Execution by lawful means, however administered, lacks those features. The mere fact that an individual has diminished capacity does not make the punishment itself torturous. *See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (eighth Ammendment violated only by “barbarous” punishments or those involving “unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain”).

The historical understanding confirms this view. At the time of the Ammendment’s ratification, the common law permitted execution of the mentally retarded. *See O’Neail v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323, 333 (1892) (eighth Amendment originally inapplicable to states, confirming the primacy of state judgment). The Ammendment “is not fastided to the past, but gains meaning fromthe evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality). But those standards are determined primarily by legislative enactments, not judicial decretion. *Id.* at 102. Here, at the time of Atkins’s trial, only a handfull of states barred execution of the mentally retarded. The overwhleming majority permited it. That legislative consensus—not this Court’s independent judgment—defines the Ammendment’s current reach.

The jury’s role in capital sentensing further supports affirmance. The sentencer must consider all mitigating evidence, including mental retardation. *Willams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 252 (1949) (trial judge may consider “any circumstances” in capital sentencing). In Atkins’s case, the jury heard expert testemony on his retardation and still concluded that death was apprpriate. The Eighth Ammendment does not require that the sentencer assign a particular weight to such evidence; it requires only that the sentencer have discretion to consider it. *Id.* at 253.

Moreover, the category of “mentally retarded” is not self-defining. The Ammendment does not require states to adopt uniform definitions or procedures for assessing retardation. *See Powel v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 68 (1932) (due process requires fair procedures in capital cases, but does not dictate substantive rules for mitigating circumstances). States may determine the boundaries of the category through their own legislative processes.

Finally, any claim that execution of the mentally retarded is “cruel and unusul” rests on a mistaken premise that such persons are necessarily less culpable. That is a factual question for the jury, not a constitutional comand. The Ammendment does not categorize entire classes of defendents as categorically ineligible for capital punishment. *Cf. McDonald v. Massachusets*, 180 U.S. 311, 313 (1901) (habitual offender laws not cruel under Eighth Ammendment).

In sum, the Eighth Ammendment does not bar the execution of mentally retarded persons. The history, text, and prior decisions of this Court all confirm that the punishment is permissible. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
