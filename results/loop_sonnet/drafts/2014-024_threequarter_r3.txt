**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that "the contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement, not to dispense with it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) is not the kind of regulatory or public-welfare measure in which this Court has permitted Congress to dispense with proof of intent altogether, such as a statute penalizing the sale of narcotics without regard to the seller's knowledge of the buyer's status. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from that regulatory context; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has likewise construed statutes to require a heightened, specific-intent element where a purely objective reading would sweep in defendants who did not understand their conduct as unlawful. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), a plurality of this Court construed a federal civil-rights statute to require proof of a specific intent to violate a defined constitutional right, precisely so that the statute would not punish defendants for conduct they could not have appreciated as wrongful. *Id.* at 101–107. This Court has also cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to reach unwitting or merely careless conduct, particularly where fair notice to the defendant is at a premium. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952); *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952) (declining to extend a criminal statute beyond the conduct fairly covered by its words). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." Only narrow, historically unprotected categories of speech fall outside the First Amendment's shelter, and this Court has defined those categories with precision. In *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568 (1942), the Court explained that "[t]here are certain well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech, the prevention and punishment of which have never been thought to raise any Constitutional problem." *Id.* at 571–572. The Court further observed that "such utterances are no essential part of any exposition of ideas, and are of such slight social value as a step to truth that any benefit that may be derived from them is clearly outweighed by the social interest in order and morality." *Id.* at 572.

This Court's own application of the true-threat concept confirms that the inquiry cannot rest on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous or hyperbolic language, but must account for the character and apparent purpose of the speech itself. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), the Court held that a statement referring to the President in political debate did not constitute a threat within the meaning of a similar federal statute, notwithstanding that some listeners might have taken the words literally, because the statement amounted to a crude form of political hyperbole rather than a serious expression of intent to do harm. *Id.* at 708. The Court's focus was on the speaker's evident purpose and the context of the statement, not simply on whether an audience could have been alarmed.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression — dark humor, hyperbole, song lyrics, and venting — because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has recognized that "First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive." *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Where scienter requirements are absent from a law regulating expression, this Court has cautioned that such a law "tends to impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter." *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 153 (1959). Uncertainty about how one's words will later be judged inevitably deters a speaker from venturing near a forbidden zone that the Constitution does not actually require him to avoid. *See Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 97–98 (1940). And where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before criminal liability may be imposed. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense — punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. As set forth above, this Court has repeatedly cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice is at a premium. *Boyce Motor Lines*, 342 U.S. at 340; *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–107 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
