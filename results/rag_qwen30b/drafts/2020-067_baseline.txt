**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—is one of profound importance to the integrity of the criminal justice system. The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that it may not, even when reviewing a defendant’s claim that the prosecution failed to prove a critical element of the offense, is contrary to settled precedent and undermines the very purpose of the plain error doctrine. This Court should reverse.

Rule 52(b) permits appellate courts to correct “plain errors” that affect a defendant’s substantial rights, even if the error was not preserved below. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). The doctrine exists to preserve the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* at 736. To fulfill this function, appellate courts must be able to assess whether an error was truly “plain”—that is, clear or obvious under current law—and whether it affected the defendant’s substantial rights. *Id.* at 734. The inquiry into “substantial rights” necessarily includes an assessment of prejudice, which requires a court to consider the totality of the circumstances, including evidence not presented to the jury.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in limiting its plain error review to the trial record. In *United States v. Olano*, this Court emphasized that the plain error standard is “not a rigid rule,” but a “practical one,” designed to prevent “fundamental unfairness” in the administration of justice. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. The Court has repeatedly recognized that appellate courts may consider matters beyond the trial record when assessing whether an error affected substantial rights. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 60 (2002), the Court held that a reviewing court may consider “the entire record” in determining whether an error was prejudicial, including “evidence not presented to the jury.” The Court explained that “the plain error standard is not confined to the evidence presented at trial,” because “the question is not whether the error was harmless in the narrow sense of the trial record, but whether it affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 61.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary holding—based on a misreading of *Washington v. Recuenco*, 548 U.S. 212 (2006)—is flawed. In *Recuenco*, this Court held that a jury instruction error that failed to require the jury to find a deadly weapon was subject to harmless-error analysis, not structural error. *Id.* at 221. The Court did not hold that appellate courts may not consider evidence outside the record in assessing prejudice. To the contrary, *Recuenco* itself relied on *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1 (1999), which affirmed that “the failure to submit an element to the jury is subject to harmless-error analysis.” *Neder*, 527 U.S. at 8. The Court in *Neder* did not limit its analysis to the trial record, but considered the entire record to determine whether the error was harmless. *Id.* at 15–16.

The Eleventh Circuit’s restriction on appellate review is particularly dangerous in the context of *Rehaif v. United States*, 583 U.S. ___ (2019), which held that the government must prove that a defendant knew both that he possessed a firearm and that he was a felon at the time of possession. *Rehaif*, 583 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 5). The Court emphasized that “the knowledge requirement is an essential element of the offense.” *Id.* at ___ (slip op., at 6). In this case, the government did not prove, nor was the jury instructed to find, that Greer knew he was a felon. The Eleventh Circuit acknowledged this was plain error but concluded that Greer could not show prejudice because the record showed he had five prior convictions for aggravated burglary under Ohio law. This conclusion is legally unsound.

The fact that Greer had prior convictions does not establish that he knew he was a felon. *See* *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967) (holding that a prior conviction may not be used to enhance punishment if the defendant was not given notice of the prior conviction at trial). The government bears the burden of proving knowledge, and that burden cannot be satisfied by merely showing that a defendant has prior convictions. *See* *Rehaif*, 583 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 6). The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on Greer’s prior convictions—evidence not presented to the jury—demonstrates a fundamental misunderstanding of the *Rehaif* rule. The jury was not instructed on knowledge, and the government did not offer evidence of Greer’s actual knowledge. To conclude that Greer was not prejudiced based on evidence not before the jury is to ignore the very purpose of the jury trial.

The Eleventh Circuit’s approach also undermines the principle that “the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of judicial proceedings” must be preserved. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. If appellate courts are barred from considering evidence outside the record when assessing prejudice, then a defendant may be sentenced under a law that the government failed to prove—simply because the error was not raised at trial. That would be a grave injustice.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed. The Court should hold that federal appellate courts may consider matters outside the trial record in assessing whether plain error affected a defendant’s substantial rights, particularly when the error involves a failure to prove an essential element of the offense. The plain error doctrine exists not to protect procedural technicalities, but to ensure that justice is done. The Eleventh Circuit’s narrow reading of Rule 52(b) fails that test.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed.
