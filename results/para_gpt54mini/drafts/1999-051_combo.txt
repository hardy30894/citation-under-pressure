**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse the procedural default of another claim unless the prisoner first establishes a valid, nondefaulted Sixth Amendment claim in the manner that federal habeas doctrine requires. This Court has long held that federal habeas review respects state procedural rules and bars collateral review where the petitioner has defaulted in state court absent a legitimate showing of cause and prejudice. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438-39 (1963). But the premise of “cause” is not mere allegation; it is an actually viable constitutional excuse. *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986). A claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot supply that excuse unless the petitioner also satisfies the independent rules governing that claim’s default. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991).

The Sixth Circuit’s approach would collapse these doctrines. It treated the ineffective-assistance claim as automatically available to excuse the default of the sufficiency claim, even though the ineffective-assistance claim had itself been dismissed as untimely in state court. That is inconsistent with the Court’s settled understanding that “[a]ttorney error short of ineffective assistance of counsel does not constitute cause” and that cause must be something external to the defense. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488. An attorney’s constitutional ineffectiveness may qualify as cause, but only when the ineffectiveness claim itself has been properly presented and preserved or is excused in its own right. *Id.* at 488-89; *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-53.

This Court’s pre-1970 decisions on waiver and procedural regularity reinforce the point. Federal courts have always required litigants to present their objections in the prescribed manner before obtaining review. *Baldwin v. Traveling Men’s Assn.*, 283 U.S. 522, 524-25 (1931), held that a party cannot “lie in wait” and later raise a point not properly presented below. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 113 (1945), likewise emphasized the need for fair notice and orderly procedure. And where a federal claim is not timely or properly asserted, the Court has declined to excuse the default by invoking a different issue that was never preserved. *United States v. Mendenhall*, 446 U.S. 544, 551 (1980), reflects the same basic principle of issue preservation. The habeas doctrine is a specialized application of that familiar rule. *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 87; *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107, 129 (1982).

The Court should also reject the notion that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim can be bootstrapped into a cause argument without independent review. In *Massachusetts v. Hurley*, 195 U.S. 391, 402-03 (1904), the Court explained that a litigant must satisfy the conditions of the claim he invokes; one cannot obtain relief on an unestablished premise. Likewise, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), teaches that waiver doctrine turns on a knowing relinquishment of a right, not on a post hoc assertion. Habeas cause doctrine works the same way: the petitioner must show an actual constitutional defect and an actual excuse for failing to present it. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488; *Smith v. Murray*, 477 U.S. 527, 533-34 (1986).

Nor can respondent’s position be dismissed as formalism. The cause-and-prejudice rule exists to preserve comity, finality, and federalism. *Francis v. Henderson*, 425 U.S. 536, 538-39 (1976); *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 88-89. If a petitioner could rely on a procedurally defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse any other default, the state courts’ procedural rulings would be circumvented, and “the State’s interest in enforcing its procedural rules” would be defeated. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750. The Court should not permit that end-run.

Finally, no miscarriage-of-justice concern is present here. The narrow exception for actual innocence is reserved for extraordinary cases, not ordinary claims of legal insufficiency. *Sawyer v. Whitley*, 505 U.S. 333, 339-40 (1992); *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 496. Carpenter’s claim concerns the sufficiency of the evidence and alleged appellate error, not a showing that he is actually innocent. On this record, the procedural defaults remain intact.

For those reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
