**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires This Court to Construe §875(c) to Demand Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended His Communication as a Threat.**

Section 875(c) is silent as to the mental state required for conviction, but that silence does not authorize a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that the requirement of a culpable mental state is a foundational principle of Anglo-American criminal law, one so deeply rooted in our jurisprudence that Congress will not be presumed to have abandoned it without a clear statement to that effect. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the traditional mens rea requirement, not to dispense with it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here, because §875(c) does not fall within the narrow category of public-welfare offenses in which this Court has permitted Congress to dispense with scienter for regulatory violations involving inherently dangerous or heavily regulated conduct. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for "threat[s]" transmitted through ordinary speech is far removed from the regulatory context of *Balint*; it is a true crime carrying substantial felony penalties and stigma, the paradigm case for reading in a subjective intent element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260.

This Court has repeatedly construed statutes to require specific intent where a purely objective reading would create constitutional difficulty. In *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), a plurality of this Court construed the term "willfully" in a federal civil-rights statute to require a specific intent to violate a constitutional right, precisely so that defendants would not be punished for conduct they did not appreciate as unlawful. *Id.* at 101–104. Likewise, this Court has instructed that criminal statutes touching on protected liberty must be construed narrowly, resolving doubt against extending criminal liability by inference rather than by clear congressional command. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176 (1952). The same interpretive caution compels reading §875(c) to require that the defendant subjectively intend to communicate a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of subjective intent before speech may be punished as a "true threat." Only narrow, historically unprotected categories of speech fall outside the First Amendment's shelter. This Court has held, for example, that obscenity is "not within the area of constitutionally protected speech or press," *Roth v. United States*, 354 U.S. 476, 485 (1957), a holding that rests on a considered judgment that certain narrowly defined categories of expression contribute nothing of value to the marketplace of ideas. Such categories must be defined with precision, and the definition cannot rest on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous or hyperbolic language. This Court's own application of the true-threat concept confirms as much: in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), the Court held that the defendant's statement about the President did not constitute a threat because it amounted to political hyperbole rather than a serious expression of intent to do harm, even though a purely literal, objective reading of the words could have been understood by some listeners as menacing. *Id.* at 708. The Court's focus was on the character of the speech and the speaker's evident meaning, not simply on audience perception.

An objective standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression—dark humor, hyperbole, song lyrics, and venting—because speakers cannot know in advance how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has recognized that the First Amendment demands breathing room for such expression, and that statutes regulating speech must not be construed to sweep protected activity within their reach simply because some hearers might take alarm. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 432–433 (1963). Where a statute lacks a scienter requirement in a context touching on expression, this Court has cautioned that such an omission risks deterring protected speech by leaving speakers uncertain of the line between lawful and unlawful expression. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 153–154 (1959). Where advocacy or expression is at issue, this Court has likewise insisted on proof of intent before liability may be imposed for the words spoken. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective, Negligence-Based Standard Punishes the Merely Careless and Cannot Be Reconciled With Due Process.**

Finally, dispensing with subjective intent effectively converts §875(c) into a negligence offense—punishing defendants for failing to anticipate how their words might be received, rather than for what they meant to convey. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice to the citizen is at a premium. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952); *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101–104 (plurality opinion). The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statement to be understood as a threat.
