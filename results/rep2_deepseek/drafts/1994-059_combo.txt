**ARGUMENT**

The sole question presented is whether a State may superimpose additional qualifications for the United States Congress beyond those enumerated in the Constitution. It cannot. The Qualifications Clauses, U.S. Const. Art. I, § 2, cl. 2 (House) and Art. I, § 3, cl. 3 (Senate), establish the exclusive criteria for service—age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. Arkansas’s Amendment 73, by barring ballot access to incumbents who have served three House terms or two Senate terms, impermissibly adds a durational qualification. This Court has long recognized that the Constitution’s enumerated qualifications are fixed and may not be altered by the States.

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969), this Court held that “the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” The reasoning applies with equal force to States: because the Framers “intended that the qualifications for membership in Congress be fixed in the Constitution,” id. at 539, no other governmental body—state or federal—may add to them. That conclusion flows from the text and structure of Article I. The Constitution’s careful enumeration of qualifications, and its omission of any state role in defining them, is the “exclusive” source of eligibility. Id. at 547 & n.38.

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that “uniformity of qualifications is paramount, and individual states are not free to engraft variations.” That principle is ancient. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884), the Court declared that the right to vote for members of Congress “is a right guaranteed by the Constitution” and “derived from” the federal government, not the States. The States cannot condition that right on additional qualifications, for that would “defeat the purpose of the Constitution.” *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 315 (1941) (holding that the right to choose congressional representatives is a right “secured by the Constitution” and “subject to the protection of Congress”).

The Elections Clause, Art. I, § 4, cl. 1, does not rescue Amendment 73. That clause grants States authority to regulate the “Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections” but cannot be used to “change the qualifications.” *Smiley v. Holm*, 285 U.S. 355, 366 (1932) (State’s power over “manner” is procedural, not substantive). A ballot-access restriction that operates as a durational bar is a substantive qualification, not a mere procedural regulation. *See also Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 384 (1879) (state laws governing congressional elections are “subject to the control of Congress” and cannot “contravene” federal constitutional requirements).

The Framers’ intent is dispositive. The Constitutional Convention rejected proposals to allow state legislatures to set property qualifications for Congress. *See Powell*, 395 U.S. at 534–36. Instead, they adopted the current Qualifications Clauses as “the sole source of qualifications.” *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. 221, 228 (1920) (the Constitution’s provisions are “supreme” and “cannot be altered” by state action). *Leser v. Garnett*, 258 U.S. 130, 137 (1922), reaffirmed that the federal Constitution fixes the “qualifications of electors” for federal offices, and States cannot “add to or subtract from” them.

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 is a transparent attempt to impose a term-limit qualification that the Constitution does not contain. The State cannot circumvent the exclusive nature of the Qualifications Clauses by labeling its restriction a “ballot access” measure. *See State ex rel. Davis v. Hildebrant*, 241 U.S. 565, 568 (1916) (distinguishing between permissible procedural regulations and impermissible alterations of substantive rights). The effect is identical: it disqualifies candidates who would otherwise be eligible under the Constitution. *Powell* forecloses this subterfuge.

Because the Arkansas Amendment alters the qualifications for Congress, it violates the Constitution. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
