# ARGUMENT

## I. Punitive Damages Have Long Required a Showing of Wanton, Malicious, or Oppressive Conduct, Independent of Mere Intent

This Court's punitive-damages jurisprudence has never rested on the bare showing of intentional wrongdoing. Rather, from the earliest cases recognizing exemplary damages, this Court required proof that "the defendant has acted wantonly, or oppressively, or with such malice as implies a spirit of mischief or criminal indifference to civil obligations." *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 108 (1893). The Court in *Prentice* was explicit that "such guilty intention on the part of the defendant is required in order to charge him with exemplary or punitive damages." *Id.* Mere intentional conduct—without the aggravating quality of wantonness or oppression—has never been sufficient.

This principle traces directly to *Day v. Woodworth*, cited approvingly in *Prentice*, and was reaffirmed in *Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris*, 122 U.S. 597 (1887), where the Court permitted punitive damages only upon a finding that "the defendant acted with bad intent, and in pursuance of an unlawful purpose." *Id.* at 601. The touchstone has consistently been the character of the conduct—its wantonness, oppression, or maliciousness—not the mere fact that the actor knew his conduct was legally proscribed.

Respondent recognizes that Petitioner will point to language in *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), suggesting a flexible, jury-driven standard for punitive awards. But *Barry* addressed only the *amount* of damages once a proper predicate for punitive liability was established; it did not disturb the threshold requirement that the conduct itself be of an aggravated character. Similarly, *Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society*, 320 U.S. 238 (1943), addressed only whether punitive damages must bear a fixed mathematical relationship to compensatory damages, noting that Alabama law required consideration of "[t]he nature of the case," "the character and extent of injury," and "all the attendant circumstances." *Id.* at 240. Nothing in that decision eliminated the independent requirement that the underlying conduct be egregious.

## II. This Court's Defamation Jurisprudence Confirms That "Malice" Alone, Untethered to Aggravated Conduct, Does Not Automatically Warrant Punitive Damages

The defamation cases relied upon by Petitioner actually support Respondent's position that punitive damages require something beyond the state-of-mind showing necessary for liability itself. In *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), the trial court found that the publisher's conduct, while perhaps negligent, could not "be construed as that entire want of care which would amount to a conscious indifference to the rights of plaintiff," concluding that "[n]egligence, it may have been; malice, it was not." *Id.* at 156. This demonstrates that courts have long distinguished between the culpability sufficient for liability and the heightened culpability necessary for punitive exposure.

*St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727 (1968), likewise required proof that the defendant "recklessly disregarded whether the statements . . . were true or false"—a standard demanding more than simple intent to publish. *Id.* at 731. And in *Henry v. Collins*, 380 U.S. 356 (1965), this Court permitted an inference of malice only where the jury could find "culpable recklessness or a wilful and wanton disregard of the rights and interests of the person defamed"—language the trial court itself used to instruct the jury, confirming that "malice" in this punitive context has always connoted something beyond ordinary intentional conduct. *Id.* at 357.

Even *Ginzburg v. Goldwater*, 396 U.S. 1049 (1969) (Black, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari), which discusses the interplay between the actual-malice standard and punitive damages, reflects that courts applying the *New York Times* framework have treated the punitive inquiry as requiring proof of knowing falsity or reckless disregard—a standard demonstrably more demanding than simple intentional conduct.

## III. Title VII's Punitive Damages Provision Should Be Construed Consistently With This Settled Common-Law Tradition

Congress enacted 42 U.S.C. § 1981a against the backdrop of this settled common-law understanding that punitive damages require conduct rising above the level necessary to establish liability. Nothing in the statutory text or structure suggests Congress intended to abandon a century of this Court's punitive-damages doctrine. Where Congress borrows a common-law term such as "malice" or "reckless indifference," it is presumed to adopt the term's settled common-law meaning—a meaning this Court's own precedent makes clear demands proof that the defendant "acted wantonly, or oppressively, or with such malice as implies a spirit of mischief or criminal indifference to civil obligations." *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 108.

To hold otherwise—to permit punitive damages upon nothing more than the intentional discrimination necessary to establish liability under Title VII itself—would collapse the distinction this Court has maintained since *Prentice*, *Harris*, and their progeny between compensable wrongdoing and punishable wrongdoing. Respondent respectfully submits that an employer's conduct must be shown to be egregious, in the sense that it exceeds the culpability inherent in the underlying discrimination claim, before punitive damages may be sustained.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
