**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A "CLASS OF ONE" CLAIM WHERE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ILLEGITIMATE, ARBITRARY, OR SPITEFUL REASONS.**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that a plaintiff may bring an equal protection claim as a "class of one" where the challenged government action was motivated by "a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Village of Willowbrook v. Olech*, 160 F.3d 386, 388 (7th Cir. 1998) (quoting *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886)). This Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational government action, even absent discriminatory intent toward a protected class. *See Romer v. Evans*, 517 U.S. 620, 633 (1996) ("The Constitution’s guarantee of equality 'must at the very least mean that a bare ... desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest.'") (quoting *Department of Agric. v. Moreno*, 413 U.S. 528, 534 (1973)).

The "class of one" theory is firmly rooted in this Court’s equal protection jurisprudence. In *Yick Wo*, this Court invalidated a facially neutral ordinance enforced discriminatorily against Chinese laundry operators, holding that "though the law itself be fair on its face and impartial in appearance, yet, if it is applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances," the law violates the Equal Protection Clause. *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74. Subsequent decisions have reaffirmed that arbitrary enforcement of neutral laws can violate equal protection. *See, e.g., Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445 (1923) ("[T]he equal protection clause is offended by a law or regulation that is applied unequally unless there is some reasonable basis for the classification.").

The Seventh Circuit’s "class of one" framework aligns with this precedent. It permits a plaintiff to challenge government action that, while facially neutral, was motivated by illegitimate, arbitrary, or spiteful reasons. *Olech*, 160 F.3d at 388. This approach does not require the plaintiff to allege membership in a protected class or group; instead, it focuses on whether the government’s conduct was rational or arbitrary. *See Romer*, 517 U.S. at 631–32 (invalidating a law targeting a politically unpopular group without requiring proof of discriminatory intent).

**II. THE VILLAGE’S CONDITIONAL DEMAND FOR AN 18-FOOT EASEMENT VIOLATES THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE BECAUSE IT WAS MOTIVATED BY SPITE AND ARBITRARINESS.**

Olech’s complaint adequately alleged that the Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement—nearly double the 15-foot easement required from similarly situated property owners—was motivated by spite and lacked any rational basis. *See Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 92 F. Supp. 2d 1009, 1012 (N.D. Ill. 2000) (denying the Village’s motion to dismiss). The Village’s action was arbitrary because it imposed a disproportionate burden on Olech without any legitimate justification. *See City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446 (1985) ("The general rule is that legislation is presumed to be valid and will be sustained if the classification drawn by the statute is rationally related to a legitimate state interest.").

The Seventh Circuit correctly recognized that such arbitrary treatment can form the basis of an equal protection claim. *Olech*, 160 F.3d at 388. The Village’s conduct—demanding an excessive easement from Olech while imposing lesser requirements on others—demonstrates the kind of "unequal hand" condemned in *Yick Wo*. 118 U.S. at 373–74. Because the Village’s action was not rationally related to any legitimate state interest, it violated the Equal Protection Clause.

**III. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT’S "CLASS OF ONE" FRAMEWORK DOES NOT IMPERMISSIBLY EXPAND THE SCOPE OF THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

The Village argues that the Seventh Circuit’s "class of one" theory impermissibly expands equal protection jurisprudence by allowing plaintiffs to challenge arbitrary government action without alleging membership in a protected class. This argument misapprehends the nature of the "class of one" theory. As this Court has recognized, the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational government action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a protected class. *See Romer*, 517 U.S. at 631–32; *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 446.

The Seventh Circuit’s framework merely applies this principle to cases where the government’s action is motivated by spite or arbitrariness. *Olech*, 160 F.3d at 388. This approach does not create a new cause of action; it simply recognizes that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary enforcement of neutral laws. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373–74. The Village’s reliance on *United States v. Carolene Prods. Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), is misplaced, as that case addressed the standard of review for economic regulations, not the prohibition on arbitrary government action. *See id.* at 152–53.

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that the Equal Protection Clause recognizes a "class of one" claim where state action is motivated by illegitimate, arbitrary, or spiteful reasons. Olech’s complaint adequately alleged such conduct, and the Village’s conditional demand for an excessive easement violated the Equal Protection Clause. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
