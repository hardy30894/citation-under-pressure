**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S FACILITIES WERE A “LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM” NOT OPEN TO RELIGIOUS SPEECH.**

The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the school facilities were a “limited public forum” not open to religious speech by policy or practice is contrary to governing Supreme Court precedent. In *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a state university’s policy of accommodating student meetings created a forum generally open to student groups, and thus the university could not exclude religious speech without violating the First Amendment. The Court reasoned that “[t]hrough its policy of accommodating their meetings, the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and that “the campus of a public university, at least for its students, possesses many of the characteristics of a public forum.” *Id.* at 267 & n.5, 102 S.Ct. at 273 & n.5.

Here, the School District’s facilities were opened to a wide range of community groups, including secular organizations, for after-hours use. The District’s policy did not expressly exclude religious speech, and its practice of allowing non-religious groups to use the facilities demonstrates that it had created a forum generally open to the public. The Second Circuit’s reliance on the absence of prior religious use is misplaced. As the Supreme Court explained in *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985), a forum’s designation is determined by the government’s policy and practice, not by the content of the speech previously permitted. The Court held that “the government’s consistent policy has been to deny access to its nonpublic forums,” but where the government “has not, by policy or practice, opened [a forum] for indiscriminate use by the general public,” it retains the right to limit access. *Id.* at 803, 105 S.Ct. at 3449.

The School District’s policy and practice of allowing community groups to use its facilities for secular purposes created a forum open to the public, and thus the District could not exclude religious speech based on its content. *See Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983) (“[I]n a public forum the government may not regulate speech based on its substantive content.”).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S DENIAL OF ACCESS WAS VIEWPOINT-NEUTRAL.**

The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the School District’s denial of access was viewpoint-neutral is unsupported by the record. The District’s policy explicitly prohibited “religious purposes,” and its application of that policy to exclude Lamb’s Chapel’s religious-oriented films was content-based discrimination. As the Supreme Court held in *Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995), “[d]iscrimination against speech because of its message is presumed to be unconstitutional.” The Court explained that “[w]hen the government targets not subject matter, but particular views taken by speakers on a subject, the violation of the First Amendment is all the more blatant.” *Id.* at 831.

Here, the School District’s policy and practice of excluding religious speech while allowing secular speech on similar topics was a clear violation of the First Amendment. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show its religious-oriented films while permitting secular groups to use the facilities for similar purposes was content-based discrimination. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393 (1993) (holding that the District’s policy violated the First Amendment because it discriminated based on the content of the speech).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S DENIAL OF ACCESS DID NOT VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.**

The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the School District’s denial of access did not violate the Establishment Clause is also erroneous. The Supreme Court has held that a government policy that discriminates against religious speech violates the Establishment Clause. In *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 106 (1968), the Court held that “[t]he First Amendment mandates governmental neutrality between religion and religion, and between religion and nonreligion.” The Court explained that “[g]overnment in our democracy, state and national, must be neutral in matters of religious concern.” *Id.*

Here, the School District’s policy of excluding religious speech while allowing secular speech on similar topics was not neutral. The policy favored secular speech over religious speech, and thus violated the Establishment Clause. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 841 (holding that a university’s policy of excluding religious speech while allowing secular speech violated the Establishment Clause).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Second Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
