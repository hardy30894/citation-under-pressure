**ARGUMENT**

The Florida capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has long recognized that the Constitution leaves to the States the manner in which capital sentences are determined, so long as the procedures are fundamentally fair. Florida’s scheme—which entrusts the jury with an advisory role and the judge with ultimate sentencing authority—comports with centuries of common-law practice and this Court’s precedents.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Factfinding on Aggravating Factors or Unanimous Jury Sentencing.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees a jury trial only for “elements” of the offense, not for every fact that may influence a sentencing decision. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 247 (1949), this Court held that a sentencing judge is “not confined to the narrow issue of guilt” and may consider “the fullest information possible concerning the defendant’s life and characteristics.” That principle applies with equal force to capital sentencing. *See Williams v. Oklahoma*, 358 U.S. 576, 585 (1959) (upholding consideration of uncharged murder in sentencing for kidnapping). Florida’s scheme, which allows the judge to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors after receiving a jury recommendation, falls squarely within this tradition.

The jury’s role in Florida is advisory, not binding. This Court has never held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find aggravating factors unanimously or at all. In *Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948), the Court noted that “a discretion in making that choice” between life and death may be vested in the jury, but it did not mandate such a role. Indeed, in *Winston v. United States*, 172 U.S. 303, 313 (1899), the Court emphasized that the jury’s power to recommend mercy is a “privilege” granted by statute, not a constitutional right. Florida’s legislature has chosen to give the jury an advisory voice, but the Constitution does not compel more.

Nor does the Sixth Amendment require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation. In *Witherspoon v. Illinois*, 391 U.S. 510, 518 (1968), the Court addressed only the exclusion of jurors opposed to capital punishment, not the unanimity of the verdict. And in *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570, 581 (1968), the Court struck down a statute that penalized the exercise of the jury right, but it did not hold that a non-unanimous jury recommendation violates the Sixth Amendment. Florida’s scheme does not penalize any defendant; it merely permits a majority recommendation, leaving the final decision to the judge.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Determination of Mental Retardation.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishments, but it does not dictate the procedural mechanism for determining mental retardation. This Court has consistently left to the States the task of defining and implementing such protections. In *McElvaine v. Brush*, 142 U.S. 155, 159 (1891), the Court deferred to state court interpretations of state statutes, recognizing that “the general rule of decision is that this court will follow the adjudication of the highest court of a State in the construction of its own statutes.” Florida’s highest court has determined that mental retardation is a factual issue for the judge, not the jury, and that the defendant bears the burden of proof by clear and convincing evidence. That determination is entitled to deference.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require that every factual predicate for a death sentence be decided by a jury. In *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 135-36 (1879), the Court upheld a death sentence imposed without any jury involvement in the mode of execution, noting that the legislature may prescribe the method. Similarly, Florida’s legislature may prescribe the procedure for determining mental retardation. The judge’s role in making that finding is consistent with the traditional judicial function in sentencing. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. at 247 (sentencing judge may consider “any information that is relevant to the character of the defendant”).

Finally, the Eighth Amendment’s “heightened reliability” requirement does not mandate jury unanimity. In *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 567 (1953), the Court upheld a death sentence imposed after a guilty plea, where the judge alone determined the penalty. The Court found no due process violation, even though the defendant had not received a jury trial on the sentence. Florida’s scheme, which provides a full jury hearing and a judge’s independent review, affords at least as much reliability.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
