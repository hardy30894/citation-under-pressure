The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. The constitutional minimum is the standard set forth in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam): whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.”

This Court has repeatedly held that the Dusky formulation governs competency to stand trial. Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966). The same standard applies when a defendant seeks to forgo trial by pleading guilty or waiving counsel. In Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court vacated a conviction because the trial court had failed to determine whether the defendant “was competent to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel,” yet it did so by reference to the Dusky inquiry rather than any elevated test. Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975), likewise required only that a defendant “knowingly and intelligently” forgo counsel, a determination measured against the defendant’s “ability to understand the proceedings” and “capacity to make a knowing and intelligent waiver.”

No decision of this Court has imposed a higher mental-functioning requirement for waivers than for standing trial. To the contrary, the Court has treated the competency inquiry as unitary. See, e.g., Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954) (competency to stand trial encompasses capacity to “consult with counsel and to understand the proceedings”). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that a defendant must demonstrate the capacity for a “reasoned choice among the alternatives”—finds no support in the Due Process Clause or in this Court’s precedents. Because the record establishes that Moran satisfied the Dusky standard at the time of his plea and waiver, the state courts’ acceptance of those decisions comported with the Constitution.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
