**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental conflict in the interpretation of OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that California’s wage and hour laws apply on the Outer Continental Shelf (OCS) so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute’s text, ignores its legislative history, and contradicts this Court’s precedent. We respectfully urge reversal.

### I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION DOES NOT AUTOMATICALLY APPLY STATE LAW—IT REQUIRES A GAP IN FEDERAL LAW

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a broad, open-ended mandate to apply state law whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law fundamentally misconstrues the Act’s purpose and structure. The provision does not function as a general rule of incorporation. Rather, it operates as a *gap-filling mechanism*, designed to supplement federal law only where federal law is silent or inadequate.

This Court has long recognized that OCSLA’s choice-of-law clause is not a blank check for state law to supplant federal law. In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366 (1969), the Court held that state law applies on the OCS only when federal law does not provide a remedy. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA “was not intended to create a new body of law, but to extend existing federal law and the law of the adjacent state to the OCS to the extent that federal law is inadequate.” Id. at 366. The Court explicitly rejected the idea that state law could be applied “as a matter of course,” noting that “the application of state law is not automatic.” Id. at 366.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—ignores this foundational principle. Under the Ninth Circuit’s rule, California’s meal period and overtime laws would apply to every offshore worker, even if the FLSA already provides a comprehensive, enforceable remedy. That result is inconsistent with *Rodrigue* and with the settled understanding that OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision is not a vehicle for *supplementing* federal law, but for *filling gaps* where federal law fails.

### II. THE FAIR LABOR STANDARDS ACT IS A COMPREHENSIVE, SELF-CONTAINED STATUTORY SCHEME

The district court correctly concluded that the FLSA is a “comprehensive statutory scheme” that leaves no room for state law to operate as a surrogate. The FLSA is not a minimal floor; it is a detailed, nationwide framework governing minimum wage, overtime pay, recordkeeping, and child labor. See *United States v. Darby Lumber Co.*, 312 U.S. 100, 118 (1941) (“The Fair Labor Standards Act is a national labor policy, designed to eliminate labor conditions detrimental to the maintenance of the minimum standard of living necessary for health, efficiency, and general well-being of workers.”). Congress enacted the FLSA to ensure uniformity in labor standards across the country, and it explicitly preserved the right of states to impose *more protective* standards. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a).

But the FLSA’s savings clause does not mean that state laws are automatically applicable on the OCS. The clause permits *additional* protections, not *parallel* ones. As this Court has held, “the existence of a federal statute does not preclude the application of state law, but where the federal law is comprehensive and occupies the field, state law is displaced.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). The FLSA is precisely such a statute. It establishes a detailed regime for wage and hour enforcement, including administrative procedures, enforcement mechanisms, and remedies. To allow California’s wage and hour laws to operate *concurrently* with the FLSA on the OCS would create a dual system of enforcement, undermining the FLSA’s purpose of national uniformity.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), is misplaced. That case stands for the proposition that state law may be applied on the OCS only when federal law is “inadequate” or “silent.” Id. at 1036. The court in *Continental Oil* did not hold that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. Indeed, the Fifth Circuit has since reaffirmed that “the FLSA is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to fill a gap.” *Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc.*, 713 F.3d 208, 214 (5th Cir. 2013). The Ninth Circuit’s departure from this settled precedent creates a circuit split and undermines the uniformity the FLSA was designed to ensure.

### III. THE OCSLA’S LEGISLATIVE HISTORY CONFIRMS THAT STATE LAW IS A SURROGATE FOR FEDERAL LAW ONLY WHERE FEDERAL LAW IS INADEQUATE

Congress enacted OCSLA in 1953 to resolve a jurisdictional dispute over offshore oil and gas development. The legislative history makes clear that Congress intended to extend federal law to the OCS, but only to the extent that federal law was insufficient. As the Senate Report explained, Congress feared that federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 6 (1953). Thus, Congress provided for the assimilation of state law *only* to “fill the gaps” left by federal law.
