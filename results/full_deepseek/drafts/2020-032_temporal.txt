**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Properly Dismissed the Case as Moot Because the Prayer for Nominal Damages Cannot Revive an Otherwise Moot Constitutional Challenge.**

The Constitution limits federal judicial power to “cases” or “controversies.” *Aetna Life Ins. Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240 (1937). A case becomes moot—and thus outside the jurisdiction of the federal courts—“when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 496 (1969). Here, Appellants concede that their claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are moot because Georgia Gwinnett College revised its policies and no reasonable expectation of recurrence exists. The only remaining claim is for nominal damages. But a request for nominal damages, standing alone, does not create a live controversy where the underlying challenged policy has been withdrawn and no ongoing injury exists.

**A. Once the Offending Policy Is Revised, No Actual Controversy Remains.**

In *Hall v. Beals*, 396 U.S. 45, 48 (1969), this Court held that a challenge to a state election law became moot after the law was amended, even though the plaintiffs sought damages. The Court explained that the case no longer presented a “definite and concrete” controversy touching the legal relations of the parties. *Id.* at 48. Here, as in *Hall*, the challenged policies were replaced before any judgment on the merits. Appellants’ prayer for nominal damages does not transform the abstract question of the old policies’ constitutionality into a live case. *See also St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41, 42 (1943) (a case becomes moot when the alleged harm has ceased and there is no reasonable expectation that it will recur).

**B. Nominal Damages Are Insufficient to Confer Standing or Maintain a Live Controversy Absent a Showing of Actual Injury.**

Nominal damages are a remedy, not a basis for jurisdiction. To maintain a claim for damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, a plaintiff must allege actual injury. *See Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 684 (1946) (suggesting that a claim for damages must be “substantial” to invoke federal jurisdiction). Here, Appellants alleged no specific injury—no physical harm, no financial loss, no emotional distress. Their complaint mentions “injury” only in conclusory terms. Such a bare allegation cannot support a claim for compensatory damages. *See Hatahley v. United States*, 351 U.S. 173, 182 (1956) (nominal damages appropriate only when a right has been violated but no actual injury is shown; but the Court did not address mootness). Where, as here, the only remedy sought is nominal damages for a past violation that no longer has any continuing effect, the controversy is “abstract and hypothetical.” *Lord v. Veazie*, 49 U.S. (8 How.) 251, 255 (1850). The federal courts do not render advisory opinions on the constitutionality of expired policies. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 362 (1911).

**C. Appellants Cannot Rely on *Powell v. McCormack* Because That Case Involved a Live Claim for Specific Monetary Relief.**

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that a claim for back pay—a specific, ascertainable sum of money—prevented mootness even after the injunctive claim became moot. *Id.* at 496-500. The Court distinguished cases where only nominal damages were sought, noting that “the claim for back pay” was “a separate and distinct claim for relief” that “would survive the mootness of the equitable claims.” *Id.* at 496. Here, Appellants never sought back pay or any specific monetary loss. Their prayer for “nominal damages” and “damages in an amount to be determined by the evidence” does not resemble the concrete, quantifiable claim in *Powell*. The district court correctly held that *Powell* does not save this case.

**D. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Denying Leave to Amend.**

Appellants sought leave to amend only in a response to a motion to dismiss—not by a proper motion under Rule 15. This Court has long held that such an informal request does not preserve the right to amend. *See, e.g., Edwards v. City of Goldsboro*, 178 F.2d 97, 98 (4th Cir. 1949) (similar procedural requirement). Moreover, Appellants never specified what additional facts they would plead to support compensatory damages. The district court acted within its discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
