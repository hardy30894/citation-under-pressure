The United States respectfully submits that the D.C. Circuit correctly held that Guam's 2004 consent decree with the EPA triggered its right to seek contribution under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), precluding its claim under § 107(a). As the court below recognized, the Consent Decree's terms demonstrate that Guam "resolved [its] liability" to the United States for the Ordot Dump's cleanup, thereby satisfying § 113(f)(3)(B)'s requirements.

CERCLA's statutory scheme provides two avenues for potentially responsible parties (PRPs) to recoup cleanup costs: § 107(a) cost-recovery actions and § 113(f) contribution actions. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004). While § 107(a) allows for the recovery of "any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person," 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B), § 113(f) provides a contribution mechanism for PRPs that have incurred response costs. See 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f).

The distinction between these provisions is critical, as the statute of limitations for § 113(f) contribution actions is three years, whereas § 107(a) remedial actions have a six-year statute of limitations. See 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B), (g)(3). Guam's claim, filed in 2017, is timely only if it can proceed under § 107(a).

The D.C. Circuit correctly determined that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to seek contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B). The Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a "dump cover system." Consent Decree 5–12, J.A. 94–101. These terms demonstrate that Guam "resolved [its] liability" to the United States for the response action, satisfying § 113(f)(3)(B)'s requirements.

Guam's arguments to the contrary are unavailing. Its contention that the Consent Decree did not "resolve" its liability because it did not admit fault is meritless. As the Sixth Circuit has observed, "parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability." Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015).

Furthermore, Guam's assertion that the Consent Decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it addressed Clean Water Act violations is incorrect. The Complaint demanded that Guam "complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," CWA Compl. ¶ 29, and CERCLA expressly identifies the "collection of leachate and runoff" as a "remedial action." 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24).

The D.C. Circuit's decision is consistent with CERCLA's statutory scheme and the Supreme Court's precedent. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157 (2004). Guam's claim is time-barred under § 113(f)(3)(B), and the court below correctly reversed the district court's denial of the United States' motion to dismiss.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the D.C. Circuit's judgment.
