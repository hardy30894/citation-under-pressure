**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA does not reach Missouri’s appointed judges.**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person elected to public office in any State or political subdivision of any State by the qualified voters thereof, or any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level or an immediate advisor with respect to the exercise of the constitutional or legal powers of the office.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan and retained by popular vote occupy precisely this excluded category.

Congress did not manifest in “clear and unmistakable terms” an intention to intrude upon the States’ traditional authority to set qualifications for their own judges. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). The statutory text and structure confirm that appointees “on the policymaking level” encompass those who, like judges, “exercise the same sort of discretion in decisionmaking” and “engage in the same sort of thoughtful judgment” required of high-level executive and legislative officers. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (quoting 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). Because the ADEA therefore does not apply, Missouri’s constitutional retirement rule cannot be preempted.

**II. The mandatory retirement provision satisfies equal protection.**

Age is not a suspect classification. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313–14 (1976) (per curiam). The challenged provision therefore need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest. Missouri’s rule advances several such interests: it “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980); reduces delays caused by death or disability, *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979); and permits “important decisions . . . [to] be made by judges who command respect because they share experiences and understandings similar to those of the majority of adult citizens,” *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). A State “has a legitimate interest in maintaining a judiciary fully capable of performing the demanding tasks that judges must perform,” *Vance v. Bradley*, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979), and the line drawn at age seventy is a permissible means of advancing that interest.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
