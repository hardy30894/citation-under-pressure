**ARGUMENT**

I. **Section 1333(b) does not impose a free-standing situs-of-injury requirement.**

OCSLA extends LHWCA benefits for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute asks whether the injury resulted from shelf operations; it does not say the injury must occur on the shelf. Where Congress chooses a geographic limitation, courts must enforce the text Congress used. See *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986).

The statutory structure confirms that § 1333(b) is not limited to injuries physically occurring on the Shelf. OCSLA elsewhere uses explicit situs language when Congress means to confine a rule to the Shelf itself. See 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A); *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 218-19. By contrast, § 1333(b) covers injuries “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” language focused on causation, not geography. Courts should not read into the statute a limitation Congress omitted. See *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Serv., Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988).

The Third Circuit correctly held that § 1333(b) reaches injuries on land so long as they result from Shelf operations. In *Curtis*, an employee injured in a car accident while traveling to a helicopter that would take him to a Shelf platform was covered under OCSLA because his injury resulted from Shelf operations. 849 F.2d at 806, 811. That decision correctly gave effect to the broad phrase “as the result of” and refused to graft a situs-of-injury limitation onto the statute. Id. at 809-11.

II. **The Fifth Circuit’s situs rule conflicts with the text and with OCSLA’s compensatory purpose.**

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), improperly narrows § 1333(b) by requiring both a causal nexus and a Shelf situs. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 362. That reading is inconsistent with the ordinary meaning of “resulting from,” which denotes causation, not location. See *Curtis*, 849 F.2d at 809-11.

The Fifth Circuit relied heavily on *Tallentire*, but *Tallentire* expressly disclaimed any bearing on § 1333(b). 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. The Court said only that § 1333(b) was not before it. Id. A footnote that is “not a holding” cannot justify rewriting the statute. See *District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570, 631 n.25 (2008). Nor does *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), support a situs-of-injury rule. That case addressed whether a worker on a platform in state waters was engaged in “maritime employment” under the LHWCA, not the scope of § 1333(b). 470 U.S. at 415-16, 420-24.

OCSLA was enacted to provide a federal compensation remedy for injuries tied to Shelf operations. See *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219; *Curtis*, 849 F.2d at 810-11. A situs-of-injury restriction would arbitrarily exclude workers whose injuries are causally attributable to Shelf operations merely because the final accident happened on land. Nothing in the text or the Act’s remedial purpose supports that result. See *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 362 (basing rule on perceived geographic limitation, not statutory text).

III. **Under the correct standard, Valladolid’s death falls within § 1333(b).**

Valladolid’s work was overwhelmingly dedicated to offshore drilling operations on the Hogan and Houchin platforms. He spent roughly 98% of his working time aboard the Hogan and also performed maintenance related to offshore production at La Conchita. His death occurred while he was consolidating scrap generated by those offshore operations for eventual removal. The injury therefore had a substantial causal nexus to operations conducted on the Shelf.

That is enough under § 1333(b). In *Curtis*, the Third Circuit held that an employee need only establish that the injury would not have occurred but for Shelf operations. 849 F.2d at 809-11. Even if this Court prefers a formulation closer to “substantial nexus,” the injury here plainly satisfies it. The forklift accident arose from work directly connected to offshore production and the handling of materials generated by those operations. See *Curtis*, 849 F.2d at 811.

The Benefits Review Board’s situs-of-injury rule should therefore be rejected, and the judgment below reversed as to OCSLA coverage. The Ninth Circuit’s approach correctly follows the statute’s text and the Third Circuit’s decision in *Curtis*, while the Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule in *Mills* improperly adds a requirement Congress did not enact.
