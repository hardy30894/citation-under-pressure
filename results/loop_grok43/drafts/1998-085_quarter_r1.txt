**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct that is egregious or outrageous, independent of the employer's state of mind. This standard derives from the common-law rule that exemplary awards punish only aggravated wrongdoing beyond the mental element of the underlying tort.

In *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. (3 Wheat.) 546, 558 (1818), the Court explained that exemplary damages address cases of “gross and wanton outrage.” *Tracy v. Swartwout*, 35 U.S. (10 Pet.) 80, 95 (1836), permitted such awards only upon proof of “oppressive” conduct. *Wilkes v. Dinsman*, 48 U.S. (7 How.) 89, 130 (1849), likewise required conduct “characterized by aggravated circumstances” before exemplary damages could be imposed.

In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851), the Court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant's conduct has involved “aggravating circumstances.” *Philadelphia, W. & B. R.R. v. Quigley*, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202, 214 (1858), required “such a degree of negligence or misconduct as to evince a reckless disregard of the rights of others” amounting to an aggravated wrong.

Later decisions confirmed that malice or reckless indifference alone does not suffice. *Denver & Rio Grande Ry. v. Harris*, 122 U.S. 597, 609 (1887), held that the conduct must be “of an aggravated character” before exemplary damages may be imposed. *Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), stated that punitive damages “are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct.”

These authorities demonstrate that, at common law, a showing of malice or reckless indifference was never enough; the plaintiff had to prove additional aggravating facts. Because the 1991 amendments to Title VII incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
