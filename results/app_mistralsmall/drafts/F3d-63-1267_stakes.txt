**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE JURY’S VERDICT ON ROBINSON-PATMAN PRICE DISCRIMINATION MUST BE REVERSED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF SECONDARY-LINE DISCRIMINATION.**

To establish a prima facie case of secondary-line price discrimination under the Robinson-Patman Act, 15 U.S.C. § 13(a), a plaintiff must prove: (1) the defendant discriminated in price between different purchasers of commodities of like grade and quality; (2) the plaintiff and the favored purchasers competed for resale of the commodities in question; (3) the discrimination occurred in the context of contemporaneous sales; and (4) the discrimination caused or threatened to cause injury to competition. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981); *FTC v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 363 U.S. 536, 543 (1960). Stelwagon failed to satisfy the third and fourth elements.

First, Stelwagon did not demonstrate that the alleged discriminatory sales to Standard and Celotex were “contemporaneous” with its own purchases. The record shows that Stelwagon’s purchases from Tarmac occurred primarily in 1988 and early 1989, while the challenged sales to Standard and Celotex took place in mid-to-late 1989—well after Stelwagon had ceased purchasing Tarmac products in any significant volume. *See* *Moore v. Jas. H. Matthews & Co.*, 553 F.2d 1273, 1278 (9th Cir. 1977) (holding that sales must be “contemporaneous in time” to support a Robinson-Patman claim). The temporal gap between Stelwagon’s purchases and the alleged discriminatory sales fatally undermines its prima facie case.

Second, Stelwagon failed to prove that the alleged discrimination caused or threatened injury to competition. The Supreme Court has made clear that a plaintiff must show more than a mere loss of sales; it must demonstrate “diversion of sales or profits” or “a significant threat thereof.” *J. Truett Payne*, 451 U.S. at 568. Here, Stelwagon presented no evidence that Standard or Celotex gained market share at its expense, nor did it show that Tarmac’s pricing practices had any discernible effect on competition in the Philadelphia MAP market. *See* *William Inglis & Sons Baking Co. v. ITT Continental Baking Co.*, 461 F.Supp. 410, 425 (N.D. Cal. 1978) (aff’d, 668 F.2d 1014 (9th Cir. 1981)) (requiring proof of actual competitive injury). Without such evidence, the jury’s verdict cannot stand.

**II. EVEN IF A PRIMA FACIE CASE WERE ESTABLISHED, STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES, AND THEREFORE IS PRECLUDED FROM RECOVERING TREBLE DAMAGES UNDER THE CLAYTON ACT.**

The Clayton Act permits recovery of treble damages only upon proof of actual antitrust injury. *Cargill, Inc. v. Monfort of Colo., Inc.*, 479 U.S. 104, 110 n.5 (1986). Stelwagon’s damages model was fatally flawed because it assumed that every sale lost to Standard or Celotex was a direct result of Tarmac’s pricing practices, without accounting for other market factors. *See* *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 123-24 (1969) (requiring damages to be proven with reasonable certainty). The district court’s remittitur order implicitly acknowledged this deficiency by slashing the award from $2.27 million to $450,383.60—an 80% reduction—yet still permitted recovery. This was error. *See* *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (holding that damages must be supported by competent evidence and cannot be based on speculation).

Moreover, Stelwagon’s damages expert conceded that he could not isolate the effect of Tarmac’s pricing from other variables, such as changes in market demand or Stelwagon’s own business decisions. *See* *In re Industrial Silicon Antitrust Litig.*, 82 F.3d 119, 124 (3d Cir. 1996) (requiring damages to be proven with economic or statistical evidence). Without a reliable methodology to attribute lost profits to Tarmac’s conduct, the jury’s award was impermissibly speculative. *See* *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931) (damages cannot be based on conjecture).

**III. THE JURY’S BREACH OF CONTRACT VERDICT WAS ALSO ERRONEOUS BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE THE EXISTENCE OR TERMS OF THE ALLEGED ORAL AGREEMENT.**

Pennsylvania law requires that contracts for the sale of goods exceeding $500 be in writing to be enforceable under the Statute of Frauds. 13 Pa. Cons. Stat. § 2201. Stelwagon’s claim for breach of an oral exclusive distributorship agreement falls squarely within this provision. *See* *Bash v. Bell Tel. Co. of Pa.*, 422 A.2d 610, 612 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1980). Stelwagon presented no written agreement, nor did it offer clear and convincing evidence of an exception to the Statute of Frauds, such as part performance. *See* *Bortz v. Noon*, 344 A.2d 655, 658 (Pa. 1975). The district court erred in permitting the jury to enforce an unwritten agreement that violated the Statute of Frauds.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tarmac respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment below in its entirety, enter judgment in Tarmac’s favor, and vacate the award of damages.
