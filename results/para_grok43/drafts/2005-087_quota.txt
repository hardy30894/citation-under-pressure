Arizona law permissibly confines mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense. A state may allocate the burden of proving affirmative defenses without offending due process. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 205-06 (1977); Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952). The same authority extends to rules that limit the purposes for which particular evidence may be introduced. Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43 (1996).

Due process does not guarantee a defendant the right to present every piece of evidence that might bear on an element the prosecution must prove. Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 295 (1973); Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986). When a rule of evidence is grounded in a legitimate state interest—here, preventing the misuse of psychiatric testimony to nullify mens rea—the restriction survives constitutional challenge. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. at 43; Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 326 (2006).

Arizona’s formulation of the insanity defense places the burden on the defendant, a scheme this Court has repeatedly upheld. Leland, 343 U.S. at 798-99; Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 449 (1992). Allowing the same evidence to be used a second time to negate mens rea would effectively shift that burden back to the State, contrary to Patterson and Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228, 233-34 (1987). The Due Process Clause does not require such a result.

The trial court’s exclusion of the evidence therefore rested on a valid state procedural rule rather than an arbitrary bar to a meaningful defense. See Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400, 410-11 (1988); United States v. Scheffer, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998). Because Clark received a full opportunity to litigate insanity under the governing standard, no separate constitutional right to relitigate mens rea was denied.
