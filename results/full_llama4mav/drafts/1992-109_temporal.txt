The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive constitutional rights was a due process violation.

The competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is distinct from the standard for determining competency to stand trial. As this Court held in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and can assist their counsel. However, waiving constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this principle, citing Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), which held that a defendant must have the capacity for "reasoned choice" among alternatives to waive constitutional rights validly.

The trial court's failure to apply the correct standard and hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive constitutional rights was a due process error. Moran's mental state and medication regimen raised significant doubts about his competency. As this Court held in Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), a trial court must hold a hearing to determine a defendant's competency to stand trial when there is a good faith doubt about their competency. Similarly, in this case, the trial court should have held a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive constitutional rights.

The state court's post-conviction ruling did not cure this error, as it applied the wrong legal standard. The state court relied on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, not his competency to waive constitutional rights. As this Court held in Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), a state court's factual findings are not entitled to a presumption of correctness if they are based on an incorrect legal standard.

In conclusion, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine a defendant's competency to waive constitutional rights. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency was a due process violation. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
