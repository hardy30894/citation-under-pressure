ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s Railway Labor Act conspiracy claims and on the counterclaims under the Federal Wiretap Act. Summary judgment is proper only when the movant shows the absence of any genuine issue of material fact. Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 323 (1986); Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). All reasonable inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmoving party. Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986).

The Federal Wiretap Act protects only oral communications uttered under circumstances justifying an expectation that the communication is not subject to interception. 18 U.S.C. § 2510(2); Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967). An executive session of a Public Law Board convened under 45 U.S.C. § 153 occurs in the offices of a carrier and involves representatives of the carrier, the union, and a neutral arbitrator. Because the proceeding is an integral part of the mandatory arbitration process established by the Railway Labor Act, participants lack a reasonable expectation that their statements will remain free from all recording. See Berger v. New York, 388 U.S. 41, 51 (1967) (requiring particularity in describing the communications to be intercepted). The district court therefore erred in holding that Smoot’s placement of recorders in the hearing room constituted an unlawful interception.

Even if an interception occurred, the Act permits recovery only upon proof that the interceptor acted with intent to violate the statute and that the violation was not justified by any exception. United States v. Kahn, 415 U.S. 143, 155 (1974). The record contains evidence that Smoot sought only to preserve a verbatim account of the proceeding in which he was the aggrieved party, raising a triable issue whether he possessed the requisite intent.

Smoot’s conspiracy claims under the Railway Labor Act likewise rest on genuine factual disputes. A union breaches its duty of fair representation when it acts arbitrarily or in bad faith toward a member. Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co., 323 U.S. 192, 204 (1944). Evidence that UTU representatives coordinated with CSX to deny Smoot the stock distribution to which the union’s internal appeals board had already determined he was entitled creates a question of fact whether the defendants conspired to deprive him of rights secured by the collective bargaining agreement. See Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen & Enginemen, 323 U.S. 210, 213 (1944).

The district court further erred in awarding statutory and punitive damages and attorneys’ fees. An award under 18 U.S.C. § 2520 requires a showing that the plaintiff suffered actual harm or that the defendant’s conduct warrants punitive sanctions. See Doe v. B.P.S. Guard Servs., Inc., 945 F.2d 1422, 1427 (8th Cir. 1991). Because material facts remain in dispute concerning the lawfulness of the recording and the existence of any conspiracy, the awards cannot stand. See also Bartnicki v. Vopper, 532 U.S. 514, 532 (2001) (recognizing First Amendment limits on liability for disclosure of unlawfully intercepted communications when the interceptor is not a party to the underlying wrong).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
