**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial only for the “same offence.” U.S. Const. amend. V. It does not, by its own terms, regulate ordinary sentencing determinations, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized the historical distinction between trial on guilt and sentencing. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld judicial factfinding at sentencing and explained that “[t]he due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251. Later decisions have reaffirmed that sentencing is not generally subject to the same constitutional trial protections that govern adjudication of guilt. See *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 606 (1967); *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995); *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 154 (1997).

That principle controls here. Monge’s prior-conviction allegation arose in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. California’s three-strikes law did not create a new offense; it authorized an enhanced sentence based on recidivism. The Supreme Court has long treated recidivist enhancements as sentencing factors, not elements of a separate crime. See *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554, 560 (1967); *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948). Because the proceeding was part of sentencing, not a prosecution for a “same offence,” retrial after reversal for evidentiary insufficiency does not implicate the core evil the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids.

Petitioner’s position is supported by the Court’s capital-sentencing cases, which show both the narrowness of the double-jeopardy principle in sentencing and the exceptional character of the cases in which it has been applied. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a capital sentencing hearing that “resembled a trial on the issue of guilt or innocence” carried double-jeopardy consequences. *Id.* at 438. The Court later emphasized that *Bullington* was tied to the “unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1984); *Poland v. Arizona*, 476 U.S. 147, 155-56 (1986). In *Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania*, 537 U.S. 101, 109-10 (2003), the Court again stressed that capital sentencing is different because it determines whether the defendant may be put to death at all.

But those cases do not extend *Bullington* to noncapital sentencing. To the contrary, the Court has refused to transform every enhancement proceeding into a trial for double-jeopardy purposes. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court declined to treat its prior capital cases as clearly controlling in a noncapital recidivist context, observing that “Both *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding.” *Id.* at 392. That observation was not a holding that double jeopardy can never apply outside capital sentencing; it was a recognition that no precedent had dictated such a result. *Id.* at 391-93. Here, too, Monge asks the Court to expand a rule crafted for capital death-eligibility determinations into an ordinary noncapital enhancement hearing. The Constitution does not require that step.

Nor does the California procedure itself alter the federal double-jeopardy analysis. A state’s decision to provide jury trial or other procedural protections at sentencing does not transform the proceeding into a second prosecution. See *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251; *Specht*, 386 U.S. at 610. California’s statutory choice to allow a prior-conviction allegation to be tried separately, see Cal. Penal Code § 1025, is a matter of state law, not a federal mandate. The presence of some trial-like safeguards does not convert a noncapital enhancement hearing into the kind of adjudication that triggers the Double Jeopardy Clause. See *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439.

Finally, petitioner’s rule would create serious practical distortions. Enhancement allegations are commonly proved through documentary records, prison packets, and certified records of conviction. See *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224, 228 n.6 (1996); *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). Reversal for failure of proof in a first enhancement hearing should not preclude the State from trying again when the allegation concerns a prior conviction rather than the defendant’s guilt of the current offense. The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against repeated attempts to convict for the same offense; it does not immunize a sentencing allegation from retrial in a noncapital case. See *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251; *Monge v. California*, 524 U.S. 721, 728-34 (1998).

For these reasons, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of Monge’s prior-conviction allegation.
