**ARGUMENT**

I. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of a prior-conviction allegation once a sentencing court has found the allegation not proved.

The Fifth Amendment’s command that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” has long been held to forbid a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671 (1896).  In *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919), this Court reaffirmed that “[t]he principle that a man shall not be twice punished for the same offense is a fundamental one.”  When a sentencing court, acting as the factfinder, resolves a contested prior-conviction allegation against the prosecution, the acquittal-like quality of that determination triggers the Clause’s bar to retrial.  *Cf. Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173 (1873) (double jeopardy attaches when a defendant has been “passed upon” and “sentenced” for an offense).

II. The Clause applies to sentencing proceedings because they are criminal in nature and the prior-conviction allegation is an “offense” within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment.

The Double Jeopardy Clause is not confined to the guilt phase of a prosecution.  In *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957), the Court held that a conviction of a lesser included offense bars retrial on the greater offense, explaining that the Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal” and that “the principle applies whether the proceeding in which the defendant was acquitted was a trial or a plea of guilty.”  The Court has repeatedly recognized that sentencing determinations—especially those that increase punishment—are criminal in nature.  *See Gore v. United States*, 357 U.S. 386, 393 (1958) (double jeopardy applies to enhanced sentences under recidivist statutes); *Stroud*, 251 U.S. at 18 (same).

III. The Clause’s protections are not diminished merely because the sentencing court, rather than a jury, resolved the prior-conviction allegation.

The Court has never suggested that the identity of the factfinder determines the Clause’s reach.  In *Ex parte United States*, 242 U.S. 27, 42 (1916), the Court held that a judge’s factual findings at sentencing are subject to the Clause’s constraints.  The fact that California affords a statutory jury trial for prior-conviction allegations does not alter the constitutional rule: once the state has chosen to provide a trial-like proceeding to determine the truth of the allegation, the Clause’s protections attach.  *Cf. United States v. Benz*, 282 U.S. 304, 307 (1931) (double jeopardy applies to proceedings “essentially criminal” in nature).

IV. The Clause bars retrial here because the Court of Appeal’s reversal was based on evidentiary insufficiency, not on any procedural defect.

When a reviewing court reverses a conviction or sentencing finding for lack of sufficient evidence, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial.  *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72 (no retrial after acquittal for insufficiency of evidence); *United States v. Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. 85, 87 (1916) (same).  The California Court of Appeal’s decision rested on evidentiary insufficiency—it held that the prosecution failed to prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt.  That determination is indistinguishable from an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes.  *Cf. Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141, 143 (1962) (per curiam) (double jeopardy barred retrial after trial court directed verdict of acquittal for insufficient evidence).

**CONCLUSION**

The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of the prior-conviction allegation once the Court of Appeal determined that the evidence was insufficient to support the finding.  The judgment below should be affirmed.
