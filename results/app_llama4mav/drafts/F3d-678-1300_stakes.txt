## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Denying Leader's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law or, in the Alternative, for a New Trial on Invalidity

The district court's denial of Leader's motion for judgment as a matter of law or, in the alternative, for a new trial on invalidity was erroneous because the jury's verdict was not supported by substantial evidence. To invalidate a patent under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b), the accused infringer must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the invention was publicly used or on sale more than one year prior to the patent's filing date. See, e.g., Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc., 525 U.S. 55, 67 (1998). The critical issue here is whether the Leader2Leader product was "ready for patenting" and "on sale" or "publicly used" before December 10, 2002. Id. at 67.

The evidence presented at trial demonstrated that while Leader was actively developing and promoting the Leader2Leader product, there was insufficient evidence to establish that the product embodied the claimed invention. The testimony of Michael McKibben and Jeffrey Lamb, Leader's founders, established that the '761 patent's claims cover the "underlying engine," Digital Leaderboard. However, the record lacks substantial evidence that the Leader2Leader product, powered by Digital Leaderboard, was "ready for patenting" or that its sale or public use anticipated the claimed invention.

### II. The Jury's Verdict Was Not Supported by Substantial Evidence

The jury's finding that the asserted claims were invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) was not supported by substantial evidence. To prove invalidity, Facebook had to show that the Leader2Leader product embodied the claimed invention and was on sale or publicly used before the critical date. See, e.g., Sparton Corp. v. United States, 399 F.3d 1321, 1324 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The white paper presented to the Wright Patterson Air Force Base in January 2002, while indicative of Leader's commercialization efforts, does not provide clear and convincing evidence that the Leader2Leader product practiced the claimed invention.

Moreover, the testimony of Lamb, who stated that the project was completed within "a couple of years.... [m]aybe three," i.e., probably the "2002ish time frame," J.A. 24829, is too vague to establish that the Leader2Leader product was "ready for patenting" before December 10, 2002. See, e.g., Honeywell Int'l, Inc. v. Universal Avionics Sys. Corp., 488 F.3d 982, 997 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (holding that evidence of reduction to practice is necessary to establish that an invention was "ready for patenting").

### III. The District Court Should Have Granted Leader's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law or, in the Alternative, for a New Trial

Given the lack of substantial evidence supporting the jury's verdict, the district court erred in denying Leader's motion for judgment as a matter of law or, in the alternative, for a new trial. See, e.g., Cordis Corp. v. Boston Scientific Corp., 658 F.3d 1347, 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (holding that a district court's denial of a motion for judgment as a matter of law is reviewed de novo). The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support the jury's finding of invalidity, and the district court should have granted Leader's motion.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's denial of Leader's motion for judgment as a matter of law or, in the alternative, for a new trial, and remand for further proceedings.
