# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Exemption 2 Does Not Support the Ninth Circuit's "High 2" Interpretation

Exemption 2 shields from disclosure matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit's adoption of a judicially-created "predominantly internal" test, which strips the word "solely" of its ordinary meaning and ignores the statute's focus on "personnel rules and practices," cannot be reconciled with the statutory text this Court has repeatedly instructed lower courts to apply.

This Court has made clear that FOIA exemptions "must be narrowly construed" and reflect "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976) (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). The nine statutory exemptions are "explicitly exclusive." *United States Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Administrator, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)). Exemption 2's plain language—"internal personnel rules and practices"—naturally encompasses matters like employee leave policies, parking regulations, and cafeteria rules. It does not naturally encompass technical data about explosive blast ranges that has nothing to do with the Navy's management of its personnel.

The Ninth Circuit's opinion below acknowledged that the ESQD data at issue "measure[s] the effects of an explosion at a particular location" and is used to "design and construct" ammunition storage facilities. This is quintessentially technical and operational information—not a "personnel rule" or "practice" in any ordinary sense of those words. The court's willingness to stretch Exemption 2 to cover such material stems from its adoption of the D.C. Circuit's *Crooker* framework, under which "the words 'personnel rules and practices' encompass not merely minor employment matters" but virtually any agency information the agency deems sensitive. This atextual expansion inverts Congress's careful, narrow exemption scheme.

## II. This Court's Decision in Rose Forecloses the "High 2" Category

In *Rose*, this Court examined Exemption 2 in the context of Air Force Academy case summaries and explicitly limited the exemption's reach. The Court explained that Exemption 2 was intended to relieve agencies of the burden of disclosing information in which "the public could not reasonably be expected to have an interest"—paradigmatically, rules governing "parking facilities, lunch hours, [and] sick leave." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369-70 (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965)). Nothing in *Rose* sanctions a separate "High 2" category permitting withholding of substantive agency records merely because disclosure might complicate the agency's operations.

The Ninth Circuit read *Rose*'s discussion of circumvention risk as license to create a new, broader category of protected material. But *Rose* addressed circumvention only in the narrow context of information that was already a "personnel rule or practice"—not as an independent basis for exemption untethered from the statute's actual subject matter. The court below effectively allowed the circumvention inquiry to swallow the threshold statutory requirement, such that any information whose disclosure might allow a bad actor to evade agency safety measures becomes exempt, regardless of whether that information has anything to do with "personnel rules and practices" at all.

## III. The Ninth Circuit's Own Precedent Required a Narrower Reading

Before the decision below, the Ninth Circuit's leading Exemption 2 case, *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), held that "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation, are exempt under Exemption 2." *Id.* at 656. *Hardy* addressed a discrete category—law enforcement manuals concerning raids and searches—where the connection to "internal personnel rules and practices" (i.e., how agency personnel are trained to conduct their duties) was evident. The Navy's ESQD data bears no resemblance to that category; it is technical explosive-safety information, not a personnel manual describing how Navy employees are trained to perform law-enforcement-type functions.

The panel below, sitting in the same case now before this Court, extended *Hardy* far beyond its logical boundaries by formally adopting the D.C. Circuit's *Crooker* test, see *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), even though *Crooker*'s "predominantly internal" standard finds no footing in *Rose* or in the statutory text. As Judge Reinhardt's dissent below persuasively demonstrated, this expansion has no limiting principle: any technical or operational document might, in some fashion, "risk circumvention" of agency safety protocols if disclosed. That is not what Congress meant by "internal personnel rules and practices."

## IV. Exemption 2 Cannot Be Read to Duplicate Exemption 7 and Exemption 1

Congress enacted separate, carefully calibrated exemptions to address the precise concerns the Navy raises here. Exemption 7(F) protects law-enforcement-compiled records that "could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). Exemption 1 protects properly classified national security information. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). The Navy's proper avenue for protecting the ESQD data, if legitimately sensitive, was to classify it and invoke Exemption 1, or to satisfy Exemption 7(F)'s law-enforcement-compilation requirement.

Instead, the Ninth Circuit permitted the Navy to obtain the practical equivalent of an Exemption 1 or Exemption 7(F) shield through the back door of Exemption 2, without satisfying either provision's requirements. This Court has cautioned against interpretations that would allow one exemption to swallow the specific limitations Congress placed on another. *See Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. Permitting Exemption 2's "internal personnel rules and practices" language to protect blast-radius calculations because their release might theoretically aid a saboteur renders Exemptions 1 and 7(F)'s specific safeguards superfluous.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment below should be reversed. Exemption 2 protects agency "personnel rules and practices," not technical safety data merely because an agency finds disclosure inconvenient or risky in some generalized sense. The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" and "risk of circumvention" test lacks textual grounding, exceeds the boundaries this Court set in *Rose*, and improperly displaces the narrower exemptions Congress specifically crafted for security-sensitive information.
