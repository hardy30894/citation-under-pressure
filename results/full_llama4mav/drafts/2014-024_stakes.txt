ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten. The true threats exception to First Amendment protection requires a speaker to have subjectively intended their statement to be understood as a threat.

The Supreme Court's decision in Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), controls this case. In Black, the Court held that a statute prohibiting cross burning with the intent to intimidate was constitutional because it required proof of the defendant's subjective intent to intimidate. Id. at 362-63. The Court emphasized that "‘[t]rue threats' encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intention to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." Id. at 359. This language establishes that a subjective intent to threaten is a necessary element of a true threat.

The Third Circuit's reliance on its pre-Black decision in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. Kosma held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. Id. at 557. However, Black superseded this objective standard by requiring a subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit's failure to recognize this supersession led to its erroneous affirmance of Elonis' conviction.

Furthermore, the objective standard applied by the Third Circuit and the district court below fails to distinguish between protected speech and true threats. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the First Amendment protects speech that may be disturbing or unpopular. See Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam). An objective standard would criminalize speech that is merely hyperbolic or figurative, without regard to the speaker's intent. In contrast, a subjective standard ensures that only speech intended to threaten is prosecuted.

Requiring proof of subjective intent to threaten is consistent with the language and purpose of 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). The statute prohibits the transmission of communications containing a "threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another." The use of the word "threat" implies a deliberate act, rather than an accidental or unintentional statement. By requiring proof of subjective intent, the government must show that the defendant deliberately communicated a threat, rather than simply making a careless or hyperbolic statement.

In this case, the district court's jury instruction, which defined a true threat as a statement made in a context or under circumstances that would cause a reasonable person to foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat, was erroneous. This instruction allowed the jury to convict Elonis without finding that he subjectively intended to threaten. As a result, Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with a jury instruction requiring proof of subjective intent to threaten.
