# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA'S PLAIN TEXT REQUIRES ONLY THAT THE INJURY RESULT FROM OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF OPERATIONS, NOT THAT THE INJURY OCCUR ON THE SHELF ITSELF

Section 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute's causal language—"resulting from," "occurring as the result of"—asks only whether shelf operations caused the injury. It says nothing about where the injury must occur. This Court's settled interpretive principles counsel against importing a geographic limitation the statute does not contain. *See Connecticut National Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) ("courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says").

Petitioner asks this Court to read into § 1333(b) a "situs-of-injury" requirement that Congress did not enact. But where Congress intended geographic situs limitations elsewhere in OCSLA, it said so explicitly. Section 1333(a)(2)(A), for example, extends state law as surrogate federal law specifically to "the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Section 1333(b) contains no comparable locational language; it is purely causal. "Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," this Court has long recognized, "it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion." *Russello v. United States*, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983).

## PETITIONER'S RELIANCE ON *TALLENTIRE* DICTUM IS MISPLACED

Petitioner leans heavily on a footnote in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), suggesting that § 1333(b) imposes a "situs requirement." But that footnote is precisely what this Court has long instructed lower courts to disregard when unsupported by analysis: unconsidered dicta on an issue neither briefed nor decided. The *Tallentire* Court itself acknowledged that "this case does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b)," and that the provision therefore "has no bearing on this case." 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. The statement was pure dictum, offered without argument, briefing, or analysis of the causal text actually before this Court now.

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and their "possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821)). Dicta unaccompanied by reasoning carries no controlling weight, particularly where—as here—the underlying statutory text is unambiguous.

## THE FIFTH CIRCUIT'S SITUS-BASED APPROACH IN *MILLS* CANNOT BE RECONCILED WITH THE STATUTE'S CAUSAL LANGUAGE

The Fifth Circuit's en banc decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989), grafted a situs-of-injury requirement onto § 1333(b) by relying on this Court's decision in *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985). But *Herb's Welding* addressed an entirely different question: whether a worker on a platform in state territorial waters—not on the shelf itself—qualified as engaged in "maritime employment" under the Longshore Act's status test. Id. at 415-16, 422-24. The decision never construed the causal language of § 1333(b), and its observations about geographic limits concerned the incorporation of the Longshore Act's situs requirement for maritime employment generally, not an independent situs-of-injury test for OCSLA claims specifically.

By contrast, the Third Circuit's decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), correctly focused on the statute's actual causation requirement. There, an employee injured in a car accident while traveling to meet a helicopter that would transport him to an offshore platform qualified for OCSLA benefits because his injury satisfied a "but for" causal relationship to shelf operations. Id. at 809-11. The *Curtis* court properly recognized that *Tallentire*'s footnote was dictum and "simply not on point" to the causal inquiry § 1333(b) demands. Id. at 810.

## A CAUSAL-NEXUS TEST FAITHFULLY IMPLEMENTS CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND SOUND WORKERS' COMPENSATION PRINCIPLES

This Court has consistently construed the Longshore Act and its extensions with an eye toward the remedial purposes Congress sought to achieve for injured maritime workers. *See P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 82-83 (1979) (extending Longshore Act coverage to workers engaged in "intermediate steps" of maritime cargo operations, recognizing the Act's broad remedial scope). A situs-of-injury requirement would produce arbitrary results: a worker injured while performing shelf-related duties moments after stepping onto a supply boat would receive compensation, while an identical worker performing identical shelf-related work who happened to be injured while unloading platform equipment onshore would not. Nothing in the statutory text or purpose supports such an arbitrary distinction based on the fortuity of geography rather than the reality of causation.

The proper test—and the one the court below correctly adopted—asks whether "a substantial nexus" exists "between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf." This standard honors the statute's plain causal language, avoids the dictum-driven detour of *Mills*, and ensures that workers whose injuries genuinely result from shelf operations receive the compensation Congress intended, regardless of the fortuitous location where the injury occurred.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's holding that § 1333(b) contains no situs-of-injury requirement, and that coverage extends to injuries bearing a substantial causal nexus to outer Continental Shelf operations.
