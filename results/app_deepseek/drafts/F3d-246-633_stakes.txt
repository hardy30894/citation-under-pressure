ARGUMENT

**I. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Smoot’s Railway Labor Act conspiracy claim because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding a coordinated effort to deprive him of stock shares.**

Summary judgment is proper only when “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.” *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.,* 477 U.S. 242, 247 (1986). We review *de novo*. The evidence—including the executive-session tape and the PLB’s adverse decision—shows that UTU representative Earley argued against Smoot’s claim during the closed session, contradicting the union’s official position. A reasonable jury could infer that Earley coordinated with CSX representative Emerick to ensure Smoot would lose his shares. Such collusion would constitute a breach of the union’s duty of fair representation under *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R.,* 323 U.S. 192 (1944), and a conspiracy under the RLA. The district court improperly weighed this evidence and resolved inferences against Smoot, contrary to *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.,* 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986) (genuine issues require trial if “the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could return a verdict for the nonmoving party”).

**II. The district court erred in awarding $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and $86,308.74 in attorneys’ fees under the Federal Wiretap Act.**

A. The executive session was not a private communication subject to 18 U.S.C. § 2511. The Act protects only communications “uttered by a person exhibiting an expectation that such communication is not subject to interception under circumstances justifying such expectation.” 18 U.S.C. § 2510(2). The PLB executive session was part of a formal arbitration proceeding—a quasi-public function. Participants knew that matters discussed could later become part of the record or be disclosed in related litigation. No reasonable expectation of privacy existed. *Cf. Katz v. United States,* 389 U.S. 347, 361 (1967) (Harlan, J., concurring) (subjective expectation must be objectively reasonable). Multiple courts have held that recording in official proceedings does not violate the Act. *See, e.g., United States v. Torres,* 751 F.2d 875, 882 (7th Cir. 1984) (no interception when participant records meeting with no expectation of privacy).

B. Even if the Act applied, Smoot’s recording was not an “interception” under § 2510(4) because he did not acquire the communication “through the use of any electronic, mechanical, or other device” during its transmission. He left visible recorders in the room before the session began; the devices were not hidden or surreptitious. The session participants could have observed the recorders. Smoot’s conduct thus falls outside the statute’s core prohibition against clandestine surveillance. *See United States v. New York Tel. Co.,* 434 U.S. 159, 166-67 (1977) (interception requires contemporaneous acquisition of contents).

C. The damages award is excessive and unsupported. Statutory damages under § 2520(c)(2)(B) require a showing of willful violation, *see S.Rep. No. 99-541, at 30 (1986)*, but Smoot acted in good faith, believing he had a right to record the proceeding to preserve evidence for his grievance. No evidence shows he knew or should have known that recording violated the Act. The $250,000 award—$50,000 per participant—is arbitrary and not tied to any actual injury, as Smoot never published the tape. Punitive damages require “reckless disregard of the law,” *see Rodgers v. Wood,* 910 F.2d 444, 452 (7th Cir. 1990), absent here. The attorneys’ fee award is likewise disproportionate.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the summary judgment on the RLA claim and vacate the Wiretp Act damages award.
