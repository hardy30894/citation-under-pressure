ARGUMENT

I. A CREDIBLE CLAIM OF ACTUAL INNOCENCE, STANDING ALONE, WARRANTS EQUITABLE TOLLING OF AEDPA'S LIMITATIONS PERIOD.

This Court has already answered the question presented. In Souter v. Jones, 395 F.3d 577 (6th Cir. 2005), this Court held that "the AEDPA statute of limitations is subject to equitable tolling" when a habeas petitioner presents a credible claim of actual innocence, without imposing a freestanding diligence prerequisite. 395 F.3d at 599, 602. That holding rests on the Supreme Court's recognition, in Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298 (1995), that a credible showing of actual innocence functions as "a gateway through which a habeas petitioner must pass to have his otherwise barred constitutional claim considered on the merits." Id. at 315. The Schlup gateway is not a procedural nicety; it exists because "concern about the injustice that results from the conviction of an innocent person has long been at the core of our criminal justice system." Id. at 325. The Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle a decade later in House v. Bell, 547 U.S. 518 (2006), explaining that the actual-innocence exception exists precisely to prevent "a fundamental miscarriage of justice," id. at 536, and that the standard requires only that "it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have convicted" the petitioner in light of the new evidence, id. at 538 (quoting Schlup, 513 U.S. at 327).

Nothing in Schlup or House conditions the actual-innocence gateway on a separate showing of "reasonable diligence." To the contrary, Schlup instructs courts to consider "the timing of the submission and the likely credibility of the affiants" as part of the merits inquiry into whether the new evidence is reliable — not as an independent, threshold diligence requirement that forecloses review altogether. Schlup, 513 U.S. at 332. Timing bears on credibility; it is not a jurisdictional gate that slams shut regardless of the strength of the innocence showing. The district court's approach — first assuming, then rejecting, Perkins's evidence on the merits, and then holding that diligence was required in any event — inverts this framework and would permit courts to bar consideration of powerful innocence evidence for no reason other than delay, even where that delay does not undermine the evidence's reliability.

II. PACE v. DiGUGLIELMO DOES NOT IMPOSE A DILIGENCE REQUIREMENT ON THE SCHLUP ACTUAL-INNOCENCE GATEWAY.

The Warden's reliance on Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced because Pace addressed an entirely different species of equitable tolling. In Pace, the petitioner sought tolling because he had mistakenly believed his state post-conviction petition was properly filed; the Court held that "a petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling only if he shows (1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way." 544 U.S. at 418. That two-part test governs conventional equitable tolling claims premised on external impediments to timely filing — attorney error, state interference, and the like. It says nothing about the distinct, substantive gateway that Schlup and House erected to prevent the "conviction of one who is actually innocent." Schlup, 513 U.S. at 325.

Pace itself never mentions actual innocence, nor does it purport to overrule or narrow Schlup's holding that a credible innocence showing permits merits review of an otherwise barred claim. Had the Supreme Court intended Pace to graft a diligence requirement onto the fundamental-miscarriage-of-justice exception — an exception with a distinct constitutional pedigree rooted in Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986), and Schlup itself — it would have said so. Absent such an instruction, this Court should not read Pace's ordinary equitable-tolling framework into the actual-innocence gateway that Souter already recognized as controlling in this Circuit. 395 F.3d at 599.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT'S RULE WOULD GUT THE ACTUAL-INNOCENCE EXCEPTION AND SHOULD BE REJECTED.

If diligence were an absolute precondition to the Schlup gateway, meritorious innocence claims discovered late — through no fault of the petitioner, or despite reasonable effort — would be forever barred no matter how compelling the new evidence. That result cannot be squared with Schlup's admonition that "the individual interest in avoiding injustice is most compelling in the context of actual innocence." 513 U.S. at 324. It likewise cannot be squared with House, where the Court permitted review notwithstanding significant delay because the "central" concern remains whether "it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have convicted." 547 U.S. at 538.

Perkins's affidavits — implicating the State's own eyewitness in the killing — raise precisely the kind of doubt the gateway exists to address. Whatever weight the district court might properly assign to the timing of Perkins's submission in assessing credibility under Schlup, it was error to treat diligence as an independent, dispositive bar.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that reasonable diligence is not a freestanding precondition to invoking the actual-innocence gateway, vacate the judgment below, and remand for consideration of Perkins's petition on the merits.
