The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 559 U.S. 133 (2010) ("Johnson II"), regarding what constitutes a violent offense under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015) ("Johnson III"), which held that the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, is a substantive rule that applies retroactively.

The Court's decision in Welch's case is controlled by the Supreme Court's holding in Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), which established that a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively if it is substantive or a "watershed rule of criminal procedure." The rule announced in Johnson III is substantive because it "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). By holding that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague, the Court narrowed the scope of the ACCA and altered the range of conduct that is punishable under the statute.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in Welch's case, which held that his Florida strong arm robbery conviction was a violent felony under the ACCA, was based on the residual clause. However, in light of Johnson III, it is unclear whether Welch's conviction qualifies as a violent felony. The Florida robbery statute under which Welch was convicted criminalized conduct that may not have involved the use of physical force, as required by the elements clause of the ACCA. See Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1); United States v. Lockley, 632 F.3d 1238, 1246 (11th Cir. 2011).

The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson II, which held that the term "physical force" in the ACCA means "violent force—that is, force capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person," 559 U.S. at 140, is relevant to Welch's case. The Eleventh Circuit's decision in United States v. Chitwood, 676 F.3d 971 (11th Cir. 2012), which held that a conviction under the Florida robbery statute qualified as a violent felony under the ACCA, was based on the residual clause. However, in light of Johnson III, Chitwood is no longer good law.

Therefore, the rule announced in Johnson II and Johnson III applies retroactively to Welch's case, and his sentence under the ACCA should be vacated. The case should be remanded to the district court for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.
