# ARGUMENT

## I. AN INMATE HAS NO PROTECTED LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROCEEDINGS

### A. Clemency Is an Act of Grace, Not a Constitutional Entitlement

The Due Process Clause protects only interests in life, liberty, or property. This Court has long recognized that executive clemency stands apart from the ordinary criminal process precisely because it is discretionary grace, not judicial adjudication. As this Court held in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), "an inmate has 'no constitutional or inherent right' to commutation of his sentence," and the mere existence of a state clemency mechanism—even one exercised in a consistent pattern over many years—creates no liberty interest triggering due process protection. *Id.* at 464 (quoting *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979)). The Court explained that "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.*

This principle is not new. In *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. 87 (1925), Chief Justice Taft described the pardon power as one committed to executive discretion since the framing, reflecting "the paramount interest of the whole people" rather than any individual entitlement of the offender. *Id.* at 120. Clemency has never been treated as an extension of the trial or appellate process; it is a separate, extraordinary avenue for mercy that lies outside the domain of judicially enforceable rights. *See Herrera v. Collins*, 506 U.S. 390, 411–12 (1993) (clemency is the "fail safe" of the criminal justice system precisely because it operates outside ordinary legal constraints).

### B. The Sixth Circuit Erred in Manufacturing a Liberty Interest From "Distance" to Trial

The court below correctly held—and Woodard has not cross-petitioned to disturb—that no liberty interest arises from the clemency proceeding itself. But the panel majority erred in nonetheless holding that "residual" liberty interests from trial somehow attach as clemency approaches, requiring unspecified "minimal" process. That novel theory finds no footing in this Court's precedent and contradicts *Dumschat* directly.

*Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), on which the Sixth Circuit relied, is inapposite. *Evitts* held that once a State creates a first appeal as of right, due process requires that the appeal be adequate and effective, because such appeal is "an integral part of the ... system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant." *Id.* at 393 (quoting *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956)). But clemency is not an appeal; it is not part of adjudicating guilt or fixing sentence at all. It is, in Chief Justice Taft's words, an act lying "outside the judicial system." *Ex parte Grossman*, 267 U.S. at 120. Indeed, this Court has already declined to extend *Evitts*-type reasoning to postconviction proceedings once removed from trial: the right to counsel does not extend to habeas corpus proceedings, even though habeas is far more closely tied to "adjudicating guilt" than clemency could ever be. *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974); *see Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394 (citing *Ross*).

Moreover, this Court has cautioned against pushing due process logic to its breaking point merely because some logical thread can be drawn between two proceedings. In *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), the Court retreated from prior liberty-interest jurisprudence precisely because it had created "disincentives for States to codify prison management procedures in the interest of uniform treatment." *Id.* at 483. The same logic counsels against constitutionalizing clemency: if States fear that any structured clemency procedure risks judicial oversight, they will have less incentive to provide such procedures at all.

## II. THE VOLUNTARY CLEMENCY INTERVIEW OPTION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

### A. No Compulsion Exists Where Participation Is Voluntary

The Fifth Amendment protects against *compelled* self-incrimination; it does not bar a State from offering an inmate the *option* to speak. Ohio's regulation does not require Woodard to answer any question. He may request an interview or decline one; if he participates, he may answer some questions and refuse others. Nothing in the regulation compels a waiver of the privilege.

This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, the Court held that permitting adverse inferences from an inmate's silence in a prison disciplinary hearing did not violate the Fifth Amendment, because the inmate remained free to remain silent; he simply might suffer some consequence from that choice. The Court further held that any claim about *future* use of such statements in a subsequent criminal proceeding was "premature." *Id.* at 324. The same reasoning applies with even greater force here: Woodard has affirmatively stated that he "does not contemplate that this action will have any effect on his ... sentences," and any risk of future incrimination is wholly speculative, resting on proceedings that have not been—and may never be—initiated.

### B. The "Unconstitutional Conditions" Doctrine Does Not Apply

Respondent's argument that the interview option forces a "Hobson's choice" mistakes voluntary opportunity for coercion. The unconstitutional-conditions doctrine applies where the State conditions a benefit on the sacrifice of a constitutional right in a manner that leaves the individual no genuine choice. Here, Woodard loses nothing by declining the interview: his clemency application proceeds on the written record and any other materials submitted by counsel, exactly as it would if he had never been offered an interview at all. The interview is a pure addition to, not a subtraction from, the process otherwise available. There is no forced surrender of any right—only an optional opportunity that a rational petitioner may weigh as he sees fit.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
