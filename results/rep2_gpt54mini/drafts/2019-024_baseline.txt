**Argument**

ERISA’s text is straightforward: the three-year limitations period runs only “after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). Congress did not say “access to information,” “constructive knowledge,” or “could have known.” It required *actual* knowledge. This Court has repeatedly treated that requirement as a real one. See **Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan**, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988); **Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.**, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990); **Waller v. Blue Cross of Cal.**, 32 F.3d 1337, 1342–43 (9th Cir. 1994).

The Ninth Circuit erred by collapsing “actual knowledge” into constructive knowledge. It held that Intel’s disclosures were enough even though respondent testified that he did not recall reading them and was unaware that his accounts were invested in hedge funds and private equity. That approach cannot be squared with the statute or this Court’s precedents.

This Court has said that “actual knowledge” does not require knowledge of the law, but it does require awareness of the underlying facts that constitute the breach. In **Blanton v. Anzalone**, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985), the Court held that the statute was triggered by knowledge of “the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [the plaintiff’s] knowledge of the law.” **Meagher**, 856 F.2d at 1423, repeated that rule. **Waller**, 32 F.3d at 1341–43, likewise asked whether the plaintiffs knew the material facts of the challenged transaction. None of those cases held that merely making information available, without proof the plaintiff actually saw and understood it, is enough.

That is the critical distinction. A participant cannot have “actual knowledge” of a fiduciary breach if he never read the disclosure and never became aware of the relevant facts. The word “actual” does work in the statute. See **In re Internet Navigation Ass’n v. U.S. Dep’t of Justice**, 336 F.3d 211, 213 (2d Cir. 2003) (actual knowledge means “what is known” as opposed to what could be known). ERISA’s structure confirms the point: Congress also supplied a separate six-year outside limit, 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1), showing that the three-year period is the shorter, knowledge-based bar and should not be expanded into a constructive-knowledge rule. See **Tibble v. Edison Int’l**, 575 U.S. 523, 528–29 (2015).

The Ninth Circuit’s rule also defeats Congress’s choice to use “actual knowledge” rather than a filed-report or inquiry-notice standard. Before 1987, ERISA expressly included a constructive-knowledge provision based on public filings. See **Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma**, 909 F.3d 1065, 1074–75 (9th Cir. 2018). Congress removed that language in 1987. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. It would be anomalous to delete the constructive-knowledge language only to revive it under a different name. See **Mertens v. Hewitt Assocs.**, 508 U.S. 248, 261 (1993) (courts should not expand ERISA beyond its text).

Intel will likely argue that respondent had access to plan disclosures and therefore should be charged with knowledge. But access is not actual awareness. The statute does not ask when the plaintiff *could have* learned the facts. It asks when he *had* knowledge. This Court’s cases support that reading. In **Waller**, 32 F.3d at 1342, the Court emphasized the participants’ awareness of the transaction itself. In **Ziegler**, 916 F.2d at 552, the defendants were told that the challenged investment choice would have a specified consequence. And in **Blanton**, 760 F.2d at 991, the trustees themselves were parties to the transaction. Those cases involved actual awareness, not mere availability of information.

The consequences of the Ninth Circuit’s approach are severe. ERISA fiduciary-breach claims often depend on complex investment decisions buried in dense plan materials. If mere disclosure suffices, then fiduciaries can trigger the three-year bar by posting documents that participants never read. That would turn § 1113(2) into a constructive-notice rule and erode ERISA’s remedial purpose. See **Varity Corp. v. Howe**, 516 U.S. 489, 496–97 (1996); **Braden v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.**, 588 F.3d 585, 598 (8th Cir. 2009).

Because respondent did not have actual knowledge until he discovered the alleged imprudent investments, his suit—filed within six years—was timely. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
