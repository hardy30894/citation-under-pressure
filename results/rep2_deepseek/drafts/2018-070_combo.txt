**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) does not permit state law to apply whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law. Rather, state law may be adopted as surrogate federal law only when necessary to fill a gap in federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statute and conflicts with this Court’s precedent.

**I. OCSLA’s Text and Structure Require a Gap-Filling Approach**

OCSLA declares that federal law extends to the outer Continental Shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law is then incorporated only “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with … Federal laws.” § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting condition. It means state law applies only insofar as federal law does not already cover the subject. This Court has long interpreted analogous gap-filling provisions in the same way. *See United States v. Brown*, 333 U.S. 18, 25–26 (1948) (Assimilative Crimes Act adopts state law only when federal law is “deficient”); *Johnson v. Yellow Cab Transit Co.*, 321 U.S. 383, 389 (1944) (federal enclave statute adopts state law only “in the absence of federal regulation”).

Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The purpose was to fill gaps, not to supplant comprehensive federal schemes. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365–66 (1969) (state law applied under OCSLA only because federal maritime law did not cover fixed platforms). Where federal law is comprehensive, no gap exists, and state law cannot apply.

**II. This Court’s Precedent Confirms the Gap-Filling Standard**

In *Rodrigue*, this Court held that OCSLA requires fixed platforms to be treated as federal enclaves, not vessels. 395 U.S. at 365. Because the Death on the High Seas Act did not apply to accidents on platforms, there was a “void” that state law could fill. *Id.* at 366. The Court did not apply state law merely because it was “not inconsistent” with federal law; it applied state law because federal law was *absent*. *Id.* at 365–66. That is the gap-filling approach.

The Fifth Circuit correctly articulated this standard in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), holding that state law applies under OCSLA only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. That decision, decided the same year as *Rodrigue*, is consistent with this Court’s reasoning. The Ninth Circuit erred by rejecting the “necessity to fill a significant void or gap” requirement. *See* Pet. App. 4a.

**III. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Scheme That Leaves No Gaps**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive federal scheme governing wages, hours, and overtime. *See Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 706 (1945) (FLSA establishes “a comprehensive federal policy”). The FLSA sets minimum wage and overtime standards, defines the workweek, and provides exemptions. It also includes a savings clause preserving more protective state laws, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), but that clause operates only where state law *otherwise applies*. On the OCS, federal law is the exclusive source of regulation. The savings clause does not create a gap; it merely acknowledges that state law may supplement federal law in areas where Congress intended concurrent jurisdiction. But on the OCS, Congress intended federal law to be the sole governing law absent a gap.

The FLSA’s regulations further demonstrate its comprehensiveness. The Department of Labor has issued detailed rules on meal periods, on-call time, and recordkeeping. *See* 29 C.F.R. §§ 785.19, 785.16. These regulations leave no room for state law to operate. *Cf. Pacific Coast Dairy v. Dep’t of Agriculture*, 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943) (federal regulation of milk prices precluded state law on federal enclave). Because the FLSA and its regulations cover the field, there is no “significant void or gap” for California’s wage and hour laws to fill.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Would Undermine Uniformity**

Congress enacted OCSLA to ensure uniform federal law on the outer Continental Shelf. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365. Allowing state law to apply whenever it is “not inconsistent” would subject platform workers to a patchwork of state laws, defeating that purpose. This Court has consistently rejected such results in analogous contexts. *See James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 148 (1937) (federal enclave law requires uniformity); *United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 294 (1958) (Assimilative Crimes Act ensures uniform federal criminal law). The Ninth Circuit’s rule would permit California’s meal-period and overtime laws to apply on the OCS, even though the FLSA already provides a comprehensive regime. That is precisely the outcome OCSLA was designed to avoid.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. State law applies under OCSLA only when necessary to fill a gap in federal law. Because the FLSA is a comprehensive scheme that leaves no gaps, California’s wage and hour laws cannot apply to Newton’s claims.
