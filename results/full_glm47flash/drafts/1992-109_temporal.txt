**Argument**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a heightened "reasoned choice" standard to determine a defendant's competency to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Supreme Court has long held that the waiver of fundamental rights, such as the right to counsel, demands a "knowing, intelligent, and voluntary" decision. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that a defendant must be "fully advised" of his rights and must "reasonably choose" to waive them. The Court emphasized that "the right to be heard would be hollow, if one could not be heard unless he spoke intelligently."

This standard is distinct from, and higher than, the standard for competency to stand trial. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), the Court defined competency to stand trial as a "rational and factual understanding" of the proceedings. However, a defendant's ability to stand trial is a lower threshold than the ability to make a reasoned choice among complex legal alternatives. As the Court noted in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), a guilty plea is a "total surrender of all constitutional rights" except those that can be waived in a plea. Because Moran’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas involved the surrender of numerous fundamental rights, the trial court was required to ensure he possessed the capacity for a "reasoned choice" among the alternatives.

The record demonstrates Moran lacked the capacity for reasoned choice. He was heavily medicated with four different drugs, including sedatives and antipsychotics, at the time of the plea. His responses were monosyllabic, and he sought to waive counsel solely to prevent the presentation of mitigating evidence. Under the *Zerbst* and *Boykin* standards, such a waiver cannot be deemed knowing, intelligent, or voluntary. Therefore, the Due Process Clause mandates the "reasoned choice" standard.
