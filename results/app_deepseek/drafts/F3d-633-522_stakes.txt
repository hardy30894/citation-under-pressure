**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Holding That the Severance Releases Barred Appellants’ WARN Act Claims**

The releases executed by Appellants are invalid as a matter of law because they were neither knowing nor voluntary. The WARN Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101-2109, serves a uniquely protective purpose: to ensure workers receive 60 days’ advance notice of mass layoffs. *See United Steelworkers v. North Star Steel Co.*, 5 F.3d 39, 43 (3d Cir. 1993). A waiver of WARN Act rights must be measured against the “totality of the circumstances” to determine whether it was knowingly and voluntarily made. *Chaganti v. IIT Research Institute*, 2007 WL 581853, at *7 (N.D. Ill. Feb. 20, 2007) (collecting cases). Here, the circumstances demonstrate coercion, not consent.

First, the decision period for the ten-week plan was a mere two days: December 9 to December 11, 2008. See *Record* at 12–13. Courts have repeatedly held that such compressed timeframes undermine the voluntariness of a release. *See, e.g., Bormann v. AT&T Communications, Inc.*, 875 F.2d 399, 403 (2d Cir. 1989) (seven days “insufficient” for informed waiver of federal claims). The seven-day consideration period referenced in the release (App. 15) is illusory when the employer’s deadline for acceptance was only 48 hours.

Second, the economic duress was overwhelming. DHL had announced the facility closures on November 10, 2008, giving workers no realistic alternative to accepting the severance. *See* Complaint ¶ 21. The Supreme Court has recognized that waivers obtained under “extreme economic pressure” are invalid. *Brooklyn Savings Bank v. O’Neil*, 324 U.S. 697, 703 (1945) (FLSA waivers). The same principle applies to WARN Act claims. *See also EEOC v. Waffle House, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 279, 296 (2002) (releases of federal rights must be “knowing and voluntary”).

Third, the release swept far beyond the scope of any legitimate settlement. It purported to waive claims under the National Labor Relations Act, discrimination laws, and state WARN Acts—yet the negotiations were limited to severance benefits. *See* App. 18. Such “blanket” releases are presumptively invalid when the employee received no consideration beyond the severance itself. *See Torrez v. Public Service Co. of New Mexico*, 908 F.2d 687, 689 (10th Cir. 1990) (release of unknown claims invalid absent separate consideration).

**II. DHL Violated the WARN Act’s 60-Day Notice Requirement**

The WARN Act requires an employer to provide 60 days’ notice before a “mass layoff.” 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). DHL’s November 10, 2008, announcement that it would end domestic shipping on January 30, 2009, triggered this obligation. The layoffs of 506 workers at five facilities unquestionably qualify as a “mass layoff” under § 2101(a)(3). DHL provided only 50 days’ notice—10 days short of the statutory minimum.

DHL’s only conceivable defense is the “faltering company” exception, 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b)(1). But that exception is “narrowly construed” and requires that notice would “preclude” the employer from obtaining “new business or financing.” *Local 217, Hotel & Restaurant Employees Union v. MHM, Inc.*, 976 F.2d 805, 809 (2d Cir. 1992). DHL was a global enterprise; there is no evidence that a 60-day notice would have hindered its operations. *See* Complaint ¶ 42. The district court never even reached this issue because it erroneously relied on the releases.

**III. The Releases Cannot Be Used to Evade the WARN Act’s Remedial Scheme**

The WARN Act provides for back pay, benefits, and civil penalties. 29 U.S.C. § 2104(a). Permitting these releases to stand would allow employers to evade the Act’s protective purpose by offering modest severance in exchange for waiving statutory rights. This Court should not countenance such a result. *See In re Majestic Star Casino, LLC*, 716 F.3d 736, 747 (3d Cir. 2013) (WARN Act waivers must be “supervised” by the Secretary of Labor or a court).

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the releases are invalid, and DHL violated the WARN Act. The judgment of the district court should be reversed.
