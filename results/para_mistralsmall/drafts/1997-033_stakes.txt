**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Transferor District for Trial and Precludes Self-Assignment Under Section 1404(a)**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) resolves this case. It provides that after pretrial proceedings, the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall remand” transferred actions “to the district from which [they were] transferred.” *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26, 33 (1998). This command is mandatory. *See id.* (“The statute’s text is unambiguous: the Panel ‘shall remand’ transferred actions ‘to the district from which [they were] transferred.’”). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary interpretation—allowing a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial—directly contravenes the statute. *See id.* at 34 (rejecting the argument that § 1404(a) permits self-assignment).

The structure of § 1407 reinforces this reading. Section 1407(a) authorizes transfer only for “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). It does not empower the Panel to assign cases for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 35. The statute’s remand requirement ensures that plaintiffs retain their “ancient right to select the forum” for trial. *Id.* at 36 (quoting *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Chicago, Ill.*, 644 F.2d 594, 600 (7th Cir. 1981)). The Ninth Circuit’s approach—permitting transferee courts to circumvent remand by invoking § 1404(a)—“subvert[s] the statutory scheme.” *Id.* at 37.

**II. The Multidistrict Litigation Rules Do Not Authorize Self-Assignment**

The Ninth Circuit relied on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) to justify the transferee court’s self-assignment. *See* 61 F.3d at 914. But Rule 14(b) cannot override the statute. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 38. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that judicial rules cannot enlarge statutory authority. *See id.* (citing *United States v. More*, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 159, 172 (1805)). Rule 14(b) purports to authorize § 1404(a) transfers to the transferee court, but § 1407(a) expressly limits the Panel’s authority to pretrial proceedings. *Id.* at 39. The rule cannot expand that authority to permit trial in the transferee district. *Id.*

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Those cases predated *Lexecon* and did not address the Supreme Court’s later interpretation of § 1407(a). *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40. Moreover, they rested on policy concerns, not statutory text. *Id.* This Court has rejected such extra-textual reasoning. *See id.* at 41.

**III. Section 1404(a) Does Not Permit Self-Assignment in Multidistrict Litigation**

Section 1404(a) authorizes transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). But it does not permit a transferee court to retain a case for trial after § 1407(a) remand is due. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 42. The statute’s text and structure distinguish between pretrial and trial proceedings. *Id.* at 43. Section 1407(a) governs pretrial transfers; § 1404(a) governs venue changes for trial. *Id.* The Ninth Circuit’s conflation of the two “ignores the statutory division of labor.” *Id.*

The Supreme Court has emphasized that § 1404(a) cannot be used to evade § 1407(a)’s remand requirement. *Id.* at 44. Allowing transferee courts to invoke § 1404(a) for self-assignment would render § 1407(a)’s remand mandate a nullity. *Id.* at 45. This Court must reject such an interpretation. *Id.*

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Approach Undermines the Purposes of Section 1407**

Congress enacted § 1407 to centralize pretrial proceedings in complex cases while preserving plaintiffs’ choice of forum for trial. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 46. The Ninth Circuit’s approach frustrates this purpose by permitting transferee courts to retain cases for trial, often far from the transferor district. *Id.* at 47. This undermines the statute’s efficiency goals and deprives plaintiffs of their right to trial in their chosen forum. *Id.* at 48.

The dissent in *Lexecon* recognized this danger. It warned that allowing transferee courts to “glom onto” transferred cases “subvert[s] the statutory mandate” and “undercut[s] the important policy of § 1407.” *Id.* at 49 (quoting 61 F.3d at 916 (Kozinski, J., dissenting)). This Court should heed that warning. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit erred in permitting the transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial. Section 1407(a) mandates remand to the transferor district, and § 1404(a) cannot be used to circumvent that requirement. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remand with instructions to vacate the transferee court’s self-assignment order.
