# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS MERELY BECAUSE SUBMERGED LANDS ARE STATE-OWNED

Statutory interpretation begins and ends with the text where, as here, that text is unambiguous. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (courts must give effect to the unambiguously expressed intent of Congress). Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that only the "public lands" within the boundaries of a conservation system unit "shall be deemed" part of that unit, and that non-public lands "shall not be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added). The critical word is "solely." The hovercraft ban, codified at 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not a regulation that applies solely to public lands within conservation system units—it applies to all lands and waters administered by the National Park Service nationwide, public and private alike. Because the regulation does not apply *solely* to public lands within Alaska's conservation system units, § 103(c)'s carve-out is inapplicable by its own terms.

This reading follows directly from the interpretive principle that Congress "says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says." *Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992). Petitioner's construction would require this Court to read the word "solely" out of the statute, a result courts must avoid. *See Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) ("[C]ourt[s] should give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute.").

## II. ANILCA'S SAVINGS CLAUSES CONFIRM THAT CONGRESS PRESERVED EXISTING FEDERAL REGULATORY AUTHORITY

Even if the text of § 103(c) were ambiguous, ANILCA's structure forecloses Petitioner's reading. Section 1319 of ANILCA expressly provides that "[n]othing in [the statute] shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions." 16 U.S.C. § 3207. This savings clause demonstrates that Congress did not intend ANILCA to work a wholesale divestiture of NPS's pre-existing regulatory authority over conservation system units, including authority over navigable waters within those units. Where Congress includes an express savings clause preserving existing agency authority, courts should not construe other provisions of the same statute to accomplish, sub silentio, the very divestiture the savings clause forecloses. *Cf. Food & Drug Admin. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.*, 529 U.S. 120, 133 (2000) (statutes must be read as a symmetrical and coherent regulatory scheme).

## III. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT'S NAVIGATIONAL SERVITUDE AND COMMERCE CLAUSE AUTHORITY SUPPLY AN INDEPENDENT BASIS FOR REGULATION REGARDLESS OF TITLE TO SUBMERGED LANDS

Petitioner's argument conflates title to submerged lands with regulatory authority over activities occurring on the waters above them. These are distinct legal questions. As Justice Scalia recognized in his separate opinion in *Alaska v. United States*, 545 U.S. 75 (2005), "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters . . . by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." *Id.* at 112 (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). The federal navigational servitude is not extinguished by state ownership of the riverbed. *See United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967) ("[T]he interest of the United States in the flow of a navigable stream originates in the Commerce Clause. . . . [and] the United States . . . has a dominant servitude, which extends to the entire stream and the stream bed below ordinary high water mark."). Consequently, whether or not the State holds title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River under the Submerged Lands Act or the *Equal Footing Doctrine*, the United States retains independent authority to regulate activities—including hovercraft use—on the navigable waters within Yukon-Charley.

## IV. AGENCY INTERPRETATION IS ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE AND PETITIONER'S RELIANCE ON PRIOR NPS PRACTICE IS UNAVAILING

To the extent the Court finds any residual ambiguity in § 103(c), NPS's interpretation—that its regulations apply to all lands and waters within conservation system unit boundaries regardless of who holds subsurface title—is a reasonable construction entitled to deference under *Chevron*, 467 U.S. at 844. Petitioner's suggestion that NPS's pre-1996 practice of non-enforcement on state lands undermines this interpretation is legally irrelevant. As the Court has held, "[a]gency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency's interpretation under the *Chevron* framework." *National Cable & Telecommunications Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Services*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005). An agency remains free to revise its interpretation so long as the revised interpretation is itself reasonable and consistent with the statutory text—which NPS's current position plainly is.

## V. THE STATE'S SOVEREIGNTY ARGUMENTS DO NOT ALTER THE STATUTORY ANALYSIS

Petitioner's invocation of state sovereignty cannot substitute for statutory text. While states possess legitimate sovereign and quasi-sovereign interests that may support standing in appropriate cases, *Alfred L. Snapp & Son, Inc. v. Puerto Rico ex rel. Barez*, 458 U.S. 592, 601-02 (1982), those interests do not license courts to disregard the plain terms of a statute Congress enacted pursuant to its Property Clause and Commerce Clause authority over federal lands and navigable waters. Congress was free to condition the scope of NPS's regulatory retreat under § 103(c) precisely as it did—applying that retreat only to regulations that operate *solely* on public lands. This Court should not rewrite that considered legislative judgment under the guise of federalism concerns the statute's text does not support.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit insofar as it held that ANILCA § 103(c) does not preclude enforcement of the hovercraft ban against Respondent Sturgeon within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.
