**ARGUMENT**

**The Pat-Frisk of Michael Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment Because the Officers Lacked Reasonable Suspicion That He Was Armed and Dangerous.**

A police officer may conduct a brief, warrantless pat-frisk of a person only if the officer has “reasonable suspicion” that the person is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). This suspicion must be based on “specific and articulable facts” and rational inferences drawn from those facts, not an “inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or ‘hunch.’” *Id.* at 21. The burden is on the government to demonstrate that the frisk was lawful. *United States v. Romain*, 393 F.3d 63, 71 (1st Cir. 2004).

The traffic stop of the Lexus was valid. But the lawful stop of a vehicle does not, by itself, authorize a pat-frisk of its occupants. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 23. An officer may order a passenger out of a car as a safety measure, *Maryland v. Wilson*, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997), but the removal is not a frisk. To pat-frisk Soares, the officers had to have “a reasonable belief” that he was “armed and presently dangerous.” *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 24. No such belief existed here.

The facts the district court relied on—Soares’s furtive movements, his agitated behavior, and the high-crime location—do not rise to the level of reasonable suspicion. First, the observed movement “down and to their left” was vague and not particularized to Soares. The front-seat passenger motioned toward the console, but the police saw no weapon, no bulge, and no object suggesting a firearm. “Mere furtive movements” are not enough to justify a frisk. *United States v. Trullo*, 809 F.2d 108, 112 (1st Cir. 1987). Second, the high-crime nature of the area is a “relevant contextual consideration,” but it cannot “serve as a substitute” for individualized suspicion. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000). Here, there was nothing tying Soares personally to criminal activity beyond his presence in a car stopped for a minor equipment violation.

Third, Soares’s agitation, profanity, and hand movements do not establish reasonable suspicion. The encounter occurred at 1:15 a.m. with officers approaching with guns drawn and one weapon held in a “low ready” position, clearly visible to Soares. It is “unremarkable” for a citizen to react nervously when confronted by police under such circumstances. *Romain*, 393 F.3d at 73. Soares’s initial compliance, followed by escalating frustration—calling the stop “driving while Black”—was not an indication of criminality. The officer testified he “got a little uneasy,” but subjective unease, without objective factual support, is insufficient. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21.

The government may argue that Soares’s movement toward the console gave the officers reason to suspect a weapon was in the car, justifying the frisk of his person. But that inference cuts the other way: if a weapon was in the console, frisk of Soares’s *body* was not a reasonable means of discovering it. The frisk was conducted only after Soares was removed from the car, giving the officers control of the vehicle. At that point, they could have searched the console if they had any justification. They did not.

Finally, Soares’s reaction to the frisk—swatting the officer’s hand away—cannot retroactively justify what occurred before. The frisk was unlawful from the outset; his resistance does not cure it. *Romain*, 393 F.3d at 75.

Because the pat-frisk was not supported by reasonable suspicion, the loaded handgun obtained as a result of it was the fruit of an unlawful search. The district court’s denial of the motion to suppress should be reversed.
