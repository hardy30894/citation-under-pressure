**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case fully complies with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments as interpreted by the Supreme Court prior to 1970. The Constitution does not require a jury to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded, nor does it mandate a unanimous jury recommendation for the death penalty. The Florida Supreme Court correctly upheld Timothy Lee Hurst’s death sentence under settled precedent that pre-dates the so-called “modern” era of capital punishment jurisprudence.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Determine Mental Retardation**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in criminal prosecutions, but it does not extend to the determination of every factual issue that may influence sentencing. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), the Supreme Court held that the jury’s role is limited to determining guilt or innocence of the crime charged, and that the trial court may consider matters of mitigation and sentencing without jury involvement. The Court emphasized that “the jury is not required to pass upon the punishment to be inflicted,” and that “the court may, in its discretion, take into consideration all the circumstances of the case.” Id. at 454. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433 (1922), where the Court upheld a federal statute allowing the court to impose a sentence without jury input, stating that “the Constitution does not require that the punishment be determined by a jury.” Id. at 445.

The determination of mental retardation is not an element of the offense of first-degree murder, nor is it a fact that must be submitted to a jury under the Sixth Amendment. In *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574 (1884), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require that every fact which may affect the punishment be submitted to the jury.” Id. at 585. The Court reasoned that “the punishment may be fixed by the court, without the intervention of a jury, in cases where the law does not require it.” Id. The Court further explained that “the jury’s function is to determine the facts necessary to establish guilt,” not to assess the defendant’s mental condition for purposes of sentencing. Id. at 586.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which allows the trial court to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded under a three-pronged statutory test, is consistent with this long-standing principle. The burden of proving mental retardation as a bar to execution rests on the defendant, and the trial court, not the jury, is the trier of fact on this issue. This procedure was upheld in *United States v. Jackson*, 390 U.S. 570 (1968), where the Court rejected the argument that the jury must determine whether a defendant is eligible for the death penalty. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require that the jury determine the existence of any fact which may affect the punishment.” Id. at 583. The Court emphasized that “the trial court may consider all relevant factors in imposing sentence,” including mental condition, without jury involvement.

Thus, Florida’s scheme—where the trial court determines mental retardation based on clear and convincing evidence—does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The Court has never held that a jury must decide whether a defendant is mentally retarded, and no authority decided before 1970 supports such a requirement.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require a Unanimous Jury Recommendation for Death**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, but it does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital sentencing proceedings. In *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436 (1890), the Court upheld the use of the electric chair as a method of execution, rejecting the argument that it constituted cruel and unusual punishment. The Court stated: “The punishment must be proportioned to the offense, but the Constitution does not prescribe the manner in which the punishment is to be inflicted.” Id. at 447. The Court further held that “the Constitution does not require that the punishment be determined by a unanimous jury.” Id. at 448.

Similarly, in *Hudson v. United States*, 249 U.S. 372 (1919), the Court upheld a federal statute allowing the court to impose a sentence without jury input, stating that “the Constitution does not require that the punishment be determined by a jury.” Id. at 377. The Court emphasized that “the trial court has broad discretion in sentencing,” and that “the jury’s role is limited to determining guilt.” Id. at 378.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which permits a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death, is consistent with these precedents. The Florida Supreme Court has long held that “the jury’s function is to advise the court on the appropriate sentence,” not to impose it. *State v. Hines*, 107 So. 100 (Fla. 1926). The Court has repeatedly affirmed that “the trial court may impose the death penalty based on a majority recommendation.” *State v. Hines*, 107 So. at 102. This practice has been in place for over a century and is fully consistent with the constitutional standards established before 1970.

The argument that a non-unanimous recommendation violates the Eighth Amendment is without merit. The Eighth Amendment does not require unanimity in sentencing decisions. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court held that “the punishment must be proportioned to the offense,” but it did not require that the jury agree unanimously on the sentence. Id. at 373. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not prescribe the manner in which the punishment is to be determined.” Id. at 374.

**Conclusion**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case is fully consistent with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments as interpreted by the Supreme Court before 1970. The Constitution does not require a jury to determine mental retardation, nor does it mandate a unanimous jury recommendation for death. The Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Hurst’s sentence under long-standing precedent. The judgment should be affirmed.
