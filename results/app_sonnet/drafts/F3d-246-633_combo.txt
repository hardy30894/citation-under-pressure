# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot's RLA Conspiracy Claim Because Genuine Issues of Material Fact Remain as to UTU and CSX's Motive and Intent.

Summary judgment is a blunt instrument, ill-suited to disputes turning on state of mind. In *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), the Supreme Court cautioned that "summary procedures should be used sparingly" in cases "where motive and intent play leading roles," because "the proof is largely in the hands of the alleged conspirators." *Id.* at 473. That admonition governs here. Smoot's claim that UTU and CSX conspired to deprive him of his contractual stock entitlement depends on the good faith of Earley, Emerick, and the PLB executive session—precisely the kind of hidden, subjective inquiry *Poller* found unfit for summary disposition.

The record independently forecloses summary judgment under *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620 (1944), which held that even uncontradicted testimony does not entitle a movant to judgment where reasonable factfinders could draw conflicting inferences from the underlying circumstances. *Id.* at 627. The executive-session recording—in which Earley purportedly argued a position contrary to Smoot's interest before the very CSX representative whose company stood to lose the stock—permits the inference of collusion. That inference, not a contrary one favorable to Defendants, must be drawn in Smoot's favor at this procedural stage. *See Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 45–46 (1957) (relief may not be denied unless it "appears beyond doubt" that the claimant cannot recover under any version of the facts).

Substantively, the RLA imposes on UTU, as Smoot's exclusive bargaining representative, a duty enforceable in the courts to act "without hostile discrimination against" the members it represents. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co.*, 323 U.S. 192, 203 (1944). That duty demands "complete good faith and honesty of purpose in the exercise of its discretion." *Ford Motor Co. v. Huffman*, 345 U.S. 330, 337 (1953). While a union's tactical choices are ordinarily entitled to a "wide range of reasonableness," *id.*, and mere errors of judgment will not support liability, *Humphrey v. Moore*, 375 U.S. 335, 349 (1964), the record here presents more than an error in judgment—it presents evidence that Earley affirmatively worked against Smoot's interests in a closed session from which Smoot himself was excluded. Whether that conduct crossed the line from permissible discretion into bad faith is a jury question, not one properly resolved on a paper record. The district court's contrary conclusion invaded the factfinder's province and should be reversed.

## II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Statutory and Punitive Damages, and Attorneys' Fees, Under the Wiretap Act Counterclaims.

Even if some technical violation of the Wiretap Act occurred, the equitable relief and enhanced damages awarded below cannot stand, for two independent reasons rooted in settled pre-1970 doctrine.

*First*, the premise of Defendants' claim—that the executive session was a protected "oral communication"—is undermined by the governing privacy inquiry. The Supreme Court in *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), taught that privacy protection turns on whether the speaker exhibited "an actual (subjective) expectation of privacy" that "society is prepared to recognize as reasonable," and reminded courts that "the Fourth Amendment protects people, not places." *Id.* at 351, 361 (Harlan, J., concurring). The PLB executive session occurred in a room Smoot had lawfully occupied with recording equipment already present and running; the participants who wished privacy took no measure beyond asking bystanders to leave a room containing operating recorders. Under *Katz*'s reasoning, the expectation of privacy asserted by Earley and CSX was not objectively reasonable, and the statutory predicate for damages was never established.

*Second*, and independently, Earley's and UTU's own conduct bars the equitable and quasi-equitable relief they obtained. It is a maxim of long standing that "he who comes into equity must come with clean hands." *Precision Instrument Mfg. Co. v. Automotive Maintenance Machinery Co.*, 324 U.S. 806, 814 (1945); *accord Keystone Driller Co. v. General Excavator Co.*, 290 U.S. 240, 245 (1933) (denying relief to a party whose own inequitable conduct is "immediately and necessarily connected" with the equity he seeks to enforce). Earley sought and obtained an injunction silencing Smoot's use of the tape at the very moment internal union charges against Earley—charges corroborated by a second union member's independent complaint—were proceeding on their merits. Earley's use of the courts to suppress evidence of his own alleged disloyalty while that disloyalty was under formal union investigation is precisely the "unclean hands" the *Precision Instrument* and *Keystone Driller* doctrine was designed to prevent from receiving equitable sanction. The punitive damages and fee award, both parasitic on the underlying injunctive theory, cannot survive once that unclean-hands bar is applied.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment on Smoot's RLA conspiracy claim and vacate the damages, punitive damages, and fee award entered on Defendants' Wiretap Act counterclaims, and remand for further proceedings.
