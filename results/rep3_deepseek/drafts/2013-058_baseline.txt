**ARGUMENT**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds" from a debtor's estate, but an inherited IRA is not the debtor's retirement funds. The plain text, statutory context, and purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) compel the conclusion that an inherited IRA is subject to creditor claims.

**I. An Inherited IRA Does Not Contain "Retirement Funds" in the Debtor's Hands**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) exempts "retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." The term "retirement funds" is not defined, but its ordinary meaning requires that the funds be held for retirement. An inherited IRA lacks the core attributes of a retirement vehicle.

Unlike a traditional IRA, an inherited IRA prohibits new contributions and cannot be rolled over. 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C). More critically, it must begin distributing assets within one year of the original owner's death, with payout completed in as little as five years. 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B). As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, "an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner's retirement." *Clark v. Rameker*, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013). Because the funds must be distributed before the debtor's retirement, they are not "retirement funds" in any meaningful sense.

The Fifth Circuit's contrary view in *In re Chilton*, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), errs by focusing on the funds' origin rather than their current character. The funds were once Ruth Heffron's retirement funds, but upon her death they "became no one's retirement funds." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 562. The account remains a tax-deferral vehicle, but tax deferral alone does not make funds "retirement funds." *Id.* at 563. If Ruth had withdrawn the $300,000, paid the penalty, and given the cash to Heidi, the money would not be exempt. The result should be no different simply because the transfer occurred through inheritance.

**II. Statutory Context Confirms That Exemptions Depend on the Debtor's Use of the Asset**

The Bankruptcy Code contains numerous exemptions that turn on the debtor's relationship to the property. For example, §522(b)(3)(A) exempts a "domicile" in which the debtor lived for 730 days before filing. If Heidi inherited her mother's house and rented it out, she could not claim the homestead exemption. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 564. The exemption depends on how Heidi used the property, not how her mother used it. "Just so with retirement funds." *Id.*

The Fifth Circuit relied on the fact that §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) omit the phrase "the debtor's," unlike other exemptions. *Chilton*, 674 F.3d at 489. But that omission serves a different purpose: it allows new value added to a retirement fund during bankruptcy to remain exempt, even though new real property or farm tools would not. *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 563. It does not suggest that assets that are not retirement funds at any time during the bankruptcy are exempt simply because they once belonged to someone else. *Id.*

**III. Policy Considerations Do Not Justify Expanding the Exemption**

The Clarks argue that exempting inherited IRAs encourages savings and bequests. But "no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs." *Rodriguez v. United States*, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987). Section 522 provides a specific exemption for retirement funds, not a general exemption for inherited wealth. An inherited IRA "represent[s] an opportunity for current consumption, not a fund of retirement savings." *Clark*, 714 F.3d at 565. Exempting it would allow debtors to shield assets that can be freely spent before retirement, undermining the Bankruptcy Code's goal of equitable distribution to creditors.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
