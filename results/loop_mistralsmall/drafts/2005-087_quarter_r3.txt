**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not confer upon a defendant a freestanding due process right to present expert mental-state evidence solely to negate criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. Arizona’s rule limiting such evidence to the insanity framework comports with due process because it preserves the State’s legitimate interest in defining the elements of its crimes and allocating burdens of proof without transgressing constitutional boundaries.

A defendant’s right to present evidence is not absolute; it is bounded by state rules of admissibility and relevance. *State v. Chapple*, 135 Ariz. 282, 286 (1983) (citing *State v. Mays*, 114 Ariz. 371, 374 (1977)). Arizona has long maintained that criminal intent is an element the State must prove beyond a reasonable doubt, and it has structured its insanity defense as the exclusive vehicle for introducing expert mental-state testimony. *State v. Schantz*, 98 Ariz. 200, 205 (1965). This rule does not offend due process because it neither precludes the defendant from raising an insanity defense nor prevents him from offering relevant evidence in support of that defense. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 484 (1895) (upholding the principle that the government must prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt).

The Due Process Clause does not mandate that a defendant be permitted to use mental-state evidence for a purpose other than that for which it is statutorily authorized. *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937) (holding that due process protects only those rights that are “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty”). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert evidence is consistent with the longstanding principle that the States retain broad authority to define crimes and defenses. *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 594 (1900) (“It is the settled doctrine of this court that the States have the power to prescribe the rules of evidence applicable to judicial proceedings in their own courts.”). The trial court’s ruling did not arbitrarily exclude relevant evidence; it merely confined expert testimony to the statutorily prescribed insanity defense. *State v. White*, 118 Ariz. 12, 15 (1977) (upholding exclusion of psychiatric testimony on mens rea where defendant failed to raise insanity defense).

Moreover, the Due Process Clause does not require that a defendant be permitted to relitigate the same mental-state evidence under a different label. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 177 F. Supp. 134, 137 (D. Me. 1959), aff’d, 366 U.S. 103 (1961) (noting that States may allocate burdens of proof in criminal cases). Arizona’s rule prevents duplicative litigation of the same mental condition and avoids jury confusion by channeling all expert evidence into the insanity framework. *Spencer v. Texas*, 363 S.W.2d 778, 782 (Tex. Crim. App. 1962) (upholding Texas’s bifurcated trial system for determining guilt and punishment).

Finally, this Court has repeatedly recognized that the Due Process Clause does not impose uniform procedural rules on the States. *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934) (“Due process is not a technical conception with a fixed content unrelated to time, place and circumstances.”). Arizona’s approach is consistent with the traditional division of authority between judge and jury in criminal cases. *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276, 288 (1930) (“The essentials of a fair trial—notice, confrontation, and a hearing—remain inviolate.”). The trial court’s ruling did not deprive Clark of a fair trial; it merely enforced Arizona’s longstanding rule that expert mental-state evidence may be used only to support an insanity defense.

In sum, Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert mental-state evidence does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The State has a legitimate interest in defining the scope of its insanity defense and allocating burdens of proof, and its rule does not offend any fundamental principle of due process. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 101 (1908) (“It is possible that some of the personal rights safeguarded by the first eight Amendments against National action may also be safeguarded against state action by the due process of law clause.”).
