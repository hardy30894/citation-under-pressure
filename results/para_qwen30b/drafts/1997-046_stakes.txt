**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from the arbitrary deprivation of life or liberty without due process of law. *Santosky v. Kramer*, 455 U.S. 745, 753 (1982). While the Supreme Court has long recognized that “a State may create a liberty interest by establishing procedures that, if followed, will result in the release of a prisoner,” *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 557 (1974), it has also held that such interests are not limited to those arising solely from prison disciplinary procedures. In *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 12 (1979), the Court acknowledged that a prisoner’s liberty interest in parole is “not a mere statutory privilege,” but a “protected liberty interest” when the state has created a structured process for release. The same logic applies here: Ohio’s clemency process is not a mere administrative formality, but a structured, state-created mechanism that directly affects the duration of a prisoner’s confinement and, in the case of a death sentence, the very fact of his continued existence.

The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency review is not a discretionary act of grace, but a formalized procedure with predictable consequences. Under Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07, all applications for executive clemency must be submitted to the Authority, which is statutorily required to conduct a review 45 days before a scheduled execution unless stayed. The Authority’s own regulation, the “Death Penalty Clemency Procedure,” mandates a pre-hearing interview and a formal clemency hearing. These procedures are not optional in practice; they are triggered automatically by the execution date. The State’s own actions demonstrate that the clemency process is not a mere “courtesy” but a critical component of the death penalty system. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “when a State creates a procedure that has a significant impact on an individual’s liberty, due process requires that the State adhere to fair procedures.” *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976).

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that no liberty interest exists in the clemency process. The Court in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), held that a prisoner has no protected liberty interest in a pardon or commutation, but that decision rested on the absence of any structured, predictable process. In *Dumschat*, the Board denied applications without explanation and without any formalized review. Here, by contrast, Ohio has established a clear, mandatory procedure with defined steps, including a pre-hearing interview and a formal hearing. The existence of such a process transforms what might otherwise be a mere act of executive grace into a matter of constitutional significance. As the Court stated in *Wolff*, “the liberty interest at stake is not the right to be free from punishment, but the right to be free from punishment beyond that which the law allows.” 418 U.S. at 557. When the state creates a process that can result in the termination of a death sentence, it creates a liberty interest that must be protected by due process.

The dissent’s reliance on *Dumschat* is misplaced. *Dumschat* did not hold that all clemency processes lack due process protection; it held only that a state is not constitutionally required to provide a reason for denying clemency. The Court in *Dumschat* did not address whether a structured, mandatory clemency process creates a liberty interest. Indeed, the dissent’s own citation to *Wolff* and *Greenholtz* undermines its position: both cases recognized that structured procedures create liberty interests. The dissent’s fear that recognizing a liberty interest would “open the door” to judicial review of clemency decisions is unfounded. The Due Process Clause does not require courts to second-guess the merits of clemency decisions, only that the process be fair and predictable. *See* *Mathews*, 424 U.S. at 335.

II. The clemency interview procedure imposes an unconstitutional condition by coercing a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects individuals from being compelled to be a witness against themselves. *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 460 (1966). The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a person may not be compelled to incriminate himself,” and that “the privilege against self-incrimination is a fundamental personal right.” *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609, 614 (1965). When the state conditions the receipt of a benefit on the waiver of a constitutional right, it creates an “unconstitutional condition” that violates due process. *Cleveland Board of Education v. Loudermill*, 470 U.S. 532, 542 (1985).

Ohio’s clemency process presents a classic example of such an unconstitutional condition. The State offers a prisoner the opportunity to participate in a personal interview with the Parole Authority, but only if he waives his Fifth Amendment privilege. The regulation provides that any statement made during the interview “shall be considered at the clemency hearing, along with all other relevant information.” This creates a direct incentive to speak: the prisoner who remains silent may be deemed uncooperative, while the one who speaks may be seen as remorseful or contrite. The State’s own materials acknowledge that the interview is “an important part of the clemency review process.” The practical effect is clear: a prisoner faces a Hobson’s choice—speak and risk self-incrimination, or remain silent and forfeit any chance of clemency.
