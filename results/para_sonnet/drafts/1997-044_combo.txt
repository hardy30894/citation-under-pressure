**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner Cohen asks this Court to hold that a debtor who defrauds his creditors may discharge in bankruptcy all liability beyond the bare sum he pocketed. That position finds no support in the statutory text or in this Court's longstanding construction of the fraud exception to discharge. From Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704 (1877), through Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234 (1934), this Court has held that once a debtor's liability is shown to rest on positive fraud, the whole of that liability—not merely the dollar amount the debtor initially received—survives bankruptcy.

**I. The Fraud Exception Attaches to the Entire Debt Created by the Fraud.**

Section 523(a)(2)(A)'s predecessor, § 17a(2) of the Bankruptcy Act, excepted from discharge liabilities for obtaining money or property by false pretenses or false representations. This Court held early on that the exception reaches only "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong, and not implied fraud." Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877). But once that threshold of actual fraud is crossed, the Court has never confined the creditor's nondischargeable recovery to the amount of money or property the debtor literally received.

In Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), the debtor obtained money by false pretenses, and the creditor recovered a judgment including not only the principal sum but interest accrued after the fraud occurred. This Court held that the entire judgment—principal and interest alike—was excepted from discharge, because the liability as a whole was created by the fraud; discharge could not sever the accretions of that liability from its fraudulent source. Id. at 181–83.

This Court reaffirmed that approach in Tindle v. Birkett, 205 U.S. 183 (1907), holding that a judgment for the price of goods obtained through false representations remained excepted from discharge even though the creditor sued in a contract form of action rather than in tort for deceit; the Court looked past the form of the pleading to the fraudulent substance of the transaction. See also Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 187 (1904) (the character of a debt for discharge purposes turns on the true nature of the wrong, not the label given the cause of action).

In Friend v. Talcott, 228 U.S. 27 (1913), this Court resolved doubts about the scope of a fraud-tainted debt against the discharged debtor, holding that where a debtor cannot show that intervening payments should be allocated away from the fraudulently incurred balance, the creditor may treat the entire remaining debt as nondischargeable. That rule—resolving ambiguity in favor of the defrauded creditor—applies here: Cohen's debt to respondents originated entirely in his fraudulent overcharge of rent, and the treble damages, fees, and costs are simply the measure of liability New Jersey law attaches to that same fraudulent conduct.

Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558 (1915), is not to the contrary. There the Court held that a claim for professional services rendered in reliance on a debtor's fraudulent misrepresentations fell outside the exception because services are neither "money" nor "property" within the statute's terms. Id. at 562. Gleason thus marks the outer boundary of the exception—whether money or property was obtained by fraud at all—not a rule limiting recovery, once that threshold is met, to the exact sum obtained. Here there is no dispute that Cohen obtained money—the excess rent—by fraud; Gleason has no application, and the settled rule of Forsyth, Tindle, and Friend controls.

**II. The Bankruptcy Act's "Fresh Start" Is Reserved for the Honest Debtor, Not the Fraudfeasor.**

This Court has explained that the discharge provisions exist "to relieve the honest debtor from the weight of oppressive indebtedness and permit him to start afresh," giving him "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of pre-existing debt." Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). That solicitude has always run to the honest debtor. A landlord who extracts money from his tenants through fraudulent overcharges, and who fails to make them whole even after the local rent board orders him to do so, is not the beneficiary the discharge provisions were designed to protect. See Williams v. United States Fid. & Guar. Co., 236 U.S. 549, 554–55 (1915) (exceptions to discharge attach according to the true character of the underlying wrong, regardless of the posture in which the claim later arises).

Petitioner's rule would perversely reward the defrauder who inflicts the greatest injury: the larger the fraud, and the more substantial the damages a State chooses to impose upon proof of fraud, the greater the amount petitioner would place beyond his creditors' reach through bankruptcy. Nothing in this Court's precedents commends so anomalous a result. The treble damages, fees, and costs awarded to respondents are the debt created by Cohen's fraud, Forsyth, 177 U.S. at 181, and that debt, in its entirety, survives his discharge.
