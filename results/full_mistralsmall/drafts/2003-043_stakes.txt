**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS FOR COLLECTION FROM THAT PARTNER.**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations for collection from the individual partners. *Galletti v. United States*, 298 F.3d 1107, 1114 (9th Cir. 2002). This conclusion is compelled by the plain text of the Internal Revenue Code and this Court’s precedents.

Section 6502(a) of the Internal Revenue Code provides that the ten-year limitations period for collection runs only from the date of an assessment *against the taxpayer from whom collection is sought*. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) (“Where the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto, such tax may be collected by levy or by a proceeding in court...”). The statute does not permit the IRS to extend the limitations period for one taxpayer by assessing another. *See United States v. Updike*, 281 U.S. 489, 493 (1930) (“The statute of limitations is a jurisdictional prerequisite to the bringing of an action by the United States.”); *Bull v. United States*, 295 U.S. 247, 260 (1935) (holding that the limitations period runs separately for each taxpayer).

The IRS’s contrary argument—that a partnership assessment suffices to extend the limitations period for its partners—ignores the statutory definition of “taxpayer.” Under § 7701(a)(14), an individual partner is a distinct “taxpayer” from the partnership. *See also* 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1 (requiring that an assessment “provide identification of the taxpayer”). Because the partners were not assessed, the limitations period for collection from them remained the ordinary three years. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a); *Coson v. United States*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien cannot attach to a partner’s property absent an individual assessment).

The IRS cites *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), but that case is inapposite. There, the IRS had assessed *both* the partnership *and* the individual partners, extending the limitations period for all. *Wright*, 57 F.3d at 562. Here, by contrast, no individual assessments were made. The Ninth Circuit properly distinguished *Wright* on this ground. *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1115.

**II. THE IRS CANNOT CIRCUMVENT THE ASSESSMENT REQUIREMENT BY RELYING ON STATE PARTNERSHIP LAW.**

The IRS alternatively argues that it may collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners under California’s partnership law, which imposes joint and several liability. *See* Cal. Corp. Code § 16306. However, this argument fails because the Bankruptcy Code disallows claims that are unenforceable under applicable nonbankruptcy law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

Under California law, a creditor cannot satisfy a partnership debt from a partner’s assets without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)*; *Witkin, Summary of California Law* § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001). The IRS has neither obtained such a judgment nor timely assessed the partners. Thus, its claim is unenforceable. *See Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1120-21.

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Texas*, 507 U.S. 529 (1993), is misplaced. That case involved a federal statute expressly overriding state law to permit collection from partners. No such statute applies here. *See also United States v. Dack*, 256 F.3d 144, 148 (3d Cir. 2001) (holding that the IRS must comply with state law procedures to collect from partners).

**III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION DOES NOT CONFLICT WITH ANY OTHER COURT OF APPEALS.**

The IRS contends that the Ninth Circuit’s decision conflicts with decisions from other circuits, but these cases are distinguishable. For example, the Fifth Circuit in *United States v. Texas*, 507 U.S. 529 (1993), addressed a federal statute expressly permitting collection from partners, and the Second Circuit in *United States v. Western Products, Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d 84 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), involved a judgment against the partners. Neither case addressed the assessment requirement at issue here.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the IRS must individually assess each partner to extend the limitations period for collection from that partner. The IRS’s failure to do so bars its claims. The Court should affirm.
