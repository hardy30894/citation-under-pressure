ARGUMENT

I. THE ALASKA NATIONAL INTEREST LANDS CONSERVATION ACT OF 1980 AUTHORIZES THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE TO REGULATE ACTIVITIES ON STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) of ANILCA does not preclude the National Park Service (NPS) from regulating activities on state-owned lands and navigable waters within Conservation System Units (CSUs). The plain text of § 103(c) provides that "only those public lands within the boundaries of [CSUs] ... shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). This provision does not limit the applicability of NPS regulations to non-federal lands within CSUs.

The Ninth Circuit's interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Riverside Bayview Homes, Inc., 474 U.S. 121 (1985), where the Court held that the Corps of Engineers' authority to regulate wetlands adjacent to navigable waters was not limited by the statutory definition of "navigable waters." Id. at 131-32. Similarly, § 103(c) does not restrict NPS's authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within CSUs.

Sturgeon's argument that § 103(c) limits NPS's regulatory authority is also contrary to the principle that "the plain meaning of a statute should be given effect unless it is clear that the legislature intended a different meaning." United States v. Locke, 471 U.S. 84, 93 (1985) (citing Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917)). Here, the plain text of § 103(c) does not support Sturgeon's interpretation.

Furthermore, ANILCA § 1314, which governs the administration of CSUs, provides that the Secretary of the Interior "shall administer [CSUs] in accordance with the applicable provisions of this Act and the laws, rules, and regulations applicable to units of the [National Park System]." 16 U.S.C. § 3194. This provision authorizes NPS to regulate activities within CSUs, including those on non-federal lands.

The Supreme Court has recognized that "Congress has the power to regulate activities on non-federal lands within national parks." Kleppe v. New Mexico, 426 U.S. 529, 540-41 (1976) (citing Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897)). In Camfield, the Court upheld a federal statute that prohibited the construction of fences on private lands within a national forest, holding that "the power of the United States to thus regulate the use of private lands within a national forest is not limited by the fact that the lands are privately owned." 167 U.S. at 525-26.

In addition, the Ninth Circuit's decision is consistent with the principle that "an agency's interpretation of a statute is entitled to deference under Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984)." Nat'l Cable & Telecomms. Ass'n v. Brand X Internet Servs., 545 U.S. 967, 980-81 (2005). Here, NPS's interpretation of § 103(c) as authorizing regulation of activities on non-federal lands within CSUs is reasonable and entitled to deference.

Sturgeon's reliance on the legislative history of ANILCA is misplaced. The Supreme Court has cautioned against relying on legislative history to interpret a statute when the plain text is clear. See, e.g., Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Allapattah Servs., Inc., 545 U.S. 546, 568 (2005) ("[L]egislative history is not the law."). Moreover, the legislative history cited by Sturgeon does not clearly support his interpretation of § 103(c).

The Ninth Circuit's decision is also consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Lujan v. National Wildlife Federation, 497 U.S. 871 (1990), where the Court held that an agency's regulatory authority is not limited by the fact that the regulated activity occurs on non-federal lands. Id. at 887-88.

Finally, the principle that "a state's ownership of submerged lands does not preclude federal regulation of activities on those lands" is well-established. See, e.g., United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967) (citing Gibson v. United States, 166 U.S. 269 (1897)). Therefore, NPS's regulation of hovercraft use on state-owned waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is authorized by ANILCA.

II. STURGEON LACKS A VALID PRIVILEGES OR IMMUNITIES CLAUSE CLAIM.

Sturgeon's claim that the NPS regulation implicates his right under the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to navigate the Nation River is without merit. The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. 36 (1872), which Sturgeon relies on, held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protects citizens from state infringement on their rights, not federal infringement. Id. at 77-78. Moreover, the Court has since limited the scope of the Privileges or Immunities Clause to a narrow set of rights. See Saenz v. Roe, 526 U.S. 489, 502-04 (1999).

In conclusion, the Ninth Circuit correctly held that ANILCA authorizes NPS to regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs. The plain text of § 103(c), combined with the relevant case law and statutory provisions, supports this interpretation. Sturgeon's claims are without merit, and the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
