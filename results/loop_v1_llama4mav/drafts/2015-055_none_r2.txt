The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The principle that the Supreme Court's decisions construing federal statutes apply retroactively supports the retroactive application of Johnson, as established in United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801), where the Court held that a court must apply the law as it exists at the time of its decision.

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum sentence of 15 years for defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e), and defines a "violent felony" in a manner that has been challenged for vagueness. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibits, Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939); Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926), and is unconstitutional if it allows for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement, Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445, 465 (1927); United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921).

The Johnson decision held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague, failing to provide clear guidance on what constitutes a violent felony. This ruling is a substantive change to the ACCA, as it alters the definition of a violent felony and thus the range of conduct the statute punishes. As a substantive rule, Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case. See Davis v. United States, 417 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1974); Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643, 657-58 (1961). The vagueness doctrine, as established in Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101-02 (1945), requires that a penal statute be sufficiently explicit, further supporting the retroactive application of Johnson.

The retroactive application of Johnson to Welch's case is consistent with the long-standing principle that courts must apply the law as it exists at the time of their decision. Given that the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing.
