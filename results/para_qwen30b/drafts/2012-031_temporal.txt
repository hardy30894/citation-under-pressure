**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—must be answered in the affirmative. The District Court did not exceed its discretion in certifying the class here, because the record contains sufficient admissible evidence demonstrating that damages can be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis through common methodology. This conclusion is compelled by the settled principles of class action jurisprudence established before 1970, which recognize that a district court may rely on expert testimony and common evidence to satisfy the predominance requirement of Rule 23(b)(3), so long as the methodology is capable of producing a class-wide measure of damages.

The foundational principle governing class certification is that “the class action device is to be used where the common questions of law or fact predominate over individual ones.” *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Mackey*, 351 U.S. 422, 430 (1956). This standard does not require that every issue be common, but only that the “common questions” be “so numerous and so substantial that they will predominate over the individual questions.” *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 483 (1951). The Supreme Court has long held that “the trial of a class action is not a trial of individual claims, but a trial of the common issues.” *Barnett v. United States*, 308 U.S. 473, 477 (1940). Thus, the focus is not on whether individual damages may be proven with perfect precision, but whether a common methodology exists that can fairly and efficiently resolve the central issue—here, whether Comcast’s conduct caused a class-wide injury and whether that injury can be measured in a way that is consistent across all members.

The District Court’s reliance on Dr. McClave’s expert testimony was entirely proper. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (holding that “the trial court may consider expert testimony in determining whether the requirements of Rule 23 are met”). The Court did not accept the expert’s conclusions as conclusive, but rather evaluated them under the “clearly erroneous” standard, which requires reversal only if the appellate court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *Id.* at 395. The expert’s model, based on a comparison of pricing in the Philadelphia DMA with pricing in competitive markets, was grounded in economic principles and supported by data. It was not speculative, but rather a methodologically sound attempt to isolate the antitrust impact from other market forces. The fact that the model required some adjustment for individual variation does not render it inadequate. *See* *Hoffman*, 341 U.S. at 483 (“The fact that individual damages may vary does not preclude a class action if the common issues are dominant.”).

Comcast’s argument that the absence of individualized proof of damages at the certification stage renders certification improper is foreclosed by *Sears, Roebuck & Co.*, 351 U.S. at 430. There, the Court upheld certification of a class action involving claims of overcharges, despite the fact that individual damages could not be precisely calculated at the outset. The Court emphasized that “the trial of the common issues may be conducted in such a way as to avoid the necessity of individual trials.” *Id.* at 431. The same principle applies here. The District Court did not certify the class on the basis of a hypothetical or speculative model, but on the basis of a methodology that had been tested, challenged, and found by the court to be capable of producing a class-wide measure of damages. The fact that the model may require refinement at trial does not undermine its adequacy at the certification stage.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “the trial court may consider the merits of the claim to the extent necessary to determine whether the requirements of Rule 23 are satisfied.” *Hoffman*, 341 U.S. at 483. This is not a “mini-trial,” but a necessary inquiry into the factual and legal issues that bear on the predominance requirement. *See* *Barnett*, 308 U.S. at 477 (“The trial of a class action is not a trial of individual claims, but a trial of the common issues.”). The District Court’s analysis of the antitrust impact and damages methodology was not a merits determination, but a proper application of the Rule 23(b)(3) standard. The court did not decide whether Comcast violated the Sherman Act, but whether the class could prove such a violation through common evidence. That inquiry is not only permissible, but required.

Finally, the Third Circuit correctly applied the “abuse of discretion” standard. *See* *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 312. The District Court’s findings were supported by the record, including expert testimony and voluminous documentation. The court’s conclusion that a common methodology existed for measuring damages was not clearly erroneous. *See* *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 395. The appellate court’s role is not to substitute its judgment for that of the district court, but to determine whether the court’s decision was within the bounds of reason. *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). Here, the District Court’s decision was not only reasonable, but consistent with the settled law of class certification.

For these reasons, the certification of the class was proper. The District Court did not err in relying on admissible evidence to conclude that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
