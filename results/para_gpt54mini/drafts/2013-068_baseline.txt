**ARGUMENT**

I. **The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts statute as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time, place, and manner regulation.**

The First Amendment permits the government to regulate speech in a public forum if the regulation is content neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 481 (1988). The Massachusetts statute easily satisfies that standard.

The State’s interests are substantial. Protecting patient access, ensuring public safety, and preserving order at clinic entrances are classic government interests. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000); *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994). The statute does not bar petitioners from speaking to the public generally, and it does not prohibit all speech near clinics. It imposes a limited buffer zone at entrances, exits, and driveways so patients may enter and leave safely. That is the sort of location-based regulation the Court has repeatedly upheld. *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 381-82 (1997); *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 769-70.

The First Circuit also correctly concluded the law is content neutral. A law is not content based simply because enforcement requires reference to a facility’s status or because its effects are felt most strongly by one group of speakers. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 720-21; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The statute applies to all persons who knowingly remain within the marked zone, subject to narrow exemptions for persons entering or leaving the facility, employees and agents, and others using the sidewalk for unrelated purposes. It regulates where speech may occur, not what may be said. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719-20.

Petitioners argue the statute is not narrowly tailored because the State could use less restrictive means. But narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. A regulation is valid if it promotes a substantial government interest that would be achieved less effectively absent the regulation, and if the burden on speech is not substantially greater than necessary. *Id.* at 799; *McCullen v. Coakley*, 573 U.S. 464, 486-87 (2014). Here, the record showed that the buffer zones address real congestion, confrontation, and obstruction at clinic entrances, while still allowing petitioners to speak outside the zone, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and engage passersby at close range when outside the marked area. That leaves ample alternatives. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726-30; *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 483-84.

The First Circuit therefore did not err in applying settled First Amendment doctrine. It faithfully followed *Hill* and other precedents recognizing that the State may protect access to healthcare facilities without suppressing pro-life advocacy. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 725-30; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 377-82.

II. **If *Hill v. Colorado* is implicated, the Court should reaffirm it; at minimum, it should not overrule it in this case.**

Petitioners ask the Court to limit or overrule *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000). That request should be rejected. Stare decisis has “special force” in the First Amendment context when the Court has already supplied a workable rule on which lower courts and legislatures have relied. *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000); *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991). *Hill* has been repeatedly applied in clinic-access cases, including *Schenck* and *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d 167, 181-82 (1st Cir. 2009), and it remains fully consistent with the Court’s public-forum jurisprudence. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715, 719-30.

Nothing about this case presents a proper occasion to revisit *Hill*. The law here is narrower than the statute upheld in *Hill*, and it is justified by the same interests the Court recognized there. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-16. The statute does not criminalize persuasion, leafletting, or prayer; it limits the precise spatial tactics that experience showed were disrupting clinic access. That is exactly the sort of regulation the Court approved in *Hill* and *Madsen*. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726-30; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-73.

Overruling *Hill* would also unsettle a long line of cases distinguishing content-neutral access regulations from content-based speech bans. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791-802; *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 483-84; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376-82. The First Circuit did not err in relying on that settled doctrine. If anything, the Court should reaffirm that states may adopt reasonable buffer zones to protect access to healthcare facilities while preserving robust channels for advocacy. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 728-30; *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 486-90.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
