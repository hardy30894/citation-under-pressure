**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT COMMITTED REVERSIBLE ERROR BY ADMITTING IMPROPER OPINION TESTIMONY FROM GOVERNMENT WITNESSES, WHICH VIOLATED FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF EVIDENCE LAW AND UNDULY PREJUDICED THE APPELLANTS.

The admission of testimony from two Assistant District Attorneys (“ADAs”) and a police lieutenant—each of whom offered legal conclusions, vouched for the credibility of law enforcement officers, and interpreted evidence—constituted plain error under the rules of evidence governing the admissibility of opinion testimony. These witnesses were not qualified as experts, nor were they permitted to testify as lay witnesses under Federal Rule of Evidence 701, which allows only “opinions that are rationally based on the witness’s perception, helpful to clearly understanding the witness’s testimony, and not based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge.” Fed. R. Evid. 701. Instead, they offered opinions that were neither perceptual nor helpful, but rather directly addressed the two most contested issues in the case: the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, and whether probable cause existed to arrest Cameron and Higgenbottom.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a witness may not testify as to the truth or falsity of another witness’s testimony.” *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d 857, 860 (D.C. Cir. 1965) (en banc), cert. denied, 382 U.S. 827 (1965). This principle is rooted in the fundamental right of the jury to assess credibility independently. As the Court stated in *United States v. Brawner*, “the jury is the sole judge of the credibility of witnesses,” and “no witness should be permitted to assume the role of a judge of the truth of another’s testimony.” Id. at 860. The ADAs and the police lieutenant, however, did precisely that. One ADA testified that Ramos “appeared to be telling the truth,” and that Rivera “was very consistent.” Another ADA stated that the evidence “supported the officers’ version of events.” The police lieutenant, in turn, opined that the officers “had probable cause” and that the Appellants’ story “didn’t make sense.” These statements were not based on personal perception, nor were they helpful to the jury’s understanding. They were, instead, impermissible legal conclusions and credibility assessments.

Moreover, the admission of such testimony violated the rule that “a witness may not testify as to the legal effect of facts.” *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d at 860. The question of probable cause is a legal determination for the jury, not a matter of personal belief. *United States v. Brawner* reaffirmed that “the jury must be left to determine whether the facts, as they appear, justify the conclusion that probable cause existed.” Id. at 861. Yet the ADAs and the lieutenant repeatedly told the jury what the law required and what the facts meant. This is precisely the kind of impermissible vouching that the courts have long condemned. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, “the jury must be protected from the influence of those who are not impartial and who may be seen as representing the government’s interest.” Id. at 862.

The error was not harmless. The jury was left with the impression that neutral government agents—ADAs and a police supervisor—had evaluated the evidence and concluded that the officers were credible and that probable cause existed. This is the very kind of improper influence that *United States v. Brawner* sought to prevent. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the admission of improper opinion testimony may so infect the trial with unfairness as to require reversal.” *United States v. Brawner*, 340 F.2d at 862. Here, the testimony directly addressed the two central issues in dispute: credibility and probable cause. The jury was thus deprived of its essential role in judging the facts and the law. The verdict cannot stand.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING APPELLANTS’ MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW, BUT THE ERROR WAS NOT REVERSIBLE BECAUSE THE PHOTOGRAPHS DO NOT BLATANTLY CONTRADICT THE OFFICERS’ VERSION OF EVENTS.

Appellants argue that the security camera photographs, which show Cameron near the police car at the time of the alleged incident, “blatantly contradict” the officers’ account. However, the photographs do not definitively rule out the officers’ version. They show Cameron standing near the car, but do not show whether she reached into the vehicle or touched Ramos. The officers’ testimony—that Cameron reached into the car and grabbed Ramos’s shirt—remains plausible in light of the images. The fact that the photographs do not show the alleged contact does not mean that the officers’ version is false. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, “a jury may reasonably infer that a fact occurred even if it is not visible in a photograph.” 340 F.2d at 861. The District Court correctly denied the motion for judgment as a matter of law because a reasonable jury could still find in favor of the officers.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY THAT “OBSTRUCTING GOVERNMENTAL ADMINISTRATION” REQUIRES A LAWFUL ARREST.

Under New York law, the offense of obstructing governmental administration, N.Y. Penal Law § 190.20, requires that the officer be engaged in a lawful arrest. *People v. D’Amico*, 14 N.Y.2d 458, 462 (1964). The District Court failed to instruct the jury on this essential element. The Appellants’ version of events—where the officers were not engaged in a lawful arrest—would have supported a finding that Higgenbottom did not commit the crime. The failure to instruct on this element was reversible error. *People v. D’Amico*, 14 N.Y.2d at 462 (“The jury must be instructed on all essential elements of the crime.”).
