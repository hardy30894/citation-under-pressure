I. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle's Request for an Evidentiary Hearing

The district court's denial of Riddle's request for an evidentiary hearing was improper. In Walker v. Johnston, 312 U.S. 275 (1941), the Supreme Court held that a habeas corpus petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing when the allegations in the petition, if true, would entitle him to relief.  Id. at 285. Riddle's allegations regarding the prosecutor's improper testimony and the impact it had on the jury's verdict, if true, would entitle him to relief. Therefore, the district court should have granted an evidentiary hearing to resolve these factual disputes.

Furthermore, in Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293 (1963), the Supreme Court outlined the circumstances under which a federal court must hold an evidentiary hearing in a habeas corpus proceeding. One such circumstance is when "the facts are in dispute." Id. at 313. Here, the facts surrounding Hill's testimony and its impact on the trial are in dispute, and an evidentiary hearing is necessary to resolve these issues.

II. The Prosecutor's Testimony as a Witness and Continued Participation in the Trial Constituted a Violation of Riddle's Due Process Rights

The prosecutor, Hill, testified as a witness and continued to prosecute the case, which raises serious due process concerns. In Gajewski v. United States, 321 F.2d 261 (8th Cir. 1963), the court held that a prosecutor's testimony as a witness can be grounds for reversal if it prejudices the defendant. Id. at 268-269. Here, Hill's testimony was used to rebut Riddle's claims, and the assistant prosecutor used it to argue that Riddle was a liar during closing argument. This prejudiced Riddle and undermined the fairness of the trial.

Moreover, in Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263 (1929), the Supreme Court emphasized the importance of maintaining the separation between the prosecutor's role as advocate and witness. The Court noted that a prosecutor should not be permitted to "mingle" the two roles. Id. at 299. By testifying and continuing to prosecute, Hill blurred the lines between these roles, compromising the integrity of the trial.

The district court's grant of summary judgment was improper, and Riddle is entitled to an evidentiary hearing to resolve the factual disputes surrounding Hill's testimony and its impact on the trial. The prosecutor's testimony and continued participation in the trial raised serious due process concerns, and the district court erred in denying Riddle's request for an evidentiary hearing.
