## ARGUMENT

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) compels remand after pretrial proceedings. It commands that actions transferred for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings “shall be remanded by the panel” to the transferor district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” This mandatory language admits no exception. A transferee court cannot invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial because the power to transfer venue under § 1404(a) is a “pretrial proceeding” only if it serves the purposes of multidistrict coordination—and retaining a case for trial does not. The statute’s design is clear: the Panel transfers cases solely for pretrial efficiency; trial venue remains with the original forum.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that when a statute’s language is unambiguous, “the inquiry is complete.” *Conn. Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992). Section 1407(a) could not be more explicit. Yet the Ninth Circuit, relying on a line of cases beginning with *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), held that the district court’s self-assignment for trial under § 1404(a) was not “clearly erroneous.” That conclusion defies the statutory command and should be reversed.

The leading case correctly applying § 1407 is *In re Multi-Piece Rim Products Liability Litigation*, 612 F.2d 377 (8th Cir. 1980). There, the Eighth Circuit held that a transferee court “cannot retain jurisdiction for trial by ordering a change of venue under § 1404(a) after the conclusion of the pretrial proceedings.” *Id.* at 380. The court explained that § 1407(a) “clearly directs that the Panel ‘shall remand’ each action,” and that the transferee court’s “power is limited to pretrial proceedings.” *Id.* at 378–79. That reasoning is unimpeachable.

The *Pfizer* court, by contrast, acknowledged that “there is nothing in the language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) or 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.” *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971). Yet the *Pfizer* court nonetheless endorsed the practice, relying on policy rather than text. That approach violates the fundamental principle that courts “cannot ignore the plain language of the statute on the ground that the result seems absurd.” *Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989). Here, the result is not absurd; it is Congress’s considered choice to preserve plaintiffs’ choice of forum for trial.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) is also misplaced. An administrative rule cannot override a statute. *Chrysler Corp. v. Brown*, 441 U.S. 281, 295–96 (1979). Rule 14(b) purports to authorize what § 1407(a) prohibits. The Panel’s rule is “inconsistent with the statute” and therefore invalid. *See* *Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co.*, 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994) (federal courts possess only the jurisdiction conferred by statute).

The cases cited by the real parties in interest are distinguishable or wrong. *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612 (1964), and *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335 (1960), address the standards for § 1404(a) transfers generally, but neither case involved a transferee court’s attempt to reassign a case to itself after multidistrict pretrial proceedings. Those cases did not purport to limit the mandatory remand provision of § 1407. Indeed, the *Van Dusen* Court emphasized that § 1404(a) “was drafted in accordance with the doctrine of *forum non conveniens*,” *id.* at 634, and has nothing to say about the specialized multidistrict procedure.

The dissent below correctly recognized that the district court’s order was “an egregious, oft-repeated error.” *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 61 F.3d 911, 915 (9th Cir. 1995) (Kozinski, J., dissenting). The dissent noted that “§ 1407 reflects an important policy choice: Cases may be transferred to remote locations, but only for the duration of pretrial procedures.” *Id.* That policy choice is binding on every court.

Other circuits have also rejected the *Pfizer* approach. *See, e.g., In re Plumbing Fixture Cases*, 298 F. Supp. 484, 489 (J.P.M.L. 1968) (Panel itself stating that “the transferee court’s jurisdiction is limited to pretrial proceedings”); *In re Bendectin Prods. Liab. Litig.*, 801 F.2d 647, 648 (6th Cir. 1986) (noting that after pretrial, “the Panel is required to remand each action to the district from which it was transferred”). The unified weight of authority—and the statute’s plain text—compels the conclusion that the district court lacked authority to assign the case to itself for trial.

The Ninth Circuit’s denial of mandamus was an abuse of discretion because the district court’s order was clearly erroneous as a matter of law. *Bauman v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 557 F.2d 650, 653 (9th Cir. 1977). The error is not merely debatable; it is a direct violation of the statutory mandate. Accordingly, this Court should reverse and direct the district court to remand the case to the Northern District of Illinois.
