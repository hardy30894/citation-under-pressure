**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of statutory text and settled principles of statutory interpretation. The answer is no. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the District of Arizona’s transfer of this case to itself for trial rests on a fundamental misreading of § 1407(a), which unambiguously mandates remand to the original district for trial. The court’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and a handful of non-binding precedents fails to overcome the clear command of Congress.

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Original District for Trial—No Exception for § 1404(a) Transfers**

The text of § 1407(a) is unequivocal: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (emphasis added). This is not a permissive directive; it is a mandatory command. The phrase “shall be remanded” is a legislative imperative, not a suggestion. As this Court has repeatedly held, “where the statute uses the word ‘shall,’ it is generally understood to be mandatory.” *United States v. James*, 478 U.S. 597, 602 (1986). The Panel’s authority to transfer cases for pretrial proceedings is limited in time and scope. Once those proceedings conclude, the statute requires the Panel to remand the case to the district where it was originally filed. There is no provision in § 1407(a) authorizing the transferee court to assume jurisdiction for trial, nor does the statute grant the district court any independent power to retain the case.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that the district court may “transfer” the case to itself under § 1404(a) is legally untenable. Section 1404(a) permits a court to transfer a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only when the case is “in a district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). A case transferred under § 1407(a) is not “in a district or division where it might have been brought” for trial purposes. The original district—here, the Northern District of Illinois—was the only proper venue for trial under the statute’s framework. The transferee court’s jurisdiction under § 1407(a) is derivative and limited to pretrial proceedings. *See* *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, N.H.*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971) (“There is nothing in the language of § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.”). To allow a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial is to transform a temporary, limited transfer into a permanent reassignment—precisely what § 1407(a) was designed to prevent.

**II. The Ninth Circuit’s Reliance on Rule 14(b) and Precedent Is Misplaced**

The Ninth Circuit justified the district court’s action by citing Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b), which permits a transferee court to transfer an action to itself for trial under § 1404(a). But rules of procedure cannot override the clear mandate of a statute. *See* *United States v. Mead Corp.*, 533 U.S. 218, 226 (2001) (“When a statute is clear, courts must follow it, even if a rule of procedure purports to allow otherwise.”). The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) has no authority to amend or circumvent § 1407(a). The Panel’s power is derived solely from the statute, and its rules must be consistent with it. *See* *In re Air Crash Disaster*, 342 F. Supp. at 909. To the extent Rule 14(b) permits a transferee court to transfer a case to itself for trial, it is invalid as an impermissible expansion of the Panel’s authority.

The Ninth Circuit also relied on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), as support for the transferee court’s power. But these cases are not binding on this Court, and they rest on a flawed premise: that the statutory mandate to remand is “absurd” or “inconvenient.” *Pfizer*, 447 F.2d at 124–25. That argument has no place in statutory interpretation. Congress has spoken clearly: remand is required. Courts may not rewrite statutes based on policy preferences. *See* *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984) (“When a statute is silent or ambiguous with respect to the specific issue, the question for the court is whether the agency’s answer is based on a permissible construction of the statute.”). Here, the statute is neither silent nor ambiguous—it is explicit. The judiciary has no authority to “correct” a statute by ignoring its plain language.
