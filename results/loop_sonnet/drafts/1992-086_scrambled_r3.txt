# ARGUMENT

## I. Because Lamb's Chapel Sought to Use School Property for Constitutionally Protected Expression, the District's Exclusion Based on the Religious Content of That Expression Violates the First Amendment.

Government property need not be a street or park to trigger First Amendment scrutiny of content-based exclusions. Once a governmental body opens its property to expressive use by outside groups, it may not selectively bar categories of speech because of the ideas they convey. This principle has deep roots in this Court's recognition that public places long associated with communicative use warrant heightened protection against discriminatory exclusion of speech. See *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939) (invalidating a restriction on the distribution of literature on public streets as an impermissible burden on liberty of expression). The Center Moriches School District, by regularly permitting outside organizations—civic groups, garden clubs, and cultural performers—to use its auditorium after hours, created precisely the kind of forum for community expression that cannot then be closed selectively to religious speech alone.

That the expression here took the form of a film series does not diminish its constitutional protection. This Court held decades ago that motion pictures are "a significant medium for the communication of ideas" entitled to the same First Amendment protection as the printed or spoken word. *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495, 501 (1952). The District cannot avoid its constitutional obligations by treating a film about family values differently than it would treat a lecture or a live presentation on the identical subject, merely because the film happened to be produced from a religious perspective. See *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943) (recognizing that "freedom to differ is not limited to things that do not matter much" and that the First Amendment protects the right to differ on matters that "touch the heart of the existing order," including matters of religious conviction). A "function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute," *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), and the District's effort to silence Lamb's Chapel because its message on child-rearing was religiously informed strikes at the very core of that function.

## II. Excluding Religious Speech While Permitting Comparable Secular Speech Is Impermissible Viewpoint Discrimination.

This Court has repeatedly condemned government action denying access to public facilities specifically because the excluded speaker's message was religious, while similar secular uses were tolerated. In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court struck down the denial of a park permit to Jehovah's Witnesses where other groups, religious and secular alike, had been permitted to use the same park, and the denial rested on disagreement with the group's religious views rather than any neutral, generally applicable standard. The Court reached the same result in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), invalidating the discriminatory application of a park ordinance against a Jehovah's Witness religious meeting where other groups conducting similar gatherings were left undisturbed.

This Court has likewise made clear that officials may not wield discretionary control over access to public facilities in a manner that permits the suppression of disfavored speech. In *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558 (1948), the Court invalidated a licensing scheme that vested unchecked discretion in a local official to decide who could use sound equipment in public places, recognizing the danger that such discretion invites viewpoint-based suppression. The same danger is realized here: the District's own clerk repeatedly characterized Lamb's Chapel's applications as refused because the proposed film "does appear to be church related," while comparable secular and quasi-spiritual programming—including a lecture series touching on "spiritual" and "metaphysical" topics and musical performances with religious overtones—was permitted. That selective, standardless exclusion is precisely the discrimination condemned in *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, and *Saia*.

## III. Public School Property Is Not an Enclave Where Officials May Suppress Disfavored Viewpoints.

This Court has long insisted that publicly funded educational institutions remain subject to the constraints of the First Amendment when they regulate expression on their premises. As this Court explained, "[i]n our system, state-operated schools may not be enclaves of totalitarianism." *Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503, 511 (1969). School officials do not possess unlimited authority to suppress expression simply because it occurs on school property, for "[i]t can hardly be argued that either students or teachers shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate." *Id.* at 506. If students and teachers retain their expressive rights within the schoolhouse itself, the District cannot claim greater authority to suppress the speech of outside community groups using school facilities after hours for purposes wholly unconnected to the school's educational mission. Nor does the associational and expressive character of the group seeking access lose protection simply because it is a religious congregation rather than a civic club; the right to associate for the advancement of shared beliefs is itself protected activity. See *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 460–61 (1958) (recognizing that "effective advocacy of both public and private points of view, particularly controversial ones, is undeniably enhanced by group association" and that freedom to engage in such association is protected by the First and Fourteenth Amendments).

## CONCLUSION

The District's blanket exclusion of religious speech from a forum otherwise open to comparable secular expression cannot be reconciled with *Schneider*, *Niemotko*, *Fowler*, *Saia*, *Burstyn*, *Terminiello*, *Barnette*, *Tinker*, or *NAACP v. Alabama*. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
