## Argument

The district court's judgment should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial due to several errors that affected the outcome of the trial.

### I. The District Court Erred in Allowing American Steamship's Expert to Testify as to the Cause of the Line's Parting

The district court abused its discretion when it allowed American Steamship's expert to offer an opinion on the cause of the line's parting. The expert's testimony was not based on specialized knowledge, but rather on a factual determination that was within the province of the jury. As the Supreme Court held in Salem v. United States Lines Co., 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962), expert testimony is only admissible when it will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence. Here, the expert's opinion was not necessary to understand the evidence, and the district court erred in admitting it. See also, McCormick v. United States, 251 F.2d 550, 553 (3d Cir. 1958) (expert testimony should be excluded when it invades the province of the jury).

Furthermore, the expert's opinion was based on an incomplete understanding of the facts, and the district court should have excluded it under the rule announced in The Tom Lysle, 48 F. 690, 692 (D. Md. 1891), which held that expert testimony must be based on a thorough understanding of the facts. The admission of this testimony was prejudicial error, as it likely influenced the jury's determination of comparative negligence. See, e.g., The Joseph B. Thomas, 86 F. 658, 661 (6th Cir. 1898) (erroneous admission of evidence requires reversal when it affects the outcome of the trial).

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Issue of Unseaworthiness

The district court erred when it denied Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. A vessel is unseaworthy when it lacks proper equipment or a safe working environment. See, e.g., Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co., 321 U.S. 96, 99 (1944) (a vessel is unseaworthy when it lacks proper equipment). Here, the vessel was unseaworthy because the line supporting the bos'n's chair was not properly maintained. The crew had left the line rigged during the summer months, and it had been exposed to the elements, which likely caused it to deteriorate. See, The Arizona v. Anelich, 298 U.S. 110, 121 (1936) (a vessel owner has a duty to provide a seaworthy vessel).

Moreover, the crew's failure to change the grease in the boom's bearings from summer to winter grease rendered the vessel unseaworthy. See, e.g., Grillea v. United States, 232 F.2d 919, 922 (2d Cir. 1956) (unseaworthiness can be established by a single instance of negligence). The district court should have granted Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on this issue, as there was no genuine issue of material fact. See, e.g., Brady v. Southern Ry. Co., 320 U.S. 476, 479-80 (1943) (a court should grant judgment as a matter of law when there is no genuine issue of material fact).

### III. The District Court Did Not Err in Admitting Evidence of Cook's Alcohol-Related Ailments

The district court did not err when it allowed American Steamship to introduce evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments. The evidence was relevant to Cook's damages claim, as it related to his overall health and ability to work. See, e.g., Thompson v. Camp, 163 F.2d 396, 399 (6th Cir. 1947) (evidence of a plaintiff's pre-existing condition is admissible to mitigate damages). The district court properly admitted this evidence, and Cook's objection to it is without merit.

In conclusion, the district court erred in admitting American Steamship's expert testimony and in denying Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. However, it did not err in admitting evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments. Therefore, the judgment should be vacated, and the case remanded for a new trial.
