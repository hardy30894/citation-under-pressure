**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause guarantees a defendant the right to present a complete defense, including the right to introduce relevant and reliable expert evidence to challenge the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt. However, this right is not absolute, and it does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert testimony on mental state for purposes beyond the scope of a statutorily defined insanity defense—particularly where the state has clearly delineated the permissible uses of such evidence under its own criminal procedure framework. Here, the Arizona Supreme Court correctly upheld the trial court’s exclusion of Eric Michael Clark’s expert testimony on mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, because Arizona law properly limits the use of such evidence to the affirmative defense of insanity. The state’s procedural rules do not violate due process, and Clark’s conviction must be affirmed.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that due process requires a defendant to have a meaningful opportunity to present evidence in support of a defense. See *California v. Trombetta*, 467 U.S. 479, 485 (1984) (“The Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.”). But this right is not unlimited. *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998), reaffirmed that “the Constitution does not require a State to admit all relevant evidence,” and that “a State may exclude evidence that it deems unreliable or that would disrupt the trial process.” The Court has also held that states may define the scope of affirmative defenses and the evidentiary standards for proving them. *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 697 (1975) (“The State may define the elements of a crime and the defenses to it, and may place the burden of proof on the defendant as to any element or defense.”).

Arizona law, consistent with this principle, treats the insanity defense as an affirmative defense that must be proven by clear and convincing evidence. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501. The state has further clarified that expert testimony on mental state may be admitted only to support the insanity defense and not to negate the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent. This limitation is not arbitrary or irrational; it reflects a legitimate state interest in maintaining the integrity of the criminal trial process by ensuring that the burden of proof on criminal intent remains with the prosecution, while reserving the use of expert testimony on mental condition to the specific, statutorily defined defense of insanity.

Clark argues that excluding his expert testimony on mental state—evidence that he was suffering from paranoid schizophrenia and believed he was acting under alien control—violated his due process rights because it prevented him from challenging the prosecution’s claim that he formed the requisite intent to commit murder. But the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to use expert mental health testimony to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof on intent. *United States v. Brawner*, 547 F.2d 1003, 1010 (9th Cir. 1976), cert. denied, 431 U.S. 907 (1977), held that “a defendant may not use expert testimony to negate an element of the crime unless the state’s law permits such a defense.” The Supreme Court has similarly emphasized that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence that would undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof unless the State has created a defense that allows such evidence.” *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952).

Moreover, the Arizona Supreme Court’s interpretation of its own law was neither arbitrary nor contrary to federal constitutional standards. The state’s rule does not prevent Clark from presenting any evidence of his mental condition; he was permitted to introduce expert testimony to support his insanity defense. The trial court’s limitation on the use of that evidence—prohibiting its use to negate criminal intent—was a reasonable application of state law. The Court has repeatedly upheld such state-defined boundaries on the use of evidence. *Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222, 225 (1971) (“The Constitution does not require a State to admit evidence that it deems unreliable or that would disrupt the trial process.”).

Clark’s claim also fails because he did not demonstrate that the exclusion of his expert testimony deprived him of a fair trial or that the outcome would have been different had the evidence been admitted. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), requires that a constitutional error be “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt” to avoid reversal. Here, the prosecution presented overwhelming evidence of Clark’s intent: he shot the officer in the back during a traffic stop, used a firearm, and had a history of violent behavior. The expert testimony, even if admitted, would not have negated the prosecution’s case on intent under Arizona law, which requires only that the defendant acted with “premeditation, deliberation, or intent to kill.” Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-1105. The expert evidence, while relevant to the insanity defense, did not establish that Clark lacked the capacity to form intent under the legal standard.

In sum, the Arizona Supreme Court correctly upheld the trial court’s evidentiary ruling. The state’s limitation on the use of expert mental health testimony to the insanity defense is a reasonable and constitutionally permissible application of state criminal procedure. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to allow a defendant to use such evidence to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent. Clark’s due process rights were not violated, and his conviction must be affirmed.
