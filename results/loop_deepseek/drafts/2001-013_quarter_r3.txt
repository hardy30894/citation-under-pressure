**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over Rig 52. The Act covers “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Rig 52 is a workplace because it is not a “vessel in navigation” for maritime purposes.

This Court has long held that floating structures used for stationary work are not vessels. *Cope v. Vallette Dry Dock Co.*, 119 U.S. 625 (1887), held that a floating dry dock was not a vessel because it was not used in navigation. *Evans v. United States*, 252 U.S. 584 (1920), similarly held that “a floating dry dock, permanently moored in the river, [is] not a ‘vessel’ within the maritime law.” *Gahagan Construction Corp. v. Armao*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam), reached the same conclusion for a worker on a stationary dredge. These decisions reflect the settled definition of a vessel: a watercraft used as a means of transportation on water. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557 (1870). Rig 52 was anchored while drilling an oil well; it was not engaged in transportation. It was therefore a land-based workplace, not a maritime vessel.

Section 4(b)(1) preempts OSHA only where another federal agency actually “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards” over the same working condition. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Preemption requires real conflict, not speculative overlap. As this Court has explained, the question is whether the state law stands as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). Preemption also requires that the federal scheme be so “pervasive as to make reasonable the inference that Congress left no room for the States to supplement it.” *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is the opposite of pervasive; it extends only to fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. It does not cover blowout prevention, well control, or the other conditions that caused this explosion.

Moreover, section 4(b)(1) is an exemption from a remedial statute and must be construed narrowly. *A.H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490 (1945); *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940). The Coast Guard has not exercised authority over Rig 52’s drilling operations, so the exemption is inapplicable. The court below created an industry-wide exemption for all vessels, but its approach cannot be squared with the statute’s plain text or this Court’s precedent. Because Rig 52 is not a vessel in navigation, its workers are not seamen subject to exclusive Coast Guard regulation. The judgment must be reversed.
