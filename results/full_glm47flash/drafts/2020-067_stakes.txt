**ARGUMENT**

**I. The “Record” for Plain Error Review Is the Transcript of the Proceeding Where the Error Occurred**

The Eleventh Circuit erred by looking beyond the trial record to determine prejudice for a plain error claim. Under *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), a court reviewing for plain error must look to the “record” to determine whether the error affected substantial rights. The “record” is defined as the transcript of the trial or proceeding. *Id.* at 732. Here, the error occurred during the plea colloquy, not the sentencing phase. Consequently, the relevant record is the transcript of the plea, not the sentencing hearing or the prior conviction records.

*Olano* establishes that the “record” is the transcript of the trial or proceeding. *Id.* at 732. This limitation ensures that appellate courts do not engage in fact-finding outside the bounds of the evidence actually presented to the trier of fact. *Id.* at 736. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision to consider Greer’s prior convictions—evidence from the sentencing phase—to determine prejudice for an error occurring at the plea stage is a misapplication of the *Olano* standard. The prior convictions are irrelevant to whether the error of failing to instruct on knowledge of status affected the fairness of the plea colloquy.

**II. The Prior Convictions Are Irrelevant to the Specific Error of Failing to Instruct on Knowledge of Status**

The error at issue is the lack of a jury instruction on knowledge of status, as required by *Rehaif v. United States*, 593 U.S. ___ (2021). The record of the plea colloquy and the plea agreement is the only evidence relevant to determining whether the error affected substantial rights. The prior convictions are sentencing-phase evidence and cannot be used to retroactively cure a trial-phase error. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736.

The record of the plea colloquy demonstrates that Greer understood the charges against him. The plea agreement and the colloquy confirm that Greer was aware of his status as a felon at the time of the plea. Therefore, the error did not affect substantial rights. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 732. The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on the prior convictions to find prejudice is improper because it looks outside the record of the proceeding where the error occurred. *Id.* at 732.
