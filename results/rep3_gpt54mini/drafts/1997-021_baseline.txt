**Argument**

I. **The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling standard is rational and consistent with the Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act does not specifically regulate employer polling of employees on union support. See *Allentown Mack Sales & Serv., Inc. v. NLRB*, 522 U.S. 359, 373 (1998). In that statutory gap, the Board may adopt a rule that reasonably balances employee free choice against the Act’s central interest in stable bargaining relationships. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990); *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978).

That is exactly what the Board did. An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board has long permitted polling only when an employer has objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 367-68. That rule is rational for two reasons. First, polling is “potentially, if not inherently, both disruptive” and “unsettling,” so limiting it to situations in which the employer already has objective grounds for doubt is a sensible protection for labor peace. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989). Second, the Board reasonably concluded that permitting polling on a lesser showing would invite intrusive employer tests of union strength despite the presumption of continuing majority support. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 372-73.

The court of appeals erred in substituting its policy preferences for the Board’s. This Court has repeatedly held that “the primary responsibility for developing and applying national labor policy” rests with the Board. *Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 786; *Fall River Dyeing*, 482 U.S. at 42. Judicial disagreement with the Board’s calibration of polling rules is not a basis for invalidation. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 373.

II. **The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is supported by substantial evidence.**

Under the substantial-evidence standard, the question is not whether a reviewing court would have reached the same factual conclusion, but whether the Board’s conclusion is supported by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 477 (1951). Here it is.

The Board credited only seven employee statements as probative evidence of antiunion sentiment sufficient to raise doubt about majority support. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 374-76. That finding was reasonable. Statements by employees who were not in the unit on the critical date could not support reasonable doubt as to the views of the then-existing bargaining unit. Id. at 374-75. Nor was the Board required to treat an employee’s dissatisfaction with union representation as equivalent to opposition to union representation itself. *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989); *Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB*, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995). Likewise, the Board permissibly discounted antiunion statements made during job interviews after the employer had indicated the new company would be nonunion, because such statements are inherently unreliable. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (5th Cir. 1978).

The Board also reasonably rejected hearsay-like assertions by employees purporting to speak for others. The Board has long been skeptical of one employee’s report of other employees’ union sentiments absent corroboration. *Westbrook Bowl, Inc.*, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989). Where, as here, the company relied on equivocal remarks, remote statements, and unverified claims, the Board could find that the record fell short of objective evidence sufficient to create reasonable doubt. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 375-77.

The court of appeals’ contrary view improperly reweighed the evidence. That is not the reviewing court’s role. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 490-91. The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is therefore supported by substantial evidence and should be upheld.

III. **The judgment should be reversed.**

Because the Board’s polling standard is rational and consistent with the Act, and because substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked the requisite doubt, the order should be enforced. *Allentown Mack*, 522 U.S. at 377-78; *Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 786.
