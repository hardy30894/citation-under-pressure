**ARGUMENT**

The conviction must be reversed because 18 U.S.C. §875(c) requires proof that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats. The district court’s objective “reasonable speaker” instruction—asking only whether a reasonable person would foresee the statements as threatening—eliminates the mens rea that the statute and the First Amendment demand.

**I. Section 875(c) Requires a Subjective Intent to Threaten**

At common law, a culpable mental state was an element of every crime. United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922) (“the general rule at common law was that the scienter was a necessary element in the indictment and proof of every crime”). Although Congress may dispense with that requirement for public welfare offenses, nothing in §875(c) suggests it did so. A “threat” is inherently intentional: a communication is not a threat unless the speaker communicates it to intimidate or coerce. Because §875(c) is a criminal statute implicating speech, the rule of lenity also demands that any ambiguity be resolved in favor of requiring subjective intent. United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (the rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly is perhaps not much less old than construction itself); see also United States v. Reese, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1876) (criminal statutes must give fair warning of prohibited conduct). The objective standard adopted below reads the intent element out of the statute.

**II. The First Amendment Requires Proof That the Speaker Intended His Words to Be Taken as a Threat**

This Court has long protected speech that is provocative and challenging. Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949). Speech may be punished only when the speaker intends to produce the harm that the State may prevent. In Schenck, the test was whether the words were used “in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent.” Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). But the speaker’s intent remains central. In Brandenburg, this Court made that requirement explicit: the government may not punish advocacy of force “except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969). The same principle governs true threats. A true threat is a statement intentionally communicated as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969).

An objective “reasonable speaker” standard collapses the distinction between a threat and protected speech. It punishes Elonis not for what he intended, but for how a hypothetical listener might react. That is no different from punishing provocative speech simply because it disturbs the listener—a result Terminiello forbids. Terminiello, 337 U.S. at 4. Under the objective standard, a rap lyric, a joke, or a moment of emotional overstatement can become a federal felony, even though the speaker had no intent to place anyone in fear. The First Amendment requires more. Watts, 394 U.S. at 708.

**III. The Objective Standard Is Unconstitutionally Vague**

A statute that criminalizes speech without a subjective intent requirement gives no fair warning of the line between protected expression and crime. United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (invalidating a statute that left the definition of prohibited conduct to courts and juries). Under the objective standard, a speaker cannot know in advance whether his words will be deemed threatening. It is not until a jury later decides that a reasonable person would have foreseen a threat that liability attaches. This is precisely the kind of “net large enough to catch all possible offenders” that this Court rejected in Reese. Reese, 92 U.S. at 221. The rule of lenity therefore requires reading §875(c) to require subjective intent. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. at 95.

The court of appeals’ contrary holding—that an objective standard is necessary to protect individuals from fear of violence—is backwards. Fear is a relevant consequence, but the speaker’s intent is what distinguishes a criminal threat from protected speech. A speaker who does not intend his words as a threat may still cause fear; but that fear cannot justify punishing speech absent a culpable state of mind. Watts, 394 U.S. at 708.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find beyond a reasonable doubt that Elonis subjectively intended his communications as threats.
