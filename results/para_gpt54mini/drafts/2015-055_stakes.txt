**Argument**

Johnson v. United States announced a new substantive rule that applies retroactively on collateral review because it altered the range of conduct the Armed Career Criminal Act (“ACCA”) punishes and narrowed the class of persons eligible for the Act’s mandatory minimum. See Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591, 603-04 (2015); Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989). Under *Teague*, new substantive rules “generally apply retroactively,” while only new procedural rules are ordinarily prospective. Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 351-52 (2004). A rule is substantive when it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” Id. at 353. Johnson fits that description precisely: it invalidated the ACCA’s residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), because the clause “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges.” Johnson, 576 U.S. at 597.

The Supreme Court has already held that Johnson announced a substantive rule. In Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120, 135 (2016), the Court explained that Johnson “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act, altering the range of conduct or the class of persons that the Act punishes.” Because Johnson “affected the reach of the underlying statute rather than the judicial procedures by which the statute is applied,” it is retroactive. Id. at 129, 135. That conclusion follows directly from the Court’s retroactivity precedents. In Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998), the Court held that decisions narrowing the scope of a criminal statute apply retroactively because they establish that the defendant’s conduct “was not prohibited by law.” Likewise, in Schriro, 542 U.S. at 351-52, the Court distinguished substantive rules from procedural ones and held that only the former apply retroactively as a matter of course.

Johnson is not merely a procedural decision about the method for finding predicates under the ACCA. It invalidated one of the Act’s three definitional clauses. Before Johnson, a prior conviction could qualify as a “violent felony” under the residual clause if it “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). After Johnson, that clause no longer exists as a valid basis for enhancement. See Johnson, 576 U.S. at 597, 606. That is the paradigm of a substantive change, because it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353.

The government may argue that Johnson is only procedural because it concerns judicial reasoning about the level of risk posed by crimes. But the Court rejected that characterization in Welch. The retroactivity inquiry asks whether the decision “narrowed the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms” or “placed particular conduct or persons covered by the statute beyond the State’s power to punish.” Schriro, 542 U.S. at 351-52. Johnson did both: it removed the residual clause from the ACCA and eliminated a category of predicate offenses that had supported mandatory fifteen-year sentences. Welch, 578 U.S. at 129, 135. That kind of decision is substantive even when it arises from constitutional invalidation rather than statutory interpretation. See Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 200-01 (2016).

Retroactivity here also serves the central fairness function recognized in *Teague*. New substantive rules apply to cases on collateral review because “there is no possibility that a defendant stands convicted of ‘an act that the law does not make criminal’” or remains subject to punishment the law no longer authorizes. Schriro, 542 U.S. at 352 (quoting Bousley, 523 U.S. at 620). Welch is serving a sentence that depended in part on the now-invalid residual clause. If his Florida robbery conviction qualifies, it must do so under the ACCA’s elements clause, not the void residual clause. See Johnson, 576 U.S. at 606; Descamps v. United States, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013). If it does not qualify under a remaining clause, then the enhanced sentence cannot stand.

Finally, retroactive application is especially appropriate because the Court in Johnson held the residual clause void for vagueness under the Due Process Clause. See Johnson, 576 U.S. at 595-606. A conviction or sentence based on a void clause is not merely erroneous; it is imposed under an unconstitutional law. See Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 377 (1879). The ordinary presumption against retroactivity does not permit continued imprisonment under a clause the Court has declared invalid. See Welch, 578 U.S. at 129-35.

For those reasons, the Court should hold that Johnson applies retroactively on collateral review and grant relief to Welch.
