**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE CONSTITUTION ESTABLISH THE EXCLUSIVE CRITERIA FOR SERVICE IN CONGRESS**

The Constitution specifically enumerates the qualifications for members of the House of Representatives and the Senate. Article I, §2, cl. 2 requires that a Representative be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the state at the time of election. Article I, §3, cl. 3 imposes analogous requirements for Senators: age thirty, citizen for nine years, and inhabitant of the state. These provisions are “the sole source” of qualifications for congressional office. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 532 (1969). In *Powell*, this Court held that the Constitution’s text and structure demonstrate that the Framers “intended the qualifications for membership in Congress to be *exclusive*” and that neither Congress nor the States may add to them. *Id.* at 533, 550 n.53.

The historical record confirms this exclusivity. The Framers considered and rejected proposals to give Congress or the States power to impose additional qualifications. As *Powell* explained, the “carefully defined” qualifications were “exhaustive” and any addition would “alter the qualifications constitutionally required.” *Id.* at 539-40. This Court has never deviated from that holding. *See* *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 307 (1941) (the “right to choose a Representative” is “derived from the Constitution” and “Congress is empowered to regulate” its exercise but not to restrict the class of candidates). The States possess no reserved power under the Tenth Amendment to add conditions to federal office because “the qualification requirements for membership in Congress were intended to be uniform throughout the Nation.” *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540.

**II. AMENDMENT 73’S BALLOT ACCESS RESTRICTION IS A DISGUISED ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATION**

Arkansas’s Amendment 73 does not purport to add a qualification by name, but its effect is precisely that: it bars from the ballot any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate. This Court has long recognized that a State cannot accomplish indirectly what the Constitution forbids directly. *See* *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 793 (1983) (state election laws that “unnecessarily burden” the right to vote or associate with candidates “cannot survive constitutional scrutiny”). A ballot restriction that permanently disqualifies incumbents based solely on prior service is the functional equivalent of a durational qualification. *Cf.* *Burdick v. Takushi*, 504 U.S. 428, 433 (1992) (state election laws are evaluated by the “character and magnitude” of the burden on federal constitutional rights).

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that “the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp” and that the State “has no authority to add any qualifications to those in the United States Constitution.” *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 328 Ark. 377, 394, 399 (1994) (opinion below). The ballot-access restriction “penalizes” incumbents who choose to run again, effectively creating a “qualification” of term-limited eligibility. This Court has never permitted a State to impose such a penalty. *See* *Cook v. Gralike*, 531 U.S. 510, 525 (2001) (state law requiring ballot notations critical of candidates who failed to support term limits “appears to be a penalty” that “regulates the conduct of candidates” and exceeds state power). By locking out candidates who satisfy the constitutional qualifications, Amendment 73 “adds” a requirement that the Constitution does not contain.

**III. THE STATE’S POLICE POWER DOES NOT EXTEND TO ALTERING FEDERAL QUALIFICATIONS**

The State argues that its reserved police power encompasses regulation of ballot access for all offices. But the power to regulate federal elections is subject to constitutional limitations. *See* *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884) (the right to vote for a Representative “is a right secured by the Constitution”). The Qualifications Clauses occupy the field; they “leave no room for [the States] to impose additional qualifications.” *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 541. Even if the State’s motivation is laudable—to combat entrenchment—that cannot justify an intrusion into the exclusive federal domain. “Any state power to add to the constitutional qualifications would … undermine the principle of uniform national standards that has been a cornerstone of the Constitution.” *Id.* at 540.

The Arkansas Supreme Court thus correctly invalidated Section 3 of Amendment 73. The judgment should be affirmed.
