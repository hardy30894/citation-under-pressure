**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Disclosure Provisions Are Facially Overbroad and Violate the First Amendment**

The compelled disclosure of lobbying expenditures and their sources impermissibly chills associational and expressive freedoms protected by the First Amendment. This Court has long recognized that “[i]t is hardly a novel perception that compelled disclosure of affiliation with groups engaged in advocacy may constitute an effective restraint on freedom of association.” *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958). In *NAACP*, the Supreme Court struck down a state demand for membership lists because disclosure exposed members to “public hostility” and “economic reprisal.” *Id.* at 462-63. Similarly, in *Bates v. Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516, 523 (1960), the Court invalidated a disclosure ordinance where the state failed to demonstrate a “substantial” interest that could not be achieved by less restrictive means.

Florida’s statute requires lobbyists to disclose every expenditure—whether made by the lobbyist or the principal—and the source of all funds. This sweeping mandate reaches far beyond the narrow disclosure upheld in *United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41, 47 (1953), which was limited to “the principal purpose of lobbying” and did not require itemization of every dollar spent. Here, the statute compels disclosure of expenditures for “research,” “publications,” and “travel”—categories that encompass protected speech and association. Such overbreadth cannot stand. *See Shelton v. Tucker*, 364 U.S. 479, 488 (1960) (invalidating a statute requiring teachers to list all organizational affiliations because “even though the governmental purpose be legitimate and substantial, that purpose cannot be pursued by means that broadly stifle fundamental personal liberties when the end can be more narrowly achieved”).

Moreover, the statute fails to exempt anonymous or minor expenditures, thereby violating the right to speak anonymously recognized in *Talley v. California*, 362 U.S. 60, 64-65 (1960) (striking down ordinance requiring handbills to identify sponsors). The threat of reprisal—which appellant’s members fear—is precisely the harm the First Amendment guards against. *See NAACP*, 357 U.S. at 462-63.

Because the disclosure provisions sweep so broadly, they are facially invalid under the overbreadth doctrine. *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 97-98 (1940) (holding that a statute “which upon its face . . . infringes the freedom of speech” may be struck down without requiring proof of a specific unconstitutional application). *Accord Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940); *Lovell v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 451-52 (1938). The district court erred by requiring appellant to meet the stringent standard of *United States v. Salerno*—a case decided after 1970 and inapplicable to First Amendment overbreadth challenges. The proper standard is that a statute is facially invalid if it “sweeps so broadly as to bring within its scope activities that are constitutionally protected.” *Thornhill*, 310 U.S. at 97. Florida’s disclosure provisions do exactly that.

**II. The Contingency-Fee Ban Unconstitutionally Restricts the Right to Petition and Freedom of Speech**

The absolute prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying abridges the right “to petition the Government for a redress of grievances,” a right that “was not intended to be limited to the mere right to present a petition.” *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 552 (1875). The right to petition necessarily includes the right to hire and compensate agents to assist in that effort. *See Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 513 (1939) (recognizing the right to “communicate with the public” and “to discuss political issues” as part of the freedom of assembly). By banning a common and effective method of compensation, the statute directly chills the incentive to engage in protected lobbying activity.

This ban operates as a prior restraint on speech. *See Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931) (holding that prior restraints are presumptively unconstitutional). The state cannot condition the right to lobby on the forfeiture of a particular compensation arrangement without demonstrating a compelling interest and narrow tailoring. *See Grosjean v. American Press Co.*, 297 U.S. 233, 250 (1936) (invalidating a tax that “operate[d] as a prior restraint” on the press). Here, the state has offered no evidence that contingency fees cause corruption distinct from other lobbying fees. The ban is a blunt instrument that eliminates a legitimate economic incentive for speech, thereby violating the First Amendment.

Furthermore, the ban infringes the liberty of contract protected by the Due Process Clause. *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390, 399 (1923) (holding that liberty includes the right “to engage in any of the common occupations of life”). Lobbyists and their principals have a fundamental right to agree upon terms of compensation, and the state may not prohibit a particular form of payment absent a compelling justification. No such justification exists here.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed, and Chapter 93-121 should be declared facially invalid.
