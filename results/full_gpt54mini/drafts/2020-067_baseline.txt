Greer’s request asks this Court to expand plain-error review beyond its established limits. It should decline. The familiar plain-error framework requires a party to show an error that is plain, affects substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732-36 (1993). That inquiry is not a license for appellate courts to become factfinders by searching outside the trial record for new evidence to prove prejudice.

This Court has repeatedly held that plain-error review is constrained by the appellate record. In United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1985), the Court explained that plain-error review is to be exercised “sparingly” and only in “those circumstances in which a miscarriage of justice would otherwise result.” The Court emphasized that the reviewing court must assess the effect of the asserted error in light of the proceedings below, not by reconstructing the case with extra-record materials. Id. at 16-17. Likewise, in Olano, the Court stated that an error affects substantial rights only when it is prejudicial in the sense that it affected the outcome of the district court proceedings. 507 U.S. at 734. That necessarily looks to what happened at trial, not to evidence introduced for the first time on appeal. Id.

The Court’s more recent plain-error cases confirm the point. In Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461, 466-67 (1997), the Court assessed prejudice under Rule 52(b) from the record of the trial and the strength of the government’s proof, explaining that the question is whether the error affected the verdict actually rendered. And in Puckett v. United States, 556 U.S. 129, 135 (2009), the Court reiterated that plain-error review is limited and discretionary, with the burden on the defendant to show prejudice and a serious effect on the proceedings. Nothing in those decisions suggests a court of appeals may leave the record behind and consult new facts to decide whether the defendant would have fared differently.

Greer relies on Rehaif v. United States, 588 U.S. 176, 191-92 (2019), but Rehaif addressed the government’s burden of proof at trial; it did not alter the settled appellate rule that plain-error prejudice is judged on the record made below. In fact, the Court’s plain-error disposition in Rehaif itself did not authorize extra-record factfinding. See id. at 201-02 (Alito, J., dissenting) (warning that the majority’s decision would produce difficult plain-error questions on remand). The proper application of Rule 52(b) remains what it has always been: the defendant must demonstrate a reasonable probability of a different result based on the trial record. Olano, 507 U.S. at 734; Puckett, 556 U.S. at 135.

That conclusion is particularly important in this case. Greer pleaded guilty and had multiple prior felony convictions. The record before the Eleventh Circuit showed that he had served more than a year in prison on several occasions, a fact that strongly undermines any claim that he did not know he was a felon. A court of appeals may consider such record evidence in assessing prejudice; it may not supplement the record with new materials, however, to reach the same result. See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 74-75 (2002) (on plain-error review of a guilty plea, the appellate court may consult the whole record existing at the time of review, but the defendant still bears the burden of showing prejudice from the record made below). Vonn confirms the opposite of Greer’s position: review is broad enough to include the existing record, but not outside evidence developed for the first time on appeal. Id.

Nor does the “fairness, integrity, or public reputation” prong permit extra-record factfinding. That final prong is a discretionary safeguard, not a separate evidentiary regime. Olano, 507 U.S. at 736; United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 631-34 (2002). In Cotton, the Court declined to notice an indictment error because the record showed overwhelming evidence of the omitted fact, underscoring that appellate courts evaluate the seriousness of the error from the trial record and the proceedings below. Id. at 632-34. If the record demonstrates that an error did not affect the verdict, a court need not — and should not — search beyond it to determine fairness. Id.

Greer’s proposed rule would also conflict with the ordinary role of appellate courts. Appellate review is for legal error, not de novo factual development. This Court has long held that “[i]t is the province of the jury to determine the credibility of witnesses,” not of appellate judges to gather and weigh new evidence. United States v. Bailey, 444 U.S. 394, 415 (1980). Plain-error review does not displace that division of labor.

The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted correctly when it relied on the existing record, including Greer’s prior convictions, to conclude that he failed to show prejudice or a serious effect on the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Under Olano, Cotton, Vonn, and Puckett, that was the proper inquiry. The judgment should be affirmed.
