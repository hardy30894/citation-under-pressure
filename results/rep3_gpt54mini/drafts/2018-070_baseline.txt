**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals’ rule cannot be squared with the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act. Congress directed that adjacent state law becomes federal law on the Outer Continental Shelf only “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with” federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That text permits borrowing state law when it fits the subject matter and does not conflict with governing federal law; it does not require a court first to identify a “significant void or gap” in federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach rewrites the statute and conflicts with this Court’s OCSLA decisions.

This Court has repeatedly explained that the OCSLA was designed to create a comprehensive federal regime for the seabed and fixed structures on the Outer Continental Shelf, while borrowing adjacent state law only as surrogate federal law. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Court held that fixed drilling platforms are “artificial islands” governed by the OCSLA, not by maritime law, and that state law applies through § 1333(a)(2) when federal maritime law does not govern “of its own force.” Id. at 355, 366. The Court did not announce a freestanding “gap” prerequisite; it looked instead to whether the federal rule that might displace state law actually applied. Id. at 366. Likewise, in Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court treated Louisiana law as surrogate federal law under the OCSLA because the Act “made the law of the adjacent State applicable.” Id. at 103-04. And in Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Court recognized that state law is borrowed on the OCS unless displaced by federal law, emphasizing the Act’s incorporation of adjacent state law “to the extent” it is not inconsistent with federal law. Id. at 478-79.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the OCSLA’s structure and purpose. Congress enacted the statute because federal law alone was not intended to be “a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). It chose to borrow state law to fill the legal order governing fixed OCS installations. Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355. That borrowing rule would make little sense if state law could never apply whenever Congress had enacted any federal regulation touching the subject. Such a reading would nullify Congress’s directive that state law applies whenever “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

The Ninth Circuit relied on the wrong analytic framework. The Fifth Circuit’s earlier “gap-filling” formulations, including Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Insurance Ass’n, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), do not control the meaning of § 1333(a)(2)(A). Later Fifth Circuit cases recognized that the inquiry is whether state law is applicable and inconsistent with federal law, not whether federal law leaves a void. See Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990); Grand Isle Shipyard, Inc. v. Seacor Marine, L.L.C., 589 F.3d 778, 781-82 (5th Cir. 2009) (en banc). The statutory words “applicable” and “not inconsistent” are separate requirements, and neither is satisfied by a gap-only test.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule is especially mistaken here because the federal wage-and-hour laws that govern Parker’s operations do not preclude borrowed state law merely because they provide baseline protections. The Fair Labor Standards Act establishes minimum federal standards, not exclusive ones. See 29 U.S.C. § 218(a); Pac. Merch. Shipping Ass’n v. Aubry, 918 F.2d 1409, 1414-15 (9th Cir. 1990). Where Congress has preserved more protective state wage laws in the FLSA itself, there is no basis for concluding that the OCSLA silently strips those laws of effect offshore. See Fort Halifax Packing Co. v. Coyne, 482 U.S. 1, 21 (1987); California Div. of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Constr., N.A., Inc., 519 U.S. 316, 325 (1997).

California’s meal-period and wage-payment rules are also not “inconsistent” with the FLSA within the meaning of § 1333(a)(2)(A). Preemption turns on conflict, not on whether federal law already regulates the field. See Wyeth v. Levine, 555 U.S. 555, 565-71 (2009); English v. General Electric Co., 496 U.S. 72, 79 (1990). The OCSLA incorporates adjacent state law unless it conflicts with federal law; it does not erase state law simply because federal law addresses related subjects. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A).

This Court should reverse. The correct rule is the one the Ninth Circuit adopted at first, before it changed course: state law applies on fixed OCS platforms when it is applicable and not inconsistent with federal law. Because California’s wage-and-hour protections fit that rule, Newton’s claims should proceed.
