**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits a defendant from being “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb” for the same offense. *U.S. Const., amend. V; Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This constitutional protection is not limited to the trial on guilt or innocence; it extends to any proceeding that constitutes a “second prosecution for the same offense” after an acquittal. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a defendant may not be retried on a sentencing enhancement after a prior sentencing hearing resulted in an acquittal on that enhancement, even in a noncapital case. The Court emphasized that the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” — including the burden of proof, the right to a jury, and the finality of the determination — are present in sentencing proceedings that determine the length of a defendant’s punishment. *Id.* at 439, 441. The Court concluded that such proceedings are “in essence a second trial” for the purposes of double jeopardy. *Id.* at 441.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California* — that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings — is irreconcilable with *Bullington* and its progeny. The lead opinion attempts to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that it was a capital case, but the Supreme Court has never held that *Bullington*’s reasoning is limited to capital sentencing. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court observed that *Bullington* and *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), were “capital cases,” but it did not hold that their double jeopardy analysis was inapplicable to noncapital cases. *Id.* at 392. The Court’s statement was made in the context of determining whether a new rule of law required prospective application under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), not in rejecting the applicability of *Bullington* to noncapital cases. Indeed, the Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that the double jeopardy protection applies to any proceeding that “involves the same essential elements” as the original prosecution. *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995).

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), to justify the absence of double jeopardy protection in sentencing is misplaced. *Williams* upheld a death sentence based on judicial factfinding from probation reports and other out-of-court sources, but it did not address the double jeopardy implications of retrial on sentencing enhancements. The Court in *Williams* explicitly stated that the due process clause does not require “freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” *Id.* at 251. However, *Williams* did not purport to exempt sentencing proceedings from the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court has since made clear that the Clause applies to any proceeding that “involves the same essential elements” as the original offense. *Witte*, 515 U.S. at 398. The fact that a sentencing proceeding may be less formal than a trial on guilt does not strip it of double jeopardy protection when it determines a defendant’s punishment based on prior convictions.

The California Supreme Court also argues that the procedural protections in *Penal Code § 1025* trials are statutory, not constitutional, and therefore do not trigger double jeopardy. This argument fails. The Supreme Court has held that the presence of procedural safeguards — such as the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt — is not a prerequisite for double jeopardy protection. In *Bullington*, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause even though the defendant had not been entitled to a jury trial under Missouri law. *451 U.S. at 439*. The Court emphasized that the “critical inquiry” is whether the proceeding is “in essence a second trial.” *Id.* at 441. The fact that California provides a jury trial for prior conviction allegations under § 1025 does not diminish the constitutional significance of the proceeding; rather, it underscores that the state treats such determinations as serious, fact-intensive inquiries akin to a trial on guilt.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that the severity of the punishment determines the applicability of double jeopardy. In *United States v. Halper*, 490 U.S. 435 (1989), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies even when the second punishment is civil in form but punitive in effect. *Id.* at 447. The Court reasoned that the Clause protects against “multiplicity of punishments” for the same offense, regardless of the label attached to the penalty. Similarly, in *U.S. v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148 (1997), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings where the defendant is subjected to a longer sentence based on factual determinations. *Id.* at 155. The Court emphasized that “the sentencing judge’s determination of the facts underlying the sentence is not a mere administrative act.” *Id.* at 156.

The California Supreme Court’s assertion that the “degree of mental anguish” or the “procedural differences” between capital and noncapital cases justify a distinction is legally untenable. In *Rumsey*, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings even when the defendant is not facing death. *467 U.S. at 209*. The Court stated that “the nature of the proceeding, not the severity of the penalty, determines whether double jeopardy applies.” *Id.* at 210.
