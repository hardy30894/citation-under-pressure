**Argument**

The judgment should be affirmed because the FDCPA’s text and structure distinguish sharply between “debt collectors” and creditors, and Santander—having purchased the loans and then collected them for its own account—was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector. The Act defines “debt collector” principally as one “whose principal purpose” is debt collection or one who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). It separately defines “creditor” as a person to whom a debt is owed. Id. § 1692a(4). As the Court has recognized, the FDCPA’s coverage turns on whether the defendant is collecting “owed [to] ... another,” not on whether the debt was once owed to another. Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995).

That reading follows from the statute’s ordinary structure. Congress enacted the FDCPA “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” not to regulate creditors collecting their own accounts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e); see Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 817 F.3d 131, 136-37 (4th Cir. 2016). The second prong of the “debt collector” definition reaches those who “regularly collect[] ... debts owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). If a company purchases a debt and then seeks payment for itself, it is collecting a debt “owed” to itself, not “owed or due another.” Id. That is true even if the debt was in default before purchase. Default status is relevant only to one statutory exclusion, not to the threshold question whether the collector is acting “owed or due another.” See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii).

Petitioners’ contrary theory collapses the statute’s separate definitions and makes default dispositive. But the text does not support that shortcut. Section 1692a(4)’s creditor definition excludes a person who receives an assignment of a defaulted debt “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4) (emphasis added). That exclusion confirms the basic rule: the Act distinguishes between those collecting for another and those collecting for themselves. Likewise, § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) excludes persons collecting debts “owed or due another” where the debt “was not in default at the time it was obtained.” Id. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). That exclusion presupposes that the person otherwise fits within the principal definition—i.e., that it is collecting for another. See Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314-15 (11th Cir. 2015).

This Court’s precedents reinforce the point that the relevant inquiry is whether the defendant is collecting for another at the time of the challenged conduct. In Heintz, the Court explained that the FDCPA covers a person who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] ... another.” 514 U.S. at 293 (alteration in original). So too here: once Santander purchased the receivables, it was no longer collecting “owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The Fourth Circuit correctly held that “the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition.” Henson, 817 F.3d at 136.

Petitioners’ reliance on cases treating some assignees of defaulted debts as debt collectors is misplaced. Those decisions involve entities that purchased defaulted debts while acting as servicers or agents for others, or that otherwise fit the statutory definition apart from ownership. See Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012); Ruth v. Triumph P’ships LLC, 577 F.3d 790, 796-97 (7th Cir. 2009); FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007). Santander, by contrast, purchased the loans and then attempted to collect them on its own account. That makes it a creditor, not a debt collector. See Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A., 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013).

Finally, petitioners’ functional argument proves too much. Their rule would subject any company that sometimes collects for others to the FDCPA whenever it collects for itself, simply because it once handled defaulted accounts. Nothing in the Act suggests Congress meant to transform creditors into debt collectors based on prior ownership history. The statute requires that the challenged conduct occur while the defendant is acting as a debt collector. 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(6), 1692e. Because Santander was collecting debts it owned, the FDCPA does not apply.

Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed.
