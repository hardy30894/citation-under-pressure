Milford’s judgment should be affirmed because the school’s exclusion of the Good News Club from after-hours use did not violate the Club’s speech rights, and in any event the exclusion was justified by Milford’s obligation to avoid Establishment Clause violations. This Court has long recognized that a State may draw lines in use of its property and may decline to subsidize or sponsor religious exercise. See Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16-18 (1947); McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 210-12 (1948); Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314-15 (1952).

First, Milford did not suppress protected expression in a public forum. The school opened its facilities only for specified community uses, not for all expressive activity. A public body that opens property for limited, designated purposes may reserve the forum to those purposes and exclude uses outside them. See Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574-76 (1941). Milford’s policy permitted “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” while expressly forbidding “religious purposes.” That is a permissible line-drawing rule. See Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 406-08 (1953) (upholding exclusion where State regulated the use, not the content, of a forum).

The Club’s proposed meetings were not merely discussion of morals in the abstract. They included prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as Savior. Milford could reasonably determine that those activities fell on the forbidden side of its policy because they were religious exercise, not the sort of civic or recreational use the school had chosen to permit. The Constitution does not require a public school to transform a restricted, after-hours community-use program into an open platform for worship. See Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39, 47-48 (1966) (State may preserve property for its intended use); Johnson v. United States, 333 U.S. 10, 14 (1948) (government may impose reasonable limits on use of its property).

Second, even if the Club could characterize the denial as speech discrimination, Milford’s position was justified because allowing the Club’s meetings would risk a violation of the Establishment Clause. This Court has repeatedly held that school-sponsored or school-accommodated religious activity is constitutionally fraught. In McCollum, the Court invalidated religious instruction conducted in public school buildings during school hours, explaining that the use of tax-supported property for religious teaching crossed constitutional lines. 333 U.S. at 209-12. In Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 424-25 (1962), the Court held that the State could not compose or require prayer in public schools. And in School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 225-27 (1963), the Court rejected Bible reading and recitation in public schools because the Establishment Clause forbids governmental aid to religious exercise.

Milford’s concern was therefore not speculative. The Club sought access to elementary-school premises for a program directed at children, led by adults, under school auspices, in a setting that bore the imprimatur of the public school. That setting raises exactly the danger identified in McCollum: government property and authority being used to promote religious training. 333 U.S. at 209-12. A school is entitled, indeed obliged, to avoid even the appearance of advancing religion. See Everson, 330 U.S. at 15-16; Engel, 370 U.S. at 431-32.

The Club may argue that it wanted only equal access. But equality does not compel a school to permit religious worship where it has chosen to permit only secular community uses. The Constitution does not require the State to “lend its power” to religion or to make public facilities available for exercises that would place the State in the position of facilitating devotion. McCollum, 333 U.S. at 210-11. Zorach upheld a released-time program precisely because the State kept its hands off the religious instruction and did not use public facilities to conduct it. 343 U.S. at 312-15. Milford’s exclusion is the same constitutional caution in reverse: by declining to host the Club’s devotional meetings, the school avoided entanglement and endorsement.

Nor does the Free Speech Clause require a contrary result. The Court has long recognized that freedom of speech does not disable government from managing its own property or from preventing uses inconsistent with the purpose for which the property is held. Hague, 307 U.S. at 515-16; Cox, 312 U.S. at 574-76; Adderley, 385 U.S. at 47-48. Here, Milford did exactly that. Its policy allowed community use, but not religious purposes. That limitation was reasonable, viewpoint-neutral in the sense that it excluded an entire class of use from school property, and constitutionally prudent in light of the Establishment Clause.

Because Milford’s denial of access was a permissible restriction on the use of school property and, independently, was justified by the school’s duty to avoid unconstitutional support of religion, the judgment below should be affirmed.
