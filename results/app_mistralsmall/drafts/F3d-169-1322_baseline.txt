**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Appellees Were Not Joint Employers Under the AWPA**

The AWPA defines an "employer" to include "any person acting directly or indirectly in the interest of an employer in relation to an employee." 29 U.S.C. § 1802(5). The Supreme Court has held that joint employment exists where two or more entities "share or co-determine those matters governing the essential terms and conditions of [a] worker’s employment." *NLRB v. Browning-Ferris Indus., Inc.*, 483 U.S. 274, 289 (1987). The district court erred in failing to apply this standard, instead requiring a showing of "control" akin to that required under the FLSA. *See Charles v. Burton*, 857 F.Supp. 1574, 1583 (M.D.Ga. 1994).

The AWPA’s legislative history confirms that Congress intended a broad definition of "employer." *See* H.R. Rep. No. 100-145, pt. 1, at 10 (1987), *reprinted in* 1987 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2607, 2616. Courts interpreting the AWPA have accordingly applied the *Browning-Ferris* joint-employer test. *See, e.g., Doe v. Dust Devil Agric. Co-op., Inc.*, 127 F.3d 1216, 1220 (9th Cir. 1997) (applying *Browning-Ferris* to AWPA claims). Under this standard, appellees were joint employers.

First, appellees shared control over the essential terms of the workers’ employment. Little Rock and Hall provided the seeds, boxes, and trailer for the snap bean harvest, and the Burtons agreed to market the crop through Little Rock. *See* Record at 123-24. This interdependence demonstrates that appellees co-determined the workers’ conditions of employment. *See Browning-Ferris*, 483 U.S. at 290 (joint employers need not "actually exercise control" but must have "unexercised authority" over essential terms).

Second, appellees’ financial arrangements with Luxama further support joint-employer status. The Burtons paid Luxama per box picked, and Little Rock and Hall shared in the profits. *See* Record at 123. This profit-sharing arrangement evidences a joint enterprise. *See Donovan v. DialAmerica Mktg., Inc.*, 757 F.2d 1376, 1383 (3d Cir. 1985) (profit-sharing indicative of joint employment).

The district court’s reliance on *Usery v. Pilgrim Equip. Co.*, 527 F.2d 1308 (5th Cir. 1976), was misplaced. *Pilgrim* applied the FLSA’s "economic reality" test, which is distinct from the AWPA’s statutory definition. *See id.* at 1311. The AWPA’s text and legislative history compel application of *Browning-Ferris*.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Actual Damages for Violations of the AWPA’s Registration Provision**

The AWPA requires employers to verify a farm labor contractor’s (FLC) registration before utilizing their services. 29 U.S.C. § 1842. The district court held that appellees were not "employers" under the AWPA and thus could not be liable for failing to verify Luxama’s registration. *Charles*, 857 F.Supp. at 1583. This was error.

The AWPA’s registration provision imposes liability on "any person" who utilizes an unregistered FLC. 29 U.S.C. § 1842. The statute does not limit liability to "employers" as defined in § 1802(5). *See id.* § 1842(a) ("No person shall utilize the services of a farm labor contractor... unless..."). Thus, even if appellees were not joint employers, they violated the AWPA by failing to verify Luxama’s registration.

The district court’s contrary holding conflicts with the AWPA’s plain text. *See Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (courts must give effect to a statute’s plain meaning). The registration provision’s broad language—"no person shall utilize"—encompasses appellees’ conduct. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1842.

Moreover, the AWPA’s remedial scheme authorizes actual damages for registration violations. 29 U.S.C. § 1854(c)(1) (providing for "actual damages" for violations of § 1842). The district court’s refusal to award actual damages was thus contrary to the AWPA’s text and purpose.

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to Little Rock and Hall on the "Utilized" Claim**

Little Rock and Hall argued they did not "utilize" Luxama’s services under 29 U.S.C. § 1842. The district court agreed, finding no evidence they directed or supervised the workers. *See* Order at 4. This was error.

The AWPA defines "utilize" broadly to include "any person who... secures the services of a farm labor contractor." 29 U.S.C. § 1802(10). Little Rock and Hall’s contractual arrangement with the Burtons—whereby they shared profits, provided supplies, and marketed the crop—demonstrates they "secured" Luxama’s services. *See* Record at 123-24. The district court’s narrow construction of "utilize" conflicts with the AWPA’s remedial purpose. *See Tony & Susan Alamo Found. v. Sec’y of Labor*, 471 U.S. 290, 302 (1985) (AWPA must be liberally construed to effectuate its purpose).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed. The appellees were joint employers under the AWPA, violated the registration provision, and are liable for actual damages. Little Rock and Hall "utilized" Luxama’s services under § 1842. The court’s contrary rulings were erroneous.
