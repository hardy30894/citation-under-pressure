# ARGUMENT

This case asks whether a consent decree that expressly disclaimed any "finding or admission of liability" nonetheless "resolved" Guam's liability so as to convert its six-year cost-recovery claim into a time-barred contribution claim. Settled principles of construction—developed long before CERCLA existed—compel the conclusion that it did not, and that Guam's suit against the Navy remains timely.

## I. The Consent Decree's Own Terms, Construed According to the Parties' Manifest Intent, Did Not Resolve Guam's Liability.

A judicially approved settlement, like any consent instrument, must be read according to what the parties actually agreed to, not what might be inferred from provisions read in isolation. This Court has long instructed that decrees entered by consent are to be construed within their four corners, and that courts should not extend such instruments beyond the scope the parties themselves marked out. *See United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 116 (1932) (a consent decree "must be read in the light of the conditions that produced it"); *Hughes v. United States*, 342 U.S. 353, 357 (1952) (a consent judgment binds parties only to the extent of its actual terms).

Here, the 2004 Consent Decree could not be clearer: it was entered "based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." That disclaimer is not surplus language to be brushed aside—it is the parties' manifest intent, and under *Swift* and *Hughes* it controls the meaning of the instrument as a whole. A reading that treats the Decree as resolving liability notwithstanding its explicit disclaimer disregards the very "conditions that produced" the settlement. *Swift*, 286 U.S. at 116.

This Court has also long cautioned against literalistic readings of instruments that produce results the drafters plainly did not intend. "All laws should receive a sensible construction. General terms should be so limited in their application as not to lead to injustice, oppression, or an absurd consequence." *United States v. Kirby*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486–87 (1868). And "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892). Reading a disclaimer-laden settlement as an admission of liability by another name is precisely the sort of technical, letter-bound construction those cases forbid.

## II. Doubts About the Decree's Effect Must Be Resolved to Preserve, Not Forfeit, Guam's Remedy.

Even if the Decree's language were thought ambiguous, that ambiguity cannot be resolved against the party the underlying remedial scheme was designed to protect. Remedial statutes—and the settlements implementing them—are to be construed broadly to serve their protective purposes rather than narrowly to defeat them. *Tcherepnin v. Knight*, 389 U.S. 332, 336 (1967) (remedial statutes are to be read to effectuate their protective purposes, not restricted by technical readings). Similarly, where competing interpretations of an agreement are available, courts favor the reading that preserves rather than extinguishes a party's substantive right. *Mastro Plastics Corp. v. NLRB*, 350 U.S. 270, 285 (1956) (construing ambiguous contractual language so as not to work a forfeiture of statutory rights).

Guam's position—that the Decree left its liability unresolved, exactly as its text says—preserves Guam's ability to seek full recovery from the party actually responsible for creating the hazard. The Navy's position—that the same disclaimer-laden document silently extinguished Guam's six-year remedy and substituted a three-year window that expired before Guam even finished building the required cover system—produces exactly the sort of forfeiture *Mastro Plastics* forbids.

## III. Even If Section 113 Governs, Guam's Claim Is Not Untimely Because the Navy's Own Conduct Concealed the Basis for Guam's Claim.

Limitations periods have never been rigidly applied where the defendant's own conduct obscured the plaintiff's ability to discover its claim. "[W]here a party seeking to avail himself of [a defense] has, by fraud or otherwise, prevented [a] party from availing himself of it, the party thus prevented has his equitable rights, and the courts of equity will preserve them." *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 349 (1874). This equitable tolling principle applies with full force to federal statutes of limitations generally. *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 397 (1946).

The Navy operated the Ordot Dump for decades, depositing military waste—including DDT and Agent Orange—without disclosing the scope of contamination to the civilian government that inherited the site. Guam could not have known, at the time of the 2004 Decree, the full extent of the Navy's responsibility for what it was being asked to remediate. Under *Bailey* and *Holmberg*, any limitations period should run from discovery of the Navy's role, not from the Decree's entry.

## Conclusion

Guam, an unincorporated territory that has long depended on the United States for its basic governance, *see Downes v. Bidwell*, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901), should not be made to forfeit its remedy against the Navy through a hyper-technical reading of an instrument that expressly disclaimed the very liability now invoked against it. The judgment below should be reversed.
