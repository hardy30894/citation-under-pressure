# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court's Finding of Consent Is Reviewed for Clear Error, and the Record Compels Reversal.

Because the district court's determination that Gonzalez-Caballero consented to Danelsy's removal and retention was a factual finding, this Court reviews for clear error. A finding is clearly erroneous when, "although there is evidence to support it, the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." Anderson v. City of Bessemer City, 470 U.S. 564, 573 (1985). That standard is met here. The district court's finding rests on an isolated, ambiguous exchange at a Panama City hotel, while it disregards the overwhelming weight of Gonzalez-Caballero's contemporaneous conduct—her accompaniment of Danelsy only to obtain exit paperwork, her attempt to contact Mena the day after his departure, and her immediate recourse to Panamanian police, the Institute for Children, the Children's Commission, and ultimately the Office of the President—all of which unambiguously demonstrate that she never consented to Danelsy's permanent removal from Panama.

## II. Consent Is a Narrow Affirmative Defense That Mena Bore the Burden of Proving, and the Convention's Remedial Purpose Requires Strict Construction of That Defense.

The Hague Convention "is generally intended to restore the pre-abduction status quo and to deter parents from crossing international boundaries in search of a more sympathetic court." Friedrich v. Friedrich, 78 F.3d 1060, 1064 (6th Cir. 1996). Consistent with that remedial purpose, the defenses to return—including consent—are "narrow" exceptions that must not be construed to swallow the rule of return. Id. at 1067; see also Nunez-Escudero v. Tice-Menley, 58 F.3d 374, 376 (8th Cir. 1995) (recognizing the Convention's purpose of restoring the status quo ante). Congress codified this allocation of burdens in the implementing statute, placing on the respondent the obligation to establish consent by a preponderance of the evidence. 42 U.S.C. § 11603(e)(2)(B). Courts applying the Convention have consistently held that this burden is not met by equivocal or partial evidence of agreement. See Miller v. Miller, 240 F.3d 392, 402 (4th Cir. 2001) (respondent bears burden of proving affirmative defenses); Diorinou v. Mezitis, 237 F.3d 133, 142 (2d Cir. 2001) (affirmative defenses to return are to be narrowly applied).

Mena did not carry that burden. Gonzalez-Caballero's own testimony was, at most, that she agreed to a temporary visit conditioned on Mena's assistance with her immigration, or alternatively that any removal was contingent on her ability to follow and reassume custody. Neither version establishes unconditional consent to Danelsy's permanent retention in the United States. Where the evidence of consent is this internally inconsistent and conditional, it cannot satisfy the respondent's burden under the Convention's narrowly drawn defense. See Mozes v. Mozes, 239 F.3d 1067, 1075–76 (9th Cir. 2001) (emphasizing that a parent's purported agreement must be assessed for the "shared, specific intent" of the parties regarding the child's residence, not inferred loosely from ambiguous conduct).

## III. Consent to a Limited or Conditional Arrangement Does Not Establish Consent to Permanent Retention.

Even crediting Mena's account of the hotel conversation, at most it shows that Gonzalez-Caballero contemplated a plan by which she, Mena, and Danelsy would ultimately reside together in the United States—not that she relinquished her custodial rights to a unilateral, permanent removal. Courts have repeatedly distinguished between consent to a child's temporary travel or a conditional family arrangement and consent to permanent retention, holding that only the latter satisfies the Convention's consent defense. In Wanninger v. Wanninger, 850 F. Supp. 78, 81–82 (D. Mass. 1994), the court held that a parent's agreement to a child's visit abroad did not constitute consent to the child's retention beyond the agreed period. Similarly, in Currier v. Currier, 845 F. Supp. 916, 922–23 (D.N.H. 1994), the court found that ambiguous statements permitting a child's trip abroad were insufficient to prove the affirmative defense of consent to retention. The Eighth Circuit reached the same conclusion in Rydder v. Rydder, 49 F.3d 369, 373 (8th Cir. 1995), rejecting a claim of consent where the petitioning parent's actions after the child's removal—much like Gonzalez-Caballero's—were inconsistent with any intent to permit permanent retention. And in Zuker v. Andrews, 2 F. Supp. 2d 134, 138–39 (D. Mass. 1998), aff'd, 181 F.3d 81 (1st Cir. 1999), the district court likewise held that a parent's limited or conditional acquiescence to a child's stay abroad did not establish the affirmative defense of consent under the Convention.

The same principle controls here. Gonzalez-Caballero's undisputed, near-immediate efforts to locate Danelsy through Panamanian authorities—efforts she pursued relentlessly for months, culminating in a government-sponsored trip to the United States—are wholly incompatible with the district court's finding that she consented to Danelsy's permanent retention. Just as the courts in Wanninger, Currier, Rydder, and Zuker refused to permit ambiguous, conditional statements to override a parent's subsequent unequivocal conduct demonstrating non-consent, this Court should hold that the district court clearly erred in crediting Mena's version of an ambiguous hotel conversation over the entirety of Gonzalez-Caballero's contemporaneous conduct.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the district court's finding of consent was clearly erroneous. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to order Danelsy's return to Panama pursuant to the Hague Convention and 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601–11610.
