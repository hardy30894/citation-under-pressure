# ARGUMENT

## I. The Board's Insistence on an Identical Evidentiary Threshold for Polling and for Outright Withdrawal of Recognition Is Not a Rational Exercise of Administrative Judgment.

An agency's rule must rest on "a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made." Burlington Truck Lines, Inc. v. United States, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962). And "the orderly functioning of the process of review requires that the grounds upon which the administrative agency acted be clearly disclosed and adequately sustained." SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80, 94 (1943). The Board's polling standard fails both requirements.

This Court has long recognized that a certified union's majority status carries only a rebuttable presumption. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944). Once that presumption is subject to challenge, an employer confronts a genuine dilemma: bargaining with a union that has lost majority support itself violates the Act, yet withdrawing recognition from a union that retains majority support is equally unlawful. A poll exists precisely to resolve that uncertainty before the employer commits itself irrevocably in either direction. The Board itself has recognized that polling, conducted under the procedural safeguards of Struksnes Construction Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)—secret ballot, notice of purpose, assurance against reprisal, and a poll addressed only to majority status—is a legitimate device for testing employee sentiment.

But the Board demands that an employer possess the very same "reasonable doubt" before it may take a poll that it must possess before it may withdraw recognition outright. That equivalence drains the polling mechanism of function. If an employer already has evidence sufficient to justify outright withdrawal, it has no need of a poll; if it lacks such evidence, the Board forbids the poll that might have supplied it. The standard is thus self-defeating: it authorizes polling only in the circumstance in which polling is superfluous. An agency rule that "runs counter to the evidence before the agency," Burlington Truck Lines, 371 U.S. at 168, or that cannot be reconciled with the very purpose the agency says the mechanism serves, is not entitled to deference merely because the agency invokes its expertise. See NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963) (Board's judgment must still be "consonant with the purposes of the Act").

Nor can this incoherence be excused as inherent caution against "disruptive" polling. This Court has recognized that an employer's efforts to ascertain employee sentiment through lawful means, absent coercion, further rather than frustrate the Act's purposes of industrial peace and employee free choice. See Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 797–798 (1945) (Board must accommodate employer prerogatives with organizational rights, not needlessly extinguish the former). A poll conducted under Struksnes safeguards is the least disruptive method available for testing the very question—majority support—that determines whether the employer may lawfully continue to deal with the union at all. Collapsing the polling standard into the withdrawal standard does not protect employees; it simply forecloses the accurate, low-risk fact-finding that this Court's precedents contemplate an employer may lawfully undertake. See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 596–597 (1969) (an employer with a good-faith and reasonably grounded doubt about a union's majority status may test that doubt without committing an unfair labor practice).

## II. Even Under the Board's Own Standard, Its Finding That Allentown Lacked a Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence on the Record as a Whole.

Review of Board factfinding is governed by the substantial-evidence standard, which requires "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion," Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938), assessed not in isolation but "on the record as a whole," including "whatever in the record fairly detracts from its weight." Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 477, 488 (1951). A court may not treat the Board's choice among reasonable inferences as conclusive where the Board has disregarded evidence that cuts the other way. Id. at 490.

Here the Board's own factfinder credited a series of unsolicited employee statements disclaiming continued support for Local 724, made by workers drawn from a bargaining unit that itself had been substantially reconstituted through the sale. The Board discounted several of these statements not because they were incredible, but by applying rigid categorical rules—treating statements made during hiring interviews as inherently suspect and treating expressions of dissatisfaction with union dues as legally irrelevant to sentiment about representation—without weighing them against the whole record, as Universal Camera requires. 340 U.S. at 488. Given a workforce reduced from forty-five to thirty employees, a handful of disregarded statements can readily tip the balance between reasonable doubt and its absence. The Board's mechanical exclusions, rather than a considered weighing of "whatever in the record fairly detracts" from the conclusion reached, id., cannot substitute for the reasoned, whole-record analysis this Court has demanded since Consolidated Edison and Universal Camera.

For these reasons, the Board's order should be vacated.
