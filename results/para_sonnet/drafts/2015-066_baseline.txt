**ARGUMENT**

**I. Nichols Authorizes Reliance on Uncounseled Convictions Only as Sentencing Factors, Not as Elements of a Substantive Offense.**

In *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), this Court held only that "an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott* because no prison term was imposed, is also valid when used to enhance punishment at a subsequent conviction." *Id.* at 746–47. That holding rests entirely on the sentencing context: courts may consider prior convictions to calibrate punishment under a preponderance standard, and a defendant may "convince the sentencing court of the unreliability of any prior valid but uncounseled convictions." *Id.* at 747–48. Section 117(a) does something categorically different. It makes the prior convictions themselves *elements of the crime*, which the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. *See In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (the reasonable-doubt standard is "a prime instrument for reducing the risk of convictions resting on factual error"). Nothing in *Nichols* purports to authorize using an uncounseled conviction to satisfy *Winship*'s demand. To transplant *Nichols* into the guilt phase would collapse the very distinction the Court drew between proof of guilt and calculation of punishment.

**II. Using a Constitutionally Infirm Conviction to Establish Guilt of a New Offense Violates the Principles of *Burgett v. Texas*.**

This Court has already held that a conviction obtained without counsel cannot bear the weight of proving guilt or enhancing punishment for a later offense. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), the Court explained that "[t]o permit a conviction obtained in violation of *Gideon v. Wainwright* to be used against a person either to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense . . . is to erode the principle of that case." *Id.* at 115. The Court warned that "the accused in effect suffers anew from the deprivation of that Sixth Amendment right." *Id.* Section 117(a) does precisely what *Burgett* forbids: it uses Bryant's uncounseled tribal convictions to supply the very fact that converts otherwise noncriminal conduct into a federal felony. The absence of counsel in the earlier proceedings thus reaches forward to taint the current prosecution, just as *Burgett* held it could not.

**III. Reliability Concerns Underlying the Right to Counsel Are at Their Zenith When a Conviction Is Used to Prove an Element of Guilt.**

The Sixth Amendment right to counsel exists because counsel is essential to the reliability of criminal adjudication. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 344 (1963) ("reason and reflection require us to recognize that in our adversary system of criminal justice, any person haled into court . . . cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him"). Where a prior conviction results in imprisonment, this Court has treated the absence of counsel as rendering that conviction inherently suspect for use in later proceedings. *See United States v. Tucker*, 404 U.S. 443, 448 (1972) (remanding for resentencing where the sentencing judge gave "explicit attention" to constitutionally invalid prior convictions); *Alabama v. Shelton*, 535 U.S. 654, 661–62 (2002) (no term of imprisonment, even suspended, may rest on an uncounseled conviction). Tribal misdemeanor convictions under § 117(a) frequently result in imprisonment, see 25 U.S.C. § 1302(a)(7)(B)-(C), yet the tribal courts below afforded Bryant no counsel at all. Permitting the government to rely on these convictions to prove an essential element of a federal felony—carrying up to ten years' imprisonment, 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)—imports precisely the unreliability that *Gideon*, *Burgett*, and *Tucker* condemn.

**IV. The Tribal Origin of These Convictions Does Not Diminish the Sixth Amendment Concern When They Are Repurposed as Federal Predicate Offenses.**

That tribal courts are not themselves bound by the Sixth Amendment, *see Plains Commerce Bank v. Long Family Land & Cattle Co.*, 554 U.S. 316, 337 (2008), does not answer whether the federal government may use those convictions to establish guilt in a *federal* prosecution. The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized in *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), that "the government may not use prior tribal court misdemeanor convictions that do not provide an equivalent right of counsel as evidence of guilt in a subsequent federal prosecution." The question is not whether tribal courts violated the Sixth Amendment—they could not, since it does not apply to them—but whether the *federal* government's own prosecution, which must satisfy the federal Constitution, may rest guilt on convictions lacking the safeguards that would be constitutionally required had they occurred in state or federal court. Congress itself has recognized as much, conditioning expanded tribal sentencing authority on the provision of counsel equivalent to the Sixth Amendment. *See* 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c) (Tribal Law and Order Act); *id.* § 1304 (Special Domestic Violence Criminal Jurisdiction). Section 117(a) cannot be read to circumvent that considered judgment by permitting the federal government to do indirectly—through use of the convictions as elements—what it could not do directly.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
