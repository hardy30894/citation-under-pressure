**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Coast Guard’s Regulatory Authority Over Uninspected Vessels Is Limited and Does Not Preclude OSHA Jurisdiction.**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act’s preemption provision, Section 4(b)(1), excludes only those working conditions “with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is confined to specific safety equipment and does not encompass the comprehensive occupational safety and health standards that OSHA enforces.

The Coast Guard’s statutory grant for uninspected vessels is limited to regulations for fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency locating equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (1966). This narrow authorization reflects Congress’s intent to leave other working conditions unregulated by the Coast Guard. This Court has long recognized that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction over vessels is not plenary. In *The Steamer “R. W. Bache,”* 1 Ben. 35 (S.D.N.Y. 1878), the court held that the Coast Guard’s authority extends only to matters of navigation and safety of life at sea, not to general working conditions of employees. The drilling barge here, while stationary and engaged in extracting oil, was not in navigation. Because the Coast Guard lacks statutory authority to regulate the occupational safety and health of workers on such uninspected vessels in the manner that OSHA does, Section 4(b)(1) does not preempt OSHA’s jurisdiction.

**II. The Employees on the Drilling Barge Are Not “Seamen” Under Pre-1970 Maritime Law.**

The court below erred in characterizing the employees as “seamen” based on post-1970 decisions. Pre-1970 precedent demonstrates that workers on drilling barges are not seamen when the barge is not in navigation. In *Senko v. La Crosse Dredging Corp.,* 352 U.S. 370, 372 (1957), this Court held that seaman status depends on “the nature of the vessel and the employee’s relation to it.” A drilling barge moored to the bottom and engaged in oil and gas exploration is not a “vessel in navigation” for seaman-status purposes. See *The Steamer “H. M. Hayes,”* 1 Sawy. 170 (N.D. Cal. 1870) (holding that a vessel used as a stationary work platform is not a vessel in navigation). The employees here were not contributing to navigation or transportation; they performed oilfield work. Under pre-1970 law, such workers are not seamen and thus are not subject to the exclusive maritime jurisdiction that the lower court mistakenly invoked.

**III. The OSH Act Fills a Gap Not Covered by the Coast Guard’s Minimal Regulation.**

Congress enacted the Occupational Safety and Health Act to ensure safe and healthful working conditions for every working person. The Act’s broad coverage is limited only where another federal agency has “statutory authority” to prescribe or enforce occupational safety and health standards. The Coast Guard’s regulatory scheme for uninspected vessels is not such a comprehensive scheme. Pre-1970 cases emphasize that federal agencies do not occupy a field unless Congress has clearly manifested that intent. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.,* 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). The Coast Guard’s limited equipment regulations do not “occupy the field” of occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels. Thus, OSHA properly exercised its jurisdiction to cite Mallard for violations that led to the deaths and injuries of its employees.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the citation reinstated.
