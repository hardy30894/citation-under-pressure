The Florida Supreme Court correctly held that the state's death penalty sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. 

Hurst's claim that the Florida death penalty scheme violates the Sixth Amendment is foreclosed by this Court's decision in Hildwin v. Florida, 497 U.S. 638 (1990), and Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), which upheld the constitutionality of Florida's capital sentencing scheme. Although the Court later decided Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), which held that a jury must find aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty to death, the Court did not overrule Hildwin or Spaziano. In fact, the Ring Court explicitly distinguished the Arizona scheme from Florida's, noting that Florida's scheme "does not ... specify that the jury’s verdict is ... an advisory one." Ring, 536 U.S. at 609 n.7 (quoting Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2)-(3)). 

Moreover, the Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that Ring does not require a unanimous jury verdict or a jury determination of mental retardation. See, e.g., Coday v. State, 946 So.2d 988, 1003 (Fla. 2006); Blackwood v. State, 946 So.2d 960, 974 (Fla. 2006). This interpretation is consistent with the principle that "the Sixth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in state criminal trials." Apodaca v. Oregon, 406 U.S. 404, 406 (1972). 

Hurst's Eighth Amendment claim is also unavailing. While the Court has emphasized the importance of "heightened reliability" in capital sentencing, Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), it has not held that a unanimous jury verdict is required. The Florida Supreme Court has noted that Florida is an outlier among death penalty states in not requiring a unanimous jury finding of aggravating circumstances, but this alone does not render the scheme unconstitutional. See Hurst v. State, 147 So.3d 435, 445 (Fla. 2014) (Pariente, J., dissenting). 

The dissenting justices in the Florida Supreme Court cited Justice Sotomayor's dissent from the denial of certiorari in Woodward v. Alabama, 134 S.Ct. 405 (2013), which expressed concerns about Alabama's death penalty statute. However, that case is distinguishable, as Alabama allows judges to override a jury's recommendation of life imprisonment, whereas Florida does not. See Ala. Code § 13A-5-47(e); Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3). 

In conclusion, the Florida Supreme Court correctly upheld Hurst's death sentence, and its decision should be affirmed. The state's death penalty sentencing scheme, while perhaps an outlier among death penalty states, does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments.
