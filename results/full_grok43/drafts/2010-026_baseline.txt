**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the ESQD documents are exempt under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). That exemption covers only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The court’s adoption of a “predominantly internal” test detached from the statutory text and from this Court’s precedent cannot be reconciled with the command that FOIA exemptions “are to be interpreted narrowly.” Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592, 48 L.Ed.2d 11 (1976).

In Rose, this Court explained that Exemption 2 was intended to relieve agencies of the burden of assembling and maintaining “vast bodies of material relating to the ‘internal personnel rules and practices’ of an agency—such as employee parking facilities, lunchrooms, and sick leave.” Id. at 363, 96 S.Ct. 1592 (quoting S.Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965)). The Court rejected any reading that would permit withholding of materials merely because they concerned “internal” agency operations. Id. at 369-70, 96 S.Ct. 1592. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard, drawn from Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051, 1074 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), and applied in Maricopa Audubon Soc’y v. U.S. Forest Serv., 108 F.3d 1082, 1085 (9th Cir. 1997), substitutes a judicially created balancing test for the statutory language and thereby expands the exemption beyond its text.

The ESQD arc maps and related calculations do not qualify even under the broader formulation the court adopted. They are technical planning documents used to site ammunition magazines and to determine maximum explosive loads. As the Navy’s own declarations establish, the arcs “aid[] the Navy in storing ordnance” and serve as “a long term planning tool.” They contain no instructions governing employee conduct, no personnel policies, and no enforcement procedures. They therefore bear no relation—let alone a “sole” relation—to “internal personnel rules and practices.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2).

The additional requirement that disclosure “risk circumvention of agency regulation,” Rose, 425 U.S. at 369, 96 S.Ct. 1592, cannot supply the missing statutory element. Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980), permitted withholding of law-enforcement manuals only because those documents directly concerned internal practices for executing warrants. The ESQD materials were not compiled for any adjudicative or enforcement purpose. They therefore fall outside both the narrow category recognized in Hardy and the still narrower category recognized in Rose.

Because the documents do not satisfy the statutory predicate of Exemption 2, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
