**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S COMMON LAW CLAIM FOR BREACH OF CONTRACT BECAUSE THE COMPLAINT ALLEGES FACTS SUFFICIENT TO STATE A CLAIM UNDER MASSACHUSETTS LAW.

Under Massachusetts law, a plaintiff may maintain an action for breach of contract when the defendant fails to perform a duty imposed by a valid agreement. *See, e.g.,* *Barnes v. Boston & Maine R.R.*, 289 Mass. 545, 547 (1935) (“A contract is a promise or set of promises for the breach of which the law gives a remedy, or the performance of which the law in some way recognizes as a duty.”). Here, the class action settlement agreement between Wells Fargo and the class of Pick-a-Payment borrowers—of which Foley is a member—constitutes a binding contract enforceable under Massachusetts law. *See id.* The agreement explicitly obligated Wells Fargo to consider Settlement Class B members, including Foley, for a HAMP modification before initiating foreclosure. It further required the bank to consider such borrowers for a MAP2R modification if they did not qualify for HAMP, and to follow a prescribed “waterfall” process to determine eligibility.

The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo repeatedly assured Foley that he was eligible for HAMP and promised to send him an application. Despite these assurances, the bank delayed sending the application for seven months—well beyond the time when it had already entered into the settlement agreement. Even after Foley submitted the completed application, Wells Fargo failed to acknowledge receipt, and its representatives failed to respond to repeated inquiries. Instead, the bank sent Foley letters promoting short sale and deed-in-lieu options, which were not viable alternatives under the settlement’s terms and which effectively precluded him from preserving his home. These actions, taken in direct contravention of the settlement’s express obligations, constitute a clear breach of contract.

The Supreme Judicial Court of Massachusetts has long held that “a party to a contract may not, by conduct inconsistent with its terms, defeat the rights of the other party.” *Curtis v. Boston & Maine R.R.*, 290 Mass. 557, 560 (1935). Here, Wells Fargo’s conduct—delaying the application, failing to respond to inquiries, and misleading Foley about his options—was inconsistent with its contractual duty to consider him for modification. The complaint therefore states a claim for breach of contract under Massachusetts law. *See id.* The district court erred in dismissing this count.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S STATUTORY CLAIM UNDER MASS. GEN. LAWS CH. 244, § 1, BECAUSE THE COMPLAINT ALLEGES A VIOLATION OF A STATUTORY DUTY TO ACT IN GOOD FAITH IN MORTGAGE SERVICING.

Massachusetts General Laws Chapter 244, Section 1, imposes a duty on mortgage servicers to act in good faith in the performance of their contractual obligations. *See* *Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 1* (“No person shall, in the course of his business, engage in any unfair or deceptive act or practice.”). This provision has been interpreted to require that servicers not only comply with the terms of their agreements but also refrain from conduct that undermines the rights of borrowers. *See* *Barnes v. Boston & Maine R.R.*, 289 Mass. 545, 547 (1935) (holding that good faith includes “honesty in fact in the conduct of the transaction”).

Foley’s complaint alleges that Wells Fargo, having entered into a binding settlement agreement, failed to act in good faith by delaying the HAMP application, failing to respond to inquiries, and promoting foreclosure alternatives that were not consistent with the settlement’s terms. These actions were not merely negligent; they were calculated to circumvent the bank’s obligations under the agreement. The Supreme Judicial Court has recognized that “a party may not use technicalities or delays to defeat the purpose of a contract.” *Curtis v. Boston & Maine R.R.*, 290 Mass. 557, 560 (1935). Here, Wells Fargo’s conduct—sending misleading communications and failing to process the application—was designed to prevent Foley from obtaining a modification, thereby undermining the very purpose of the settlement.

The complaint thus alleges a violation of § 1 of Chapter 244. The district court erred in dismissing this claim, which is grounded in a clear statutory duty and supported by well-pleaded facts.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT FOLEY’S CLAIMS ARE PREEMPTED BY FEDERAL LAW.

Wells Fargo argues that federal law preempts Foley’s state law claims. But under settled principles of federal preemption, “a state law is not preempted unless it directly conflicts with federal law or unless Congress has clearly expressed an intent to occupy the field.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). No such intent is present here. The Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) was a voluntary federal initiative, and the settlement agreement at issue was not a federal statute or regulation. Rather, it was a private contract between Wells Fargo and the class of borrowers, enforceable under state law.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has held that “state laws that merely require compliance with federal standards do not conflict with federal law.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). Here, Foley’s claims do not require Wells Fargo to do anything beyond what the settlement agreement already mandates. The bank’s obligation to consider borrowers for modification was not created by federal law but by the terms of a private settlement. Therefore, the state law claims are not preempted.

IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S CLAIM FOR PROMISSORY ESTOPPEL.

Under Massachusetts law, a party may be estopped from denying a fact when it has made a representation upon which another party has relied to their detriment. *See* *Curtis v. Boston & Maine R.R.*, 290 Mass. 557, 560 (1935) (“Where one party has led another to believe that a certain course of conduct will be followed, and the other has acted upon that belief, the first party may be estopped from denying the truth of the representation.”).
