**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Confines Nondischargeability to the Amount Actually Obtained by Fraud.**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge a debt "to the extent obtained by" false pretenses, false representation, or actual fraud. The phrase "to the extent obtained" is a measure of value, not a trigger for open-ended liability. It asks how much the debtor actually acquired through the fraudulent act, not what a State chooses to award as a penalty for that act. This Court has long insisted that the words Congress used in defining exceptions to discharge be given their natural, limiting effect. In *Forsyth v. Vehmeyer*, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), the Court examined a judgment debt and confined the nondischargeable portion to the sum that represented the fraud itself, rather than treating the whole judgment, including elements unrelated to the fraudulent taking, as automatically excepted. The same textual discipline governs here: only the $31,382.50 in rent that Cohen actually obtained through the alleged misrepresentation about lawful rent levels falls within the "extent obtained by" fraud. The treble-damages multiplier and the fee award are creatures of the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act's remedial scheme, not sums "obtained by" Cohen from his tenants at all — they are amounts a state legislature has chosen to impose on top of the debt, and they cannot be squeezed into a statutory phrase that speaks only to what the debtor obtained.

**II. This Court's Precedents Require That Exceptions to Discharge Be Construed Narrowly, in Favor of the Debtor's Fresh Start.**

The bankruptcy discharge exists to give "the honest but unfortunate debtor . . . a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Because exceptions to discharge cut against that central purpose, this Court has repeatedly held that they must be read narrowly and resolved in the debtor's favor. See *Gleason v. Thaw*, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915) (construing the scope of debts "for obtaining property by false pretenses" narrowly, so as not to sweep in obligations Congress did not plainly except); *Williams v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 236 U.S. 549 (1915) (declining to extend the fraud exception beyond debts arising from the debtor's own actual wrongdoing). The same principle animates *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68 (1904), where the Court cautioned against reading bankruptcy provisions so broadly as to defeat the statute's basic design of relieving debtors of the burden of past obligations except where Congress plainly said otherwise. And in *Perry v. Commerce Loan Co.*, 383 U.S. 392 (1966), the Court reaffirmed that the "central purpose" of the discharge provisions is to secure the debtor's fresh start, a purpose that counsels against expanding nondischargeability beyond what the statutory text requires.

That interpretive discipline is especially important where, as here, the "fraud" exception is at issue. In *Neal v. Clark*, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877), this Court construed the predecessor fraud exception to reach only "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong," and not every obligation that might loosely be called fraudulent under state law. That same care must attend the measure of the excepted debt: just as the *kind* of fraud that triggers the exception is narrowly limited to actual, intentional wrongdoing, so too the *amount* excepted from discharge must be limited to what that wrongdoing actually yielded the debtor. To hold otherwise — to let a state statute's independent choice to treble damages or award fees dictate the scope of a federal bankruptcy exception — would let the exception swallow the rule and would make the reach of federal discharge law turn on the happenstance of varying state remedial statutes, a result inconsistent with the uniform administration of bankruptcy law that this Court protected in *Stellwagen v. Clum*, 245 U.S. 605 (1918).

**III. Treating the Full New Jersey Judgment as Nondischargeable Would Convert a Compensatory Exception into a Punitive One, Contrary to Congress's Design.**

The Third Circuit reasoned that because New Jersey's treble-damages remedy is mandatory rather than discretionary, it was "entirely foreseeable" to Cohen and therefore part of the debt he "incurred through his conduct." But foreseeability of a penalty is not the same as obtaining money through fraud. Nothing in the text of § 523(a)(2)(A) makes foreseeability the touchstone; it makes the extent of what was "obtained by" the fraud the touchstone. Just as this Court in *Crawford v. Burke*, 195 U.S. 176 (1904), was careful to root the fraud exception in the debtor's own moral delinquency and the property or value he wrongfully acquired, so too should the *amount* excepted from discharge be tethered to that same wrongful acquisition — not to a multiplier a state legislature has attached for punitive or deterrent purposes extrinsic to the fraud itself.

For these reasons, this Court should hold that § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only the $31,382.50 that Cohen actually obtained from his tenants through the challenged conduct, and that the balance of the state-law judgment — the treble-damages multiplier, attorney's fees, and costs — remains dischargeable.
