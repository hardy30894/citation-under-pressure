**ARGUMENT**

**I. A State May Constitutionally Restrict the Use of Mental-Disease Evidence to the Insanity Defense, Where the Defendant Bears the Burden of Proof, Without Violating Due Process.**

Petitioner contends that the Fourteenth Amendment compels Arizona to admit expert evidence of his mental condition to negate *mens rea*, even though such evidence is already admissible on his insanity plea. This argument conflates two distinct concepts: capacity *vel non* to form criminal intent, and the defense of legal insanity. This Court has long recognized that States possess wide latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses available to criminal defendants. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-799 (1952) (upholding Oregon’s requirement that defendants prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, and noting that the State has broad power to define the crimes within its borders).

In *Leland*, the defendant argued, as petitioner does here, that the jury might find him mentally incapable of the premeditation and deliberation required to support a first degree murder verdict yet not find him legally insane. *Id.* at 793. This Court rejected that argument, holding that the State may require mental incapacity to be proved as part of an affirmative insanity defense rather than as a negation of *mens rea*. *Id.* at 798-799. Arizona’s rule is substantially similar: it channels evidence of mental disease into the insanity determination, where the defendant bears the burden of proof, rather than permitting that evidence to fragment the prosecution’s burden on intent.

**II. Longstanding Precedent Confirms that States May Allocate the Burden of Proof on Mental State Without Offending Due Process.**

Importantly, this Court has never held that due process requires a State to admit evidence of mental disease to negate a specific intent element once the State provides a sanity determination. To the contrary, this Court has consistently permitted States to define the relationship between mental condition and criminal responsibility. *Davis v. United States*, 165 U.S. 373, 378 (1897), upon which petitioner relies, addressed a federal prosecution and held that once the defense raises a reasonable doubt as to sanity, the prosecution must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt. But *Davis* did not purport to establish a constitutional rule binding on the States. To the extent *Davis* reflects a common-law preference, it is superseded by the States’ sovereign authority under the Fourteenth Amendment. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798.

Indeed, *Leland* explicitly distinguished *Davis* as a case involving the rule in the federal courts and declined to impose that rule on Oregon. *Id.* at 794. Arizona has similarly chosen a different rule—one that respects the jury’s ability to consider mental condition on the insanity defense while avoiding the confusion that would arise from having the same evidence serve contradictory purposes.

**III. Petitioner’s Evidence Was Fully Considered Under the Insanity Standard, and Arizona Was Not Required to Allow Its Fragmented Use.**

Arizona’s approach does not deprive the defendant of a fair opportunity to present a defense. Here, the trial court permitted Clark’s expert psychiatric testimony—it simply confined its relevance to the insanity plea. This is constitutionally permissible. *Blackburn v. Alabama*, 361 U.S. 199, 207 (1960), acknowledged that a defendant may introduce evidence of his mental condition but did not hold that a State must admit such evidence for all purposes. Rather, due process is satisfied so long as the defendant has a meaningful opportunity to present his claim of non-responsibility. *See Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 377 (1966) (focusing on the competency to stand trial, not on the admissibility of mental-state evidence to negate intent).

Moreover, the Confrontation Clause concerns that animate some of this Court’s evidentiary decisions are absent here. Clark’s evidence was admitted; the only question is whether Arizona violated due process by limiting its use. Nothing in the Fourteenth Amendment prevents a State from channeling mental-disease evidence through a single, coherent defense. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 799; *Hotema v. United States*, 186 U.S. 413, 421 (1902) (approving a jury instruction that required proof of insanity beyond a reasonable doubt and did not separately instruct on *mens rea*).

**IV. Permitting the Fragmentation Petitioner Seeks Would Undermine State Sovereignty and the Integrity of Criminal Proceedings.**

If this Court were to hold that due process requires every State to admit mental-disease evidence to negate *mens rea*—separate and apart from an insanity defense—it would unsettle settled state criminal procedure. *Leland* rejected precisely this argument. *See also Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108-109 (1954) (recognizing state discretion in defining insanity standards); *United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 568 (1953) (noting that the State’s determination of the procedure for trial of criminal cases is entitled to great weight).

Further, permitting a defendant simultaneously to argue (1) that he was so mentally ill he could not form intent, and (2) that he was nonetheless not insane, would invite jury confusion. The State has a compelling interest in clarity and consistency in the administration of its criminal laws. *Lynch v. Overholser*, 369 U.S. 705, 712 (1962) (discussing Congress’ intent in the context of the insanity defense). Arizona’s rule achieves that clarity without offending fundamental fairness.

**V. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
