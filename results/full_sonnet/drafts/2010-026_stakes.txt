# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2's Text Limits Its Scope to "Internal Personnel Rules and Practices," Not to Any Agency Record That Might, If Disclosed, Complicate Government Operations

Exemption 2 permits withholding only of matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This Court has repeatedly instructed that FOIA exemptions "must be narrowly construed," *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 361 (1976), because the statute embodies "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure." *Id.* at 360-61. The nine exemptions are "explicitly exclusive." *United States Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Administrator, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

The Ninth Circuit's "High 2" doctrine abandons this textual discipline. By asking only whether disclosure "may risk circumvention of agency regulation," the court below effectively wrote the phrase "internal personnel rules and practices" out of the statute. The ESQD arc maps at issue here are technical and scientific data about the physical effects of explosions—engineering calculations, not "personnel" rules of any kind. They do not govern who may work at Indian Island, how they are trained, disciplined, or promoted. They are, instead, physical facts about the world: how far a given quantity of explosive will propel debris or overpressure. Congress used the word "personnel" deliberately, and this Court should give it effect. *See Rose*, 425 U.S. at 362-63 (Exemption 2 was intended to cover matters of "predominantly internal significance" to agency employees, such as working conditions).

The Ninth Circuit relied on the D.C. Circuit's decision in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), which stretched "personnel rules and practices" to include "significant matters like job training for law enforcement personnel." *Id.* at 1056. Even accepting that expansion for training manuals directed at how employees should act, it cannot rationally extend to raw data about the physics of an explosion. The Navy did not compile ESQD information to instruct personnel on a rule of internal conduct; it compiled the information to describe an external, physical phenomenon. Extending Exemption 2 to cover such material eliminates any limiting principle: virtually any technical data an agency uses for internal planning purposes could be recharacterized as an "internal practice."

## II. The "Risk of Circumvention" Test Was Developed for Law-Enforcement Manuals and Cannot Be Untethered From That Context to Swallow Ordinary Agency Records

This Court in *Rose* addressed circumvention only in dicta, reserving the question whether Exemption 2 might cover materials whose disclosure would help someone evade a legal requirement. 425 U.S. at 369-70. Circuits applying that dicta—including the Ninth Circuit's own prior precedent in *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653, 656 (9th Cir. 1980)—confined the doctrine to "law enforcement materials," such as manuals for executing search warrants, where disclosure would show criminals how to evade detection. *Id.*

The decision below transforms this narrow safety-valve into a freestanding balancing test that has nothing to do with law enforcement or even with personnel matters. The Ninth Circuit reasoned that because the arc maps could theoretically assist a terrorist in planning an attack, they are "predominantly" for internal use and therefore exempt. But nothing in the ESQD data concerns enforcement of any law, rule, or regulation that individuals might "circumvent" through ordinary noncompliance. The information is exactly what Milner sought it for: to understand the danger that explosive storage poses to the surrounding civilian population. That is a quintessential public interest in disclosure, not a private interest in evading regulation.

Moreover, this Court has cautioned that a statutory term cannot be stretched to serve purposes better addressed by other, more specifically tailored exemptions. *See Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151. Here, Congress specifically provided Exemption 1 for information properly classified in the interest of national defense, 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1), and Exemption 7 for information compiled for law-enforcement purposes whose release could endanger life or safety, *id.* § 552(b)(7)(F). The Navy never classified the ESQD arc maps under Exemption 1, and the Ninth Circuit majority itself did not rely on Exemption 7(F). Allowing Exemption 2's "personnel" language to fill this gap—precisely the outcome the dissenting judge below warned against—"distorts Congress's careful balance" between disclosure and secrecy. *See John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989) (FOIA reflects "a balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure").

## III. The Practical Consequences of the Ninth Circuit's Rule Confirm That It Cannot Be the Law

If technical safety data can be withheld merely because an agency asserts it is "predominantly internal" and that release could complicate agency operations, then Exemption 2 becomes a virtually limitless catch-all, contrary to this Court's repeated admonition that FOIA's exemptions are to be read narrowly and that disclosure is the rule, not the exception. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361. Communities living near hazardous government installations—as Milner and his neighbors do near Indian Island—would be left without any meaningful way to learn the risks the government has calculated for their own backyards, even though such risk data lies at the heart of what FOIA exists to expose: government action affecting public safety, hidden from public view.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
