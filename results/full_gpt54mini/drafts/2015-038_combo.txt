Respondent should prevail because ANILCA does not strip the National Park Service of authority to regulate conduct on non-federal lands and waters merely because those lands and waters lie within the exterior boundaries of a park unit. The statutory text, structure, and historical background all point the same way: Congress preserved the special federal jurisdiction that attaches to National Park System units, while carving out only a narrow exception for state, Native, corporate, and private lands as property interests—not as jurisdictional safe harbors from otherwise applicable federal regulation.

The starting point is the statute itself. In construing congressional enactments, the Court gives effect to the whole text and avoids readings that render language surplusage or produce absurd results. United States v. Katz, 271 U.S. 354, 362 (1926); Montclair v. Ramsdell, 107 U.S. 147, 152-53 (1883). ANILCA’s § 103(c) provides that no “public lands” within a conservation system unit “shall be deemed to be included as a part of the unit” and that lands “which are validly selected by, but not yet conveyed to” the State or a Native corporation “shall be considered to be within the unit.” 16 U.S.C. § 3102(c). Congress thus addressed ownership status, not a wholesale withdrawal of federal regulatory power. Read as a whole, the provision preserves federal administration of park units while recognizing that nonfederal lands remain nonfederal. See United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805) (a statute should not be construed to defeat its evident purpose).

That reading fits ANILCA’s structure. Courts must harmonize related provisions rather than isolate a single clause. Sutherland v. Mayer, 271 U.S. 272, 278 (1926); FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc., 312 U.S. 349, 350-51 (1941). Elsewhere, Congress expressly preserved existing federal authority over Alaska lands and waters. See, e.g., ANILCA § 1319, 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Where Congress wanted to limit federal action, it spoke expressly. The absence of a comparable express prohibition against NPS regulations on nonfederal lands within park boundaries is decisive. See Schwegmann Bros. v. Calvert Distillers Corp., 341 U.S. 384, 389 (1951).

Pre-1970 public lands cases likewise show that federal power over land does not disappear simply because title is in private hands or state ownership lies beneath the surface. The United States may regulate conduct affecting federal purposes even where private interests are present. Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 524-25 (1897), upheld federal action necessary to prevent evasion of federal land policy by adjoining private owners. United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264, 267 (1927), likewise recognized that Congress may protect federal lands through regulation of adjacent or connected private lands when needed to make the federal regime effective. And United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-18 (1911), sustained federal regulations governing conduct on forest reservations as a valid incident of federal land management. Those decisions defeat petitioner’s premise that mere nonfederal ownership within exterior boundaries automatically disables federal regulation.

Petitioner’s contrary theory would also upset longstanding doctrine respecting navigable waters and federal supremacy. The federal government has power to regulate commerce and navigation even where states hold submerged title. Gibson v. United States, 166 U.S. 269, 271-72 (1897), recognized the dominant federal navigational power over waters within a state. In Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 122-23 (1921), the Court confirmed that state ownership of submerged lands does not prevent federal regulation necessary to protect navigation and other federal interests. These cases show why ANILCA should not be read to create an implied exemption for state-owned riverbeds and waters inside a park.

Nor does petitioner’s reliance on ownership-based jurisdiction fare better under the Court’s settled treatment of territorial statutes. Congress may reserve federal authority over lands and waters notwithstanding statehood, title, or local interests when it acts pursuant to an enumerated power. Coyle v. Smith, 221 U.S. 559, 567-68 (1911); United States v. Winans, 198 U.S. 371, 381-82 (1905). ANILCA was enacted against that backdrop and should be understood as preserving, not diminishing, federal management of park units.

Finally, petitioner’s interpretation would create an unadministrable patchwork in which the scope of federal regulation turns not on park boundaries, but on micro-parcels of underlying title and submerged ownership. The Court has repeatedly rejected constructions that defeat a statute’s practical operation. See United States v. Oregon, 366 U.S. 643, 648-49 (1961); United States v. Midwest Oil Co., 236 U.S. 459, 474-75 (1915). Congress did not write ANILCA to hamstring the Park Service in precisely the places where park resources are most vulnerable.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
