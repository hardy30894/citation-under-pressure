**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text of OCSLA Requires a Gap/Necessity Test**

The Ninth Circuit erred in interpreting 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) to allow state law to apply concurrently with federal law whenever it is "applicable." The statutory language, "To the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent," imposes a limitation, not a blanket adoption. The Fifth Circuit articulated the correct standard in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners' Mut. Ins. Ass'n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), holding that state law applies only to fill a "significant void or gap" in federal law. The Supreme Court in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), confirmed that the statute adopts state law "to the extent that they are applicable." If state law were to apply concurrently with federal law regardless of a gap, the "to the extent" qualifier would be superfluous.

**II. The Purpose and History of OCSLA Support the Gap Test**

Congress enacted OCSLA to supplement, not replace, federal law. *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 26 (1988). The purpose was to define a body of law for the seabed, recognizing that federal law was "never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself." *Id.* at 27. *Rodrigue* further explained that Congress feared federal law would be inadequate and thus borrowed state law to fill the void. This legislative history supports the "gap" test, as state law is intended to be a surrogate for federal law only where federal law is silent or incomplete.

**III. The "Gap" Test is the Precedent in the Circuits**

The Fifth Circuit, the primary interpreter of OCSLA, consistently applies the "gap" test. *Continental Oil* remains the controlling precedent. The Fifth Circuit has reiterated that maritime law applies "of its own force" only if it fills a gap in federal law. *Barker v. Hercules Offshore, Inc.*, 713 F.3d 208, 214 (5th Cir. 2013); *Grand Isle Shipyard, Inc. v. M/V M/V M/V M/V*, 589 F.3d 780, 788 (5th Cir. 2009). *Demette v. Falcon Drilling Co.*, 280 F.3d 492, 505 n.2 (5th Cir. 2002), and *Oceanic Butler, Inc. v. Nordahl*, 842 F.2d 773, 777 n.4 (5th Cir. 1988), similarly rely on the necessity of a gap to apply state law. The Ninth Circuit’s departure from this well-established precedent is error.

**IV. The FLSA is a Comprehensive Scheme, Leaving No Gap**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive statutory scheme that leaves no room for state law to address wage and hour grievances. *Continental Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036. The Ninth Circuit’s own precedent, *Ecological Rights Found. v. U.S. Dep’t of Interior*, 874 F.3d 1088, 1095 (9th Cir. 2017), holds that state law is inconsistent with federal law when it conflicts with federal statutes. Because the FLSA is comprehensive, there is no "significant void or gap" for California’s wage and hour laws to fill. Therefore, the district court correctly granted judgment on the pleadings.
