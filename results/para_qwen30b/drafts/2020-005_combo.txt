**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privileges of conducting activities within the forum state, such that the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). The Montana Supreme Court’s decision in this case—holding that Ford Motor Company is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana based on the mere sale of vehicle parts within the state, despite the fact that Ford did not design, manufacture, or sell the vehicle in Montana and the accident occurred there—violates this foundational principle. The Court’s reasoning rests on a fundamental misreading of *International Shoe* and ignores the clear line of precedent establishing that jurisdiction must be grounded in a causal connection between the defendant’s forum-related conduct and the plaintiff’s claims. The judgment below must be reversed.

The Due Process Clause does not permit jurisdiction based on “random, fortuitous, or attenuated contacts.” *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 292 (1980) (citing *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985)). In *International Shoe*, the Court held that “the mere fact that a defendant has sold goods into a state does not, without more, subject him to suit there.” 326 U.S. at 319. The Court emphasized that “the defendant’s conduct must be such that he should reasonably anticipate being haled into court in the forum state.” Id. at 317. Here, Ford did not sell the vehicle in Montana, did not advertise in Montana, and did not engage in any activity in Montana that could reasonably be expected to lead to litigation arising from a tire failure on a Montana highway. The sale of parts to a dealership in Washington State, followed by a chain of resales culminating in the vehicle’s presence in Montana, is precisely the kind of “attenuated contact” that *International Shoe* forbids.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on Ford’s sale of parts within the state is legally unsound. The sale of parts to a third party in another state, without any connection to the product’s use or the injury, cannot form the basis for specific jurisdiction. *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 358 (1927), held that a defendant cannot be subjected to jurisdiction in a state where it has no “purposeful availment” of the forum’s benefits. The Court rejected jurisdiction over a New Hampshire driver who had merely passed through Massachusetts, noting that “the mere fact that a person drives through a state does not subject him to the jurisdiction of that state.” Id. Similarly, Ford’s mere sale of parts to a Washington dealer does not constitute a purposeful act within Montana. The sale occurred in Washington, not Montana, and the parts were not used in Montana until after the vehicle was resold and driven there.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana is unsupported by precedent. *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957), established that a corporation may be subject to jurisdiction in a state where it has “continuous and systematic” contacts. But the Court in *McGee* emphasized that the insurance policy at issue was “delivered and paid for in California,” and the insured’s injury occurred there. Id. at 221. The Court held that the defendant’s “continuous and systematic” business in the state, including the delivery and payment of the policy, created a sufficient connection. Here, Ford has no such continuous or systematic contacts with Montana. It is not incorporated in Montana, does not maintain offices there, and has not sold vehicles or parts to Montana residents. The mere fact that parts may have been sold to a dealer in a neighboring state does not equate to “systematic” activity in Montana.

Moreover, the Montana Supreme Court’s decision ignores the principle that jurisdiction must be based on a “substantial connection” between the defendant’s forum-related conduct and the plaintiff’s claim. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (citing *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 292). In *World-Wide Volkswagen*, the Court held that a New York dealer could not be sued in Oklahoma for a car accident that occurred there, even though the car had been sold in New York and driven into Oklahoma. 444 U.S. at 291. The Court emphasized that “the foreseeability that is critical to due process analysis is not the mere likelihood that a product will find its way into the forum state, but whether the defendant’s conduct may be fairly regarded as having created a substantial connection with the forum.” Id. at 294. Ford’s conduct—selling a vehicle in Kentucky and parts in Washington—does not create a substantial connection with Montana. The tire failure occurred in Montana, but Ford had no role in the vehicle’s presence or operation there.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Conway v. Benefis Health System*, 2013 MT 73, 297 P.3d 1200, and *Newbury v. State Farm*, 2008 MT 156, 184 P.3d 1021, is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance coverage and the collateral source rule, not personal jurisdiction. They do not address the constitutional limits on jurisdiction. *Harris v. Billings Clinic*, 2013 MT 207, 305 P.3d 852, similarly dealt with the admissibility of medical bills, not jurisdiction. These cases are irrelevant to the question of whether Ford may be haled into court in Montana.
