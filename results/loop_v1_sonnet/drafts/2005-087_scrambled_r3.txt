**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. The insanity defense as historically understood, from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent issue by issue. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation between the insanity inquiry and the State's mens rea burden.

This Court's decision in *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), confirms that the Constitution leaves the States broad room to define the boundaries of criminal responsibility, so long as the resulting scheme still requires proof of a criminal act rather than mere status. Arizona's rule does exactly that: it requires the State to prove the mental element of the offense while confining specialized psychiatric evidence concerning mental disease to the defense specifically designed to address it. Nothing in this Court's due process jurisprudence transforms that traditional channeling rule into a constitutional violation.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules. In *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the Court held that the States retain broad authority to shape their own modes of procedure and are not bound to follow any single model, so long as the resulting process does not offend basic fairness. *Id.* at 106–11.

*Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), applied these principles to a defendant's claim that exclusion from a portion of the proceedings violated due process, and rejected the claim because the exclusion did not undermine the basic fairness of the trial. *Id.* at 105–08. And in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the Court explained that only those rights that are "so rooted in the tradition and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," *id.* at 325, are protected against state abridgment. The Court there declined to treat every procedural variation among the States as a due process violation, holding instead that only hardships that are acute and shocking to the conscience justify constitutionalizing a particular procedural rule. *Id.* at 328. Arizona's channeling rule does not approach that threshold; it merely directs psychiatric evidence to the historically established vehicle—the insanity defense—for its consideration, while still requiring the State to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

*Adamson v. California*, 332 U.S. 46 (1947), reaffirmed that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate every specific procedural guarantee found elsewhere in the Constitution, but instead asks whether the challenged state practice is consistent with a fair trial considered as a whole. *Id.* at 53–54. Likewise, *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), recognized that due process is "not a fixed body of rules," but a flexible concept that accommodates variation among the States in matters of criminal procedure. *Id.* at 461–62. Arizona's rule—which permits full consideration of psychiatric evidence within the insanity framework while reserving the State's mens rea proof for ordinary adversarial testing—falls well within the flexibility these decisions contemplate.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona has not denied Clark any opportunity to present his psychiatric evidence—it has directed that evidence to the channel in which American law has traditionally placed it, consistent with the State's broad authority under *Twining* to structure its own procedures, and consistent with the fundamental-fairness limitation recognized in *Snyder*, *Palko*, *Adamson*, and *Betts*. Because Arizona's rule offends no principle "so rooted in the tradition and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," *Palko*, 302 U.S. at 325, and because the States retain wide latitude to define criminal responsibility consistent with *Robinson* and the historical insanity framework reflected in *M'Naghten's Case*, this Court should affirm the judgment below.
