# ARGUMENT

## THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE BARS RETRIAL OF THE PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION

The Double Jeopardy Clause guarantees that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that the Clause "protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal." *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Through the Fourteenth Amendment, this protection binds the States because it is "fundamental to the American scheme of justice." *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The California Supreme Court's holding—that this bedrock protection evaporates whenever a State labels a proceeding "sentencing" rather than "trial"—cannot be reconciled with this Court's precedent in *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and its progeny.

### A. *Bullington* Establishes That Double Jeopardy Protection Attaches Where Sentencing Proceedings Bear the "Hallmarks of the Trial on Guilt or Innocence"

In *Bullington*, this Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred Missouri from seeking the death penalty on retrial after a jury had first sentenced the defendant to life imprisonment. 451 U.S. at 445-46. The Court reasoned that Missouri's capital sentencing proceeding was not a traditional exercise of sentencing discretion but instead resembled "the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence." *Id.* at 439. Specifically, the State was required to prove aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, the defendant enjoyed the right to present evidence and cross-examine witnesses, and the jury's role was to make an "affirmative decision" analogous to acquittal or conviction. *Id.* at 438.

Three years later, in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), this Court reaffirmed and applied *Bullington*'s functional test, asking whether the proceeding possessed "characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." *Id.* at 209-10. Nothing in either decision confined this analysis to capital cases as a matter of constitutional principle. Rather, the Court's inquiry turned on the *procedural characteristics* of the proceeding, not the ultimate severity of the punishment at stake.

### B. California's Trial of the Prior Conviction Allegation Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial on Guilt or Innocence

California's own law confirms that a proceeding to determine a prior conviction allegation is functionally indistinguishable from a trial. The prosecution must prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993); *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 862 (1974). The defendant enjoys the privilege against self-incrimination, *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d at 863 n.5, the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996) (quoting *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967)), and the protection of the ordinary rules of evidence, *People v. Myers*, 5 Cal. 4th 1193, 1201 (1993). Indeed, the California Supreme Court itself acknowledged that "the next step in the logical progression of these decisions" would be to extend double jeopardy protection as well. Pet. App. at [cite].

These are precisely the "hallmarks" that triggered double jeopardy protection in *Bullington*. A proceeding requiring proof beyond a reasonable doubt, adversarial testing through cross-examination, and adherence to evidentiary rules is a trial in every meaningful sense—regardless of the label California attaches to it.

### C. The Distinction the California Supreme Court Draws Between Capital and Noncapital Proceedings Finds No Support in This Court's Precedent

The state court relied on dicta in *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), suggesting that "*Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases" whose reasoning was "based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding." *Id.* at 392. But *Caspari* did not hold that *Bullington* is limited to capital cases; it held only that, for purposes of *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), retroactivity analysis, extending *Bullington* to noncapital proceedings was not "dictated by precedent" at the relevant time. *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 393. That is a statement about the *novelty* of a legal rule for habeas purposes, not a substantive limitation on the Double Jeopardy Clause's reach.

Nor does *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), support the distinction. That summary disposition did not purport to resolve whether *Bullington*'s reasoning extends to noncapital sentencing; it addressed a different procedural posture entirely.

Petitioner recognizes that this Court's decision in *Monge* itself may ultimately resolve this question by declining to extend *Bullington* beyond the capital context. But nothing in the text of the Double Jeopardy Clause, nor in the functional analysis this Court employed in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*, supports confining double jeopardy protection to cases where death is a possible punishment. The Clause protects against being "twice put in jeopardy," full stop—it does not by its terms distinguish between trials that may result in death and trials that may result in a doubled prison term under a recidivist statute. Where, as here, a State has chosen to erect a proceeding with all the procedural trappings of a criminal trial—proof beyond a reasonable doubt, confrontation, cross-examination, and evidentiary rules—the Double Jeopardy Clause's core purpose, protecting against the governmental oppression inherent in repeated attempts to convict, applies with full force. *See Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717.

### CONCLUSION

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed, and the Double Jeopardy Clause should bar retrial of the prior conviction allegation against petitioner.
