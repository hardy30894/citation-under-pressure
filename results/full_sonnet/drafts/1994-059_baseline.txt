# ARGUMENT

## I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF ARTICLE I ESTABLISH THE EXCLUSIVE AND EXHAUSTIVE CRITERIA FOR SERVICE IN CONGRESS

Article I, Section 2 provides that a Representative must be twenty-five years of age, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State from which elected. Article I, Section 3 sets parallel qualifications for Senators. These provisions were not drafted as a floor beneath which the States might add further conditions; they were drafted, after exhaustive debate at the Constitutional Convention, as an answer to the question of who may serve. This Court's decision in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), confirms that the Qualifications Clauses are fixed and exclusive, holding that Congress itself lacks the power to add qualifications beyond those the Constitution specifies. The Court there reasoned that "the Convention's decision to require a specified list of qualifications" reflects a considered rejection of open-ended qualification-setting authority. *Id.* at 539. If Congress—the body most directly interested in the makeup of its own membership and vested with an express constitutional role in judging the "Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members," Art. I, § 5—cannot supplement the constitutional list, it defies reason that individual States, which possess no comparable textual warrant whatsoever, may do so.

The logic of *Powell* rests on the historical record now before this Court: the Framers considered and rejected proposals to allow property or wealth qualifications, and settled instead on a spare, uniform standard designed to prevent factional manipulation of eligibility for national office. As the Arkansas Supreme Court itself recognized below, "the action finally taken by the framers of the constitution, following exhaustive debates, is strong evidence that term limits for senators and representatives was rejected." That determination by the Framers is not a gap for the States to fill; it is a considered constitutional judgment that forecloses supplementation.

## II. MEMBERS OF CONGRESS HOLD OFFICES OF NATIONAL, NOT STATE, CREATION, AND THE STATES POSSESS NO RESERVED POWER TO REGULATE ELIGIBILITY FOR THOSE OFFICES

Petitioners' reliance on the Tenth Amendment inverts the structure of the Constitution. The reserved-powers doctrine, as this Court explained in *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), protects only that authority which existed in the States independent of the federal Constitution. But the offices of Senator and Representative did not exist before the Constitution created them; they are, in a sense that state offices are not, entirely creatures of the national charter. There was no antecedent state power to "regulate" membership in a legislature that did not yet exist. Amendment 73 does not exercise a reserved power—it purports to exercise a power that never belonged to Arkansas in the first instance.

This structural point is reinforced by the uniformity concern that pervades Article I. Congress is a national institution; its members, once seated, legislate for citizens of every State, not merely their own. Permitting each State to impose its own supplemental eligibility criteria would produce, in the circuit court's apt phrase, "a hodge-podge of qualifications" inconsistent with the national character of the federal legislature. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that "[c]ongressional officeholders partake of the same national character as the President of the United States," and that "[u]niformity of qualifications is paramount," concluding that "individual states are not free to engraft variations." That reasoning tracks this Court's own recognition in *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974), that the States' authority over the "Times, Places and Manner" of federal elections under Article I, Section 4 is a power to regulate procedure, not a license to add substantive barriers to candidacy that the Constitution itself does not authorize.

## III. A BALLOT-ACCESS BAR KEYED TO PRIOR SERVICE IS FUNCTIONALLY A QUALIFICATION AND CANNOT BE SUSTAINED BY RELABELING

Petitioners' attempt to characterize Section 3 as a mere ballot-access regulation rather than an additional qualification cannot survive scrutiny. This Court has long recognized that election regulations which operate to exclude candidates from the ballot must be evaluated according to their practical effect on candidacy and voter choice, not their formal label. *Anderson v. Celebrezze*, 460 U.S. 780, 786 (1983) (assessing "the character and magnitude of the asserted injury" imposed by ballot restrictions). A rule that renders an individual permanently ineligible to have his name appear on the ballot for a congressional office, solely because of the number of prior terms served, is indistinguishable in substance from a qualification barring that same individual from "holding" the office. The Arkansas Supreme Court recognized as much, observing that "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp," and that the "ballot-access terminology" was itself a device adopted because "the sponsors of Amendment 73 obviously knew that section 3 was of questionable constitutionality." Substance, not label, must govern; Section 3 imposes a qualification the Constitution forbids the State to add.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Section 3 of Amendment 73 violates the Qualifications Clauses of Article I and cannot stand.
