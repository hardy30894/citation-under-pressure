The petitioners challenge the constitutionality of the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics, arguing that it restricts their First Amendment rights. However, the First Circuit correctly upheld the law as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government has the power to regulate the use of public property, including sidewalks, to prevent interference with their intended use. See Amalgamated Food Employees Union Local 590 v. Logan Valley Plaza, Inc., 391 U.S. 308, 320 (1968) (citing Cameron v. Johnson, 390 U.S. 611, 616). In Bi-Metallic Investment Co. v. State Board of Equalization, 239 U.S. 441, 445 (1915), the Court held that the government may regulate the use of public property without providing individualized hearings, as long as the regulation is reasonable.

The Massachusetts law at issue here is a reasonable regulation aimed at ensuring the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities. See Patterson v. Colorado Ex Rel. Attorney General of Colo., 205 U.S. 454, 462 (1907) (recognizing the state's interest in maintaining public order). The law is content-neutral, as it applies equally to all individuals, regardless of their viewpoint or message. See Selover, Bates & Co. v. Walsh, 226 U.S. 112, 124 (1912) (upholding a state law that was "within the power and authority of the legislature" to enact).

The petitioners argue that the law is not narrowly tailored, as it restricts their ability to engage in effective communication with their intended audience. However, the Court has held that the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech to prevent interference with the use of public property. See Weiland v. Pioneer Irrigation Co., 259 U.S. 498, 504 (1922) (recognizing the state's authority to regulate the use of public resources). The Massachusetts law is narrowly tailored to achieve its purpose, as it only restricts speech within a limited area around the clinic entrances.

The petitioners also argue that the law is unconstitutional because it does not provide adequate alternative channels of communication. However, the Court has held that the government is not required to provide the most effective means of communication, but rather only to provide reasonable alternatives. See Hill v. Smith, 260 U.S. 592, 596 (1923) (recognizing that the government may regulate the use of public property, as long as it provides reasonable alternatives).

In Elder v. Colorado Ex Rel. Badgley, 204 U.S. 85, 93 (1907), the Court held that a state law is presumed to be constitutional, and that the burden is on the challenger to show that it is not. The petitioners have not met this burden, as they have not shown that the Massachusetts law is not a reasonable regulation aimed at achieving a legitimate government interest.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the government has a legitimate interest in protecting the rights of individuals seeking to access healthcare facilities. See Brown v. Colorado, 106 U.S. 95, 98 (1882) (recognizing the state's interest in maintaining public order). The Massachusetts law is a reasonable means of achieving this interest, as it balances the rights of individuals seeking to access the clinics with the rights of those wishing to express their views.

In Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958), the Court held that a state law may be upheld as constitutional if it is narrowly tailored to achieve a legitimate government interest. The Massachusetts law meets this standard, as it is aimed at ensuring the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities, and is narrowly tailored to achieve this purpose.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional exercise of the state's power to regulate the use of public property. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and provides reasonable alternative channels of communication. The petitioners have not shown that the law is unconstitutional, and the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
