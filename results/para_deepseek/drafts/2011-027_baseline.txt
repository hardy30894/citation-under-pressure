ARGUMENT

The constitutional test for title navigability requires that a river be navigable at the time of statehood. The Montana courts correctly applied this standard, considering historical evidence of actual commercial use in 1889. Their factual findings are entitled to deference, and neither federal law nor precedent mandates a rigid segment-by-segment analysis.

I. THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR TITLE NAVIGABILITY FOCUSES ON NAVIGABILITY AT STATEHOOD.

Under the equal footing doctrine, a State acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable when it entered the Union. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931). Navigability for title purposes is determined by whether the river was “used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water.” *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1871). The critical inquiry is the river’s condition at statehood, not its present or potential utility. *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 56 (1926).

Montana became a state on November 8, 1889. The trial court properly examined evidence of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as they existed in 1889, relying on historical records, newspaper accounts, government surveys, and testimony from experts. This evidence demonstrated actual commercial use—steamboat traffic, log drives, and other trade—on these rivers at or before statehood. Such evidence satisfies the constitutional standard. *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586 (1922) (navigability may be shown by “acts of commerce” occurring before statehood).

II. THE TRIAL COURT’S FACTUAL FINDINGS ARE ENTITLED TO DEFERENCE.

Whether a river was navigable at statehood is a factual question. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935). The Montana Supreme Court affirmed the trial court’s findings after reviewing the extensive historical record. This Court has long recognized that “findings of fact by the state court on the issue of navigability are entitled to great weight” when supported by evidence. *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9, 10 (1971) (per curiam). The record here includes government reports, ship manifests, and eyewitness accounts of steamboat operations on the Missouri River above Fort Benton, log drives on the Clark Fork, and commercial fishing on the Madison. The trial court properly credited this evidence over PPL’s contrary expert testimony. The Montana Supreme Court did not clearly err in upholding those findings. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (deference to state court navigability findings).

III. NEITHER FEDERAL LAW NOR PRECEDENT REQUIRES A SEGMENT-BY-SEGMENT ANALYSIS.

PPL argues that title navigability must be assessed for each discrete segment of a river, but this Court has never imposed such a rigid requirement. Navigability is determined “in reference to the navigable capacity of the river as a whole.” *The Montello*, 87 U.S. 430, 441 (1874). A river may be navigable for title even if some stretches contain obstructions, so long as those obstructions can be overcome by portages or other customary means. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87–88. Indeed, the Court in *Utah* upheld a finding of navigability for the entire Green River despite rapids and canyons that required temporary portages. *Id.* at 88–89.

Here, the trial court examined the relevant reaches of each river—those stretches underlying PPL’s dams—and found them navigable at statehood. The historical evidence showed that steamboats regularly reached the sites of the Missouri River dams, that log drives passed the Clark Fork dams, and that the Madison was used for commerce. Even if a segment-by-segment analysis were required, each segment at issue was navigable. The Montana Supreme Court so held. There is no constitutional mandate to dissect a river into microscopically small sections; the test is whether the river in its ordinary condition at statehood was capable of commercial use.

IV. CONCLUSION.

The constitutional test for title navigability is navigability at statehood. The Montana courts applied that test correctly, relying on substantial historical evidence. Their factual findings are entitled to deference, and no segment-by-segment analysis is required. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
