**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right—distinct from the insanity defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state in order to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent. Arizona’s rule, which limits the use of such evidence to the affirmative defense of insanity, is consistent with constitutional due process and comports with long-standing precedent recognizing that states retain broad authority to define the scope and procedural contours of criminal defenses.

The Due Process Clause protects a defendant’s right to present a complete defense, but that right is not absolute. As this Court has repeatedly held, “the right to present a defense is not a license to introduce any and all evidence, regardless of its relevance or reliability.” *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998). The Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert testimony on mental state to negate criminal intent unless such use is logically tied to a recognized defense. Here, Clark sought to use expert testimony not only to support his insanity defense—where the burden of proof lies with the defendant—but also to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving intent beyond a reasonable doubt. This dual use of evidence, however, is not constitutionally compelled.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the insanity defense is a creature of state law, and states may define its elements, burden of proof, and permissible evidence. *M’Naghten’s Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843); *People v. Turner*, 11 Cal. 4th 623, 640 (1995). Arizona law, like that of many jurisdictions, treats the insanity defense as an affirmative defense requiring the defendant to prove by clear and convincing evidence that he lacked the capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the law. Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-501. The state’s decision to restrict the use of expert testimony to this specific defense is a valid exercise of legislative authority. *See, e.g., United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 980 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (en banc) (upholding state’s exclusion of expert testimony on mental state when not offered in support of insanity defense).

Moreover, the Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to use expert mental health testimony to attack the prosecution’s burden of proof on an element of the crime—such as criminal intent—unless the defendant is asserting a defense that negates that element. *See, e.g., *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895) (holding that a defendant may not introduce evidence of insanity to negate intent unless the insanity defense is properly raised). The Court has consistently rejected the notion that a defendant has a constitutional right to introduce evidence that would effectively allow him to “defeat the prosecution’s case on an element of the crime without first satisfying the burden of proof on a recognized defense.” *See, e.g., *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 980.

Clark’s argument rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the relationship between the insanity defense and the elements of the crime. The prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Clark had the requisite criminal intent to commit murder. Clark bore no burden to prove he lacked intent; the state bore that burden. His expert testimony, even if admitted, could not have shifted that burden. The state’s rule merely prevents a defendant from using expert testimony to circumvent the burden of proof on a recognized defense. *See *State v. Hines*, 147 Ariz. 505, 510 (1985) (holding that a defendant may not use expert testimony to “undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof on an element of the crime” unless the defense is properly pleaded).

The Arizona Supreme Court correctly affirmed that Clark’s expert testimony was properly limited to the insanity defense. The trial court did not deny Clark a fair opportunity to present his case. He was permitted to introduce expert evidence on his mental state, and the jury was instructed on the insanity defense. The fact that the jury rejected that defense does not render the trial unconstitutional. *See *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 11 (1985) (“The Constitution does not require that a defendant be given a perfect trial, only a fair one.”).

Finally, Clark’s claim is foreclosed by *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307 (1979), which established that a conviction violates due process only if, after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, no rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. The record here shows ample evidence of Clark’s intent: he shot a police officer during a traffic stop, after a confrontation, with a firearm. The jury’s verdict was supported by substantial evidence. The exclusion of expert testimony for purposes beyond the insanity defense did not deprive Clark of a fair trial.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to use expert mental health evidence to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such use is tied to a recognized defense. Arizona’s rule is constitutional, and Clark received a fair trial under the law.
