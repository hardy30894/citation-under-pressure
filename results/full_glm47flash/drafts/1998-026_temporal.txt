**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ERROR WAS HARMLESS BECAUSE THE DEFENDANT WAS AWARE OF HIS RIGHT TO APPEAL.**

The purpose of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal. This purpose is fully served when the defendant is already aware of that right. Here, the record demonstrates that Manuel D. Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. Consequently, the error in failing to advise him of that right did not prejudice his ability to exercise the right.

In *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218 (1967), the Supreme Court held that "a defendant who knowingly and intelligently waives a right... cannot later complain that the waiver was invalid." The Court reasoned that a defendant who is aware of a right and chooses not to exercise it cannot later claim that the procedural mechanisms designed to protect that right were defective. The same logic applies here. The failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was a technical violation of Rule 32(a)(2), but because he was aware of the right, the rule’s purpose was not defeated. The error did not affect the outcome of the proceeding, and therefore, it cannot serve as a basis for habeas relief.

**II. THE ERROR IS SUBJECT TO HARMLESS ERROR ANALYSIS, NOT AUTOMATIC REVERSAL.**

The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is a procedural error, not a structural error, and is therefore subject to harmless error analysis. The Court must determine whether the error affected the verdict. In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), the Supreme Court held that "the Constitution does not require that a conviction be set aside whenever a constitutional error has occurred." Under this standard, a conviction will be upheld if the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

Here, the error was clearly harmless. The record shows that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal and elected not to exercise it. Because the error did not affect the verdict, the District Court properly denied habeas relief. The Court of Appeals correctly applied the harmless error standard, and this Court should affirm.
