**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard—what this Court has described as the capacity for “reasoned choice”—when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Court has long recognized that the waiver of fundamental constitutional rights, such as the right to counsel and the right to a jury trial, demands more than mere awareness of the proceedings. It requires a defendant to possess the mental capacity to make a deliberate, intelligent, and rational decision among available legal alternatives. The trial court’s failure to apply this standard in the present case violated due process and rendered petitioner’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas constitutionally infirm.

This Court has consistently held that the right to counsel is not merely procedural but essential to the fairness of a criminal proceeding. In *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 53 (1932), this Court emphasized that “in a capital case, the right to counsel is a fundamental necessity.” The right to counsel is not a mere formality; it is “a right to the effective assistance of counsel in the defense of the accused.” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 463 (1938). When a defendant chooses to represent himself, he does not merely forgo the assistance of counsel—he waives a host of other constitutional protections, including the right to compulsory process, the right to confront witnesses, the right to a public trial, and the privilege against self-incrimination. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969) (per curiam) (citing *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), cert. denied, 411 U.S. 933 (1973)).

The Court has made clear that such a sweeping waiver cannot be presumed. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, this Court stated that “the right to counsel is a fundamental right, and the waiver of that right must be made with full understanding of its nature and consequences.” 304 U.S. at 463. The Court further held that “the burden is upon the Government to show that the waiver was made voluntarily, knowingly, and intelligently.” Id. This standard, however, is not satisfied by a mere showing that the defendant understood the charges or the possible penalties. The Court has repeatedly stressed that the waiver must be “a deliberate choice,” not a product of confusion, coercion, or mental incapacity.

In *Patterson v. United States*, 292 U.S. 317, 320 (1934), this Court held that a defendant must be “mentally competent to make a rational choice” in order to validly waive counsel. The Court observed that “a man who is not in his right mind cannot be said to have made a voluntary choice.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), cert. denied, 411 U.S. 933 (1973), where the court stated that “the defendant must be able to understand the nature and consequences of his decision to proceed pro se, and to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives.” This standard—what the Ninth Circuit correctly identified as the “reasoned choice” standard—requires more than a basic understanding of the proceedings. It demands that the defendant be capable of evaluating the risks and benefits of proceeding without counsel, of comprehending the legal consequences of his actions, and of making a decision that reflects his own judgment rather than the influence of mental illness, medication, or emotional distress.

The record in this case demonstrates that petitioner was not capable of such a reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, was in a state of severe depression, and was taking four different medications, including Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, Inderal, and Vistaril—drugs known to impair judgment, memory, and cognitive function. The trial court made no inquiry into the effects of these medications, despite petitioner’s own admission that he was on medication. The court accepted petitioner’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas after a series of monosyllabic responses to leading questions. This is not the conduct of a defendant making a deliberate, informed decision. It is the behavior of one whose mental faculties were compromised.

The psychiatric evaluations relied upon by the state court were conducted solely to determine petitioner’s competency to stand trial, not his capacity to make a reasoned choice. The standard for competency to stand trial—“the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense”—is a lower threshold than that required for waiver of counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975) (citing *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966)). The fact that a defendant may be competent to stand trial does not mean he is competent to waive counsel. As this Court has held, “a defendant may be competent to stand trial and yet not competent to waive counsel.” *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d at 975.

The trial court’s failure to conduct a hearing to assess petitioner’s capacity for reasoned choice, despite substantial evidence of mental instability and medication use, violated due process. The Court has long recognized that “when there is a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency, the court must initiate a hearing to determine the issue.” *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973). The record here presents such a doubt. Petitioner’s suicide attempt, his emotional state, his medication, and his monosyllabic responses all point to a defendant who lacked the mental capacity to make a rational decision about his legal rights.
