**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Provide a Cause of Action for a “Class of One” Absent Allegations of Membership in a Protected Class or Group.**

The Fourteenth Amendment commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1. This Court has consistently interpreted the Equal Protection Clause to protect individuals from discrimination that is arbitrary or irrational, but only when the plaintiff demonstrates that she has been treated differently from others who are similarly situated *and* that the differential treatment stems from her membership in a particular class or group. *See, e.g., Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940) (Equal Protection “requires that all persons subjected to [state] legislation shall be treated alike, under like circumstances and conditions, both in the privileges conferred and in the liabilities imposed”); *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886) (holding that equal protection is violated when a law is administered “with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances”). The gravamen of an equal protection claim is that the State has singled out the plaintiff for disfavored treatment *because of* some personal characteristic that defines a class. *See Skinner v. Oklahoma*, 316 U.S. 535, 541 (1942) (striking down a statute that discriminated on the basis of “an inherently arbitrary classification”).

The Court has never recognized a cause of action under the Equal Protection Clause for a plaintiff who alleges only that she was treated differently from others, without also alleging that the differential treatment was motivated by her membership in a class or group. *See, e.g., Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911) (holding that a classification is constitutional if “any state of facts reasonably can be conceived that would sustain it”); *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920) (requiring that a classification “rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation”). In *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949), this Court explained that the Equal Protection Clause “does not require that all persons be dealt with identically, but it does require that a distinction made have some relevance to the purpose for which the classification is made.” The focus is on the rationality of the classification, not on the subjective intent of the decisionmaker. *See Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) (upholding a statute that treated opticians differently from optometrists because the classification was rationally related to a legitimate state interest).

The Seventh Circuit’s holding that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him” is unprecedented and inconsistent with this Court’s precedents. *See, e.g., Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (holding that the Equal Protection Clause is not violated by “a mere denial of a right or privilege to one person while granting it to another, unless the denial is based on an unjustifiable classification”); *Truax v. Corrigan*, 257 U.S. 312, 332 (1921) (stating that the Equal Protection Clause “does not prevent a State from adopting a policy of its own in the matter of classification, provided it does not deny to any person the equal protection of the laws”). The Court has consistently required that a plaintiff demonstrate that the State’s action was “based on a classification that is arbitrary or capricious,” *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155 (1897), not merely that the action was motivated by ill will.

**II. The Seventh Circuit’s “Class of One” Theory Would Inundate Federal Courts with Trivial Disputes.**

The Seventh Circuit’s rule would transform every routine, discretionary decision by a local government into a potential federal constitutional claim. This Court has long cautioned against expanding the Equal Protection Clause to encompass such claims. *See, e.g., Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*, 272 U.S. 365, 386-87 (1926) (upholding zoning ordinances against equal protection challenges because the classifications were rationally related to public health, safety, and welfare); *Zucht v. King*, 260 U.S. 174, 176 (1922) (rejecting an equal protection challenge to a school vaccination requirement because the classification was reasonable). The Equal Protection Clause is not a “general source of authority to federal courts to review the wisdom or fairness of state and local government actions.” *See, e.g., Munn v. Illinois*, 94 U.S. 113, 134 (1877) (holding that the Constitution does not empower courts to sit as “supervisory boards” over state economic regulations).

**III. The Plaintiff’s Allegations Fail to State a Claim Under Any Recognized Equal Protection Theory.**

Grace Olech’s complaint alleges only that the Village of Willowbrook demanded a 33-foot easement from her while requiring only a 15-foot easement from other property owners. She does not allege that the Village’s action was based on her race, religion, national origin, or any other characteristic that would define a protected class. *See, e.g., Strauder v. West Virginia*, 100 U.S. 303, 307-08 (1880) (holding that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits discrimination based on race). Nor does she allege that the Village’s action was part of a pattern or practice of discrimination against a particular group. *See, e.g., Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346-47 (1880) (holding that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits “purposeful discrimination” against a class). Her claim is, at most, that the Village treated her differently from others for reasons that she believes are arbitrary. But this Court has held that “the Equal Protection Clause does not forbid discrimination as to individuals within a class, but only as to the class itself.” *Truax v. Corrigan*, 257 U.S. at 332.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
