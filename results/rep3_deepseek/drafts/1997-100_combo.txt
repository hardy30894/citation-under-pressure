ARGUMENT

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE, BY ITS TERMS, BARS RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION AFTER AN APPELLATE ACQUITTAL FOR INSUFFICIENT EVIDENCE.

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” This protection applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969). The Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, a second prosecution after conviction, and multiple punishments for the same offense. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). When a prior conviction allegation is tried to a jury, the defendant is placed in jeopardy. An appellate finding of insufficient evidence constitutes an acquittal. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669–70 (1896); *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904). Retrial of that allegation would put Monge twice in jeopardy for the same alleged prior conviction.

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause’s text—“offence”—is not limited to substantive crimes. A prior conviction allegation, when proven, increases the maximum punishment for the current offense. Under California’s three-strikes law, the prior conviction functions as an element that alters the grade of punishment. Thus, the Double Jeopardy Clause applies. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1874) (Clause bars multiple punishments for the same offense). The Court has never held that the Clause applies only to capital sentencing. *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (acknowledging that the Clause “protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal” without limiting to capital cases). Indeed, *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 192 (1957), held that the Clause bars retrial for a greater offense after an implied acquittal, reasoning that the “defendant’s right to have his trial completed by a particular tribunal” is fundamental. That principle applies equally to a separate factual determination of a prior conviction.

II. THE STATE’S CHOICE TO PROVIDE A TRIAL WITH FULL PROCEDURAL PROTECTIONS TRIGGERS DOUBLE JEOPARDY PROTECTION.

California elects to provide a jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, confrontation, and cross-examination for prior conviction allegations. These are the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” By providing such protections, the State places the defendant in jeopardy. *Fong Foo v. United States*, 369 U.S. 141, 143 (1962) (directed acquittal bars retrial). The Double Jeopardy Clause applies whether the proceeding is labeled “sentencing” or “trial.” *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 736 (1963) (Clause applies to any proceeding that “could result in conviction”). Here, the trial court held a separate evidentiary hearing on the prior conviction allegation, with the State bearing the burden of proof. The Court of Appeal reversed for insufficient evidence—an acquittal. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 669–70. Retrial would violate the Clause.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* held that the Due Process Clause does not require a formal trial at sentencing, but it did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. Once the State chooses to provide a trial, it must respect the Clause’s protections. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 130 (acquittal is final “whether the jury heard the case or the court”); *Green*, 355 U.S. at 192. The distinctions drawn between capital and noncapital sentencing are unsupported by the text or history. The Clause protects “any person” from twice being “put in jeopardy of life or limb.” “Life or limb” encompasses any punishment that may be imposed. *Lange*, 85 U.S. at 173. The enhanced sentence here potentially doubled Monge’s prison term—a significant deprivation of liberty.

III. THE APPELLATE ACQUITTAL ON THE PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION IS FINAL.

The Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to prove that Monge “personally” used a deadly weapon. This is a factual determination that the State failed to meet its burden. Under *Ball*, *Kepner*, and *Fong Foo*, that determination is final. The State cannot cure its evidentiary deficiency by retrying the allegation. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. The Double Jeopardy Clause’s “acquittal” bar is “absolute.” *Fong Foo*, 369 U.S. at 143. No exception exists for sentencing allegations. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 794.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed. Retrial of the prior conviction allegation is barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment.
