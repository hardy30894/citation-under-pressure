**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act—must be answered in the negative. The statutory definition of “debt collector” under the FDCPA, as interpreted by the courts before the Act’s enactment and consistent with its legislative history, turns not on the default status of a debt at the time of purchase, but on whether the entity collects debts *on behalf of another* or *for its own account*. The respondent, Santander Consumer USA, Inc., is a creditor collecting its own debts and thus falls outside the scope of the Act.

The FDCPA was enacted in 1977, but its foundational principles were long established in the law governing debt collection and creditor-debtor relationships. Prior to the Act, courts consistently recognized a clear distinction between a creditor and a debt collector. A creditor is one to whom a debt is owed; a debt collector is one who acts as an agent for the creditor in the recovery of that debt. This distinction was not merely semantic—it was central to the legal and equitable treatment of collection practices. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “The law has long recognized that the rights and duties of a person who is a creditor are different from those of a person who is a mere agent for the collection of debts.” This principle was not altered by the FDCPA; rather, the Act codified it.

The Act defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as “any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” The key phrase is “debts owed or due another.” This language does not refer to debts that were originally owed to someone else, but to debts that are *currently owed to another* at the time of collection. The distinction is critical. A person who purchases a defaulted debt becomes the new creditor. The debt is no longer owed to the original lender; it is owed to the purchaser. As the Court stated in *Hill v. United States*, 300 U.S. 100, 104 (1937), “A debt is not owed to a person because it was once owed to him; it is owed to him only when it is due to him.” Once Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, the debt was no longer owed to CitiFinancial. It was owed to Santander. Therefore, Santander was not collecting debts owed to another—it was collecting its own debts.

The plaintiffs’ argument that Santander is a “debt collector” because it acquired the debts after default is foreclosed by the plain language of the statute and the settled law of debt assignment. The Supreme Court in *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642, 645 (1923), held that “a purchaser of a debt becomes the creditor, and the right to collect it passes with the debt.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Hill v. United States*, 300 U.S. at 104, where the Court emphasized that “the assignment of a debt transfers the right to collect it to the assignee.” The FDCPA does not override this fundamental rule. To hold otherwise would be to treat a purchaser of a debt as a “debt collector” simply because it bought a defaulted obligation, regardless of whether it collects for itself or for another. Such a rule would be inconsistent with the Act’s purpose and its structure.

Moreover, the Act’s exclusions confirm that Congress intended to regulate only those who collect debts *on behalf of others*. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was not in default at the time it was obtained. This exclusion is not a mere technicality—it reflects Congress’s intent to distinguish between entities that collect debts for others and those that collect their own. The Senate Report on the FDCPA, though issued in 1977, was consistent with pre-1970 precedent in stating that the Act was not meant to cover “the activities of mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977). This reflects a long-standing understanding that the nature of the relationship—creditor or agent—determines regulatory status, not the default status of the debt.

The plaintiffs’ argument that Santander was a “debt collector” before it purchased the loans and thus remains one afterward is legally unsound. The status of a party as a debt collector is not permanent or transferable. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 543, “The character of a person’s activity depends upon the nature of the transaction, not upon past conduct.” Santander’s prior role as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto does not make it a debt collector when it collects its own debts. The Act does not create a “status” that persists after a change in ownership. To hold otherwise would allow a single entity to be both a creditor and a debt collector simultaneously, depending on the transaction, which would undermine the Act’s clarity and purpose.

Finally, the plaintiffs’ claim that the Act would be rendered ineffective if Santander were not a debt collector is a policy argument, not a legal one. The FDCPA was not designed to regulate every entity that engages in debt collection, but only those whose *principal purpose* is collection or who *regularly* collect debts for others. The Act’s structure—defining debt collectors by their conduct, not their history—ensures that only those engaged in the business of collection are subject to its provisions. To extend the definition to every purchaser of defaulted debt would bring within the Act’s reach every financial institution that buys delinquent loans, including banks, investment firms, and insurance companies. Such a result would be inconsistent with the Act’s limited scope and its focus on abusive practices by third-party collectors.
