The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's facilities, although not traditional public forums, were opened for use by the general public for certain purposes, thereby creating a limited public forum.

The government's power to regulate expression in a public forum is limited. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the First Amendment prohibits the government from abridging the freedom of speech in public forums, unless the regulation is necessary to serve a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that interest. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939). In Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941), the Court emphasized the importance of equal access to public forums, holding that the government may not discriminate against certain speakers or viewpoints.

The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series, described as "Family oriented movie from a Christian perspective," was based on the religious content of the speech. Local Rule No. 7, which prohibits the use of school premises "by any group for religious purposes," constitutes a content-based exclusion. The Supreme Court has consistently held that content-based exclusions are subject to strict scrutiny, and that the government must demonstrate a compelling state interest to justify such exclusions. Lovell v. Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 451 (1938); Staub v. City of Baxley, 355 U.S. 313, 321-22 (1958).

The District has not demonstrated a compelling state interest in excluding Lamb's Chapel's film series. In fact, allowing the film series would not have violated the Establishment Clause. As the Court noted in Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), "we are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being." The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral. The Court has long recognized that regulations that grant or deny permits based on content are not viewpoint-neutral. Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951); Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 294 (1951).

Furthermore, the lower court's reliance on the fact that the District had not previously allowed religious groups to use the facilities is misplaced. The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not justified by a history of prior religious uses. As the Court stated in Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948), "any restraint on speech must be justified by a clear public interest, threatened, not doubtfully or remotely." The District has not demonstrated such a clear public interest.

In conclusion, the District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral and does not serve a compelling state interest.
