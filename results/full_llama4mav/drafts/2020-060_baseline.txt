The United States respectfully submits that the D.C. Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim under CERCLA § 107 is barred because the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B).

As this Court has recognized, CERCLA provides two distinct causes of action for parties seeking to recover costs associated with the cleanup of hazardous waste sites: a cost-recovery action under § 107 and a contribution action under § 113(f). Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161 (2004). The distinction between these two provisions is critical, as the statute of limitations for a contribution action is three years, whereas the statute of limitations for a remedial cost-recovery action is six years. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B), (g)(3).

The D.C. Circuit correctly held that Guam's 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B). Section 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party that "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement shall be entitled to seek contribution" from other potentially responsible parties. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The Consent Decree required Guam to take specific remedial actions, including closing the Ordot Dump and designing and installing a cover system, which constitutes a "response action" under CERCLA. See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24) (defining "remedial action" to include "construction of . . . other engineering controls").

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree did not "resolve [its] liability" within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B) is unpersuasive. The Decree's substantive provisions, including Guam's obligations to close the Ordot Dump and implement a cover system, demonstrate that Guam's liability was, in fact, resolved. See Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries, 937 F.3d 928, 931 (7th Cir. 2019) (holding that a consent decree triggered § 113(f)(3)(B) despite a disclaimer of liability). The Disclaimer of liability in the Consent Decree does not negate the fact that Guam's liability was resolved for purposes of § 113(f)(3)(B). Asarco LLC v. Atl. Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017).

Furthermore, Guam's contention that the Consent Decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it concerned non-CERCLA pollutant discharges is without merit. The Complaint demanded that Guam "complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," which is a "remedial action" under CERCLA. See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24).

Finally, Guam's due process argument is forfeited because it was not raised in the district court. Keepseagle v. Vilsack, 815 F.3d 28, 36 (D.C. Cir. 2016). In any event, Guam's loss of its contribution claim is a result of the statute of limitations, not a deprivation of due process.

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the D.C. Circuit's judgment that Guam's claim under CERCLA § 107 is barred.
