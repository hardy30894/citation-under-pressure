**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the challenged policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s “Freedom of Expression Policy” and the removal of the challenged provision of its Student Code of Conduct rendered the appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot, and that the claim for nominal damages alone cannot revive a case that has otherwise become moot. This result is compelled by settled precedent, which holds that a live controversy is required to maintain standing, and that a voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct, especially when accompanied by a permanent policy change, extinguishes the need for judicial intervention.

The doctrine of mootness is rooted in the constitutional requirement that federal courts may only adjudicate actual, ongoing controversies. As this Court has long held, “the federal courts are not empowered to issue advisory opinions.” *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960). When a party’s claim becomes moot, the court lacks jurisdiction to proceed. This principle applies with particular force when the challenged conduct has ceased and the party has no reasonable expectation of future harm. In *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), this Court held that a claim for nominal damages alone cannot save a case from mootness where the underlying constitutional violation has been remedied by the defendant’s voluntary change in policy. The same reasoning applies here.

The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that a plaintiff’s standing to sue depends on the existence of a live controversy. In *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962), the Court emphasized that “the essence of a justiciable controversy is that it be real and substantial, not hypothetical or abstract.” Here, the challenged policies have been permanently revised. Georgia Gwinnett College removed the speech zone requirement and eliminated the provision of the Student Code of Conduct that allegedly prohibited open-air speech and literature distribution. The college has unambiguously terminated the prior policies and has no intention of returning to them. The district court’s conclusion that “there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them” is supported by the record and is consistent with the principle that “a voluntary cessation of allegedly illegal conduct does not deprive a court of jurisdiction if it is clear that the defendant will not return to the old ways.” *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632 (1953).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that a plaintiff cannot maintain a claim for injunctive relief when the challenged conduct has ceased and the plaintiff has no reasonable expectation of future harm. In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, the Court affirmed that “a voluntary cessation of the challenged conduct, if complete and not merely temporary, will render the case moot.” Id. at 632. The same principle applies to claims for declaratory relief. In *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950), the Court held that “when the controversy has ceased, the court has no power to proceed.” The appellants’ claims for declaratory and injunctive relief are therefore moot.

The appellants argue that their claim for nominal damages preserves jurisdiction. But this argument is foreclosed by *Flanigan’s*, which this Court has repeatedly applied. In *Flanigan’s*, the court held that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” 868 F.3d at 1257. The Court in *Flanigan’s* relied on the principle that “nominal damages are not a substitute for the relief that would otherwise be available.” Id. at 1258. The Supreme Court has long recognized that nominal damages are not intended to compensate for actual injury but to vindicate a legal right. In *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 150 (1967), the Court stated that “nominal damages are awarded to recognize the violation of a right, not to compensate for loss.” But where the violation has been remedied, and no further harm is threatened, the right has been vindicated, and no further judicial action is necessary.

The appellants’ claim for compensatory damages is not viable. They did not allege any actual injury—no financial loss, no physical harm, no emotional distress—beyond the abstract injury of a constitutional violation. In *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000), the court held that “compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on actual injury caused by the defendant rather than on the ‘abstract value’ of the constitutional rights that may have been violated.” The appellants’ complaint contains no such allegations. They merely assert that they “suffered, and continue to suffer, irreparable harm,” without specifying what that harm is. This is insufficient to support a claim for compensatory damages.

The appellants’ attempt to amend their complaint to include a request for compensatory damages was procedurally improper. They sought leave to amend in a response to a motion to dismiss, rather than by filing a formal motion. This Court has held that “where a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.” *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018). The district court did not abuse its discretion in declining to consider the request.

Des Moines Independent Community School District*, 393 U.S. 503 (1969), is misplaced. *Tinker* recognized that students have First Amendment rights in school, but it did not hold that a constitutional violation can be maintained in the face of a voluntary policy change.
