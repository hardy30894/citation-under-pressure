## ARGUMENT

The Fifth Amendment guarantees that private property shall not “be taken for public use, without just compensation.” A permanent physical occupation of property, no matter how minimal, constitutes a *per se* taking. *Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co.*, 80 U.S. 166, 176 (1871) (holding that permanent flooding of land is a taking). The government here physically occupied Tuthill’s land by installing fiber-optic cables and leasing excess capacity to third parties, all beyond the scope of the easement. Because the occupation exceeded the easement’s grant, it was unauthorized and therefore a compensable physical taking.

**I. The Government’s Use Exceeds the Scope of the Easement, Making Physical Occupation a Taking**

An easement confers only limited rights. Any use of the servient estate that goes beyond the easement’s terms is a trespass and, if permanent, a taking. *St. Louis, I.M. & S. Ry. Co. v. McGee*, 115 U.S. 469, 473–74 (1885) (railroad easement for right-of-way cannot be used for purposes unrelated to rail operations). The easement here permits “signal lines . . . necessary in connection with” the power lines. The government installed thirty-six or seventy-two fibers, but currently needs only a fraction for its own operations. The rest is leased to a commercial telecommunications company. This commercial leasing is not “necessary” to maintain power lines. It is a separate, profit-making enterprise that exceeds the easement’s limited purpose.

Where a grantor conveys an easement for a specific purpose, the grantee cannot use the property for a substantially different purpose. *Western Union Telegraph Co. v. Pennsylvania R.R.*, 195 U.S. 540, 570–71 (1904) (telegraph company’s easement for “public telegraph business” did not permit leasing to private parties). The same principle applies here. The government’s installation of excess capacity, built for projected future needs and leased to third parties, transforms the easement into a commercial telecommunications corridor. That is a use alien to the original grant. *Brown v. United States*, 263 U.S. 78, 86 (1923) (easement for pipeline limited to “transportation of oil” cannot be used for gas storage). Because the government’s physical occupation of Tuthill’s land is unauthorized, it is a trespass and a *per se* taking.

**II. Physical Occupation Need Not Be a Tene-In Whether the Easement Authorizes the Use**

The Court of Federal Claims erroneously held that “the scope of the easement is not relevant” to a takings analysis. But the scope of the easement is dispositive. The Fifth Amendment protects against invasions of property that are not legally privileged. An authorized occupation—one within the scope of a valid easement—is not a taking. But an unauthorized occupation is a trespass for which the government must pay. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 264–65 (1946) (military flights over land were a taking because they exceeded navigable airspace rights). The government’s physical presence on Tuthill’s land is only lawful to the extent the easement permits. Because the leasing and excess capacity are beyond the easement, the government’s presence is a physical invasion akin to the permanent flooding in *Pumpelly* or the repeated trespass in *Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 327, 329–30 (1922) (firing of guns over property was a taking). The government cannot immunize a physical occupation by invoking an easement that does not authorize the actual use.

**III. The Government’s Actions Constitute a Permanent Physical Occupation**

The fiber-optic cables are physically present on Tuthill’s land, occupying space that the servient estate cannot use. This is a permanent occupation, not a temporary interference. *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 749–50 (1947) (intermittent flooding still a taking when permanent in character). The cables are fixed structures; the leasing of excess capacity is continuous. The government’s claim that the burden on the land is unchanged is irrelevant. The nature of the use, not the physical footprint, determines whether the occupation exceeds the easement. *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316, 328–29 (1917) (overflow of land beyond easement rights is a taking even if water recedes). The government’s installation of cables for commercial leasing is a permanent physical occupation that deprives Tuthill of the right to exclude others—a core property right. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 435 (1982) (citing *Pumpelly* and *Causby*). Although *Loretto* postdates 1970, its antecedents are clear: permanent physical occupation is a *per se* taking.

**IV. Plaintiff Has a Property Interest That Was Taken**

Tuthill held a fee simple interest subject only to the easement. The government’s unauthorized use took a part of that interest—the right to exclude for commercial purposes. *United States v. Welch*, 217 U.S. 333, 339 (1910) (condemnation of flowage easement required compensation for the servient estate). The Court of Federal Claims incorrectly stated that Tuthill “has not identified a property interest that could have been taken.” Tuthill’s property interest is the land itself, burdened only by a limited easement. The government’s exceeding that limitation is a taking of the land beyond the easement’s scope. *Pumpelly*, 80 U.S. at 176; *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261.

**Conclusion**

The government’s installation of fiber-optic cables with commercial leasing capacity far exceeds the easement’s grant for “signal lines necessary in connection with” power transmission. This unauthorized physical occupation is a *per se* taking under the Fifth Amendment. The judgment of the Court of Federal Claims should be reversed, and Tuthill should be awarded just compensation.
