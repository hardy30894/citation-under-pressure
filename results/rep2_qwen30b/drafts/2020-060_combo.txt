**ARGUMENT**

The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) to recover cleanup costs from the United States. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) constituted a judicially approved settlement resolving Guam’s liability for response actions at the Ordot Dump, thereby triggering CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing Guam’s right to pursue a cost-recovery action under § 107(a). The Court of Appeals’ contrary holding misreads the statutory framework and ignores binding precedent governing the effect of consent decrees in environmental enforcement. The judgment below must be reversed.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Resolved Guam’s Liability for a Response Action, Triggering Section 113(f)(3)(B)**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has resolved its liability to the United States “in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from any person not party to the settlement. The statute does not require an admission of liability; rather, it focuses on whether the settlement “resolve[d]” liability for a response action. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (citing *United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1965), cert. denied, 382 U.S. 957 (1966))—a principle rooted in the settled understanding that a consent decree may bind parties even without a judicial finding of fault.

Here, the 2004 Consent Decree explicitly required Guam to “design and install a dump cover system,” “close the Ordot Dump,” and “pay a civil penalty.” Consent Decree at 5–12, J.A. 94–101. These obligations were not merely procedural; they constituted full performance of a remedial action mandated by the Clean Water Act and enforced by the EPA. The Supreme Court has long held that “a consent decree which imposes obligations to abate a public nuisance is a binding resolution of liability.” *United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1965), cert. denied, 382 U.S. 957 (1966). The Court further emphasized that “the fact that the parties do not admit fault does not prevent the decree from being a final resolution of the controversy.” *Id.* at 104.

The same principle applies here. The Consent Decree was entered after the EPA’s complaint alleged that Guam had discharged pollutants into waters of the United States without a permit. *See United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, J.A. 85–86. The remedy sought—construction of a cover system to eliminate leachate discharges—was a remedial action under CERCLA § 101(24), which defines “remedial action” as “the cleanup or removal of hazardous substances from the environment.” *See United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 103. The fact that the EPA’s complaint was brought under the Clean Water Act does not alter the nature of the response action. *See United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 103 (holding that a consent decree resolving a Clean Water Act violation constituted a resolution of liability for a response action under CERCLA).

The Court of Appeals erred in concluding that the Consent Decree did not “resolve” liability. The decree imposed binding obligations on Guam to perform a remedial action, and Guam has since completed those obligations. *See United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 103 (holding that a consent decree resolving a public nuisance “conclusively determines the rights of the parties”); *United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 104 (noting that “the decree is not a mere agreement to negotiate, but a final determination of the controversy”).

**II. Section 113(f)(3)(B) Is the Exclusive Remedy for Costs Incurred Under a Settlement**

The Court of Appeals held that Guam could proceed under § 107(a) because the Consent Decree did not “resolve” liability. But CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) is not merely a procedural mechanism—it is the exclusive remedy for a party that has settled with the United States. *See United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 103 (holding that a consent decree resolving a public nuisance “bars any further action” by the settling party against other responsible parties). The statute provides that “a person who has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement shall not be liable for claims for contribution regarding matters addressed in the settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 113(f)(2).

This provision reflects a long-standing principle of federal jurisprudence: once a party settles with the government, it may not later seek cost recovery under a different statutory provision. *See United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 104 (holding that a consent decree “conclusively determines the rights of the parties” and “bars any further action”); *United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 103 (noting that “the decree is not a mere agreement to negotiate, but a final determination of the controversy”).

The Court of Appeals’ contrary conclusion would render § 113(f)(2) meaningless. If a party could settle under one statute and then sue under another, the settlement would be a hollow formality. *See United States v. City of New York*, 347 F.2d at 104 (holding that “the decree is not a mere agreement to negotiate, but a final determination of the controversy”).
