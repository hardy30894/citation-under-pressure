**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment should be affirmed.

The Constitution does not permit a defendant to forfeit fundamental rights—such as the right to counsel or the right to a fair trial—without a knowing, voluntary, and intelligent waiver. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). Such waivers are not lightly presumed. *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 885 (9th Cir.1986) (en banc) (per curiam). When a defendant seeks to represent himself or change a plea to guilty, the court must ensure that the decision is not the product of mental incapacity, coercion, or confusion. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights is higher than the standard for competency to stand trial.

The standard for competency to stand trial—articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960)—requires only that the defendant have “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975). But competency to waive counsel or plead guilty demands more: the defendant must possess the capacity for a “reasoned choice” among the available alternatives. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (en banc). This higher standard reflects the gravity of the decisions at issue—waiving counsel and pleading guilty are not mere procedural formalities, but irreversible acts that surrender core constitutional protections.

The record here demonstrates that the trial court failed to apply this heightened standard. At the time of the plea, Richard Moran was recovering from a suicide attempt, had been taking four psychotropic medications—including Inderal, Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Vistaril—was severely depressed, and communicated with the court in monosyllabic responses. The court made no inquiry into the effects of the medications, despite Moran’s own admission that he was on medication. *See* *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (a court must initiate a competency inquiry when there is “substantial evidence of incompetence”). The court accepted Moran’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas without any meaningful assessment of his mental state or capacity to make a reasoned choice.

The state court’s postconviction ruling—that Moran was competent—was based on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were designed solely to assess competency to stand trial, not to evaluate the higher “reasoned choice” standard. *See* *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir.1973) (competency to waive counsel requires a different standard than competency to stand trial). The reports of Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky did not address the effects of medication, the defendant’s ability to weigh alternatives, or his understanding of the consequences of waiving counsel. *See* *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. The state court’s reliance on these reports to support a finding of competency to waive counsel was legally erroneous. *See* *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315, 83 S.Ct. 745, 758, 9 L.Ed.2d 770 (1963) (a federal court may not assume a state court applied the correct legal standard unless the record clearly shows it did).

The Due Process Clause does not permit a court to rely on post hoc evaluations to cure a failure to conduct a contemporaneous inquiry. *See* *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (en banc). The decision to waive counsel and plead guilty must be made with full awareness of the consequences and the ability to make a rational choice among alternatives. *See* *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. The record here shows no such capacity. The trial court’s failure to apply the “reasoned choice” standard deprived Moran of due process.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply the heightened “reasoned choice” standard when a defendant seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty. The state court failed to do so, and the federal court correctly reversed.
