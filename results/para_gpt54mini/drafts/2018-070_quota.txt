### Argument

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act permits adjacent state law to become federal law only when that state law is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). This Court should reject Respondent’s attempt to recast that standard as a mere preemption inquiry. The statute does not ask whether state law concerns the same subject matter as the dispute and escapes direct conflict with federal law. It asks whether, in the first place, there is a gap in federal law to fill. That is the rule reflected in the statute’s text, structure, and this Court’s precedents.

OCSLA was enacted to create a complete federal law for fixed structures on the Outer Continental Shelf, while borrowing adjacent state law only as surrogate federal law where federal law is silent. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-66 (1969). In Rodrigue, the Court explained that fixed platforms are not maritime vessels and that state law applies through OCSLA only once any “obstacle” to its application has been removed. Id. at 366. That formulation is incompatible with the Ninth Circuit’s rule that state law automatically applies whenever it is not inconsistent with federal law. Rodrigue did not treat state law as independently operative alongside federal law; it treated state law as borrowed only when federal law does not already occupy the field for the issue at hand.

Subsequent decisions confirm that OCSLA supplies a federal-enclaves-style choice-of-law regime, not an open-ended concurrency regime. In Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp., 453 U.S. 473 (1981), the Court described OCSLA as extending federal law to the shelf and borrowing state law only as surrogate federal law when appropriate. See id. at 478-84. In O’Melveny & Myers v. Federal Deposit Insurance Corp., 512 U.S. 79 (1994), the Court emphasized that where federal law governs, state law is displaced except where Congress has authorized borrowing; OCSLA is such a borrowing statute, but only to the extent federal law leaves room. See id. at 84-85. Likewise, in Parker Drilling Management Services, Ltd. v. Newton, 875 F.3d 953 (9th Cir. 2017), the panel below departed from the statutory design by making “not inconsistent” do all the work and nullifying the threshold question whether federal law is already governing the issue.

This Court’s federal-enclave cases reinforce Parker’s position. In James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94 (1940), the Court held that state law becomes federal law in an enclave only insofar as existing federal law does not supply the rule. Id. at 99-100. In Paul v. United States, 371 U.S. 245 (1963), the Court explained that later-enacted state law does not automatically apply absent congressional adoption. Id. at 263-69. And in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), the Court upheld prospective adoption only because Congress expressly incorporated future state criminal laws. Id. at 293-94. OCSLA contains a similar incorporation mechanism, but Congress still limited it to law that is “applicable and not inconsistent.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Those words presuppose that federal law first determines the operative rule of decision.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach also conflicts with the Fifth Circuit’s most faithful reading of OCSLA. In Continental Oil Co. v. London Steam-Ship Owners’ Mutual Insurance Association, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law may be assimilated only to “fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. Id. at 1036. Later decisions from the Fifth Circuit have continued to recognize that OCSLA does not authorize state law where federal law already speaks to the issue. See Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc., 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990); Grand Isle Shipyard, Inc. v. Seacor Marine, L.L.C., 589 F.3d 778, 786-88 (5th Cir. 2009) (en banc). That reading is also consistent with Tallentire, which held that OCSLA’s reference to state law does not create a freestanding state-law remedy untethered to the federal scheme. Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207, 218-22 (1986).

Here, the federal law on point is the Fair Labor Standards Act. The FLSA establishes minimum wage, overtime, and related rules for covered employees. See 29 U.S.C. §§ 206, 207. It also contains a savings clause preserving more protective state standards for workers covered by state law. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). That clause matters, but it does not answer the OCSLA question. It shows only that Congress knew how to allow state wage rules to coexist with federal law. It did not authorize state law to be borrowed on the Outer Continental Shelf where federal law already occupies the subject.

California law therefore cannot be applied as surrogate federal law here. Because the FLSA addresses wage-and-hour protections, there is no gap for OCSLA to fill. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule converts OCSLA into a blanket adoption of adjacent state labor law whenever not expressly preempted, a result the statute’s text and this Court’s precedents do not permit. The judgment should be reversed.
