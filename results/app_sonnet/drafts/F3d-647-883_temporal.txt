**ARGUMENT**

**I. This Court's Order Recalling the Mandate Restarted the Ninety-Day Period for Seeking Certiorari.**

A judgment of this Court is not final for purposes of further review while the mandate remains recalled. Supreme Court Rule 13(3) recognizes this principle in the analogous context of rehearing: where "the lower court appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing," the time to petition for certiorari "runs from the date of the denial of rehearing." Sup. Ct. R. 13(3). The same logic governs here. When this Court recalled its mandate on June 12, 2007, to entertain Buckles's Rule 4-1(e) motion, it necessarily reopened the case; the mandate that reissued on June 20, 2007, was the operative mandate from which the ninety-day certiorari period should run.

This reading accords with the Supreme Court's repeated insistence that procedural deadlines not be so technically applied as to defeat a litigant's right to be heard where the delay is attributable to the very process designed to protect him. In *Fallen v. United States*, 378 U.S. 139 (1964), the Court held that a habeas petitioner's notice of appeal, though filed after the deadline because of circumstances the petitioner could not control, satisfied the timeliness requirement because he had done "all that could reasonably be expected" to comply. *Fallen*, 378 U.S. at 142. Buckles likewise did all that could reasonably be expected: he timely invoked Rule 4-1(e), and the delay in resolving that motion — not any lack of diligence on his part — pushed his certiorari filing past what the Supreme Court Clerk calculated as the deadline.

Similarly, in *United States v. Robinson*, 361 U.S. 220 (1960), the Court excused an untimely notice of appeal filed after the deadline because the delay resulted from the failure of appointed counsel to act promptly, reasoning that a defendant should not forfeit his right to appellate review because of a breakdown in the machinery meant to secure that right. The same reasoning applies with even greater force where, as here, the "machinery" at issue is this Court's own order recalling its mandate to consider a motion Buckles was entitled to file. Buckles should not be penalized for the time consumed by proceedings on a motion this Court itself agreed to entertain.

**II. Even If the Certiorari Petition Was Untimely, Buckles Is Entitled to Equitable Tolling Because He Reasonably Relied on Erroneous Information from the Clerk of This Court.**

If the Court concludes that the certiorari clock ran from the original denial of rehearing rather than from reissuance of the mandate, Buckles is nonetheless entitled to equitable relief because his delay resulted from his reasonable reliance on information supplied by an officer of this Court.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a litigant should not be charged with the consequences of relying on erroneous assurances from a court or its clerk regarding the timeliness of a filing. In *Harris Truck Lines, Inc. v. Cherry Meat Packers, Inc.*, 371 U.S. 156 (1962) (per curiam), the Court held that where a party relied on the district court's own action in permitting a filing believed to preserve appellate rights, the resulting delay presented "unique circumstances" excusing strict compliance with the appeal deadline. The Court reaffirmed that doctrine two years later in *Thompson v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 375 U.S. 384 (1964) (per curiam), holding that a party's reasonable reliance on a court's own assurance concerning the effect of a pending motion on the time to appeal may excuse an otherwise untimely filing.

Buckles's circumstances are indistinguishable in substance. His sister was told by an employee in the office of the Clerk of this Court that "the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued." Buckles reasonably understood that statement to mean his ninety days would run from the date the mandate was recalled — precisely the reading urged in Part I above. That understanding was not unreasonable; it came from the office charged with administering this Court's own docket.

The equitable principle underlying *Harris Truck Lines* and *Thompson* traces to the older and more general rule that a party will not be permitted to invoke a limitations defense where its own conduct — or the conduct of those acting under its authority — induced the opposing party's delay. In *Glus v. Brooklyn Eastern District Terminal*, 359 U.S. 231 (1959), the Court held that a defendant may be estopped from asserting a statute of limitations where its own representations caused the plaintiff to delay filing suit, explaining that "no man may take advantage of his own wrong." *Glus*, 359 U.S. at 232–33. So too here: if the Clerk's office misinformed Buckles's sister about the operative deadline, the government should not now be heard to insist on the very deadline its own officer misdescribed.

Finally, the equitable roots of this doctrine reach back to *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342 (1874), where the Court held that a limitations period does not run against a party who has been misled to his detriment through no fault of his own, until he discovers — or reasonably should have discovered — the truth. Buckles discovered the truth only when the Supreme Court Clerk notified him, after the fact, that his petition was deemed untimely.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, Buckles's certiorari petition — and consequently his § 2255 motion — should be deemed timely, or, in the alternative, the case should be remanded to determine whether equitable tolling applies based on the Clerk's misinformation.
