ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Prejudicial and Improper Opinion Testimony from the ADAs and Police Lieutenant

The trial was tainted by the admission of improper opinion testimony from two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant. These witnesses were permitted to vouch for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, offer conclusions on the existence of probable cause, and interpret evidence that the jury was equally capable of analyzing. This testimony was highly prejudicial and requires a new trial.

It is a "bedrock principle" of evidence law that witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses. United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 1988). The admission of such testimony "constitutes an impermissible invasion of the jury's province to determine credibility." Id. Here, the ADAs and police lieutenant were allowed to testify that Ramos and Rivera were credible, which directly impacted the jury's assessment of the officers' testimony.

Furthermore, the witnesses were permitted to testify in the form of legal conclusions, specifically on the issue of probable cause. This is also improper, as it is the jury's role to determine whether probable cause existed. See Hygh v. Jacobs, 961 F.2d 359, 365 (2d Cir. 1992) ("[T]he ultimate legal conclusion is for the court, not for the witnesses."). The admission of such testimony "usurps the court's role in instructing the jury on the law and the jury's role in applying that law to the facts." Id.

Finally, the witnesses interpreted evidence that the jury was capable of analyzing on its own, which is also improper. See United States v. Bilzerian, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991) ("[T]he jury was in as good a position as [the expert] to analyze the evidence and draw conclusions."). The admission of this testimony was not harmless, as it allowed ostensibly neutral government agents to speak directly to the two most hotly contested issues in the case: the credibility of Ramos and Rivera, and the existence of probable cause.

II. The District Court Should Have Given Appellants' Requested Jury Instructions on Obstructing Governmental Administration and Punitive Damages

Given that we are remanding for a new trial, we consider Appellants' challenges to the jury instructions in the interest of judicial economy.

A. Obstructing Governmental Administration

Under New York law, the offense of obstructing governmental administration requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. See People v. Lupinacci, 191 A.D.2d 589, 590 (N.Y. App. Div. 1993). The District Court should have instructed the jury accordingly. See Lennon v. Miller, 66 F.3d 416, 424 (2d Cir. 1995) ("A jury instruction that misleads the jury as to the correct legal standard or does not adequately inform the jury on the law is grounds for reversal.").

B. Punitive Damages

Appellants' version of events, if credited by the jury, would have allowed a jury to infer that Ramos and Rivera acted with malice, entitling them to a punitive damages instruction. See King v. Macri, 993 F.2d 294, 298 (2d Cir. 1993) ("Punitive damages are available in a § 1983 action when the defendant's conduct is shown to be motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others."). The District Court should have given a punitive damages instruction, as Appellants requested.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.
