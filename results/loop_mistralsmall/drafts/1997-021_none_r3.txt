**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The Board’s rigid standard for polling undermines the Act’s core purpose of protecting employee free choice. *NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1946) ("The Board’s function is to provide a method by which the true desires of the employees may be ascertained."). By requiring employers to possess "objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt" before polling, *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987), the Board effectively renders polling meaningless when compared to withdrawal of recognition or RM elections under the same standard. This approach conflicts with the Act’s remedial purpose. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42 (1937) ("The Act’s policy is to encourage the practice and procedure of collective bargaining and to protect the exercise by workers of full freedom of association.").

The Board’s approach ignores the practical realities of labor relations. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 346 (1938) ("The Act does not prevent employers from refusing to bargain with a union that has lost majority support."). Employers face an untenable dilemma: either risk an unfair labor practice by withdrawing recognition without verification or conduct a poll that the Board has made prohibitively difficult. This standard frustrates the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships.

II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

The Board erred in discounting substantial evidence of waning union support. The Act does not require mathematical precision in proving a union’s loss of majority status. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 479 (1941) ("The Board is not required to establish its case by evidence which would be admissible in a court of law."). Allentown Mack presented significant evidence, including employee statements and a secret ballot poll showing 19 employees opposed to the union, that raised a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.

The Board improperly excluded probative employee statements. For example, the Board refused to credit statements made during job interviews where employees were told the company would remain non-union. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) ("The Board may consider circumstantial evidence and draw reasonable inferences from it."). Such statements, though made in a non-coercive context, were probative of employee sentiment. *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 584, 597 (1941) ("The Board’s findings are conclusive if supported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.").

The Board also erred in disregarding the poll results. The poll was conducted under strict procedural safeguards, supervised by a neutral third party, and yielded a clear anti-union majority. *NLRB v. Bradford Dyeing Ass’n*, 310 U.S. 318, 339 (1940) ("The Board may rely on circumstantial evidence and draw reasonable inferences from it."). The Board’s refusal to credit the poll undermines the Act’s goal of ascertaining true employee desires. *Waterman S.S. Corp.*, 309 U.S. at 226.

III. The Board’s standard is arbitrary and capricious.

The Board’s polling standard lacks a rational basis in the Act or its legislative history. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944) ("The Board’s construction of the Act is entitled to great weight."). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling can serve a legitimate purpose in verifying employee sentiment, yet it imposes a standard that renders polling meaningless. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. at 479 ("The Board’s findings must be upheld if supported by substantial evidence.").

The Board’s approach is inconsistent with prior judicial decisions upholding employer polls based on lesser evidence. *NLRB v. Pacific Gas & Elec. Co.*, 306 U.S. 100, 103 (1939) ("The Board’s order must be enforced if supported by substantial evidence."). The Board’s refusal to defer to its own precedent in this case is arbitrary and capricious. *NLRB v. Bell Aircraft Corp.*, 306 U.S. 233, 240 (1939) ("The Board’s construction of the Act is entitled to deference.").

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s polling standard is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
