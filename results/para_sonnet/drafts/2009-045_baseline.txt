# ARGUMENT

## THE LODESTAR MAY BE ENHANCED, IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASES SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE, TO ACCOUNT FOR SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS

### A. This Court Has Never Categorically Foreclosed Enhancement for Quality of Performance and Results Obtained

This Court's fee-shifting jurisprudence has consistently left room for enhancement of the lodestar in the rare case where the quality of representation and the results achieved exceed what the lodestar itself captures. In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), the Court explained that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee," and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435. That language was not incidental; it framed the standard this Court adopted the following Term.

In *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), the Court rejected an enhancement, but not because such enhancements are categorically unavailable. Rather, the Court held that the attorneys had failed to "offer[] specific evidence" showing that "the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.* at 899. By articulating a standard for when enhancement is warranted—specific evidence of superior service and exceptional success—the Court necessarily contemplated that such a showing could be made in an appropriate case. Had the Court intended a categorical bar, it would have said so; instead it described the circumstance as a "rare case" rather than a nonexistent one. *Id.*

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), confirms this reading. The Court reiterated that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). While the Court cautioned that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar," *id.* at 566 (emphasis added), the word "ordinarily" is the linchpin of the holding: it preserves the extraordinary case. The Court had every opportunity in *Delaware Valley I* to overrule *Blum* outright and adopt a categorical rule. It declined to do so, instead conducting a case-specific inquiry into whether the record before it satisfied the *Blum* standard. *Id.* at 566–68.

### B. Every Court of Appeals to Consider the Question Has Reached the Same Conclusion

The uniform view among the courts of appeals—including the decision below—confirms that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* preserve a narrow enhancement mechanism rather than abolish it. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all agree that a district court may enhance a fee award for exceptional performance and results when the record contains the specific evidentiary support *Blum* requires. *See Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004) (concluding that *Delaware Valley I* permits enhancements based on quality of representation and results obtained in rare and exceptional cases); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). This is not a case in which the courts of appeals are divided and this Court must choose a side; it is a case in which every court to consider the question has read this Court's precedent the same way. That unanimity is itself powerful evidence of the correct interpretation of *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*.

### C. The Record Here Satisfies the *Blum* Standard for a Rare and Exceptional Enhancement

The district court did not rely on generalized praise of counsel's diligence—the very showing *Blum* held insufficient. Rather, the court made detailed, case-specific findings that the "superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." The court further grounded its assessment in "its own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court's observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs' counsel throughout this long and difficult case." *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006). This is precisely the kind of "detailed findings by the lower court[]" that *Delaware Valley I* requires to support an enhancement. 478 U.S. at 565.

The results obtained were likewise exceptional—not merely favorable, but transformative for the class of foster children whose care had been the subject of protracted, complex, and hard-fought litigation. Where, as here, counsel's performance and the results achieved are shown by specific record evidence to exceed what the hourly rate alone could have produced, *Blum*'s standard is met, and the district court's considered judgment—reviewed only for abuse of discretion—is entitled to deference. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901.

### CONCLUSION

Because this Court's precedents preserve, rather than foreclose, enhancement for superior performance and exceptional results in rare cases supported by specific evidence, and because the district court's findings satisfy that standard, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
