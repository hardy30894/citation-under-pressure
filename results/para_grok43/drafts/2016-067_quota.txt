The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only “debt collector[s],” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), a term that excludes creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The Act’s text distinguishes the two roles by whether collection activity is undertaken “on behalf of another” or “for its own account.” Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1315-16 (11th Cir. 2015). Santander purchased the loans outright and thereafter sought to recover sums due to itself; it therefore acted as a creditor. Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013).

Petitioners urge that default status at acquisition converts any purchaser into a debt collector. That reading finds no support in the statutory language. The phrase “debts owed or due another” refers to debts owed to another at the time of collection, not at origination. Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318; see also Paroline v. United States, 134 S. Ct. 1710, 1721 (2014) (applying the “natural construction” rule of Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920)). Because Santander held the debts as its own when it communicated with petitioners, the “another” element is absent.

The exclusions in § 1692a(6)(F) do not alter this conclusion. Those clauses operate only after a person first satisfies one of the affirmative definitions of “debt collector.” Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1314. Petitioners’ reliance on Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355 (6th Cir. 2012), FTC v. Check Investors, Inc., 502 F.3d 159 (3d Cir. 2007), and Ruth v. Triumph P’ships, 577 F.3d 790 (7th Cir. 2009), therefore misplaces the analytical sequence. Those decisions address entities that continued to collect for third parties; none holds that a purchaser collecting solely for its own account becomes a debt collector merely because the debt was in default at acquisition.

Congress confirmed the distinction when it excluded from the definition of “debt collector” persons collecting debts “not in default at the time [they were] obtained.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). The exclusion protects loan servicers; it does not expand coverage to creditors. The legislative history likewise limits regulation to “third persons who regularly collect debts for others.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977). Santander’s purchase and subsequent collection for its own account fall outside that category.

Because the complaint alleges no facts showing that Santander was collecting debts “owed or due another” when it contacted petitioners, the complaint fails to state a claim under the Act. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
