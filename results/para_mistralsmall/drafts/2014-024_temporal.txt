**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Amendment’s “True Threat” Exception Requires Proof of the Defendant’s Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“[A] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger.”). But the Court has long recognized that words intended to place another in fear of bodily harm fall outside constitutional protection. *Cf. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942) (holding that “fighting words” are not protected by the First Amendment).

In *Giboney v. Empire Storage & Ice Co.*, 336 U.S. 490, 498 (1949), the Court reaffirmed that “[t]here are some activities which, although constituting an exercise of First Amendment rights, are nevertheless subject to regulation under the police power of the states.” The Court has never suggested that the government may punish speech as a “true threat” without regard to the speaker’s intent. To the contrary, in *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 376 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring), the Court explained that the government may not suppress speech unless the speaker intended to incite unlawful action.

This principle applies with equal force to threats. The Court has repeatedly held that criminal liability must be predicated on a culpable mental state. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 253 (1922) (“The principle that ignorance of the law is no excuse applies only where the law is such that the average citizen would know what conduct is proscribed.”). In *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952), the Court reiterated that “[t]he contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil.”

Thus, to sustain a conviction for a “true threat,” the government must prove that the defendant subjectively intended his words to be taken as a threat. *See United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 7 (1947) (“The existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception from, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence.”). This requirement is consistent with the First Amendment’s protection of speech, for it ensures that only those who deliberately seek to terrorize others are punished. *Cf. Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (“The offense known as breach of the peace embraces a great variety of conduct destroying or menacing public order and tranquility.”).

**II. The Government’s Objective Standard Fails to Protect Innocent Speech and Violates the First Amendment.**

The government contends that a reasonable person would interpret Elonis’s posts as threats, and that this objective standard suffices. But this approach risks punishing protected speech. As the Court cautioned in *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 537 (1945), “[t]o say that a state may not wholly bar the use of an idea in expressing one’s views is not to say that in every circumstance it must tolerate in advance the use of words which can be put to evil or illegitimate use.”

An objective standard would chill constitutionally protected expression. *See Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 98 (1940) (“This Court has recognized the importance of the right of free speech in the face of an attempted restriction by a state.”). The Court has long held that speech may not be suppressed based on the fears of those who hear it. *See Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (“It is settled that a statute which, by its terms, is so vague and indefinite as to permit the punishment of the fair use of this opportunity to speak is void on its face.”).

Moreover, an objective standard would permit conviction for satire, parody, or artistic expression that a reasonable person might misinterpret as a threat. *See Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507, 510 (1948) (“The test of obscenity is whether, to the average person, applying contemporary community standards, the dominant theme of the material taken as a whole appeals to prurient interest.”). The First Amendment does not permit such a sweeping restriction on speech. *See Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 273 (1951) (“The right to equal protection of the laws, in the exercise of those freedoms so fundamental to the maintenance of democratic institutions, has been settled by many decisions of this Court.”).

**III. The Court’s Precedent Requires Proof of Subjective Intent in Threat Prosecutions.**

In *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931), the Court held that a statute prohibiting the display of a red flag “as a sign, symbol or emblem of opposition to organized government” was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to require proof of intent. The same principle applies here. The government cannot punish speech as a “true threat” without proving that the speaker intended his words to be taken as a threat.

This Court has repeatedly required proof of subjective intent in analogous contexts. *See United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 152 (1938) (holding that a statute regulating the shipment of filled milk was constitutional only because it required proof of intent to misbrand). The government’s failure to prove Elonis’s subjective intent renders his conviction unconstitutional.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
