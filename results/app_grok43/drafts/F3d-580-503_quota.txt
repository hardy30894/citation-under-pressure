The district court abused its discretion by denying Winbush’s motion for a fingerprint expert and accompanying continuance. See Ungar v. Sarafite, 376 U.S. 575, 589 (1964); Morris v. Slappy, 461 U.S. 1, 11 (1983). A criminal defendant must be afforded “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense.” Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986); Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 324 (2006). This right encompasses access to expert assistance when such testimony is necessary to rebut the prosecution’s forensic evidence. Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 77 (1985); see also Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932).

Winbush sought an expert solely to explain the significance of the government’s own finding that no latent fingerprints of value existed on the recovered items. The government had noticed its own fingerprint and narcotics experts only weeks earlier, and the parties had stipulated to the absence of usable prints just days before Winbush’s request. Denying the motion on the eve of trial after more than two years of pendency foreclosed Winbush from testing the probative value of that forensic gap. See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993); Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137, 147 (1999).

The error was not harmless. Fingerprint testimony directly implicated the sole disputed element of the § 924(c) count and the identity of the distributor. See Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973); Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). Because the district court’s ruling deprived Winbush of the only means to challenge the government’s forensic narrative, the convictions and sentence must be vacated.
