**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit erred in dismissing Harold Wasek’s claim of hostile work environment harassment under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), and Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act (“EL-CRA”), MCL 37.2101 et seq., on the ground that Wasek could not prove the harassment was based on his gender. The record establishes that Wasek was subjected to a severe and pervasive pattern of sexually charged conduct—including unwelcome touching, lewd comments, and retaliatory acts—by his coworker Paul Ottobre, which created a hostile work environment. The employer’s failure to respond to repeated complaints, including the supervisors’ encouragement of physical violence, constitutes actionable failure to intervene. The district court’s conclusion that Wasek’s claim fails because he could not prove the harassment was gender-based misapplies the law and ignores the well-established principle that a plaintiff need not prove the harasser’s motive was gender-based to establish a violation of Title VII when the conduct is objectively sexual in nature.

**I. A Hostile Work Environment Based on Sex Requires Only That the Conduct Be Sexually Harassing, Not That the Harasser Be Motivated by Gender Bias**

The Supreme Court has made clear that Title VII prohibits not only discrimination based on gender but also harassment that is “so severe or pervasive as to create a work environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive.” Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986). The Court has further held that “the conduct must be severe or pervasive enough to create an objectively hostile or abusive work environment—an environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive.” Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). Critically, the Court has emphasized that “the victim’s subjective perception is not dispositive,” but rather “the environment must be one that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive.” Id. at 21–22.

In this case, the conduct was not merely offensive—it was sexually explicit and physically intrusive. Ottobre repeatedly touched Wasek’s buttocks, poked him with a hammer handle and a long sucker rod, and made sexually suggestive remarks such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” These acts were not isolated incidents but part of a sustained pattern of conduct. The Supreme Court has recognized that “sexual harassment may include unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature.” Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc., 523 U.S. 75, 78 (1998). The Court further held that “the conduct need not be motivated by sexual desire” to constitute harassment under Title VII. Id. at 80. “The critical issue is whether the conduct is sufficiently severe or pervasive to create a work environment that a reasonable person would find hostile or abusive.” Id.

Here, the conduct was not only sexual in nature but also targeted Wasek in a manner that was degrading and humiliating. The repeated touching and lewd comments, particularly in the context of a male-on-male relationship, created a work environment that a reasonable person would find abusive. The fact that Ottobre may have been bisexual does not negate the sexual character of the conduct. As the Court stated in Oncale, “the fact that the harasser and the victim are of the same sex does not bar a claim under Title VII.” Id. at 78. The same principle applies under EL-CRA, which prohibits “discrimination because of sex” and “harassment because of sex.” MCL 37.2103(1)(a).

**II. The Employer’s Failure to Respond Constitutes a Failure to Exercise Reasonable Care**

Even if Wasek could not prove that Ottobre’s conduct was motivated by gender bias, the employer’s response—or lack thereof—was legally significant. Under Title VII, an employer is liable for a hostile work environment created by a supervisor if the employer “knew or should have known” of the harassment and failed to take prompt and appropriate corrective action. Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998); Faragher v. City of Boca Raton, 524 U.S. 775, 807 (1998). The employer’s failure to act is not excused by the fact that the harassment was not gender-based; rather, the employer has a duty to prevent and correct harassment that is objectively sexual in nature.

Here, Wasek reported the harassment to his rig boss, Pat Tripp, who observed the conduct and laughed. Tripp advised Wasek not to “make waves” and warned that reporting to higher-ups would result in retaliation. When Wasek escalated the complaint to Dick Smillie, Smillie responded, “Why don’t you just kick [Ottobre’s] ass?” and later told Wasek to “duke it out.” When Wasek reported to Bill Strong, Strong chastised him for “whining” and again suggested physical violence. These responses were not merely inadequate—they were affirmatively hostile and encouraged retaliation. The Supreme Court has held that “an employer’s response that is so inadequate as to be tantamount to condonation of the harassment may constitute a failure to take prompt and effective remedial action.” Faragher, 524 U.S. at 807.

The Sixth Circuit’s conclusion that Wasek failed to state a claim because he could not prove gender-based animus ignores the employer’s duty to act. As the Court stated in Meritor, “the employer’s liability is not dependent on the harasser’s intent.” 477 U.S. at 67. The employer’s failure to respond to a clearly hostile environment—especially one involving sexual touching and lewd comments—constitutes a violation of Title VII and EL-CRA.
