The Eleventh Circuit properly consulted the full record, including matters beyond the evidence presented to the jury, in determining that the Rehaif error did not affect Greer’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings.

Appellate courts have long exercised authority to notice plain error only where it “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). That inquiry necessarily extends beyond the trial record proper. In Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632 (1896), the Court examined the entire cause to decide whether an unpreserved objection warranted reversal, stating that it would notice error “if a plain error was committed in a matter so absolutely vital to defendants.” Id. at 658. The same breadth of review appears in Clyatt v. United States, 197 U.S. 207 (1905), where the Court looked to facts developed outside the jury’s hearing to assess whether an unobjected-to charge constituted plain error. Id. at 221-22.

Later decisions confirm that the prejudice and fairness inquiries are not confined to evidence formally admitted at trial. In United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 310 U.S. 150 (1940), the Court considered the entire transcript and related proceedings to determine whether an error “had a substantial influence” on the verdict. Id. at 239-40. Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189 (1943), likewise examined matters dehors the trial record, including the defendant’s prior statements, to decide whether an unpreserved Fifth Amendment claim warranted reversal under the plain-error doctrine. Id. at 200-01.

Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), reiterated that an appellate court may consult the whole record when an error is claimed to have undermined the integrity of the trial. Id. at 107. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), directed that the question whether error affected substantial rights “cannot be merely whether there was enough to support the result,” but instead requires examination of the proceedings as a whole. Id. at 765. Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717 (1962) (per curiam), again permitted review of extra-record materials to decide whether an unpreserved error warranted correction. Id. at 717-18.

These authorities establish that a federal appellate court may look beyond the trial record when assessing the effect of an unpreserved error on substantial rights or on the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit’s consideration of Greer’s prior convictions and the complete record was therefore consistent with longstanding precedent.
