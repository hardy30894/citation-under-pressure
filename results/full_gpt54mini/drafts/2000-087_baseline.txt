### Argument

The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize the Attorney General to imprison a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) must be read, in light of the Constitution, to permit detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 689, 699, 701 (2001). The Court squarely held that the statute “does not permit indefinite detention” and that “once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute.” *Id.* at 699, 701.

That construction follows from the serious constitutional concerns posed by potentially permanent civil confinement. *Id.* at 690-91, 696-97. The Court emphasized that § 1231(a)(6) applies broadly to aliens removed for many reasons, not just to a narrow class of especially dangerous individuals. *Id.* at 696-97. Because the statute is susceptible to a limiting construction that avoids the “serious constitutional threat,” the Court adopted a “reasonable time” limitation. *Id.* at 689, 699. That interpretation controls here. See *Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council*, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988); *Rust v. Sullivan*, 500 U.S. 173, 190-91 (1991).

The Court also supplied the governing framework for habeas review. The habeas court must determine whether detention “exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701. If removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is “unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” *Id.* The Court further created a six-month presumptively reasonable period and held that, after that period, “once the alien provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing.” *Id.* at 701. That is the rule governing petitioner’s case.

Petitioner has satisfied that standard. He remained in custody well beyond six months after the removal period ended, and the Government failed to show a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Where removal cannot be carried out because no country will accept the alien, detention ceases to serve the statute’s basic purpose of securing the alien’s presence at the moment of removal. *Id.* at 701. The Court recognized that “the choice … is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large.’ It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” *Id.* at 700. Thus, the proper remedy is supervised release, not indefinite confinement. *Id.* at 701.

The Government may argue that detention remains permissible whenever it continues in good faith while removal efforts persist. But that was the standard the Court rejected. *Id.* at 701-02. The Court explained that such a rule would require the alien “to show the absence of any prospect of removal-no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable-which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” *Id.* at 702. The statute instead requires a showing tied to the “reasonably foreseeable future.” *Id.* at 701, 703. Once that showing is made, detention is unauthorized. *Id.* at 701.

Nor does petitioner’s criminal history change the statutory analysis. The Court acknowledged that the Government may consider “the risk of the alien’s committing further crimes” during the reasonable removal period. *Id.* at 701. But the Court did not permit criminality to justify detention after removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Id.* at 701-02. Nor did it carve out a terrorism or national-security exception here. *Id.* at 696-97. The Court’s holding applies to the ordinary removable alien and is not limited to any special category. *Id.*

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s pre-*Zadvydas* approach cannot stand. It had allowed detention so long as “good faith efforts” continued and removal had not been shown impossible. *Zadvydas v. Underdown*, 185 F.3d 279, 297 (5th Cir. 1999). The Supreme Court rejected that standard as too demanding of the alien and incompatible with the statute’s limiting construction. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 702. Petitioner therefore is entitled to habeas relief. See *Preiser v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 475, 484 (1973); *Rumsfeld v. Padilla*, 542 U.S. 426, 447 (2004) (recognizing habeas as the proper vehicle to challenge present physical custody).
