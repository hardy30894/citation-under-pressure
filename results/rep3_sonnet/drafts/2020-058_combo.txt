# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Deems TPS Recipients "Admitted" Nonimmigrants, and This Court Must Give That Deeming Clause Its Full Effect.

Section 1254a(f)(4) commands that a TPS recipient "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant." 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Nonimmigrant status, as a category, is a species of admission—every nonimmigrant classification in § 1101(a)(15) presupposes that the alien has been admitted in that classification. Congress's choice to place TPS recipients "in" that status, rather than merely exempting them from removal, reflects a considered decision to fold TPS into the ordinary admission framework of the INA, not to create some free-floating status untethered from admission.

This Court has long recognized that Congress may use statutory fictions to place an alien's immigration history on a particular legal footing regardless of the alien's actual physical circumstances. In Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590 (1953), the Court held that a lawfully resident alien returning from a brief voyage retained the legal status of one who had never left, notwithstanding his physical departure and return. And in Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), the Court confirmed that Congress's chosen statutory language—not the alien's physical presence within the territorial United States—controls whether an "entry" has occurred for purposes of the Act. These decisions establish the principle that admission, like entry, is a legal construct that Congress may define by statute; it need not track the physical act of crossing the border. Section 1254a(f)(4)'s deeming clause is precisely such a definition, and it should be read according to its terms.

A construction that strips § 1254a(f)(4) of any bearing on § 1255(a) also violates the settled rule that courts must "give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute." United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955). If the deeming clause does nothing to satisfy § 1255(a)'s threshold requirement, it becomes surplusage for the very population—unlawfully present aliens—for whom TPS was designed to matter most, since lawfully present nonimmigrants already occupy lawful status independent of § 1254a. Menasche forbids a reading that renders an entire clause "inoperative."

## II. To the Extent Any Ambiguity Remains, It Must Be Resolved in Petitioners' Favor Under This Court's Longstanding Canon of Liberal Construction in Immigration Cases.

Even if the text were thought ambiguous, this Court has for decades resolved doubts about the scope of relief provisions in the INA in favor of the alien. In Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948), the Court explained that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." The same interpretive humility applies with equal or greater force to a provision like § 1255 that determines whether a lawfully present TPS holder may ever attain permanent residence. See Barber v. Gonzales, 347 U.S. 637 (1954); Delgadillo v. Carmichael, 332 U.S. 388 (1947) (construing immigration statutes strictly against harsh consequences for the alien).

This Court reaffirmed the principle in Costello v. Immigration & Naturalization Service, 376 U.S. 120 (1964), and applied comparable reasoning to a remedial adjustment-related provision in Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Errico, 385 U.S. 214 (1966), where the Court construed an ambiguous immigration provision consistently with its remedial purpose of allowing families to remain together rather than adopting a cramped literalism that would defeat that purpose. TPS is no less remedial: Congress designed it to protect vulnerable aliens from returning to dangerous homelands. A construction of § 1255(a) that forecloses any path to permanent residence for TPS recipients who entered without inspection—even after Congress placed them in "lawful status" for years or decades—cuts directly against that remedial design and against the canon Errico applied.

## III. The Court of Appeals' Reading Produces the Kind of Absurd and Anomalous Result This Court Has Always Refused to Countenance.

This Court has never read statutory text so literally as to produce a result plainly at odds with the statute's evident design. In Church of the Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892), the Court explained that "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." And in United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486-87 (1868), the Court cautioned that "[a]ll laws should receive a sensible construction," lest "general terms" be applied so as to "lead to injustice, oppression, or an absurd consequence." See also United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (courts must look beyond literal words when a literal reading "would thwart the obvious purpose of the statute").

The Third Circuit's rule produces exactly that absurdity here. It grants TPS holders lawful nonimmigrant status for years, sometimes decades, while categorically denying them the one benefit—adjustment—that would allow that lawful status to mature into permanence, solely because of the manner of their original entry that Congress already legislated around through § 1254a. Petitioners respectfully submit that this Court should instead honor the plain design of § 1254a(f)(4) and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission for purposes of § 1255.
