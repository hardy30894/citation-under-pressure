**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause requires the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). A conspiracy is a continuing offense that endures only so long as the conspirators continue to act in concert for its purposes. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). Once a defendant withdraws by an affirmative act inconsistent with the conspiracy and communicated to his co-conspirators, he ceases to be a member. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). Continued membership during the limitations period is therefore an element the government must establish.

The court of appeals relieved the government of that burden by treating withdrawal as an affirmative defense. That treatment cannot be reconciled with *Winship*. The government must prove the existence of the charged conspiracy within the five-year period prescribed by 18 U.S.C. § 3282. When the evidence shows an earlier withdrawal, the government cannot satisfy its burden merely by proving the conspiracy’s earlier existence; it must also prove that the defendant remained a participant. *See Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 399 (1957) (government must show the conspiracy “was still in existence” and that the defendant “was still a member”).

Requiring the defendant to prove withdrawal would invert the constitutional allocation of the burden of persuasion. The government alone possesses the information necessary to establish the duration of each participant’s involvement. Placing that burden on the accused would compel him to disprove an element of the offense, contrary to *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 704 (1975).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
