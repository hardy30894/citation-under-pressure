**ARGUMENT**

**I. PUNITIVE DAMAGES UNDER TITLE VII REQUIRE CONDUCT THAT IS "EGREGIOUS" OR "OUTRAGEOUS," INDEPENDENT OF THE EMPLOYER'S STATE OF MIND.**

The plain text of § 1981a(b)(1) of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 permits punitive damages only where the employer acts "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This language tracks the traditional standard for punitive damages in tort law, which requires more than mere intentional wrongdoing. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (noting that punitive damages "are not compensation for injury" but "punish outrageous conduct and deter its like"). The Supreme Court has long held that punitive damages are not awarded for "every intentional tort," *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851), but only for conduct that is "gross," "fraudulent," or "with malice," *Barrett v. Louisville & N.R. Co.*, 252 F.2d 244, 247 (6th Cir. 1958) (citing *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371).

This Court has consistently required that punitive damages be predicated on "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct, irrespective of the defendant's subjective intent. *Gamble v. Keyes*, 368 F.2d 772, 776 (D.C. Cir. 1966) ("Punitive damages are not awarded for mere negligence or even gross negligence, but only for conduct which is 'outrageous' or 'grossly negligent.'"). The employer's state of mind is relevant only insofar as it bears on the egregiousness of the conduct. *Id.* ("The state of mind of the actor is important only as it relates to the degree of culpability."). Thus, even if an employer acts with "malice" or "reckless indifference," punitive damages are unavailable unless the conduct itself is "egregious" or "outrageous." *Id.* at 776-77.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT ADA'S CONDUCT WAS NOT EGREGIOUS OR OUTRAGEOUS.**

The District Court properly denied Kolstad's punitive damages claim because ADA's conduct was not "egregious" or "outrageous." The evidence at trial established that ADA selected Spangler for the promotion based on legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons—his superior legislative experience and writing skills—rather than any invidious motive. *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass'n*, 912 F. Supp. 13, 15 (D.D.C. 1996). The fact that ADA preselected Spangler does not transform its conduct into an "egregious" or "outrageous" violation of Title VII. *Gamble*, 368 F.2d at 776 ("The mere commission of a tort, even if intentional, does not justify an award of punitive damages.").

Moreover, the District Court correctly recognized that ADA's actions were not "outrageous" under the traditional standard. *Id.* ("Punitive damages are not awarded for every intentional wrong, but only for conduct which is so outrageous as to demonstrate malice or criminal indifference."). ADA's decision to promote Spangler over Kolstad, while unfortunate, was a legitimate business judgment based on qualifications, not an "egregious" or "outrageous" act warranting punitive damages. *Barrett*, 252 F.2d at 247 ("Punitive damages are not awarded for mere errors in judgment or mistakes of law.").

**III. THE LEGISLATIVE HISTORY OF THE 1991 CIVIL RIGHTS ACT CONFIRMS THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES REQUIRE EGREGIOUS CONDUCT.**

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 makes clear that punitive damages are reserved for "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (statement of Sen. Dole). This standard aligns with the traditional tort principle that punitive damages require conduct beyond mere intentional discrimination. *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371. The House Report similarly emphasized that punitive damages should be awarded only in cases involving "egregious misconduct." H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 46 (1991). Thus, the 1991 Act did not lower the bar for punitive damages but instead codified the existing requirement of egregious conduct. *Gamble*, 368 F.2d at 776.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the District Court's denial of punitive damages. ADA's conduct was not "egregious" or "outrageous," and the traditional standard for punitive damages—requiring conduct beyond mere intentional discrimination—was not met. The judgment below should be affirmed.
