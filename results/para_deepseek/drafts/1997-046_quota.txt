**ARGUMENT**

**I. Woodard Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Ohio’s Clemency Proceedings.**

This Court has long held that “pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981). Because clemency is an act of grace, not a right, no inmate possesses a constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process itself. *Id.* at 465. Ohio’s clemency scheme, which vests “almost unfettered discretion” in the Governor, *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520 (1994), creates no “expectation of [clemency] that rises to the level of a constitutionally protected liberty interest.” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465. The Sixth Circuit correctly concluded that Woodard has no such interest, and its partial remand on due process grounds was error.

**II. Clemency Is Not an Integral Part of the Adjudicatory Process, So Due Process Does Not Attach.**

The Sixth Circuit erroneously extended *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), which held that a state-created right to a first appeal as of right must comport with due process because it is “an integral part of the … system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant.” *Id.* at 393 (quoting *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956)). But clemency is fundamentally different. Unlike a direct appeal, clemency is “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. This Court has distinguished clemency from appellate proceedings, noting that “the Executive Branch, not the Judicial Branch, holds the power to grant clemency.” *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 280 (1998). The “great distance” between trial and clemency, *see Woodard*, 523 U.S. at 288 (O’Connor, J., concurring), precludes application of the *Evitts* framework. Indeed, this Court has never extended *Evitts* beyond the first appeal as of right, and has expressly limited the right to counsel to that stage. *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394 (citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). If habeas proceedings—which are far more closely tied to the adjudicatory process than clemency—do not trigger due process protections, *see Ross*, 417 U.S. at 610, clemency surely does not.

**III. The Voluntary Interview Option Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment protects against *compelled* self-incrimination. *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 426 (1984). Ohio’s clemency procedure does not compel any inmate to speak; it merely offers a voluntary interview. The regulation states that interview participation is “if the prisoner requests” it, and the prisoner “remains free to say as much or as little … as he wishes.” *See* Ohio Death Penalty Clemency Procedure, Part IV.C.2. There is no penalty for silence, and no adverse inference may be drawn from a refusal to participate. *Cf. Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976) (holding that adverse inference from silence in prison disciplinary hearing does not violate Fifth Amendment when no compulsion to testify). Woodard’s claim that the interview presents a “Hobson’s choice” is speculative; he has not been required to waive any rights. As this Court stated in *Baxter*, “it would be premature” for a court to adjudicate a Fifth Amendment claim based on “facts that may never materialize.” *Id.* at 324. Moreover, any statements Woodard might volunteer could be used only in future proceedings, if at all, and the risk of self-incrimination is “purely academic at this point.” *Woodard*, 523 U.S. at 290 (Rehnquist, C.J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). The Fifth Amendment does not require states to forego offering inmates an opportunity to speak in their own behalf. *See Murphy*, 465 U.S. at 429.

**IV. This Court Should Reject the Sixth Circuit’s Novel Due Process Approach.**

No other federal court has constitutionalized clemency proceedings. To do so would create “disincentives for States to codify [clemency] procedures in the interest of uniform treatment.” *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 482 (1995) (discussing similar disincentives in prison context). Ohio’s clemency process is a matter of executive grace, not judicial oversight. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. The Sixth Circuit’s remand for a determination of “minimal” due process protections is both unprecedented and unworkable, inviting endless litigation over a process that this Court has consistently held to be nonjusticiable. *See Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979) (“[T]here is no constitutional or inherent right of a convicted person to be conditionally released before the expiration of a valid sentence.”). The judgment of the district court should be affirmed in full.
