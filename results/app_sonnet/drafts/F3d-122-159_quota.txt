# ARGUMENT

Victor Roussos's drug-trafficking conviction under 21 U.S.C. § 846 does not become a "crime of violence" merely because the sentencing court applied a two-level firearm enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1. The Bureau of Prisons' Program Statement 5162.02, which converts that enhancement into a categorical bar to early-release eligibility under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), conflicts with the statute's plain text and with the settled method for identifying "crimes of violence." This Court should reverse.

## I. The Categorical Approach Governs Whether an Offense Is a "Crime of Violence."

The definition incorporated by 28 C.F.R. § 550.58—18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)—asks whether the offense "has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force," or "by its nature, involves a substantial risk that physical force ... may be used in the course of committing the offense." 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). In *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575 (1990), the Supreme Court held that whether a prior conviction qualifies as a predicate violent offense under a nearly identical statutory structure must be determined by reference to the statutory elements of the offense of conviction, not by resort to the particular facts underlying the defendant's conduct. The Court adopted this "categorical approach" precisely to avoid the case-by-case factual inquiries that the BOP's Program Statement now demands. *Id.* at 600-02.

Section 846 conspiracy has no element requiring the use, attempted use, or threatened use of force against any person or property. Roussos pled guilty to conspiring to distribute cocaine; nothing in that offense's elements involves violence. The BOP nonetheless looks past the elements to the sentencing court's separate, guideline-driven finding regarding a firearm discovered at a vacation residence unconnected to the arrest. That is the exact inquiry *Taylor* forbids. The Program Statement thus does not interpret § 924(c)(3)—it rewrites it, substituting a relevant-conduct inquiry for an elements-based one.

## II. The Program Statement Conflicts With the Plain Text of the Governing Statutes.

An agency "must give effect to the unambiguously expressed intent of Congress." *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984). Congress defined "crime of violence" in terms of offense elements and inherent risk of force "in the course of committing the offense," 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3)(B)—not in terms of ancillary sentencing enhancements imposed for relevant conduct. An agency may not, under the guise of interpretation, expand a statute beyond what Congress authorized. *See Board of Governors of the Fed. Reserve Sys. v. Dimension Fin. Corp.*, 474 U.S. 361, 368 (1986) (agency interpretation cannot be sustained where it is "inconsistent with the statutory mandate"); *MCI Telecommunications Corp. v. American Tel. & Tel. Co.*, 512 U.S. 218, 229 (1994) (an agency's authority to "modify" or "adapt" a statutory term does not permit it to make "unreasonable" or "revolutionary" alterations of statutory meaning). Here, the Program Statement transforms every § 846 drug offender whose sentence happens to include a weapon enhancement into a "violent" offender, even though Congress in § 3621(e)(2)(B) singled out drug offenders for early-release incentives precisely because Congress viewed successful treatment as warranting reward for nonviolent offenders. That congressional purpose cannot be squared with an agency rule that recharacterizes garden-variety drug conspiracies as violent crimes based on facts entirely collateral to the offense of conviction.

## III. Even Under Chevron Step Two, the Program Statement Is Entitled to Reduced Deference and Is Unreasonable.

Because Program Statement 5162.02 is an internal agency guideline issued without notice-and-comment rulemaking, it is "akin to an interpretive rule" and is not entitled to full *Chevron* deference. *Reno v. Koray*, 515 U.S. 50, 61 (1995). It is entitled only to deference commensurate with its persuasiveness. The Program Statement's equation of firearm *possession* with a "substantial risk" that force "may be used" cannot survive that more searching review. In *Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137, 143 (1995), the Supreme Court held that "use" of a firearm requires "active employment," and rejected the notion that mere presence or possession of a weapon suffices to establish use or threatened use of force. If mere possession does not constitute "use" for purposes of a substantive firearm offense, it is unreasonable for the BOP to treat a sentencing enhancement for possession—imposed here for a firearm found in a vacation home, wholly unconnected to Roussos's arrest—as conclusive proof that his offense "by its nature" involves a substantial risk of force. See also *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36, 45 (1993) (agency and Commission interpretations must be consistent with, not contrary to, the text they construe). Sentencing enhancements reflect risk-based policy judgments about relevant conduct, not findings that violence was integral to the offense; conflating the two, as the Program Statement does, is neither a reasonable nor a permissible construction. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 446-48 (1987) (agency interpretations must accord with ordinary principles of statutory construction).

For these reasons, Roussos's § 846 conviction is not, and cannot be deemed, a "crime of violence," and the judgment below denying habeas relief should be reversed.
