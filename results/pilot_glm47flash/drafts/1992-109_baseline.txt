**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATE COURT APPLIED THE WRONG LEGAL STANDARD TO DETERMINE MORAN’S COMPETENCY.**

A defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if he has the capacity for “reasoned choice” among the alternatives available to him. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987). This heightened standard is distinct from the constitutional requirement for competency to stand trial, which merely demands that a defendant possesses a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings and is capable of assisting counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904 (1975); *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 387, 86 S.Ct. 836, 843 (1966); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788 (1960). Because Moran’s plea and waiver of counsel involved the relinquishment of fundamental constitutional rights, the state court was required to apply the “reasoned choice” standard, not the lower competency-to-stand-trial standard.

The state court erred by applying the wrong standard in its post-conviction order. The court concluded that Moran was competent to waive counsel because two psychiatrists had declared him competent to stand trial. *See* Nev. Rev. Stat. § 176.045. However, the psychiatrists were appointed solely to evaluate Moran’s competency to stand trial, not his capacity to make a reasoned choice regarding the waiver of his rights. The state court’s reliance on these evaluations to determine competency to waive counsel is legally insufficient. *See* *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247, 1249 (9th Cir. 1978). The state court failed to articulate the correct standard for competency to waive rights, and by relying on the wrong standard, it reached an erroneous conclusion.

**II. THE RECORD DOES NOT SUPPORT A FINDING THAT MORAN POSSESSED THE CAPACITY FOR REASONED CHOICE.**

When the record is examined under the correct legal standard, it does not support a finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights. Moran’s plea and waiver were made while he was heavily medicated with four different drugs, including Inderal, Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Vistaril. The record shows that Moran’s responses to the court’s questions were monosyllabic and unresponsive. Furthermore, Moran had attempted suicide only a few months prior to the plea hearing. These factors constitute substantial evidence of incompetence that should have triggered a good faith doubt requiring a competency hearing. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856.

The psychiatric evaluations relied upon by the state court are inadequate to demonstrate Moran’s capacity for reasoned choice. Dr. O’Gorman’s report focused on Moran’s ability to assist counsel and understand the proceedings, which are relevant to competency to stand trial but insufficient for determining competency to waive rights. *See* *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 171. Dr. O’Gorman’s report also failed to address the effects of Moran’s medication on his decision-making capacity. Similarly, Dr. Jurasky’s report offered no additional evidence regarding Moran’s competency to make a reasoned choice. Because the state court applied the wrong legal standard and the record does not support a finding of competency under the correct standard, Moran’s waiver of counsel and guilty pleas were invalid.
