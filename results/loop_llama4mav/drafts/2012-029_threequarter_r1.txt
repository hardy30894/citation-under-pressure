The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case. The Supreme Court has established that federal jurisdiction extends to cases "in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, in that patent law is a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims." Although Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp. is not available, earlier cases provide guidance. The Court has long recognized that a case "arises under" federal law when it involves a substantial question of federal law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561 (1912). In Shulthis, the Court stated that a suit "arises under the law that creates the cause of action" and that the federal question must be "really and substantially" involved in the controversy. Id. at 569. Here, Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, specifically the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar.

The Court has also held that a case "arises under" federal law when it involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law, upon the determination of which the result depends. Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569. Minton's malpractice claim meets this standard, as the outcome depends on the resolution of the experimental use exception.

In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court cautioned against an overly broad definition of "arising under" jurisdiction. The Court instead adopted a more nuanced approach, considering whether the federal question is "necessary to the proof of the plaintiff's case." Id. at 112. Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law that is necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

The Court has also considered the importance of uniformity in the application of federal patent law. In Wheaton v. Peters, 33 U.S. 591 (1834), the Court noted the significance of federal patent laws. Id. at 658. Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that federal jurisdiction is exclusive in cases involving patent law. In Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), the Court held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents. Id. at 259. This principle is consistent with the idea that federal law is supreme in matters involving patent law.

In addition, the Court has stated that when a case involves a federal question, it is not necessary for the federal issue to be the primary issue in the case for federal jurisdiction to apply. In Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), the Court held that federal jurisdiction is proper when a case involves a federal question. Id. at 822.

Other cases also support the proposition that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over patent law cases. See, e.g., Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852); Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99 (1850). These cases demonstrate that the federal courts have long been recognized as the proper forum for resolving disputes involving patent law.

In conclusion, based on the principles established in Shulthis, Gully, Wheaton, Pratt, Osborn, Bloomer, and Wilson, it is clear that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim, which necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law.
