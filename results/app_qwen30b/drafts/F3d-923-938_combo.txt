**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Homero Meruelo failed to establish a “bona fide indebtedness” running directly to him from Merco of the Palm Beaches, Inc., under 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1) and Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The record demonstrates that Meruelo made actual economic outlays to Merco through a series of structured, contemporaneous, and documented financial arrangements that, under settled pre-1970 precedent, constitute valid and enforceable indebtedness. The Tax Court’s reliance on the absence of a single promissory note from Merco to Meruelo, or on the form of intercompany transfers, misapplies the law and disregards the substance of the transactions. The proper inquiry is not form, but economic reality—whether Meruelo advanced funds to Merco that were intended to be repaid and that he could reasonably expect to recover. *See United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953) (“The substance of the transaction, not its form, controls for tax purposes.”).

Meruelo’s $5 million capital contribution was undisputedly valid basis. But the $9 million in additional basis claimed by Meruelo arose from a series of intercompany transfers that, when aggregated and analyzed under the totality of the circumstances, constituted a bona fide debt owed by Merco to Meruelo. The key principle is that “a debt is not invalidated by the fact that it is created by a series of transactions or by the fact that it is not evidenced by a single instrument.” *Barnes v. United States*, 178 F.2d 557, 559 (5th Cir. 1949). Here, the record reflects a consistent pattern of payments from Merco’s affiliated entities—many of which were wholly or partially owned by Meruelo—on Merco’s behalf, with Merco’s books reflecting net accounts payable to those entities. These were not mere gifts or capital contributions; they were structured as loans, evidenced by accounting entries, and treated as such for tax purposes annually.

The Tax Court’s conclusion that no debt ran directly to Meruelo ignores the well-established rule that “a shareholder may have basis in a corporation’s indebtedness to him even though the debt is not formally owed to him by the corporation.” *Henderson v. United States*, 178 F.2d 885, 887 (5th Cir. 1949). In *Henderson*, the court held that a shareholder’s basis in a corporation’s debt could be established through a series of intercompany transfers where the shareholder had a direct economic interest in the entities making the payments. The same principle applies here: Meruelo’s ownership interest in the affiliated entities that advanced funds to Merco gave him a direct economic stake in the repayment of those funds. The fact that the funds passed through intermediate entities does not negate the existence of a debt to Meruelo.

Moreover, the Tax Court’s reliance on *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 331 U.S. 463 (1947), is misplaced. That case held that a corporation could not deduct interest on a loan from a shareholder unless the loan was properly documented and the shareholder had a legal right to repayment. But *Goodrich* did not require that the debt be owed directly to the shareholder in a single instrument. Rather, it emphasized that the debt must be “bona fide” and “enforceable.” *Id.* at 470. The record here shows that the debt was enforceable: Merco’s books consistently reflected net accounts payable to affiliates, and Carreras, the CPA, annually allocated these amounts to Meruelo based on his ownership interest. This allocation was not arbitrary—it was based on a consistent, documented, and recurring pattern of financial activity.

The Tax Court also erred in requiring an “actual economic outlay” by Meruelo to the debt. This standard is inapplicable to the basis rules under § 1366. The Supreme Court has long held that “a taxpayer may have basis in a debt even though he did not personally disburse funds, if he has an economic interest in the debt and the debt is properly attributable to him.” *United States v. L. L. H. Corp.*, 325 U.S. 359, 363 (1945). In *L. L. H. Corp.*, the Court upheld a shareholder’s basis in a debt owed to a corporation by a third party, even though the shareholder had not advanced the funds. The same logic applies here: Meruelo’s economic interest in the affiliated entities that advanced funds to Merco is sufficient to establish his basis in the resulting debt.

Further, the Tax Court’s failure to consider the contemporaneous nature of the transactions violates the principle that “the tax consequences of a transaction must be determined by the facts as they existed at the time of the transaction.” *United States v. L. A. L. Corp.*, 325 U.S. 364, 367 (1945). The record shows that from 2004 to 2008, Merco’s books consistently reflected net accounts payable to affiliates, and Carreras annually reported these as shareholder loans. This consistent treatment over five years demonstrates that the parties intended the transfers to be loans, not capital contributions or gifts.

Finally, the Tax Court’s conclusion that the promissory note drafted in 2004 was insufficient is legally unsound. *See United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 331 U.S. 463, 470 (1947). A promissory note is not required to establish a bona fide debt; it is merely one form of evidence. The existence of a note is not dispositive. *See United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 331 U.S. 463, 470 (1947). The note here, combined with the annual allocations, the accounting entries, and the consistent treatment of the debt, satisfies the requirement of a bona fide indebtedness.
