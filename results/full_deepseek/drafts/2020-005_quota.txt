**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). The “relate to” prong is disjunctive and does not require a causal showing that the defendant’s forum contacts proximately caused the plaintiff’s injury. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 141 S. Ct. 1017, 1026 (2021). Where, as here, the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum, and the plaintiff’s claims substantially connect to those activities, specific jurisdiction is proper.

**I. Specific Jurisdiction Requires a Connection Between the Forum and the Claims, Not Strict Causation**

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction is “minimum contacts” such that the suit “does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). The defendant must have “purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958), and the litigation must “arise out of or relate to” those contacts. *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, 582 U.S. at 262. This standard is “not synonymous with [a] but-for” test; a “strong relationship” between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s claims suffices. *Ford Motor Co.*, 141 S. Ct. at 1026.

In *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), this Court held that a defendant’s contacts must be such that it “should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there.” The defendant’s “conduct and connection with the forum State” must be such that it “should reasonably anticipate” litigation in that forum. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 474 (1985). A defendant that “delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State” may be subject to jurisdiction there. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 298. *See also Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984) (jurisdiction proper where defendant circulates magazines in the forum, even if plaintiff’s injury occurred elsewhere).

**II. Ford’s Extensive Contacts with Montana Establish a Clear Relationship to the Claims**

Ford does not dispute that it has “purposefully avail[ed] itself” of the Montana market. It advertises, sells vehicles and parts, and provides warranty services in Montana. The vehicle at issue was driven in Montana for years, and the tire failure occurred on a Montana highway. Ford’s contacts with Montana are not “random, isolated, or fortuitous.” *Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 774. Rather, Ford “systematically” serves Montana consumers, and its “economic presence” in the state is substantial. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 473.

The claims “relate to” Ford’s Montana contacts. The plaintiff alleges that the Ford Explorer was defectively designed and lacked adequate warnings. Ford’s Montana activities—including advertising the safety of its vehicles and selling replacement tires and parts—directly relate to the alleged defects. As this Court held in *Ford Motor Co.*, a “relationship” exists when “the defendant’s contacts with the forum are intimately connected with the plaintiff’s claims, even if the defendant did not cause the injury in the forum.” 141 S. Ct. at 1028. Here, Ford’s Montana contacts “gave rise to the very product that caused the injury.” *J. McIntyre Machinery, Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 891 (2011) (Breyer, J., concurring).

**III. The “Arise Out of or Relate To” Standard Does Not Require But-For Causation**

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction requires a direct causal link between its forum contacts and the plaintiff’s injury is unsupported by precedent. In *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, this Court stated that specific jurisdiction requires “a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue,” not that the claims “arise from” the defendant’s forum contacts in a “but for” sense. 582 U.S. at 265. *See also Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014) (defendant’s contacts with the forum must be “the defendant’s own choice” and “not the unilateral activity of the plaintiff”). Ford chose to conduct business in Montana, and the plaintiff’s claims relate to that business.

The “relate to” standard is “broad” and “contemplates that some relationships will support jurisdiction without a causal showing.” *Ford Motor Co.*, 141 S. Ct. at 1026. In *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 112 (1987), the Court recognized that a defendant that “regularly and extensively” serves a market may be subject to jurisdiction even if the defendant’s product reached the forum through a chain of distribution. Here, Ford’s nationwide marketing and its specific Montana activities—including the sale of replacement parts for the same model vehicle—demonstrate that it “should reasonably anticipate being haled into court” in Montana. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297.

**IV. Exercising Jurisdiction Over Ford Comports with Fair Play and Substantial Justice**

Even if minimum contacts exist, courts must consider whether jurisdiction comports with “fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. Ford is a global company with extensive resources and the ability to defend itself in Montana. The state has a “manifest interest in providing its residents with a convenient forum for redressing injuries inflicted by out-of-state actors.” *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 473. Montana’s interest is especially strong here because the injury occurred in the state and the plaintiff is a Montana resident. *See Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 776 (forum’s interest in protecting its citizens is a relevant factor).

In sum, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s contacts with the state—advertising, selling parts, and servicing vehicles—create a “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” *Bristol-Myers Squibb*, 582 U.S. at 265. The Due Process Clause does not require a showing that the defendant’s forum contacts caused the plaintiff’s injury. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
