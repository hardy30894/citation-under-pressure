**Argument**

The Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act should not be construed to insulate Pakistan from suit where it allegedly took private land, put it to use for military housing, and later attempted to resolve the matter by offering substitute property through governmental channels. The district court treated that conduct as a sovereign expropriation outside the commercial-activity exception. That reading is too narrow. When a sovereign steps into the marketplace, bargains over property, and undertakes conduct ordinarily performed by private actors, it acts not as ruler but as participant. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a sovereign may be held to the same legal consequences as a private litigant when it engages in proprietary conduct. *Bank of the United States v. Planters’ Bank of Georgia*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 904, 907–09 (1824). Likewise, the Court has distinguished public acts from those that “might be done by a private man,” holding that the latter may be treated as commercial or private in character. *The King of the Two Sicilies v. Willcox*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 12, 28–29 (1845).

I. **Pakistan’s alleged conduct was commercial in substance, because it concerned the acquisition, use, and attempted exchange of property.**

At common law, ownership and transfer of land are quintessentially proprietary dealings. A sovereign that acquires or disposes of property does not automatically act in its public capacity. *Bank of the United States v. Planters’ Bank of Georgia*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) at 907–09. The essential question is not whether the actor is a sovereign, but whether the conduct is the kind that private parties may undertake. *The King of the Two Sicilies v. Willcox*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) at 28–29. Here, Beg alleges that Pakistan took his land, used it for military housing, and then, through a representative sent to the United States, proposed an exchange for substitute land. Those allegations describe property transactions, not the formulation of law or adjudication of public rights.

The fact that the initial taking was accomplished by a government does not end the analysis. Where the government’s conduct involves the management, transfer, or exchange of property, courts have historically treated the activity as proprietary rather than purely sovereign. *Bank of the United States v. Planters’ Bank of Georgia*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) at 907–09. And where the sovereign’s acts resemble those of private parties, the law does not permit immunity to be invoked merely because the actor wears the crown. *The King of the Two Sicilies v. Willcox*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) at 28–29.

II. **The district court erred by focusing on the sovereign character of the actor rather than the commercial character of the conduct.**

Immunity doctrine has never turned solely on the identity of the defendant. The Supreme Court repeatedly held that foreign sovereigns may be subject to judicial process when they engage in private-law conduct. *Bank of the United States v. Planters’ Bank of Georgia*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) at 907–09; *The King of the Two Sicilies v. Willcox*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) at 28–29. That principle matters here because Pakistan’s alleged conduct did not consist only of a public act of expropriation. Beg further alleges post-taking negotiations, a proposed land swap, and a failed attempt by the provincial government to deliver a parcel in exchange for the original property. Those are the sorts of dealings private landowners conduct every day.

Nor can the immunity analysis depend on the label “military” attached to the eventual use of the land. A private party may buy land for housing, lease it, improve it, or exchange it. The fact that the user is an army does not transform the underlying property transaction into a noncommercial act. Under the settled common-law distinction, the relevant inquiry is whether the challenged transaction is of the kind “which a private person might do.” *The King of the Two Sicilies v. Willcox*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) at 28–29. Beg’s allegations meet that standard.

III. **At minimum, Beg’s allegations state a sufficient basis for jurisdictional discovery and further factual development.**

Even if the record is incomplete, dismissal at the threshold was premature. Jurisdictional immunity should not be upheld where the complaint plausibly alleges proprietary conduct by the sovereign and post-taking transactional dealings with the plaintiff. The Supreme Court has recognized that where a sovereign voluntarily enters private-law arrangements, it subjects itself to the ordinary incidents of litigation. *Bank of the United States v. Planters’ Bank of Georgia*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) at 907–09. Beg alleges enough to require a fuller examination of what Pakistan did with the land, who controlled the substitute parcel, and whether the government’s offer and attempted exchange constituted a commercial disposition of property.

Because Pakistan’s conduct, as pleaded, involved the taking, use, and proposed exchange of land—activities analogous to those of private proprietors—the commercial-activity exception should apply. The district court’s contrary conclusion improperly elevated sovereign status over sovereign conduct. This Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings.
