**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Habeas Relief Because the Prosecutor’s Testimony and Continued Prosecution Violated Riddle’s Due Process Rights**

A criminal defendant is entitled to a fair trial before a disinterested prosecutor. *See Marshall v. Jerrico, Inc.*, 446 U.S. 238, 249 (1980) (prosecutor must serve “the public interest” rather than a “personal or private” interest). When a prosecutor becomes a witness, he places his own credibility in issue, and his continued role as advocate creates an intolerable conflict that deprives the defendant of due process. *See United States v. Franco*, 136 F.3d 95, 101 (5th Cir. 1997) (prosecutor’s dual role as witness and advocate “undermines the fairness of the proceeding”).

Here, District Attorney Danny Hill testified to rebut Riddle’s claim that Hill threatened to seek the death penalty because Riddle had escaped. Hill’s testimony directly concerned his own conduct, making him the central witness against Riddle on that issue. He then resumed prosecution, and his assistant used Hill’s testimony to argue that Riddle was a liar. This arrangement violated the ethical rules governing prosecutors, which prohibit a lawyer from serving as both advocate and necessary witness. *See* Tex. Disciplinary R. Prof. Conduct 3.08(a) (lawyer shall not accept or continue employment when the lawyer knows or believes that the lawyer may be called as a witness on a contested issue). More fundamentally, it created an appearance of impropriety and a risk that the jury would credit the prosecutor-witness over the defendant. *See Young v. United States*, 481 U.S. 787, 814 (1987) (prosecutor’s conduct must be viewed with “particular care” because of the prosecutor’s unique role).

The State may argue that no actual prejudice was shown, but the prosecutor’s conflict here is the kind of structural error that defies harmless-error analysis. *See Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 309–10 (1991) (structural errors affect the framework of the trial). At minimum, the district court was required to hold an evidentiary hearing to determine the effect of Hill’s testimony on the jury’s credibility determinations. Its failure to do so was an abuse of discretion. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (hearing required where facts are in dispute and resolution may affect the outcome).

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying Riddle’s Request for an Evidentiary Hearing on His Claim of Prosecutorial Misconduct and Actual Innocence**

Riddle’s habeas petition and pro se motion for an evidentiary hearing raised material factual disputes regarding the prosecutor’s threats, the voluntariness of Riddle’s statements, and the circumstances of the alleged consent to enter Bennett’s home. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(e)(2), a federal court may hold an evidentiary hearing when a habeas petitioner’s claim relies on facts that are not reasonably developed in the state court record. *See Williams v. Taylor*, 529 U.S. 420, 432–33 (2000) (hearing required where petitioner “diligently” sought to develop the factual basis of his claims). Riddle was diligent: he sought appointment of counsel, filed motions for an evidentiary hearing, and presented his claims to the state court. The district court nonetheless denied the hearing and granted summary judgment to the State without addressing the disputed facts.

The district court’s summary dismissal was especially improper because Riddle’s allegations, if true, would establish that the prosecutor’s threat of the death penalty was designed to coerce a guilty plea or induce fear that tainted Riddle’s trial testimony. *See Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963) (suppression of material evidence violates due process). Moreover, Bybee’s testimony—that Riddle struck Bennett after death to test Bybee’s “stomach”—directly contradicts Riddle’s claim of a heat-of-passion response to sexual assault. Resolving this conflict required a credibility determination that only an evidentiary hearing could provide. *See Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 312.

Finally, the district court’s grant of summary judgment was improper because habeas petitions are not ordinarily resolved on summary judgment without a hearing. *See Rule 8(a), Rules Governing Section 2254 Cases* (requiring hearing if petition raises a material factual dispute). The court’s failure to comply with this rule was reversible error.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court erred by (1) denying habeas relief despite the prosecutor’s unconstitutional dual role as witness and advocate, and (2) refusing to hold an evidentiary hearing on Riddle’s claims of prosecutorial misconduct and disputed facts. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to grant an evidentiary hearing or, in the alternative, to vacate the conviction and sentence.
