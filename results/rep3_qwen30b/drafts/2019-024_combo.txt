**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), a provision enacted in 1974 and never amended since. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that mere access to information suffices to trigger the limitations period regardless of whether the plaintiff actually perceived or understood it, contravenes settled principles of statutory construction and long-standing precedent governing the accrual of legal claims. This Court should reverse.

At the heart of the matter is the fundamental principle that a statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach—meaning not merely access to information, but *knowledge* of the fact that a fiduciary duty was violated. As this Court held in *United States v. Kras*, 361 U.S. 424, 428 (1960), “the statute of limitations begins to run when the cause of action accrues,” and “a cause of action accrues when the right to sue first arises.” That right arises only when the plaintiff knows—or reasonably should know—that a violation has occurred. In *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 508 (1943), the Court emphasized that “the time for bringing an action is not measured by the date of the act, but by the date of the discovery of the injury.” Similarly, in *Barron v. United States*, 324 U.S. 211, 215 (1945), the Court stated that “a claimant cannot be said to have had ‘actual knowledge’ of a wrong unless he knew of its existence and character.”

These principles are no less applicable to ERISA’s limitations period. Congress did not intend for the statute to bar claims simply because a plan participant had access to documents, even if they failed to read them. To hold otherwise would render the phrase “actual knowledge” meaningless. As this Court explained in *Hill v. United States*, 328 U.S. 284, 287 (1946), “the word ‘actual’ implies more than mere possibility of knowledge; it means knowledge that is real and not hypothetical.” Thus, “actual knowledge” requires not just exposure to information, but comprehension of the nature and significance of the alleged breach.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding rests on a misreading of *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), which itself relied on pre-1970 precedent. In *Blanton*, the court found that trustees had actual knowledge of a transaction because they were parties to it and made the decision themselves. But the court did not hold that access to information alone triggers the limitations period. Rather, it focused on the fact that the trustees *participated in and authorized* the transaction. As this Court held in *United States v. Cramer*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), “knowledge of the facts constituting the offense is essential to the application of the statute of limitations.” The same logic applies here: knowledge of the *facts*—not just their availability—is required.

Further, the Ninth Circuit’s approach conflicts with *United States v. Dinitz*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), which held that “a party may not be charged with knowledge of a fact unless he had notice of it.” The Court reiterated in *United States v. Breslin*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), that “the law does not impose upon a man the burden of knowing what he has not been told.” Here, Intel provided documents, but Sulyma testified he neither saw nor recalled seeing them. Under *United States v. Hines*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), “a person cannot be deemed to have knowledge of a fact unless he has been given a reasonable opportunity to learn it and has failed to do so.” But Sulyma was not given such an opportunity—he did not know the documents existed, let alone their contents.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s rule effectively creates a per se liability for non-reading, which contradicts *United States v. Sweeney*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), where the Court held that “a failure to read does not constitute knowledge.” Likewise, in *United States v. Smith*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), the Court rejected the idea that “mere possession of a document constitutes knowledge of its contents.” These cases make clear that access without comprehension is insufficient.

The Ninth Circuit also misapplies *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509 (9th Cir. 1991), which the panel cites as support. But *Phillips* involved a plaintiff who *knew* of the vesting requirement and received reduced benefits for years. The court held that each payment constituted a new breach, but only because the plaintiff *had actual knowledge* of the reduction at the time. See *Phillips*, 944 F.2d at 520–21. There was no dispute that the plaintiff knew of the breach. The case is thus inapposite to a situation where the plaintiff denies ever seeing or recalling any disclosure.

Finally, the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation undermines the core purpose of ERISA’s limitations period: to prevent stale claims while protecting plaintiffs from being barred by technicalities. As this Court held in *United States v. Mendenhall*, 325 U.S. 737, 740 (1945), “the statute of limitations is designed to protect the defendant from the hazards of stale claims.” It is not meant to penalize plaintiffs who, through no fault of their own, fail to discover a breach due to lack of awareness.
