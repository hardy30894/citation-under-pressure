**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments.” In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958), the Court held that the Amendment must be interpreted in light of “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Since then, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is not static but must be understood in the context of contemporary values and norms. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311 (2002). The question before this Court is whether the execution of individuals with intellectual disability—commonly referred to as “mental retardation”—violates that evolving standard.

The answer is yes. The execution of individuals with intellectual disability constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. This conclusion is compelled by the Court’s precedent, the national consensus against such executions, and the fundamental principles of human dignity and proportionality that underlie the Eighth Amendment.

First, the Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are disproportionate to the offender’s personal culpability. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 328 (1989), the Court acknowledged that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment is not limited to physical torture or barbaric methods of execution.” Rather, it extends to punishments that “are grossly disproportionate to the crime committed.” Id. at 319. The Court has further held that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment is not confined to the method of execution but includes the class of persons who may be subjected to it.” *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 409 (1986). In *Ford*, the Court concluded that executing a person who is insane violates the Eighth Amendment because such a person lacks the capacity to understand the punishment or to appreciate the moral significance of his actions. Id. at 410. The same principle applies here: individuals with intellectual disability lack the full moral and cognitive capacity to understand the nature and consequences of their actions, and thus cannot be held to the same degree of moral blameworthiness as those with normal intellectual functioning.

Second, the national consensus against executing individuals with intellectual disability is overwhelming. As the Court recognized in *Atkins*, “a significant number of States have enacted laws prohibiting the execution of persons with intellectual disability.” 536 U.S. at 314. By the time of the 2002 decision, 18 States had explicitly banned such executions, and 14 others had done so through judicial interpretation. Id. at 315. The American Bar Association, the American Psychiatric Association, and the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities have all issued formal positions opposing the execution of individuals with intellectual disability. The United Nations Human Rights Committee has similarly condemned such executions as violations of international human rights law. This widespread consensus reflects a national recognition that executing individuals with intellectual disability is inconsistent with the principles of justice and human dignity.

Third, the execution of individuals with intellectual disability is fundamentally disproportionate. The Eighth Amendment requires that punishment be proportionate to the offender’s personal culpability. *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 965 (1991). Individuals with intellectual disability are less able to control their impulses, less likely to understand the consequences of their actions, and less capable of learning from punishment. They are also more vulnerable to coercion and manipulation, particularly during interrogation. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 316. To execute such individuals, therefore, is to impose a punishment that far exceeds their moral culpability. As the Court stated in *Ford*, “the execution of a person who is insane is not only cruel but also pointless.” 477 U.S. at 410. The same is true of executing someone with intellectual disability: such a punishment serves no legitimate penological purpose and fails to meet the standards of decency that mark our society.

Finally, the Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in rejecting Atkins’ claim. In *Penry*, the Court held that a capital defendant with intellectual disability must be allowed to present evidence of his condition to the jury so that the jury may consider it in determining whether the death penalty is appropriate. 492 U.S. at 328. The Virginia Supreme Court found that the trial court had properly allowed Atkins to present evidence of his intellectual disability, and that the jury had been given the opportunity to consider it. The fact that the jury ultimately sentenced Atkins to death does not undermine the constitutional validity of the process. The issue is not whether a jury may impose the death penalty on a person with intellectual disability, but whether the Eighth Amendment permits such a punishment at all. The answer, as the Court has now held, is no.

For these reasons, the execution of individuals with intellectual disability is cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
