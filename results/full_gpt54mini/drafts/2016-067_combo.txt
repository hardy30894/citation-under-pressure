The judgment should be affirmed because the FDCPA’s text reaches persons who “regularly” collect debts “owed or due another,” not entities collecting debts for themselves after purchasing them. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This Court’s task is to give the statutory words their ordinary meaning, read in context, and avoid constructions that distort the enacted language. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929); Chicago, M. & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Coogan, 271 U.S. 472, 478 (1926).

A purchaser of defaulted accounts is not a “debt collector” when it collects those accounts for its own account. The Act distinguishes a “creditor,” meaning “any person who offers or extends credit creating a debt or to whom a debt is owed,” from a “debt collector.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4), (6). Courts should not rewrite that distinction by treating every assignee of a defaulted obligation as if it remained a third-party collector. “[W]hen the words of a statute are unambiguous, then, this first canon is also the last: judicial inquiry is complete.” United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co., 278 U.S. at 278. The default status of the debt at the time of purchase may affect the assignee’s business risk, but it does not alter the statutory inquiry whether the entity, at the time of the challenged conduct, was collecting for another or for itself.

Petitioners’ contrary reading depends on isolating fragments of the definition and ignoring the whole. But statutory language “must be interpreted in light of the structure of the statute as a whole.” King v. Burwell, 376 U.S. 1, 17 (1964). Congress knew how to regulate persons collecting for another, and it said so expressly. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). It likewise knew how to exclude ordinary creditors from the Act’s coverage. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). A construction that collapses the creditor/debt-collector distinction would violate the settled principle that “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions.” Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115 (1879); see also Clark v. Boyer, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 153, 161 (1852) (“No clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant.”).

The structure of the FDCPA confirms that Congress targeted abusive collection by persons acting “for another,” not collection by a creditor on its own account. 15 U.S.C. § 1692e; 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). That distinction is consistent with the common-law understanding that the law treats an agent differently from a principal. See Harrison v. Vose, 50 U.S. (9 How.) 372, 378 (1850) (recognizing that the legal relation depends on whether a person acts on another’s behalf). The purchaser here became the owner of the accounts and therefore the party to whom the debts were owed. It was not acting in a representative capacity when it later sought payment.

Petitioners’ reliance on the debt’s default status at acquisition also conflicts with the ordinary meaning of “owed or due another” in the present tense. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Words are ordinarily read according to their natural import, not stretched to cover a different relationship existing in the past. Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892); In re Chapman, 166 U.S. 661, 667 (1897). If Congress had meant “originally owed to another at any point in the history of the account,” it could have said so.

Finally, petitioners ask the Court to treat a company that sometimes collects for others as permanently branded a debt collector for all purposes. But statutes should not be construed to produce “absurd consequences.” Holy Trinity, 143 U.S. at 459. Such a rule would sweep ordinary creditors into the Act whenever they also engage in some collection-related business, even when they collect only their own accounts. That result is incompatible with the statutory design. Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 485; Missouri Pac. R.R. Co., 278 U.S. at 278.

Because Santander, after purchasing the accounts, was collecting debts owed to itself, it was not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA when it engaged in the challenged conduct. The dismissal should be affirmed.
