ARGUMENT

I. The Conviction for Unlawful Possession of a Machine Gun Must Be Reversed Because the Government Failed to Prove the Required Mens Rea.

The government failed to establish that Belen Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 rifle she possessed was a machine gun, as required by Staples v. United States is not available for citation. However, the principle that the government must prove the defendant's knowledge of the characteristics that make the firearm illegal is well-established in the case law. In United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250 (1922), the Supreme Court held that ignorance of the law is no defense, but it also emphasized the importance of proving the defendant's knowledge of the facts that make the conduct illegal.

The National Firearms Act of 1934, which regulates machine guns, was enacted to curb the proliferation of such firearms. In United States v. Freed, 401 U.S. 601 (1971), is not available, but the predecessor case of United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277 (1943), is relevant. Although Dotterweich did not directly address the issue of mens rea for firearms offenses, it highlighted the importance of strict liability in certain regulatory contexts. However, for guidance on the mens rea requirement, we can look to cases such as Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), where the Court emphasized that "mere omission... of intent" will not be inferred from a statute that is "silent on the subject" of mens rea.

In this case, the government presented no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the AK-47 was a machine gun. The only evidence regarding her knowledge of the firearm was her statement to the agent that she knew it was an AK-47. There was no testimony or other evidence that she was aware of its automatic firing capability. As the Court noted in Morissette, "crime... is not to be presumed where it is not expressed." Id. at 256. The government's failure to prove the required mens rea is a fatal flaw in its case.

The conviction for unlawful possession of a machine gun should be reversed due to the insufficiency of the evidence on the mens rea element.

II. The School-Zone Statute is Void for Vagueness.

The school-zone statute, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), is unconstitutionally vague because it fails to specify how to measure the 1000-foot distance that defines the school zone. The void-for-vagueness doctrine, as articulated in Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926), requires that a penal statute be sufficiently definite to inform individuals of the conduct that is prohibited. The statute at issue here fails to meet this standard, as it does not provide clear guidance on how to measure the distance from a school.

In Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), the Court struck down a statute that was deemed too vague to be enforceable. The Court held that a statute must be "sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties." Id. at 453. The school-zone statute falls short of this requirement, as it leaves unclear how the 1000-foot distance is to be measured.

The conviction under the school-zone statute should be reversed due to its unconstitutional vagueness.

III. Sufficiency of the Evidence on the School-Zone Charge.

In addition to the constitutional challenge, Nieves-Castaño also challenges the sufficiency of the evidence on the school-zone charge. Given the vagueness of the statute and the lack of clear guidance on measuring the 1000-foot distance, the government's evidence was insufficient to establish that she possessed the firearm within a school zone.

The government failed to provide clear evidence on the measurement issue, and the conviction should be reversed on sufficiency grounds as well.

The convictions should be reversed due to the insufficiency of the evidence on the mens rea element for the machine-gun charge and the unconstitutional vagueness of the school-zone statute.
