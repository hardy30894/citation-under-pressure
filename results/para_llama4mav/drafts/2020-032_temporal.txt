The petitioners' constitutional challenge to the school policy was rendered moot when the policy was revised during litigation. The claim for nominal damages does not save the case from mootness.

The mootness doctrine is rooted in the principle that federal courts may only decide cases or controversies. As the Supreme Court held in Mills v. Green, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), a case becomes moot when the issues presented are no longer "live" or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome. Id. at 653. In this case, the revision of the "Freedom of Expression Policy" and the removal of the challenged portion of the "Student Code of Conduct" rendered the petitioners' claims for declaratory and injunctive relief moot.

The petitioners' claim for nominal damages does not revive their otherwise moot constitutional challenge. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a claim for damages can be insufficient to save a case from mootness if the damages claim is not substantial or is merely incidental to the primary claim for equitable relief. See, e.g., Barker v. Wise, 75 U.S. 729 (1869) (holding that a claim for costs alone is insufficient to save a case from mootness).

In this case, the petitioners' claim for nominal damages is merely incidental to their primary claim for equitable relief, which has been rendered moot by the revision of the school policies. The nominal damages claim does not provide a sufficient basis for continuing the litigation, as it is not a substantial or meaningful remedy.

The lower court's dismissal of the case as moot was proper, as the revision of the school policies eliminated the live controversy between the parties. The petitioners' claim for nominal damages is insufficient to overcome the mootness doctrine, and the case should be dismissed.

The judgment of the lower court should be affirmed.
