The Sixth Amendment right to counsel does not extend to proceedings in tribal courts. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896). Those courts derive their authority from inherent tribal sovereignty rather than from the Constitution, so federal counsel requirements have no application. Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832); Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556, 568-569 (1883).

Section 117(a) therefore may treat a final tribal conviction as a predicate without offending the Sixth Amendment. The statute penalizes only the new federal offense; it does not re-prosecute or enhance punishment for the earlier tribal violation. Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616, 623 (1912). Because the tribal judgments remain valid under the law that governed their rendition, their use as historical facts creates no constitutional infirmity.

Congress possesses plenary authority to define federal offenses that incorporate tribal adjudications. Lone Wolf v. Hitchcock, 187 U.S. 553, 565 (1903); United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375, 384-385 (1886). That power includes the decision to recognize uncounseled tribal convictions that satisfy tribal law, just as federal courts have long accepted foreign judgments lacking identical procedural safeguards when those judgments are final under the rendering sovereign’s rules.

The right to appointed counsel announced in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), and Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), binds only courts whose proceedings are themselves subject to the Sixth Amendment. It does not retroactively invalidate judgments rendered by tribunals outside that Amendment’s reach. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932). Accordingly, the district court properly denied the motion to dismiss.
