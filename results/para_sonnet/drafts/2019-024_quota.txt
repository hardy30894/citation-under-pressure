# ARGUMENT

**Section 1113(2)'s Three-Year Limitations Period Bars Suits Once a Plan Disclosed the Relevant Information, Regardless of Whether the Participant Chose to Read It or Later Claims Not to Remember It**

## I. The Text of § 1113(2) Requires Only Knowledge of the Facts Underlying the Breach, Not a Litigation-Ready Recollection of Having Read Every Disclosure.

Congress commanded that an ERISA fiduciary-breach action be brought within "three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation." 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). This Court has long held that a limitations period keyed to the plaintiff's knowledge of the underlying facts—rather than knowledge that those facts amount to a legal wrong—runs once the facts are known, whatever the plaintiff's later assertions of forgetfulness. *See United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 122 (1979) (rejecting the notion that "a plaintiff's ignorance of his legal rights, as opposed to the facts of his injury," should toll accrual). The Ninth Circuit's own precedent, which it reaffirmed below, recognized the same principle: the limitations clock is "triggered by [the fiduciary's] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [] knowledge of the law." *Sulyma v. Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm.*, quoting *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985). A rule that instead requires proof of subjective recall of specific documents "essentially reads the 'actual knowledge' standard out of the statute" by converting it into an evidentiary contest over memory rather than a test of what information was made available and received. *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Rest. Emps. Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520 (9th Cir. 1991).

## II. Congress's Deliberate Repeal of the Constructive-Knowledge Provision Confirms That Disclosure-Based Knowledge Satisfies § 1113(2), Not That a Plaintiff May Disregard Disclosures and Later Claim Ignorance.

When ERISA was first enacted, § 1113 contained both an actual-knowledge trigger and a constructive-knowledge trigger tied to the filing of statutorily required reports. Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330. Congress's repeal of the latter did not signal that a participant's receipt of ERISA-mandated disclosures is now irrelevant; it signaled that Congress trusted the statutory disclosure regime—summary plan descriptions, fund fact sheets, and annual disclosures required under 29 U.S.C. §§ 1024, 1025—to place plan participants on actual notice once distributed. This Court has recognized that ERISA's disclosure obligations exist precisely so that beneficiaries can "police their plans" and protect their own interests. *Curtiss-Wright Corp. v. Schoonejongen*, 514 U.S. 73, 83 (1995). That structural purpose is defeated if a participant may receive every legally required disclosure and yet indefinitely postpone the limitations period by simply declining to read them. Congress's design mirrors the trust-law principle, which this Court has held informs ERISA's fiduciary provisions, that a beneficiary furnished with an accounting is charged with the information it contains. *See Central States, Se. & Sw. Areas Pension Fund v. Central Transport, Inc.*, 472 U.S. 559, 570 (1985) (ERISA's fiduciary standards draw upon "common law of trusts").

## III. A Subjective-Recollection Standard Rewards Willful Blindness and Is Foreign to This Court's Treatment of "Actual Knowledge" in Analogous Contexts.

This Court has consistently equated actual knowledge with willful blindness where a party had access to information and deliberately avoided confirming it. In *Global-Tech Appliances, Inc. v. SEB S.A.*, 563 U.S. 754, 769 (2011), the Court explained that "persons who know enough to blind themselves to direct proof of critical facts in effect have actual knowledge of those facts." The same logic governs here: a participant who receives fund fact sheets, summary plan descriptions, and website disclosures describing the precise asset allocation later challenged as imprudent cannot transform actual knowledge into ignorance merely by failing to open the attachment. Nor has this Court hesitated to reject discovery-rule-like constructions when Congress specified a knowledge-based trigger without qualifying language. In *Rotkiske v. Klemm*, 589 U.S. 8 (2019), the Court declined to graft a discovery rule onto a statute of limitations where Congress did not provide for one, explaining that courts may not rewrite a statute's accrual trigger based on policy preferences favoring plaintiffs. And in the securities-fraud context, this Court in *Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633, 648 (2010), distinguished "actual knowledge" from a "should have known" standard, confirming that Congress's choice of "actual" knowledge—rather than constructive or inquiry notice—reflects a deliberate legislative judgment that must be respected on its own terms, not diluted by a plaintiff's self-serving deposition testimony that he does not "recall" seeing what was, in fact, disclosed to him. *Cf. TRW Inc. v. Andrews*, 534 U.S. 19, 27 (2001) (cautioning against judicially expanding limitations triggers beyond the text Congress enacted).

## IV. Conclusion

Because Sulyma received the disclosures describing Intel's challenged investment strategy more than three years before filing suit, he had "actual knowledge of the breach or violation" within the meaning of § 1113(2), and his suit is time-barred. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
