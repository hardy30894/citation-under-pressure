# ARGUMENT

## I. Exemption 2's Plain Text Limits Its Reach to "Internal Personnel Rules and Practices," Not to All Information an Agency Deems Sensitive

The Freedom of Information Act reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Dep't of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360–61 (1976) (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). Congress enumerated nine exemptions, and this Court has repeatedly instructed that they are "explicitly exclusive" and "must be narrowly construed." *U.S. Dep't of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Adm'r, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

Exemption 2 exempts matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The words Congress chose are not ambiguous: "personnel rules and practices" are rules about agency employees—hiring, discipline, leave, conduct—not technical data about the blast radius of high explosives. The Ninth Circuit's "High 2" doctrine, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), abandons this textual mooring. That doctrine asks only whether disclosure would "risk circumvention of agency regulation," collapsing the statute's specific subject-matter limitation into a freestanding risk-of-harm inquiry untethered to personnel matters at all.

This Court has never approved that transformation. In *Rose*, the Court considered Exemption 2 in the context of Air Force honor and ethics case summaries—genuinely personnel-related materials—and left open only whether *some* "internal matters of a relatively trivial nature" might also encompass materials "the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation." 425 U.S. at 369. The Court's discussion of circumvention was tied throughout to material that was already personnel-related; it was not a license to exempt any technical or operational data whose release might complicate an agency's mission.

## II. ESQD Arc Data Are Not "Personnel Rules and Practices" Under Any Fair Reading of the Statute

The documents at issue—Explosive Safety Quantity Distance calculations describing the blast effects of ordnance stored at Indian Island—are engineering and safety data, not rules governing Navy personnel. They are, in the Ninth Circuit's own description, "a long term planning tool" used to design storage facilities and calculate the physical consequences of detonation. This is scientific and technical information about explosives, indistinguishable in kind from the biological data this Court's sister circuits have refused to shoehorn into Exemption 2.

The Ninth Circuit's own precedent, prior to the panel decision below, recognized this limit. In *Maricopa Audubon Society v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir. 1997), the court held that data concerning the location of goshawk nesting sites did not "relate 'solely,' or even predominantly, 'to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.'" *Id.* at 1085 (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2)). Nesting-site data and blast-radius data share the same essential character: both are factual, scientific information about the physical world, not rules that govern how agency personnel conduct themselves. If goshawk location data cannot be squeezed into Exemption 2's personnel-rules language, blast-effect calculations cannot be either.

Nor can the Navy rely on this Court's decision in *United States Department of State v. Ray*, 502 U.S. 164 (1991), for the proposition that any information an agency prefers to keep confidential may be withheld merely upon its assertion of a security interest. *Ray* confirms only that the agency bears the burden of justifying withholding under a properly-invoked exemption, *id.* at 173; it does not relax the threshold requirement that the withheld material actually fall within the exemption's textual boundaries in the first instance. See *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

## III. The "Predominantly Internal" Standard Adopted Below Rewrites the Statute and Cannot Be Reconciled With This Court's Interpretive Method

The Ninth Circuit candidly acknowledged that it was adopting a judicially-crafted "predominantly internal" gloss from *Crooker*, reasoning that this modifier properly balances the statutory words "related" and "solely." But this Court does not sanction lower courts substituting their own preferred verbal formulations for the words Congress actually enacted. Statutory exemptions from FOIA's disclosure mandate must be interpreted according to their terms, narrowly, with any doubts resolved in favor of disclosure. *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361; *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151.

The Ninth Circuit's approach also cannot be squared with its own prior decision in *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), which limited Exemption 2's "High 2" application to "law enforcement materials, the disclosure of which may risk circumvention of agency regulation." *Id.* at 656. ESQD arc data are not law enforcement materials in any sense; they are safety-engineering specifications. The panel below strained to read *Hardy* as merely illustrative rather than limiting, but that reading transforms a case about search-and-raid manuals into open-ended authority for withholding any technical data whatsoever—a scope no prior Ninth Circuit panel had ever approved, as Judge Fletcher's dissent below recognized in concluding that "neither Exemption 2 nor Exemption 7(F) applies to the arc maps at issue in this appeal."

## IV. The Proper Vehicle for Protecting Genuinely Sensitive Security Information Is Exemption 1, Not a Judicially Expanded Exemption 2

Congress already provided the mechanism for protecting information whose disclosure would endanger national security: Exemption 1, which shields matters "kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy" and properly classified pursuant to executive order. 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1). The Navy did not classify the ESQD arc maps. Allowing Exemption 2 to function as a substitute for Exemption 1—protecting sensitive security information without the accountability that classification requires—defeats "the balance between the interests of the public in greater access to information and the needs of the Government to protect certain kinds of information from disclosure" that Congress struck. *John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989). This Court should not permit an agency to obtain through open-ended judicial construction of Exemption 2 what Congress required it to obtain through the formal classification process of Exemption 1.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
