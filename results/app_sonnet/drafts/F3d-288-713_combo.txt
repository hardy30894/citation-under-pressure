# ARGUMENT

## I. Riddle Was Denied a Fair Trial When the Chief Prosecutor Testified Against Him and Then Continued to Prosecute the Case.

Due process guarantees a criminal defendant "a fair trial in a fair tribunal." *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955). That guarantee is not satisfied merely because the formal trappings of a trial are observed; it demands that "justice must satisfy the appearance of justice." *Id.* When District Attorney Danny Hill took the stand to rebut Riddle's testimony that Hill had promised to "do his damnest to make sure [he] got the death penalty," and then resumed his position at counsel's table to continue prosecuting the very case in which he had just testified, the proceeding lost the appearance—and the substance—of impartiality that due process requires.

This Court's predecessor authority has long recognized that a prosecutor occupies a unique role, distinct from an ordinary advocate. As the Supreme Court explained in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935), the prosecutor "is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty whose obligation to govern impartially is as compelling as its obligation to govern at all; and whose interest, therefore, in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done." A prosecutor "may strike hard blows," but he "is not at liberty to strike foul ones." *Id.* Hill's decision to inject himself as a witness on the critical question of his own credibility—and then to have his assistant argue in closing that Riddle should be disbelieved because Hill had shown him to be a liar—transformed the prosecution from a search for justice into a personal vindication of the prosecutor's own veracity. That is precisely the kind of foul blow *Berger* forbids.

The structural unfairness here closely parallels that condemned in *Turner v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 466 (1965), where the Supreme Court held that a defendant was denied the "fair trial in a fair tribunal" guaranteed by due process when the jury was exposed to continuous, un-cross-examined contact with deputies who were also key prosecution witnesses. 379 U.S. at 472-73 (quoting *Murchison*, 349 U.S. at 136). Just as the *Turner* jury could not meaningfully separate the deputies' roles as courtroom officers from their credibility as witnesses, the jury here could not meaningfully separate Hill's institutional authority as chief prosecutor from his personal stake in being believed over Riddle. The blending of witness and advocate functions in a single individual, sanctioned by the trial court's failure to disqualify Hill, infected the trial with the same structural taint the Supreme Court found intolerable in *Turner*.

## II. The Prosecutor's Conduct Deprived Riddle of Due Process Independent of Any Single Trial Error.

Due process forbids the State from using its official machinery to secure a conviction through deception of the fact-finder. In *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935), the Supreme Court held that "the deliberate deception of court and jury by the presentation of testimony known to be perjured" is inconsistent with due process, regardless of whether that deception occurs through direct testimony or through argument built upon it. The Court reaffirmed and extended this principle in *Napue v. Illinois*, 360 U.S. 264 (1959), holding that a conviction obtained through the State's exploitation of testimony it should have known was misleading violates the Fourteenth Amendment. Here, the prosecution did not merely present adverse testimony; it converted its own chief officer into a witness and then argued his credibility as institutional fact, asking the jury to credit the State's veracity over the accused's simply because the prosecutor himself vouched for it. That argument exceeded the bounds of proper advocacy condemned in *Viereck v. United States*, 318 U.S. 236, 247 (1943), where the Supreme Court reversed a conviction because the prosecutor's closing argument was calculated to inflame rather than inform the jury's judgment.

## III. The District Court Erred in Denying an Evidentiary Hearing.

Because the Texas courts never adjudicated the factual predicate of this claim—whether Hill's dual role as witness and advocate actually influenced the jury's assessment of Riddle's credibility—the federal habeas court was obligated to conduct its own hearing. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963), holds that a federal habeas court "must hold an evidentiary hearing" where the material facts were not adequately developed at the state-court hearing. The district court's summary denial, without any factual inquiry into the effect of Hill's testimony and subsequent prosecution, contravenes *Townsend*'s mandate that "the trial judge must, so far as possible, be aware of the entire relevant factual picture." *Id.* at 313. This obligation is reinforced by *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), which confirms the federal court's independent duty to redetermine facts bearing on federal constitutional claims, and by *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 415 (1963), which reaffirms that the writ's "classic function" is to test the very legality of detention where constitutional rights have been compromised.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Riddle respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment below, grant an evidentiary hearing, and issue the writ of habeas corpus.
