The Equal Protection Clause safeguards against official classifications that disadvantage identifiable groups. Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944). It does not authorize federal courts to superintend every discretionary decision affecting a single property owner. See Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962) (holding that selective enforcement “is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination” against a defined class).

A plaintiff asserting an equal-protection violation must therefore demonstrate membership in a class singled out for differential treatment. Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229, 239 (1976); Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256, 279 (1979). Absent such a showing, the claim collapses into an ordinary tort or due-process action. McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279, 292 (1987).

The Seventh Circuit’s “class of one” theory erases this requirement. It permits any disappointed applicant to convert a routine administrative dispute into a federal constitutional case merely by alleging subjective animus. That approach contradicts the settled understanding that equal protection addresses “invidious discrimination” against groups, not isolated instances of alleged unfairness. City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc., 473 U.S. 432, 446 (1985).

Even in the taxation context most closely analogous to the easement demand here, the Court has required proof that similarly situated taxpayers were treated differently because of an impermissible classification. Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. Commission of Webster County, 488 U.S. 336, 345 (1989). No such classification was pleaded.

Recognizing a class-of-one claim would also undermine prosecutorial and administrative discretion long protected from judicial second-guessing. United States v. Armstrong, 517 U.S. 456, 464 (1996); Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821, 831 (1985). Every local decision to grant or withhold a permit, easement, or variance could be relitigated in federal court on the bare assertion that the decisionmaker bore personal ill will.

The complaint therefore fails to state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
