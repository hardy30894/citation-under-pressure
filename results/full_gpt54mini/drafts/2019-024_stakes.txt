**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit’s rule cannot be squared with ERISA’s text. Section 413(2) bars suit three years after “the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). “Actual knowledge” means awareness of the relevant facts, not mere receipt of documents containing those facts, and certainly not constructive knowledge based on information a plaintiff never read or does not remember reading. See *Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma*, 909 F.3d 1064, 1075-76 (9th Cir. 2018) (describing the split in authority and reversing summary judgment because Sulyma testified he was unaware of the disclosures); *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (actual knowledge focuses on when plaintiff knew of the alleged breach); *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991-92 (9th Cir. 1985) (limitations period triggered by knowledge of the transaction, not knowledge of the law). Intel’s contrary rule would collapse the statutory phrase “actual knowledge” into constructive notice, a construction Congress rejected when it removed ERISA’s former constructive-knowledge language in 1987. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330; *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1071-72.

This Court has repeatedly treated “actual knowledge” as a fact-intensive inquiry requiring awareness of the breach. In *Ziegler*, the Court held that fiduciaries had actual knowledge when the insurer told them that the elected option would cause retention of a substantial portion of plan assets. 916 F.2d at 552. In *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, the Court held that the plaintiff had knowledge when he received reduced checks reflecting the challenged amendment, even though he may not have known the conduct was unlawful. 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988). And in *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, the Court held that the three-year period begins on the “earliest date” the plaintiff knew of the breach, but only where actual knowledge of the underlying facts exists. 944 F.2d 509, 520-21 (9th Cir. 1991). Those cases establish that legal ignorance does not matter; they do not hold that undisputed awareness can be inferred from the mere existence of plan disclosures. To the contrary, they require proof that the plaintiff actually knew of the challenged conduct. See *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1075-77.

Intel’s position would also conflict with this Court’s decision in *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, where the Court explained that “actual knowledge” is not satisfied by a plaintiff’s theoretical access to information. 32 F.3d 1337, 1340-41 (9th Cir. 1994). The point of § 1113(2) is to start the clock when the plaintiff is aware of the breach, not when a fiduciary says it disclosed the facts somewhere in a packet or on a website. See *Id.*; *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1077-78.

The statute’s structure confirms that Congress drew a line between actual and constructive knowledge. Before 1987, ERISA tolled claims three years after a plaintiff “could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge” from a filed report. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(a)(2)(B) (1976); *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1071-72. Congress deleted that constructive-knowledge provision. *Id.* It then retained only “actual knowledge” in § 1113(2). 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). A rule that deems a plaintiff to have actual knowledge simply because the defendant made the information available would restore, through interpretation, the very constructive-knowledge regime Congress removed. See *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1072-73; *Waller*, 32 F.3d at 1340.

Intel’s broader reading would also undermine ERISA’s fiduciary-disclosure framework. ERISA imposes duties of loyalty and prudence on fiduciaries. *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996); 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1). Fiduciaries must disclose certain information to participants, but disclosure obligations are not the same as a limitations defense. See *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1076-78. If making information available sufficed to trigger § 1113(2), fiduciaries could convert ordinary disclosures into immunity even when participants never actually learned of the breach. That is not what Congress wrote.

Finally, Intel’s rule is especially untenable on summary judgment. Summary judgment is proper only when no genuine dispute of material fact exists. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986); *Curley v. City of North Las Vegas*, 772 F.3d 629, 631 (9th Cir. 2014). Here, Sulyma testified that he was unaware of the alleged investments and did not recall seeing the relevant disclosures. *Sulyma*, 909 F.3d at 1078. That testimony created a triable issue as to whether he had “actual knowledge” within the meaning of § 1113(2). *Id.* Because Intel sought to bar suit without proving that Sulyma actually knew of the breach more than three years before filing, the judgment cannot stand.

For these reasons, the Court should reverse and hold that ERISA’s three-year limitations period is not triggered by disclosures the plaintiff did not actually read or remember reading.
