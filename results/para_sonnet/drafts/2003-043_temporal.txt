# ARGUMENT

## I. An Assessment Against a Partnership Is Not an Assessment Against the Individual Partners, and It Does Not Extend the Limitations Period Applicable to Them

The Internal Revenue Code permits the government ten years to collect a tax "[w]here the assessment of any tax imposed by this title has been made within the period of limitation properly applicable thereto." 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). That extended period runs from "the assessment of the tax"—an act that, under § 6203, consists of "recording the liability of the taxpayer." The extension is thus tied to a specific, identified taxpayer, not to an abstract debt that happens to be owed by more than one person.

This Court's predecessors have long insisted that limitations provisions governing the government's power to collect taxes be given their precise, literal application rather than expanded by inference. See Botany Worsted Mills v. United States, 278 U.S. 282 (1929); United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489 (1930). That principle applies with equal force here: the ten-year extension is an exception to the ordinary three-year period of § 6501(a), and it attaches only to the taxpayer as to whom the statutory prerequisite—a valid assessment identifying that taxpayer—has actually been satisfied.

Respondents and the Partnership are separate taxpayers, each bearing distinct taxpayer identification. An assessment against the Partnership records only the Partnership's liability; it does not record, and cannot substitute for, an assessment recording the liability of Abel Galletti, Sarah Galletti, Francesco Briguglio, or Angela Briguglio individually. The government concedes it never made such an assessment against any Respondent within three years of the relevant returns. Absent that assessment, the six-year (now ten-year) extension of § 6502(a) never came into existence with respect to Respondents, and the ordinary three-year period of § 6501(a) governs their liability. That period expired years before the IRS filed its proofs of claim in the bankruptcy proceedings.

## II. This Court's Precedent Confirms That the Government Must Assess or Obtain a Judgment Against the Individual Taxpayer Before It May Collect Directly From That Taxpayer's Property

The government's contrary position was squarely rejected in Young v. Riddell, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831 (S.D. Cal. 1959), aff'd, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960). There, the district court recognized the very distinction Respondents press here, explaining that although state law may make a partner substantively liable for partnership debts, the government's ability to *collect* that debt without judicial process depends on whether the partner has been individually assessed. The court observed: "It is the government's contention that where an assessment names an entity such as in the instant case, that it is unnecessary to name the individual members of the entity in order to establish individual liability and that the only reason for naming such individual or adding such individuals' names as here is to enable collection of the tax without resorting to court action. With this contention I agree." Id. at 76,050. In other words, even the government's own submission in Young confirmed that an assessment naming only the partnership does not permit summary administrative collection from the individual partners; it merely leaves the government to "resort[] to court action" to establish the partner's liability. Id.

That understanding was reinforced in Coson v. United States, 169 F. Supp. 671 (S.D. Cal. 1958), aff'd on other grounds, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). The district court there held that a federal tax lien—which arises only upon a valid assessment—could not attach to the property of an individual who had never himself been assessed, even though the underlying tax had been assessed against a partnership in which he was allegedly a partner. The court explained that a valid assessment requires identification of "the taxpayer" under § 6203 and its implementing regulations, and that an assessment naming only the partnership and "an unknown number of unidentified taxpayers" did not satisfy that requirement as to the individual. 169 F. Supp. at 675. Concluding that "plaintiff never was assessed and no lien exists without such an assessment," the court held "the Government does not have any lien." Id. at 676. Although the Ninth Circuit affirmed on a separate procedural ground, it did not disturb the district court's core holding that assessment is a taxpayer-specific act that cannot be satisfied by naming only the entity.

Both decisions rest on the same principle that governs this case: the machinery of administrative collection—assessment, the resulting lien, and the extended limitations period that assessment triggers—operates only as to the taxpayer actually named and identified in the assessment. Where, as here, the government assessed only the Partnership and never assessed, sued, or obtained a judgment against the individual partners within the ordinary limitations period, it cannot invoke the extended period to resurrect a claim against those partners years later in bankruptcy.

## III. Conclusion

Because the IRS never assessed Respondents individually, and never timely obtained a judgment against them, the three-year limitations period of § 6501(a) governed their liability and has long since expired. The Court of Appeals correctly affirmed disallowance of the IRS's claims, and that judgment should be affirmed.
