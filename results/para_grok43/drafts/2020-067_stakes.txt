ARGUMENT

A federal court of appeals reviewing a forfeited claim for plain error may consult the entire record, including matters outside the trial evidence presented to the jury, when assessing whether the error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Plain-error review proceeds in four steps. The defendant must show (1) an error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732-37 (1993). The third and fourth prongs require an inquiry into prejudice and the effect of the error on the proceedings as a whole. Id. at 734-36.

That inquiry is not confined to the evidence formally admitted at trial. In United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), this Court held that a reviewing court assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11 may “consult the whole record,” including the presentence report and other materials outside the plea colloquy itself. Id. at 59. The Court explained that limiting review to the record developed at the hearing “would produce a result we do not believe Congress intended.” Id. at 74.

The same principle governs forfeited Rehaif errors. In Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court looked to the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, to determine whether an omitted element was plain. Id. at 469-70. More recently, in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 585 U.S. 129 (2018), the Court reiterated that the fourth prong calls for an examination of “the effect of the error on the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings,” an assessment that necessarily draws on the full procedural history. Id. at 142-43.

The Eleventh Circuit therefore acted properly when it examined Greer’s prior convictions and the undisputed fact that he had served more than a year in prison. Those facts, though outside the trial record, were properly considered to determine whether the Rehaif error affected substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings. Because the court of appeals applied the settled plain-error framework to the record as a whole, its judgment should be affirmed.
