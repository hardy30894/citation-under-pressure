**ARGUMENT**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. Congress has provided an “intelligible principle” to guide the Attorney General’s exercise of delegated authority, and the subsequent sub-delegation to the DEA Administrator is both authorized and constitutionally sound. This Court has long upheld similar delegations, and the Third Circuit’s conclusion that the statute passes constitutional muster is correct.

**I. Congress Has Provided an Intelligible Principle for Temporary Scheduling**

The non-delegation doctrine does not prohibit all delegations of legislative power; it only invalidates those that lack an “intelligible principle” to guide the delegate. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The principle is not that Congress must supply detailed rules, but that it must set forth a standard that defines the scope and limits of the delegated authority. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 377 (1989). Here, Congress has done precisely that.

Section 811(h) authorizes the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance if he determines that “an imminent hazard to the public safety” exists. This standard is not vague or unbounded. The statute explicitly identifies the factors the Attorney General must consider: the substance’s history and current pattern of abuse, the scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(3). These are not mere suggestions—they are mandatory criteria. The Attorney General must evaluate these factors before acting, and the DEA’s own notice and order in this case demonstrate that it did so. See 52 Fed. Reg. 30,174 (1987) (listing the drug’s potency, clandestine production, and risk of death). The requirement of a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services further ensures that the decision is grounded in expert judgment. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). These are not mere formalities; they are substantive checks on arbitrary action.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations with similarly broad standards. In *Mistretta*, Congress delegated to the Federal Sentencing Commission the power to create sentencing guidelines, with only general instructions to “promote uniformity” and “reflect the seriousness of the offense.” 488 U.S. at 377. The Court found this sufficient. *Id.* at 378. The same is true here. The “imminent hazard to public safety” standard, coupled with the enumerated factors and the HHS review, provides a clear and enforceable boundary. The fact that the standard is not mathematically precise does not render it unconstitutional. *Id.* at 377 (“Congress may delegate authority under broad general directives.”).

**II. The Sub-Delegation to the DEA Administrator Is Authorized and Proper**

The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is not only permissible but required by the structure of the Act. Congress did not prohibit sub-delegation; it authorized the Attorney General to “perform the functions” of the Act, and the Attorney General may lawfully assign those functions to subordinate agencies. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This regulation, which has been in place since 1973, was amended in 1987 to include the temporary scheduling power. The DEA Administrator is not acting in a legislative capacity; he is implementing a statutory scheme Congress has authorized. His actions are subject to notice, comment, and the HHS evaluation—procedural safeguards that ensure accountability.

The Third Circuit correctly rejected the argument that sub-delegation is impermissible. *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987); *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988). The Court in *Mistretta* emphasized that Congress may delegate to coordinate branches, including executive agencies. 488 U.S. at 654. The DEA is an executive agency, and its role in administering the CSA is well established. The delegation here is not a surrender of legislative power but a practical means of executing a complex regulatory scheme. As the Court noted in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944), “the complexity of the field” justifies delegation. The rapid emergence of designer drugs like Euphoria demands a swift response—precisely the purpose of the temporary scheduling provision.

**III. The Preclusion of Judicial Review Does Not Bar Constitutional Challenge**

The Toubys argue that § 811(h)(6), which states that temporary scheduling orders “are not subject to judicial review,” forecloses constitutional challenges. This is incorrect. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977), holds that “the availability of judicial review is presumed” and that Congress must show “clear and convincing” evidence to bar review. No such evidence exists here. The statute does not bar review of constitutional claims; it only limits review of the *merits* of the scheduling decision. The Court has consistently held that constitutional challenges to delegations are reviewable. *United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 165 (D. Del. 1987); *United States v. Pees*, 645 F. Supp. 697, 700 (D. Colo. 1986). The Toubys’ challenge is not to the substance of the scheduling but to the delegation itself—a question of constitutional law that courts must resolve.
