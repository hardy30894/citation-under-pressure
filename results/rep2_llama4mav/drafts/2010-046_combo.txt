ARGUMENT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) does not prohibit the district court's order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions in its correctional facilities. The PLRA's limitations on prospective relief are not applicable here, as the relief ordered by the district court is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of a federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct that violation, as required by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908).

The district court's order is a proper exercise of its equitable powers to remedy a constitutional violation, as established in Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954). The court's order is also consistent with the principles enunciated in United States v. Morgan, 307 U.S. 183 (1939), where the Court held that a district court's exercise of its equitable powers to remedy a constitutional violation is subject to review only for abuse of discretion.

The State's argument that the district court's order violates the PLRA is unpersuasive. The PLRA's requirement that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn" and "the least intrusive means necessary" to correct a constitutional violation is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in Louisiana v. Texas, 176 U.S. 1 (1900), that a state's sovereignty is subject to the limitations imposed by the Constitution.

Furthermore, the district court's order is not a "prisoner release order" within the meaning of the PLRA, as it is not a direct order to release prisoners, but rather a requirement that the State reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions. This is consistent with the holding in Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), that a court's order may be framed in terms of a requirement to achieve a certain result, rather than a direct command to perform a specific act.

The State's reliance on cases such as Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), is misplaced, as those cases do not address the specific issue presented here. The district court's order is a proper exercise of its authority to remedy a constitutional violation, as established in cases such as In re Debs, 158 U.S. 564 (1895).

The Supreme Court has long recognized the authority of federal courts to remedy constitutional violations through the exercise of their equitable powers, as seen in cases such as Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824). The district court's order is a proper exercise of that authority, and is consistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as United States v. Lee, 106 U.S. 196 (1882).

In addition, the court's order is supported by the principle that a state's failure to comply with a constitutional requirement may be remedied through the exercise of federal judicial authority, as established in cases such as Ableman v. Booth, 62 U.S. 506 (1859).

The State's argument that the district court's order violates the Eleventh Amendment is also unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has long held that the Eleventh Amendment does not bar federal judicial authority to remedy constitutional violations by state officials, as seen in cases such as Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), and Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the district court's order is a proper exercise of its equitable powers to remedy a constitutional violation, and is not barred by the PLRA or the Eleventh Amendment. The court's order should be affirmed.
