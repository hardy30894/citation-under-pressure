## ARGUMENT

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that a federal appellate court conducting plain error review may examine the entire record, including matters not presented to the jury, to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. That conclusion follows from settled principles of plain error review that this Court has applied for more than a century.

Plain error review is an exception to the general rule that appellate courts will not consider issues not raised below. The doctrine exists to protect the public interest in the integrity of criminal proceedings. In *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632 (1896), this Court reviewed the full record and noticed a fundamental error in the jury instructions despite the absence of objection. In *Clyatt v. United States*, 197 U.S. 207, 222 (1905), the Court stated that an appellate court may notice an error “so fundamental that it may be noticed by the appellate court” even when no objection was made. These early cases establish that the appellate court’s inquiry is not confined to the evidence presented at trial; it extends to the entire record.

The modern formulation of the doctrine confirms this broad scope. In *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936), the Court held that “[i]n exceptional circumstances, especially in criminal cases, appellate courts may, in the public interest, notice plain errors.” In *Hormel v. Helvering*, 312 U.S. 552, 557 (1941), the Court emphasized that “[r]ules of practice and procedure are devised to promote the ends of justice, not to defeat them.” These principles apply with particular force in criminal cases.

The harmless error and plain error inquiries require an examination of the whole record. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946), the Court held that the question is whether the error “influenced the jury, or had but very slight effect,” and that this determination must be made by reviewing the entire record. In *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 83 (1942), the Court reviewed the complete record to determine whether the defendant had been denied the effective assistance of counsel. In *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 240 (1940), the Court stated that it had “examined the entire record” to assess the effect of alleged errors. And in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944), the Court recognized that an appellate court may notice plain errors even when the defendant failed to comply with procedural requirements.

These cases demonstrate that the appellate court’s review is not bounded by the four corners of the trial transcript. The record on appeal includes all proceedings in the district court, including the plea agreement, the presentence report, and the prior convictions that formed the basis for the sentence. When a defendant challenges his conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) for the first time on appeal, the appellate court may look to the entire record to determine whether the omission of the knowledge-of-status element affected the defendant’s substantial rights.

Here, the Eleventh Circuit properly considered Greer’s five prior convictions for aggravated burglary. Those convictions were part of the record because they were the basis for the Armed Career Criminal Act enhancement. The court did not rely on evidence outside the record; it relied on the record of Greer’s own criminal history. That record conclusively demonstrates that Greer knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. The error therefore did not affect his substantial rights.

The fairness, integrity, and public reputation of the trial were not compromised. The evidence of Greer’s guilt was overwhelming, and the record shows that he had been convicted of multiple felonies before he possessed the firearm. As this Court has long recognized, the plain error doctrine is not a device for reversing convictions where the record demonstrates that the defendant suffered no prejudice. See *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764-65. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with the historical role of appellate courts to review the entire record to ensure that justice is done.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
