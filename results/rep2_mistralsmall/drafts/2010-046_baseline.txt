**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER IS AUTHORIZED BY 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) AND DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT (PLRA).**

The PLRA expressly permits a federal court to “impose prospective injunctive relief” in a prisoner class action “in order to remedy the deprivation of [prisoners’] rights” under the Eighth Amendment. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The statute further provides that such relief “shall extend no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” Id. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court below correctly applied these provisions when it ordered California to reduce its prison population to the least restrictive means of curing the ongoing constitutional violations documented in this case.

This Court has long recognized that “[t]he essence of the [Eighth] Amendment is that prisoners retain the essence of human dignity inherent in all persons.” *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978). When prison conditions are so deplorable as to violate the Eighth Amendment, federal courts have the duty to fashion remedies that are “both necessary and narrowly drawn.” *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 523 (2011) (quoting *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987)). The PLRA does not strip courts of this remedial authority; it merely channels it. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (limiting relief to that “necessary to correct the violation”).

The three-judge court’s order comports with these principles. After a lengthy trial, the court found that severe overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional medical and mental health care in California’s prisons. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 607 F.3d 1148, 1156 (9th Cir. 2010), *aff’d sub nom. Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493. The court determined that reducing the population to 137.5% of design capacity was the least restrictive means of remedying these violations. *Id.* at 1160. This remedy is narrowly tailored to the constitutional injury and does not exceed the scope of the PLRA’s authorization. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A).

Contrary to Petitioners’ contention, the PLRA does not impose a categorical bar on population caps. The statute prohibits only those prospective relief orders that “extend[] beyond the violation of the Federal right,” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), or that are “not narrowly drawn, extend[] no further than necessary to correct the alleged violation,” or are otherwise “inconsistent with the requirements of the PLRA,” *id.* § 3626(b)(1). The three-judge court’s order satisfies each of these requirements. It is the functional equivalent of a population cap, but it is not an impermissible “release order” under the PLRA. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(2) (defining “release order” as one that “has the purpose or effect of reducing or limiting the prison population”).

This Court has upheld similar population reduction orders in the past. In *Hutto*, the Court affirmed a district court’s order capping prison population at 95% of capacity to remedy Eighth Amendment violations. 437 U.S. at 686. The PLRA did not alter this remedial authority. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (preserving courts’ authority to impose “prospective injunctive relief” to remedy constitutional violations). The three-judge court’s order is consistent with *Hutto* and the PLRA’s remedial framework.

**II. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT PROPERLY EXHAUSTED STATE REMEDIES BEFORE IMPOSING POPULATION REDUCTION RELIEF.**

The PLRA requires that a court “give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety or the operation of a criminal justice system” when fashioning prospective relief. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court complied with this requirement by first exhausting state remedies. The court held extensive hearings, heard testimony from experts, and considered California’s own proposals for relief. *Plata*, 607 F.3d at 1156. Only after these efforts failed did the court conclude that population reduction was the least restrictive means of remedying the constitutional violations. *Id.* at 1160.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that federal courts must defer to state officials in the administration of prisons unless constitutional violations persist. *See, e.g., Bell v. Wolfish*, 441 U.S. 520, 540 (1979) (“[C]ourts are ill equipped to deal with the increasingly urgent problems of prison administration.”). The three-judge court honored this principle by exhausting state remedies before imposing population reduction relief. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (requiring courts to “give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety or the operation of a criminal justice system”).

**III. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT.**

Petitioners argue that the three-judge court’s order violates the Eleventh Amendment by commandeering state officials to reduce the prison population. This argument misapprehends the nature of the relief ordered. The three-judge court did not direct state officials to release specific prisoners or to adopt particular policies. Instead, it ordered California to achieve a population cap by whatever lawful means it chose. *Plata*, 607 F.3d at 1160. This is a permissible prospective injunction under the PLRA and does not violate the Eleventh Amendment. *See* *Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267, 290 (1977) (“[A] federal court may not order state officials to comply with a federal court decree unless the decree is directed at the state itself.”).

The three-judge court’s order is consistent with this principle. It does not require California to release prisoners or to adopt specific policies. It merely requires the state to reduce its prison population to a constitutional level. This is a permissible exercise of the court’s remedial authority under the PLRA and does not violate the Eleventh Amendment. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. at 523.
