# ARGUMENT

## I. The Equal Protection Clause Protects Classes of Similarly Situated Persons, Not the Unadorned Grievances of a Single Individual

Since this Court's earliest equal protection decisions, the constitutional wrong has been discriminatory treatment measured against a comparison class of similarly situated persons. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), this Court struck down the discriminatory administration of a laundry ordinance because it was enforced "with an evil eye and an unequal hand, so as practically to make unjust and illegal discriminations between persons in similar circumstances." *Id.* at 373-74. The wrong in *Yick Wo* was not that any one laundry operator was treated differently from the world at large; it was that Chinese operators, as a class, were denied permits routinely granted to others "in similar circumstances." *Id.*

This Court reiterated the point a year earlier in *Barbier v. Connolly*, 113 U.S. 27 (1885), explaining that the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees that no burden shall be imposed on one person's pursuits "except as applied to the same pursuits by others under like circumstances." *Id.* at 32. The touchstone is comparison to a defined class of persons under "like circumstances"—not an individualized entitlement to identical treatment divorced from any class.

This Court crystallized the requirement in *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), holding that "[t]he unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in its unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination." *Id.* at 8 (emphasis added). The phrase "those who are entitled to be treated alike" presupposes a class—a group of persons similarly situated to the plaintiff against whom the plaintiff's treatment may be measured. Olech's complaint contains no such allegation. She does not claim membership in any class of similarly situated landowners who were treated more favorably because of who they are; she claims only that she, individually, wanted a smaller easement than the Village demanded. That is a private grievance, not the class-based injury *Snowden* requires.

## II. This Court's Classification Framework in *Royster Guano* and *Railway Express* Confirms That Equal Protection Analysis Depends on a Class of Comparators

The structural analysis this Court has long applied to equal protection claims likewise depends on classification. In *F.S. Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412 (1920), the Court held that a classification "must be reasonable, not arbitrary, and must rest upon some ground of difference having a fair and substantial relation to the object of the legislation, so that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike." *Id.* at 415 (emphasis added). That formulation is meaningless without an identifiable class of the "similarly circumstanced."

*Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), reinforces the same principle: equal protection review asks whether "the classification is a hostile and oppressive discrimination against particular persons and classes," and the Court has never demanded that "all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." *Id.* at 110, 112. That deferential, classification-centered inquiry cannot be performed when a plaintiff identifies no class at all—only herself.

## III. The Tax-Assessment Decisions Relied Upon Below Do Not Support a Class-of-One Theory Because Relief There Was Measured Against a Defined Comparison Class

The Seventh Circuit's "spite" theory finds no footing in this Court's tax-discrimination precedents, because those cases, too, involved comparison to a class. In *Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Wakefield Township*, 247 U.S. 350 (1918), the Court granted relief because the assessor had valued the plaintiff's property far above "the assessed value of other taxable property of the same character," *id.* at 353—that is, relative to a class of similarly situated taxpayers, not in the abstract.

*Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441 (1923), is to the same effect: the plaintiff obtained relief by showing systematic assessment at full value while "all other property in the county" was assessed at a fraction of value. *Id.* at 445-46. Relief consisted of bringing the plaintiff *within* the class of taxpayers assessed at the lower ratio—confirming that equal protection vindicates parity with an existing class, not an unmoored individual entitlement.

## IV. Recognizing a Cause of Action Without Any Class Allegation Would Convert the Equal Protection Clause Into a Federal Forum for Ordinary Local Disputes

Olech's theory—that any person disappointed by a municipal permitting decision may sue under the Fourteenth Amendment merely by alleging "spite"—cannot be squared with *Snowden*'s demand for intentional discrimination against those "entitled to be treated alike," 321 U.S. at 8, or with the classification analysis of *Royster Guano* and *Railway Express*. Absent an allegation of class membership, Olech's grievance is a garden-variety easement dispute, not a constitutional injury. This Court should reverse.
