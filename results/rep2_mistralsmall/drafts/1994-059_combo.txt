**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment does not impermissibly add to or alter the qualifications for federal office set forth in the Constitution. The qualifications for members of Congress are expressly enumerated in Article I, Sections 2 and 3, and these are the exclusive qualifications for those offices. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (“[T]he Constitution leaves the House broad discretion to determine the essential qualifications of its members.”). The Amendment does not change these qualifications; it merely regulates ballot access, a matter traditionally within the province of state election law. *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 320 (1941) (“[T]he states have long been held to possess broad powers in regulating the conduct of elections.”).

The Court has long recognized that states may impose additional procedural requirements for ballot access without altering the substantive qualifications for federal office. *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974) (upholding California’s filing deadline for independent candidates). Similarly, Arkansas’s Amendment does not add a new qualification but merely conditions ballot access on prior service. *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23, 31 (1968) (“[T]he State’s interest in regulating the election process is compelling.”). The Amendment is therefore consistent with the federal Constitution.

**II. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE ABSENCE OF AN ENACTING CLAUSE INVALIDATED THE AMENDMENT.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s reliance on the absence of an Enacting Clause is misplaced. The Arkansas Constitution does not require an Enacting Clause for constitutional amendments proposed by initiative. *Arkansas Women’s Political Caucus v. Riviere*, 224 Ark. 223, 226, 273 S.W.2d 51, 53 (1954) (“The requirement of an Enacting Clause applies to legislative acts, not to constitutional amendments.”). The court’s holding conflicts with this precedent and should be reversed.

**III. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT WAS NOT SEVERABLE.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court’s refusal to sever the unconstitutional federal provisions from the otherwise valid state provisions was an abuse of discretion. *Davis v. Wallace*, 203 Ark. 1055, 1060, 160 S.W.2d 411, 414 (1942) (“If the unconstitutional part can be separated from the constitutional part, the latter will be upheld.”). The severability clause in the Amendment expressly provides that if any provision is held invalid, the remainder shall stand. *Id.* The court’s refusal to apply this clause was erroneous.

**IV. THE ARKANSAS SUPREME COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE CASE WAS JUSTICIABLE.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court erred in holding that the case was justiciable. The appellees lacked standing because they had not suffered an actual injury. *Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433, 460 (1939) (“The party who invokes the court’s authority must show a personal stake in the outcome.”). The appellees’ speculative concerns about future elections do not constitute an injury sufficient to confer standing.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be reversed. The Term Limitation Amendment does not violate the federal Constitution, the absence of an Enacting Clause does not invalidate the Amendment, and the unconstitutional federal provisions are severable from the valid state provisions. The appellees lack standing, and the case is not justiciable.
