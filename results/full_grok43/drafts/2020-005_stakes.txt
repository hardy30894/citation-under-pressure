**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017) (quoting Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 472-473 (1985)). This standard is satisfied here.

Ford has continuously and systematically sold, serviced, and advertised its vehicles in Montana for decades. These contacts are not isolated or fortuitous. See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson, 444 U.S. 286, 295 (1980). The claims at issue—alleging design defect, failure to warn, and negligence in a Ford Explorer—directly implicate the same product line that Ford has placed into the Montana stream of commerce and supported with replacement parts and marketing. The connection between those contacts and the claims is therefore substantial, not “attenuated.” Id. at 299.

Ford’s argument that jurisdiction fails unless its Montana contacts proximately caused the accident misreads the governing test. The Court has never required but-for causation; it has required only that the suit “relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. See Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984); see also Calder v. Jones, 465 U.S. 783, 789-790 (1984) (jurisdiction proper where effects of out-of-state conduct were felt in forum). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this “arise out of or relate to” formulation and found the requisite relationship.

Exercising jurisdiction also comports with traditional notions of fair play. Ford has long availed itself of the Montana market and can reasonably anticipate being haled into court there when one of its vehicles injures a Montana resident. See Burger King, 471 U.S. at 475-476. No other forum has a stronger connection to the litigation, and Montana has a manifest interest in providing redress for injuries occurring on its highways. See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc., 465 U.S. 770, 776 (1984).

The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
