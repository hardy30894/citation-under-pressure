The respondent, Eugene Woodard, has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not apply to clemency proceedings because they are not an integral part of the adjudicatory process. As established by the Supreme Court in Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120-21 (1925), clemency is an act of "mercy" exercised by the executive as a matter of discretion. This characterization underscores the fundamentally non-judicial nature of clemency decisions, which are not subject to the same due process requirements as the judicial process.

The Court has consistently recognized that clemency decisions are not subject to judicial review, as seen in United States v. Klein, 80 U.S. 128, 147 (1871), where the Court stated that the President's power to grant reprieves and pardons is "unlimited" and "cannot be controlled or modified by Congress." This principle is further supported by Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 603-04 (1896), which held that a pardon is a "private" act of the executive not subject to due process requirements. The distinction between the judicial process and clemency proceedings is also highlighted in United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. 150, 161 (1833), where the Court described a pardon as "an act of grace" separate from the judicial process.

Moreover, the creation of a clemency procedure by the state does not create a protected liberty interest. Murray v. Hoboken Land & Improvement Co., 59 U.S. 272, 277 (1856), established that a statutory procedure does not necessarily create a constitutional right. Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. 307, 311 (1856), further emphasized that the executive's power to grant clemency is a constitutional duty not subject to judicial oversight. These precedents collectively demonstrate that clemency proceedings are not integral to the adjudicatory process and do not implicate due process concerns.

The respondent's Fifth Amendment claim is also without merit. The Ohio clemency procedure does not compel the respondent to waive his Fifth Amendment rights, as he is not required to participate in the clemency interview. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 111-12 (1908), held that the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination is not implicated when a person is not compelled to testify against themselves. The respondent remains free to assert his Fifth Amendment privilege if he chooses to participate in the interview.

The Court of Appeals' remand for further proceedings is premature and unnecessary. Railroad Commission v. Pullman Co., 312 U.S. 496, 500-01 (1941), established that a federal court should not intervene in a state proceeding unless there is a clear constitutional violation. In this case, there is no such violation, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims was proper.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the respondent has no protected life or liberty interest in Ohio's clemency proceedings, and the state's clemency procedure does not violate his Fifth Amendment rights. Therefore, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the district court's dismissal of the respondent's claims should be reinstated.
