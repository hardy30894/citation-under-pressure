ARGUMENT

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment identify the taxpayer against whom it is made. 26 U.S.C. § 6203. An individual partner is a distinct taxpayer from the partnership. Phillips v. Commissioner, 283 U.S. 589, 593 (1931). Consequently, an assessment against the partnership alone does not extend the ten-year collection period of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) to the partners. Burnet v. Sanford & Brooks Co., 282 U.S. 359, 361 (1931).

This Court has long held that the government may not proceed against a person’s property on the basis of an assessment that fails to name that person. United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961). The same principle bars collection here. Because no assessment was made against respondents within the three-year period of 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), the ordinary limitations period governs and has expired.

State-law joint-and-several liability does not alter the result. A judgment against the partnership is not a judgment against the partner, and collection from the partner’s assets requires a separate judgment. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960). The government obtained no such judgment within the applicable limitations period. Helvering v. Mitchell, 303 U.S. 391, 401 (1938).

The government’s reliance on partnership liability therefore fails for the same reason its assessment theory fails: the statute of limitations is measured against each separate taxpayer. Commissioner v. Sunnen, 333 U.S. 591, 597 (1948). Allowing collection without an individual assessment or judgment would nullify the statutory requirement that the taxpayer be identified. Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259 (1935).

The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the claims as unenforceable. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). The district court and the court of appeals properly affirmed that ruling.
