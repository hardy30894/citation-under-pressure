**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER IS AUTHORIZED BY THE REMEDY PROVISIONS OF 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) AND DOES NOT VIOLATE THE PRISON LITIGATION REFORM ACT.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”) permits a court to “grant prospective relief” in a prisoner civil rights action “if the court finds that such relief is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right, and is the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (1996). This remedial authority is broad and reflects Congress’s recognition that “[p]rospective relief is the primary remedy available to correct constitutional violations in the prison context.” *Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367, 380 (1992) (citing *Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460, 472 (1983)). The three-judge court’s population reduction order is precisely the type of narrowly tailored, prospective relief contemplated by the PLRA.

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts possess inherent equitable power to fashion remedies necessary to correct constitutional violations, including in prison conditions cases. *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971) (“[R]emedial judgments in these cases may range well beyond the scope of the constitutional violation to cover a whole range of problems confronting an affected school system.”); *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955) (“[T]he courts will have to consider whether the action of school authorities constitutes good faith implementation of the governing constitutional principles.”). This equitable authority is not diminished by the PLRA. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (preserving equitable relief where narrowly drawn and necessary).

The three-judge court’s factual findings amply support its remedial order. The court determined that severe overcrowding was the “primary cause” of unconstitutional conditions, including inadequate medical and mental health care. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 514 (2011) (affirming the three-judge court’s order). This conclusion is consistent with this Court’s recognition that “[o]vercrowding in prisons is a major cause of deplorable prison conditions.” *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978). The PLRA does not bar relief where, as here, the violation is systemic and the remedy is the least intrusive means of correction. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); *Rufo*, 502 U.S. at 380.

**II. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT PROPERLY EXERCISED JURISDICTION UNDER 28 U.S.C. § 2284 AND 18 U.S.C. § 3626.**

The PLRA does not divest courts of jurisdiction to hear prisoner civil rights actions or to issue remedial orders. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s jurisdiction was properly invoked under 28 U.S.C. § 2284, which authorizes a three-judge court to hear actions challenging prison conditions where “an injunction or other relief is sought as to the prison system of a State.” *See also* 28 U.S.C. § 2284(a) (1949) (permitting three-judge courts in cases involving “the constitutionality of the action of any officer or agency of a State”). This Court has repeatedly upheld the constitutionality of three-judge courts in prison conditions cases. *See, e.g., Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 682 (upholding a three-judge court’s order capping prison population).

The PLRA’s exhaustion and prospective relief provisions do not repeal § 2284 by implication. *See* *Posadas v. National City Bank*, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936) (“[A] statute will not be construed as repealing earlier statutes by implication unless the later statute is so repugnant that the two cannot stand together.”). The PLRA and § 2284 operate in harmony: the PLRA governs the scope of relief, while § 2284 governs the composition of the court. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); 28 U.S.C. § 2284.

**III. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER IS THE LEAST INTRUSIVE MEANS OF CORRECTING THE VIOLATION.**

The PLRA requires that prospective relief be “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court’s population cap satisfies this standard. The court found that overcrowding was the primary cause of constitutional violations and that other remedies—such as increased funding or expanded medical staff—had failed. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. at 514. This Court has recognized that population caps are a permissible remedy where less intrusive measures have proven inadequate. *See* *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 687 (“[A] court may order the reduction of prison populations if necessary to remedy constitutional violations.”).

The PLRA does not require courts to defer to legislative or executive remedies where those remedies have demonstrably failed. *See* *Swann*, 402 U.S. at 28 (“[T]he scope of the remedy is determined by the nature and extent of the constitutional violation.”). The three-judge court’s order is narrowly tailored to address the specific constitutional violations found, and it does not impose obligations beyond those necessary to correct the harm. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A); *Rufo*, 502 U.S. at 380.

**IV. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S ORDER DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT.**

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar prospective injunctive relief against state officials to remedy constitutional violations. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908) (“[A] suit against a state official to enjoin him from enforcing a state law in violation of the Federal Constitution is not a suit against the State.”). This principle applies with equal force in prison conditions cases. *See* *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. at 686 (upholding an injunction against state officials to remedy prison conditions).

The PLRA does not abrogate *Ex parte Young*. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A) (permitting prospective relief against state officials). The three-judge court’s order is consistent with this longstanding precedent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the three-judge court’s order. The order is authorized by the PLRA and 28 U.S.C. § 2284, is the least intrusive means of correcting the constitutional violations, and does not violate the Eleventh Amendment.
