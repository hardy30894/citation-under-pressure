Fernandez-Vargas respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before the statute’s effective date, and that applying it to him would be impermissibly retroactive.

This Court has long required a clear statement before giving a statute retroactive effect. In United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806), the Court stated that a statute should not be construed to operate retrospectively absent clear intent. The same principle appears in Ogden v. Blackledge, 2 Cranch 272, 275 (1805), Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534-35 (1922), United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926), United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928), Hassett v. Welch, 303 U.S. 303, 314 (1938), and Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964). Congress must speak plainly before a court will infer that it intended to attach new consequences to completed conduct.

Section 241(a)(5) contains no such clear statement as applied to Fernandez-Vargas. He illegally reentered in 1982, fifteen years before IIRIRA made the reinstatement statute effective. His relevant conduct was therefore complete long before Congress enacted the new regime. A statute is retroactive when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994); see also Mathews v. Kidder, Peabody & Co., 161 U.S. 625, 629-30 (1896); Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304, 314-16 (1945). That is exactly what § 241(a)(5) would do here. It would transform a prior deportation order and an old illegal reentry into an automatic reinstatement mechanism, while eliminating the possibility of obtaining relief that existed under earlier law.

The government may respond that the statute governs only Fernandez-Vargas’s continued unlawful presence after 1997. But the Court has repeatedly rejected that kind of characterization when the legal disability flows from a past act. In White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545, 554 (1903), and Railroad Commission v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 216 U.S. 579, 584-85 (1910), the Court looked to the statute’s practical effect, not merely its phrasing. Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988), likewise reaffirmed that retroactive application is disfavored and must be clearly authorized. Here, the triggering event is Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 reentry; the statute operates because of that completed past act, not because of any new conduct after enactment.

The retroactive effect is particularly severe because Fernandez-Vargas later married a United States citizen and sought to adjust status under the law then in force. Section 241(a)(5), as applied to him, would foreclose that avenue entirely. The Court has long been wary of legislation that destroys settled expectations or revives old liabilities without unmistakable language. See Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 2 Gall. 105, 139-40 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.); Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134, 147-48 (1938); Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno, 494 U.S. 827, 837-38 (1990). Fernandez-Vargas could not have ordered his affairs in 1982 or 1997 to avoid a statutory disability Congress had not yet created.

Nor is the government aided by labeling his presence a continuing violation. The Court has drawn a distinction between ongoing status and a new statute that imposes consequences because of past conduct. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 269-70; Greene, 376 U.S. at 160; Magnolia Petroleum, 276 U.S. at 162-63. Section 241(a)(5) is not merely prospective regulation of future illegal entry. It uses a pre-enactment reentry as the predicate for reinstatement and elimination of relief. That is retroactive in the constitutionally relevant sense.

The better reading, and the one required by the settled presumption against retroactivity, is that § 241(a)(5) applies only to aliens who reentered after the statute’s effective date. See Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) at 413; Shwab, 258 U.S. at 534-35; St. Louis, 270 U.S. at 3; Magnolia Petroleum, 276 U.S. at 162-63; Greene, 376 U.S. at 160. At minimum, if the statute is construed to reach pre-IIRIRA reentrants, its application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive. The Court should therefore grant the petition.
