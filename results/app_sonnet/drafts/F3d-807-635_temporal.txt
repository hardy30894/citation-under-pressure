ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard the district court should have applied below. In evaluating a motion for summary judgment, "the inferences to be drawn from the underlying facts contained in [the] materials must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." United States v. Diebold, Inc., 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that summary judgment is a drastic remedy that must be employed with care, particularly where the moving party's evidence leaves room for reasonable dispute as to what actually occurred. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (holding that a court may not resolve doubts in favor of the moving party but must credit reasonable inferences favoring the nonmovant); Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary procedures must be used "sparingly" where the evidence is susceptible of divergent inferences). Ybarra, as the movant on four of the seven disputed calls, bore the burden of establishing that no genuine dispute existed as to whether DISH actually placed a prerecorded-voice "call" to him within the meaning of the statute. The district court did not hold Ybarra to that burden. Instead, it resolved every ambiguity in the call records against DISH, the nonmovant—precisely the error Diebold and Sartor forbid.

II. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because the Record Does Not Establish, Without Dispute, That a Prerecorded Voice Message Was Delivered on the Four Contested Calls

DISH concedes liability on the three calls coded "Positive Voice," because the record shows without contradiction that a live person or an answering-machine greeting triggered the Cisco Dialer and the prerecorded message played. But the remaining four calls—three coded "Answering Machine" and one coded "Data Error-No Value"—present a materially different record. As to those calls, nothing in the log establishes that the dialer's positive-voice detection was triggered or that any message was in fact transmitted to Ybarra's line. The district court nonetheless treated the statutory phrase "any call" as satisfied by the mere placement of an outbound call, without regard to whether a message was ever delivered.

That construction cannot be reconciled with the ordinary meaning of the words Congress chose. "It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). A statute directed at calls "using" a prerecorded voice presupposes that the prerecorded voice was in fact used—i.e., actually communicated—not merely that a dialer attempted to reach the number. Where a call terminates in a "Data Error," logic and the plain language alike dictate that no communication occurred at all; there is no "call" using a prerecorded voice if no signal was ever completed. Cf. Postal Telegraph-Cable Co. v. Warren-Godwin Lumber Co., 251 U.S. 27, 29–30 (1919) (recognizing that liability for a failed transmission depends on what, if anything, was actually transmitted and received, not merely attempted). The same reasoning applies with equal force to the three "Answering Machine" calls, where the record does not affirmatively show that the machine's greeting was recognized by the dialer as a "positive voice" sufficient to trigger playback of the recorded message, as opposed to a call that simply rang out unanswered.

At minimum, this uncertainty in the record creates the kind of factual dispute that precludes summary judgment for Ybarra. Under Sartor and Poller, the district court was obliged to view the ambiguous call-log entries in the light most favorable to DISH, the nonmovant, and to deny summary judgment where reasonable inferences could support DISH's position that no prerecorded message was delivered. 321 U.S. at 627; 368 U.S. at 473. Instead, the court assumed the very fact in dispute—that a message was communicated—and thereby impermissibly resolved a contested inference against DISH. Diebold, 369 U.S. at 655.

III. The Proper Disposition Is Judgment for DISH on the Four Contested Calls

Because Ybarra failed to carry his burden of showing, without genuine dispute, that DISH's Cisco Dialer actually transmitted a prerecorded voice message on the calls resulting in "Answering Machine" or "Data Error" outcomes, the district court erred in granting him summary judgment on those four calls. Under the settled principle that ambiguous evidence must be construed against the party who bore the burden of proof and in favor of the nonmovant, Sartor, 321 U.S. at 627; Diebold, 369 U.S. at 655, this Court should reverse the partial summary judgment as to those four calls and render judgment for DISH, leaving intact only the $1,500 in damages attributable to the three "Positive Voice" calls on which DISH has conceded liability.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's partial summary judgment as to the four contested calls, render judgment for DISH on those calls, and remand for entry of a corrected final judgment reflecting damages limited to the three conceded calls.
