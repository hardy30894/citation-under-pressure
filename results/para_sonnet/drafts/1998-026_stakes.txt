**ARGUMENT**

**A district court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not entitle him to relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 when the defendant already knew of that right.**

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any violation of Rule 32(a)(2), however harmless, automatically entitles a defendant to collateral relief. That position cannot be reconciled with this Court's longstanding teaching that violations of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure are subject to harmless-error analysis and that collateral relief under § 2255 is reserved for errors that actually prejudice a defendant.

**I. This Court has consistently required a showing of prejudice before a Rule violation supports collateral relief.**

In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), this Court confronted a materially identical claim—a district court's failure to comply with the predecessor to Rule 32(a) by not personally addressing the defendant before sentencing. The Court held that such a violation, standing alone, did not entitle the defendant to relief under § 2255. The Court explained that "the failure of a trial court to ask a defendant represented by counsel whether he has anything to say before sentence is imposed is not of itself an error of the character or magnitude cognizable under a writ of habeas corpus." *Id.* at 428. The Court emphasized that § 2255 relief is reserved for defects that "inherently result[] in a complete miscarriage of justice" or are "inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure." *Id.* at 428.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), holding that a technical violation of Rule 11—there, the failure to inform a defendant of a mandatory special parole term—did not warrant collateral relief absent a showing that the violation affected the defendant's ability to understand and enter his plea knowingly. The Court explained that "a defendant who is technically in violation of Rule 11 is not automatically entitled to a collateral remedy," particularly where "the defendant does not argue that he was actually unaware of the special parole term." *Id.* at 783–84. The same reasoning controls here: Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that a defendant knows of his right to appeal, and where the defendant already possesses that knowledge, the very purpose the Rule serves has been vindicated, notwithstanding the district court's failure to comply with the Rule's formal directive.

**II. Harmless-error principles govern violations of procedural rules, including Rule 32(a)(2).**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) directs that "[a]ny error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded." This Court has applied that command broadly to violations of procedural rules designed to protect specific rights. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court articulated the touchstone for harmless-error review: whether the error had a "substantial influence" on the outcome, or whether, absent the error, the result would have been the same. *Id.* at 765. When a defendant already knows of his right to appeal, a court's failure to recite that right can have had no "substantial influence" on the defendant's decision not to appeal, because the defendant's knowledge—not the court's advisement—is what matters to the exercise of that right.

This Court's decision in *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), likewise confirms that even errors implicating a defendant's rights under the Federal Rules must be evaluated for their actual effect on the proceeding. The Court there explained that a defendant seeking relief for a rule violation "must show that the error actually affected the outcome," *id.* at 734, and it rejected the notion that certain categories of error are automatically reversible without regard to their practical consequence. Applying that framework, the failure to advise a defendant who is already aware of his appellate rights cannot be said to have affected the outcome of the case in any meaningful sense.

**III. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is fully served when the defendant has actual knowledge of the right to appeal.**

Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement is not an end in itself; it is a means of ensuring that defendants are aware of appellate rights they might otherwise lose through ignorance. Where, as here, the district court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero *knew* of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, the Rule's protective function has been accomplished notwithstanding the court's failure to recite the right aloud. To hold otherwise—to grant automatic relief regardless of actual knowledge—would elevate ritual over substance and would extend § 2255 relief far beyond the "complete miscarriage of justice" standard this Court set forth in *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, and because the Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error principles consistent with *Hill*, *Timmreck*, *Kotteakos*, and *Olano*, this Court should affirm the judgment below.
