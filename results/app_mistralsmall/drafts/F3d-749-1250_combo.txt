**ARGUMENT**

**I. The admission of Agent Jones’s expert testimony on credibility violated Federal Rule of Evidence 702 and this Court’s precedents.**

Agent Jones’s testimony that Stanley’s statements were “not worthy of credence” and that his invocation of faith was “deceptive” amounted to an impermissible opinion on credibility. This Court has long held that expert testimony on the credibility of a witness is inadmissible. *United States v. Amaral*, 488 F.2d 1148, 1153 (9th Cir. 1969) (citing *United States v. Walker*, 410 F.2d 1305, 1307 (7th Cir. 1969)); *United States v. Brown*, 411 F.2d 930, 932 (7th Cir. 1969) (“Expert testimony on the credibility of witnesses is not admissible.”). The Advisory Committee’s Note to Rule 702, which tracks this Court’s prior rulings, confirms that expert testimony on credibility “invades the province of the jury.” *See* Fed. R. Evid. 702 advisory committee’s note (citing *United States v. Amaral*, 488 F.2d at 1153). Agent Jones’s opinion that Stanley’s behavior was “common among the criminal element” and that “never in [his] career” had he seen an innocent person make such statements further transgressed this boundary. *See United States v. Brown*, 411 F.2d at 932 (expert may not opine on guilt or innocence).

**II. The admission of this testimony was plain error under Federal Rule of Evidence 103(a)(1).**

Stanley did not object to the admission of Agent Jones’s testimony at trial. However, plain error review applies where the error is “particularly egregious” and “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936); *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 n.14 (1982) (citing *Atkinson*). The admission of expert testimony on credibility is precisely the type of error that undermines the integrity of the judicial process. *See United States v. Amaral*, 488 F.2d at 1153; *United States v. Brown*, 411 F.2d at 932. The error here was plain, as it contravened settled precedent and the Federal Rules of Evidence.

**III. The erroneous admission of this testimony was not harmless.**

The government’s case hinged on circumstantial evidence and witness identifications, which were not overwhelming. Agent Jones’s testimony, particularly his assertion that Stanley’s invocation of faith was deceptive, was highly prejudicial. This Court has held that the erroneous admission of expert testimony on credibility is not harmless where the testimony may have influenced the jury’s assessment of the defendant’s credibility. *See United States v. Amaral*, 488 F.2d at 1153; *United States v. Walker*, 410 F.2d at 1307. The prejudice here was exacerbated by the fact that Stanley’s identity was initially concealed, making the jury’s assessment of his credibility critical.

**IV. The district court erred in denying Stanley’s motion to suppress his post-Miranda statements.**

Stanley’s initial false statement to police—identifying himself as Daniel Hill—was made in response to custodial interrogation without a knowing and voluntary waiver of his rights. The record reflects that Stanley was not advised of his *Miranda* rights until after he provided this false identification. *See Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 444 (1966) (“The person in custody must, prior to interrogation, be clearly informed that he has the right to remain silent.”). The district court’s failure to suppress this statement was error, as it violated Stanley’s Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination. *See id.* at 478-79.

**V. The evidence was insufficient to support the conviction for bank robbery with a dangerous weapon.**

The government’s theory at trial shifted from Stanley’s direct participation in the robbery to his role as a getaway driver. However, the evidence adduced at trial—including the GPS tracking device, witness identifications, and Stanley’s presence at the East Pine residence—was insufficient to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he aided and abetted the robbery. *See United States v. Dillard*, 438 F.2d 1343, 1347 (7th Cir. 1969) (aiding and abetting requires proof of participation in the crime with specific intent to commit the offense). The evidence showed only that Stanley was present at the residence where the stolen money was found, not that he participated in the robbery itself.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Stanley’s conviction should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
