**ARGUMENT**

The Constitution’s enumeration of qualifications for Members of Congress is exclusive, and the States may not add to or alter those qualifications. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Amendment 73’s provisions imposing term limits on candidates for the United States House of Representatives and Senate violate the Qualifications Clauses of the U.S. Constitution.

**I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES PROVIDE THE EXCLUSIVE QUALIFICATIONS FOR CONGRESSIONAL SERVICE.**

Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 of the Constitution provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, a citizen for seven years, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 sets analogous qualifications for Senators: age thirty, citizenship for nine years, and inhabitancy of the State. These provisions are not mere minimums; they are the *only* qualifications that may be imposed.

This Court has long recognized that the Constitution’s specification of qualifications for federal office is exclusive. In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member who met the constitutional qualifications. The Court stated that “the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 522. The reasoning applies with equal force to the States: if Congress cannot add qualifications, neither can a State.

The Framers deliberately chose to set uniform, national qualifications to prevent the very patchwork of state-imposed restrictions that Amendment 73 represents. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), this Court emphasized that the right to vote for Members of Congress “is a right and privilege of a citizen of the United States,” not merely a matter of state law. *Id.* at 663. Allowing States to impose additional qualifications would undermine the national character of the Congress.

**II. AMENDMENT 73 IS A QUALIFICATION, NOT A BALLOT ACCESS REGULATION.**

The State argues that Amendment 73 merely regulates ballot access, not qualifications. But the Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the distinction is “too fine a point.” The Amendment provides that a person who has served three terms “shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot.” This is a flat prohibition on candidacy—a qualification, not a mere regulation.

This Court has consistently held that a State may not impose substantive disqualifications under the guise of ballot access rules. In *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23 (1968), the Court struck down Ohio’s ballot access laws that effectively barred minor-party candidates, holding that the State could not “favor two particular parties” in a way that “violates the Equal Protection Clause.” *Id.* at 29. Similarly, in *Lassiter v. Northampton County Board of Elections*, 360 U.S. 45 (1959), the Court upheld a literacy test only because it was a “neutral” voting qualification, not a discriminatory barrier. Here, Amendment 73 is a substantive barrier that adds a new qualification—term limits—not found in the Constitution.

**III. THE FRAMERS’ INTENT AND HISTORICAL PRACTICE CONFIRM THE EXCLUSIVITY OF CONSTITUTIONAL QUALIFICATIONS.**

The debates at the Constitutional Convention confirm that the Framers rejected state-imposed qualifications. James Madison argued in *The Federalist No. 52* that the qualifications for Congress “were fixed by the Constitution, and not left to the discretion of the State legislatures.” This view was echoed by Justice Story, who wrote that “the Constitution has, therefore, wisely provided, that the qualifications of the representatives shall be such only, as are prescribed by the Constitution.” *Story on the Constitution* § 820 (1833).

Historical practice supports this reading. In *Hoban v. Hall*, 229 Ark. 416, 316 S.W.2d 185 (1958), the Arkansas Supreme Court itself recognized that a state constitutional amendment requiring candidates for Congress to take a “States’ Rights” oath was invalid because it added a qualification. The court held that “the qualifications of members of Congress are fixed by the Constitution of the United States, and the states are without power to add to or change them.” *Id.* at 420, 316 S.W.2d at 188.

**IV. THE STATE’S RESERVED POWERS DO NOT INCLUDE THE POWER TO ALTER FEDERAL QUALIFICATIONS.**

The State invokes the Tenth Amendment and the “reserved powers” of the people. But the power to set qualifications for federal office is not a power reserved to the States. In *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), this Court held that the Constitution is “supreme” and that States may not “retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control” the operations of the federal government. *Id.* at 436. Adding qualifications for Congress would directly impede the federal government’s ability to select its own members.

Moreover, the Qualifications Clauses are not merely default rules; they are affirmative grants of power to the federal government. In *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court held that the right to vote for Members of Congress is “a right secured by the Constitution” and that “the states are not, by virtue of the Constitution, free to place arbitrary restrictions on the exercise of that right.” *Id.* at 315.

**V. THE SEVERABILITY CLAUSE CANNOT SAVE THE STATE OFFICE PROVISIONS.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Sections 1 and 2 of Amendment 73 are not severable from the invalid Section 3. The test for severability, as stated in *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), is whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion. Here, the voters were presented with an “all or nothing” package that lumped state and federal term limits together. There is no evidence that the voters would have approved state term limits alone.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses are exclusive, and the States may not add to them. Amendment 73’s provisions regarding federal candidates are unconstitutional, and the entire amendment must fall.
