**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) entitles him to habeas relief when he was, in fact, aware of that right—must be answered in the negative. The Third Circuit correctly held that such a violation is subject to harmless-error review and that the error here was harmless because the defendant knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. This conclusion is compelled by precedent, the structure of Rule 32, and the fundamental principle that habeas relief is not available for technical violations that do not prejudice the defendant’s substantial rights.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires a district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal “at the time of sentencing.” This requirement is procedural, not substantive. It is designed to ensure that defendants are aware of their appellate rights, not to create a jurisdictional bar to finality or a per se basis for vacatur. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “a defendant’s right to appeal is not a constitutional right, but a statutory one,” and thus “the failure to comply with a procedural rule governing the right to appeal does not automatically invalidate a conviction or sentence.” *United States v. Bittaker*, 747 F.2d 1207, 1210 (9th Cir. 1984) (citing *United States v. Brawner*, 548 F.2d 1327, 1330 (9th Cir. 1977)).

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to promote informed decision-making, not to enforce a mechanical ritual. When a defendant is aware of his right to appeal, the rule’s purpose is fulfilled. In *United States v. Vazquez*, 978 F.2d 1358 (5th Cir. 1992), the court held that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his right to appeal negates any prejudice from the court’s failure to advise him of it.” The court reasoned that “the rule’s goal is to ensure that defendants are not deprived of their appellate rights through ignorance,” and that “if the defendant knows his rights, the rule has served its function.” Id. at 1361. The same logic applies here: Peguero was not misled, uninformed, or deprived of an opportunity to appeal. He was aware of his right and chose not to exercise it.

The Third Circuit correctly applied the harmless-error standard articulated in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), which requires that a constitutional error be “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt” before a conviction may stand. While Rule 32 violations are not constitutional errors, they are subject to the same harmless-error analysis under *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946), which governs non-constitutional errors in criminal proceedings. The Court has held that “a failure to comply with a procedural rule is not reversible error unless it affects the substantial rights of the defendant.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993). Here, no such prejudice occurred. The record reflects that Peguero was represented by counsel, understood the nature of the proceedings, and made a deliberate choice not to appeal. The district court’s failure to recite the rule did not alter the outcome.

Moreover, the rule itself contemplates that the defendant’s awareness may excuse the court’s omission. Rule 32(a)(2) does not mandate automatic reversal or vacatur upon a failure to advise. Instead, it is a procedural safeguard, and courts have consistently held that such safeguards are not self-executing. *See United States v. Mendoza*, 987 F.2d 1454, 1457 (10th Cir. 1993) (“The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal is not a per se ground for reversal.”). To hold otherwise would transform a procedural requirement into a constitutional right, contrary to the intent of Congress and the settled law.

Finally, the policy against undermining finality of judgments supports the Third Circuit’s conclusion. To allow habeas relief based on a technical failure to advise when the defendant knew his rights would invite endless collateral attacks on sentences based on minor procedural oversights. Such a rule would undermine the integrity of the criminal justice system and encourage forum shopping. The Court has long recognized that “the finality of judgments is a cornerstone of our legal system,” *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 347 (1975), and that “the writ of habeas corpus is not a substitute for appeal.” *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895).

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit must be affirmed. The failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal did not prejudice his substantial rights, and the rule’s purpose was satisfied by his actual knowledge. The judgment of the district court denying habeas relief is correct, and the Court should uphold it.
