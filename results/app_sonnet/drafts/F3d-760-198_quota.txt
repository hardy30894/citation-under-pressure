ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Disregarding Facially Sex-Neutral Incidents When Evaluating Moll's Hostile Work Environment Claim

A hostile work environment claim requires courts to examine "the totality of the circumstances," including "the frequency of the discriminatory conduct; its severity; whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, or a mere offensive utterance; and whether it unreasonably interferes with an employee's work performance." Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 23 (1993). Title VII protects employees from a workplace "permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult" sufficient to alter the conditions of employment. Meritor Sav. Bank v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 65 (1986) (quotation marks omitted). Critically, this totality-of-circumstances inquiry is not limited to conduct that is overtly sexual in nature. This Court has expressly held that "facially sex-neutral incidents" may be included among the "totality of the circumstances that courts consider in any hostile work environment claim, so long as a reasonable jury could link them to the plaintiff's gender." Alfano v. Costello, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002). The corollary is equally true: conduct that appears "sexually offensive" need not be the only conduct considered, and sex-neutral mistreatment—denial of promotions, disparate scheduling accommodations, and exclusion from workplace social events—can form part of the same hostile environment when tied to gender-based animus. Id.

The district court's approach—isolating only the notes and hotel-room telephone calls as the universe of relevant conduct—contravenes Alfano's instruction to consider the "totality of the circumstances." Moll's allegations that she was denied the ability to work from home while male colleagues were permitted to do so, that she was denied vacation requests granted to less-tenured male colleagues, that she and other women were excluded from work-related social events such as hockey games, and that Irving refused to advance her for promotion while advancing two male colleagues, are all sex-neutral on their face but plausibly linked to her gender given the pattern of disparate treatment alleged. A reasonable jury, considering these incidents together with the harassing notes and calls, could find that Moll's workplace was permeated with hostility because of her sex. The district court's failure to consider this evidence in combination was error requiring reversal.

II. Moll's Hostile Work Environment Claim Is Timely Under the Continuing Violation Doctrine

Even accepting that some incidents fell outside the 300-day filing period, hostile work environment claims are different in kind from discrete discriminatory acts. The Supreme Court has held that a hostile work environment claim is composed of "a single unlawful employment practice" and that "consideration of the entire scope of a hostile work environment claim, including behavior alleged outside the statutory time period, is permissible for the purposes of assessing liability, so long as an act contributing to that hostile environment takes place within the statutory time period." Nat'l R.R. Passenger Corp. v. Morgan, 536 U.S. 101, 117, 122 (2002). Because Moll alleges conduct—including the denial of work-from-home privileges, exclusion from social events, and denial of vacation—occurring within the limitations period, the entire course of conduct, including the earlier notes and calls, is properly considered part of a single actionable hostile work environment claim. Id. The district court's contrary conclusion cannot stand.

III. The Sham Issue of Fact Doctrine Does Not Bar Consideration of Non-Party Witness Testimony That Conflicts with Earlier Statements

On summary judgment, a court must resolve all ambiguities and draw all permissible factual inferences in favor of the party against whom summary judgment is sought. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986); see also Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986). The "sham issue of fact" doctrine is a narrow exception developed to prevent a party from defeating summary judgment by submitting an affidavit that flatly contradicts that same party's own prior sworn testimony without explanation. See Perma Research & Dev. Co. v. Singer Co., 410 F.2d 572, 578 (2d Cir. 1969). This Court has cautioned, however, that the doctrine must be applied "with caution" because "it is not the province of the court itself to decide what inferences should be drawn" from conflicting testimony; that task belongs to the jury. Hayes v. N.Y.C. Dep't of Corr., 84 F.3d 614, 619 (2d Cir. 1996). The rationale underlying the doctrine—preventing a party from manufacturing a factual dispute regarding her own conduct or knowledge—simply does not extend to a non-party witness whose later testimony may reflect refreshed recollection, clarification, or correction rather than manipulation of the litigation. Where, as here, a non-party witness's subsequent testimony conflicts with an earlier statement, that conflict presents a credibility question for the trier of fact, not a basis for disregarding the testimony as a matter of law. Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255.

IV. Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, and because genuine issues of material fact remain regarding Moll's disparate treatment, hostile work environment, retaliation, and equal pay claims—each of which must be evaluated under the familiar burden-shifting framework, see McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973), and the "materially adverse" standard applicable to retaliation, see Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006)—this Court should vacate the judgment below and remand for further proceedings.
