ARGUMENT

I.   The OCSLA Extends LHWCA Coverage Only to Workers Injured on the Outer Continental Shelf Itself

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act ("OCSLA") extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act ("LHWCA") to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires a direct connection between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted this provision to require a "situs-of-injury" test, holding that an employee must be injured on the outer Continental Shelf or on the waters above it to be eligible for OCSLA benefits. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc); see also Becker v. Tidewater, Inc., 586 F.3d 358, 366-67 (5th Cir. 2009); Pickett v. Petroleum Helicopters, Inc., 266 F.3d 366, 368 (5th Cir. 2001).

The Supreme Court's dictum in Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), supports this interpretation. Although the Court did not directly address the issue, it noted that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." Id. at 219 n.2. This statement suggests that the OCSLA has a situs requirement, which is consistent with the Fifth Circuit's interpretation.

In contrast, the Third Circuit's decision in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), adopted a "but for" test, which is overly broad and ignores the statutory language requiring a direct connection between the injury and the operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Id. at 809-11. The Ninth Circuit's adoption of a "substantial nexus" test in this case is similarly flawed, as it fails to provide a clear and predictable standard for determining eligibility for OCSLA benefits.

II.  The LHWCA Claim Fails Because Valladolid Was Not Injured on a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA provides compensation for maritime employees injured on navigable waters or in adjoining areas customarily used for loading, unloading, repairing, or building vessels. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). To qualify as an "adjoining area," a facility must have a functional relationship with maritime activities. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). The facility where Valladolid was injured, La Conchita, does not meet this test.

La Conchita is not an "adjoining area" because it is not used for maritime activities, and it is not adjacent to navigable waters. See Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982). Although it is used to process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, this is not a maritime activity. Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb's Welding I), 470 U.S. 414, 422-24 (1985).

The Ninth Circuit has held that a facility must be "used as an integral part of longshoring operations" to qualify as a maritime situs. Herron, 568 F.2d at 141. La Conchita does not meet this test, as it is not used for loading or unloading vessels, and it is not adjacent to a pier or dock. The fact that scrap metal from the offshore platforms is dumped at La Conchita does not convert it into a maritime situs, as this activity is not an integral part of longshoring operations. P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69, 82-83 (1979).

The Benefits Review Board correctly denied benefits under the LHWCA, as Valladolid's injury did not occur on a maritime situs. Williams v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 825 F.2d 246, 247 (9th Cir. 1987); Hurston v. Dir., Office of Workers' Comp. Programs, 989 F.2d 1547, 1548 n.3 (9th Cir. 1993).

In conclusion, the OCSLA extends LHWCA coverage only to workers injured on the outer Continental Shelf itself, and the LHWCA claim fails because Valladolid was not injured on a maritime situs. The court should reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision and uphold the Benefits Review Board's denial of benefits.
