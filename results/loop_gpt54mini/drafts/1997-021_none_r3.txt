Petitioner is entitled to relief for two independent reasons. First, the Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” polling rule is not rationally consistent with the National Labor Relations Act. Second, even if that rule were valid, the Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

I. The Board’s polling standard is inconsistent with the Act.

The Act protects both employee free choice and stable bargaining relationships. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33-34 (1937). But it does not authorize the Board to forbid a limited, neutral employee poll unless the employer already has proof sufficient to withdraw recognition. That rule collapses two distinct inquiries: whether objective circumstances justify testing employee sentiment, and whether the employer may thereafter rely on the result. A poll is an intermediate step designed to dispel uncertainty before the employer takes the far more serious step of ending recognition. Nothing in the Act requires the Board to eliminate that step. NLRB v. Waterman S.S. Corp., 309 U.S. 206, 226 (1940); NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 130-31 (1944).

The Board’s rule is especially unsound because it turns the rebuttable presumption of continued majority support into a practical barrier to ever testing that presumption. The presumption serves stability, but it is not a command that an employer ignore objective signs of changed employee sentiment. Radio Officers’ Union v. NLRB, 347 U.S. 17, 49-50 (1954). When an employer faces credible evidence of disaffection, the Act does not require it to bargain blindly or to risk liability for conducting a safeguarded poll. This Court has repeatedly recognized that labor doctrine must be applied with attention to practical realities, not formal abstraction. NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co., 304 U.S. 333, 346-47 (1938); Southern Steamship Co. v. NLRB, 316 U.S. 31, 47 (1942).

Nor does any statutory text specifically govern polling. In the absence of a congressional command, the Board may choose among permissible policies, but its choice must be rational and faithful to the Act’s structure. NLRB v. Truck Drivers Local Union No. 449, 353 U.S. 87, 96-97 (1957); NLRB v. Erie Resistor Corp., 373 U.S. 221, 236 (1963). The Board’s rule is neither. It makes polling nearly useless by demanding withdrawal-level proof before an employer may merely inquire whether the union still has majority support. That is not a reasonable accommodation of competing interests; it is an internal contradiction.

II. The Board’s no-doubt finding is unsupported by substantial evidence.

Even if the Board’s standard were accepted, its application cannot survive substantial-evidence review. Substantial evidence requires more than selective skepticism; the agency must account for the record as a whole. Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229-30 (1938); Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488-91 (1951).

The record here contained objective indications that Local 724 had lost support. Employees made statements to Allentown Mack suggesting that the union no longer enjoyed broad backing. The company then conducted a secret-ballot poll supervised by a priest, and the result was 19 to 13 against the union. That evidence did not compel a finding in petitioner’s favor, but it plainly supplied an objective basis for reasonable doubt. The Board nonetheless discounted much of the evidence by parsing comments too narrowly and by refusing to credit statements that were directly probative of employee sentiment.

Pre-1970 precedent confirms that such evidence is relevant and must be assessed realistically. In NLRB v. Century Cement Mfg. Co., 208 F.2d 84 (2d Cir. 1953), employee expressions of dissatisfaction were relevant to the employer’s doubt. In NLRB v. R. H. Oswald Co., 217 F.2d 193 (7th Cir. 1954), the court recognized that an employer may rely on objective signs of lost support without awaiting formal decertification. In NLRB v. Somerville Packing Co., 211 F.2d 574 (1st Cir. 1954), the court required contextual evaluation of employee statements rather than mechanical dismissal. And in Columbia Envelope Co. v. NLRB, 151 F.2d 571, 575 (7th Cir. 1945), the court explained that the Board must give reasons grounded in the record when it rejects probative evidence.

The Board did not do that here. It treated the case as though petitioner had to prove actual loss of majority support, rather than objective grounds for reasonable doubt. Those are different standards, and the Board’s shift between them infected its factual analysis. Consolidated Edison Co., 305 U.S. at 229-30; Universal Camera Corp., 340 U.S. at 488-91. On this record, a reasonable factfinder could not conclude that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt.

The Court should therefore deny enforcement and hold that the Board’s polling restriction is inconsistent with the Act and that its contrary finding is unsupported by substantial evidence. Allen-Bradley Co. v. NLRB, 286 U.S. 254, 260-61 (1932); Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 798-99 (1945).
