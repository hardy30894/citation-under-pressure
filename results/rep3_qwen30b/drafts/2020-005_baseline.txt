**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state court from exercising personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant unless the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the forum state’s market and its conduct is sufficiently connected to the plaintiff’s claims. In this case, the Montana Supreme Court erred in concluding that Ford Motor Company is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana based on the mere sale of vehicle parts within the state—conduct entirely unrelated to the fatal tire failure that caused Markkaya Jean Gullett’s death. The Court’s decision contravenes binding precedent and undermines the foundational principle that personal jurisdiction must be grounded in a causal nexus between the defendant’s forum-related activities and the plaintiff’s injury.

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires a Causal Connection Between the Defendant’s Forum Activities and the Plaintiff’s Claims**

Personal jurisdiction under the Due Process Clause is not merely a matter of form; it demands substance. A state may exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when “the litigation arises out of or relates to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). This requirement ensures that the defendant has fair notice that its actions could give rise to litigation in a particular jurisdiction—and that the forum state’s interest in adjudicating the dispute is legitimate. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980).

Here, Ford did not manufacture, sell, or distribute the defective Ford Explorer in Montana. The vehicle was assembled in Kentucky, first sold to a dealership in Washington State, then transferred to an Oregon resident, and finally brought into Montana by a private purchaser. No Ford sales representative ever interacted with Gullett in Montana. Nor did Ford advertise or promote the vehicle in Montana. The only alleged connection is Ford’s sale of replacement parts to dealerships in Montana—activities wholly disconnected from the design, manufacturing, or distribution of the vehicle involved in the accident.

This isolated, peripheral activity cannot satisfy the due process requirement. As this Court held in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985), “a defendant’s mere ‘random,’ ‘fortuitous,’ or ‘attenuated’ contacts with the forum are insufficient.” The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself of the privilege of doing business” in Montana by selling parts fails to recognize that such transactions are routine, incidental, and unrelated to the product at issue. The sale of spare parts does not transform Ford into a general presence in Montana, nor does it create a foreseeable link to a defect in a vehicle that never entered the state through Ford’s own channels.

**II. The Montana Supreme Court’s Rationale Conflicts with Precedent and Undermines Predictability**

The Montana Supreme Court relied on *Conway v. Benefis Health System*, 2013 MT 73, 297 P.3d 1200, and *Newbury v. State Farm*, 2008 MT 156, 184 P.3d 1021—cases involving insurance coverage and contract disputes—not on any authority addressing personal jurisdiction over manufacturers. These decisions do not support the proposition that a corporation can be subjected to jurisdiction based on passive, indirect sales of components. Indeed, they concern the admissibility of medical bills, not the reach of personal jurisdiction—a distinction critical to the analysis.

More fundamentally, the Montana Supreme Court’s approach disregards the constitutional limits established in *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987), which emphasized that “the relationship between the defendant, the forum, and the litigation” must be “substantial” and “not arbitrary or unfair.” Here, the relationship is attenuated: Ford had no role in the vehicle’s final transfer to Montana, no control over its use, and no foreseeability that a defect in a part manufactured years earlier would cause harm in a state where the company had no direct business operations.

The Court’s reasoning invites a dangerous expansion of personal jurisdiction. If a manufacturer can be haled into court in every state where it sells parts—even if those parts were never used in the product that caused harm—the result is a system of “forum shopping” and unpredictable liability. Such a regime violates the fundamental fairness required by due process. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945) (“[A] defendant sued in a foreign jurisdiction must have certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.’”).

**III. The Absence of Causation Renders Jurisdiction Unconstitutional**

The crux of the inquiry is whether Ford’s contacts with Montana caused Gullett’s injuries. The answer is unequivocally no. The tread separation occurred during normal operation on a Montana highway, but the vehicle had been sold and transported through multiple states before reaching Montana. The defect originated in the manufacturing process, not in any act or omission by Ford in Montana. There is no evidence that Ford designed, tested, or marketed the tire for use in Montana. Nor is there any indication that Ford’s Montana-based part sales influenced the design or safety of the vehicle.

To hold otherwise would permit plaintiffs to litigate in any state where a component was sold—even if that sale played no role in the injury. That would violate the core principle that jurisdiction must be tied to the cause of action. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 924 (2011) (“[T]he mere fact that a product is sold in a State does not establish sufficient contact with that State to justify the exercise of personal jurisdiction.”).

**Conclusion**

For the reasons stated above, the Montana Supreme Court’s decision exceeds the bounds of due process. Personal jurisdiction must be rooted in a meaningful connection between the defendant’s forum-related conduct and the plaintiff’s claim. Here, Ford’s sale of parts in Montana bears no causal relationship to the tire failure that killed Gullett. To subject Ford to jurisdiction in Montana would be arbitrary, unfair, and contrary to settled constitutional principles. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be reversed.
