**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits to employees injured “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This provision does not create a federal workers’ compensation regime for injuries occurring anywhere on land or water simply because the employee works for an offshore operator. Rather, it applies only when the injury occurs *on the outer continental shelf itself*—that is, on the submerged lands seaward of state jurisdiction, or on artificial islands or fixed structures erected thereon. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore even where there is no physical or functional connection to the shelf, misreads the statutory text, contradicts binding precedent, and undermines the carefully drawn geographic boundaries of OCSLA.

### I. THE OCSLA’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION PROVISION APPLIES ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF

The plain language of § 1333(b) makes clear that coverage is triggered only when an injury occurs “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The phrase “operations conducted on” the outer continental shelf is not a mere formality—it establishes a *situs-of-operations* requirement, which necessarily implies a *situs-of-injury* requirement. As the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized, “Congress generally intends the words of a statute to be given their ordinary meaning.” *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 223, 231 (1989). The ordinary meaning of “on” is spatial: an injury occurring “on” the outer continental shelf must occur *at* or *upon* that geographic area.

This interpretation is consistent with *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), where the Court held that OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, § 1333(a)(2)(A), applies only to “the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” Id. at 219. The Court explicitly noted that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to § 1333(b). If the scope of OCSLA is determined by *locale*, then an injury occurring onshore—outside the outer continental shelf—cannot fall within the statute’s reach, regardless of the employee’s job duties or the nature of the operations.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, which permits recovery for injuries occurring onshore so long as there is a “but for” connection to offshore operations, ignores this foundational principle. In *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), the Third Circuit rejected such a broad reading, holding that “the injury must occur as a result of operations on the shelf, not merely as a result of employment with a company engaged in such operations.” Id. at 810. The court correctly observed that “Congress did not intend to extend federal workers’ compensation coverage to every injury suffered by an employee of an offshore operator.” Id. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to adopt a “substantial nexus” test, which effectively eliminates the geographic boundary of the outer continental shelf, is inconsistent with *Tallentire* and *Curtis*.

### II. THE SUPREME COURT’S FOOTNOTE IN TALLENTIRE IS NOT DISPOSITIVE, BUT IT REINFORCES THE SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

The Ninth Circuit suggests that the Supreme Court’s footnote in *Tallentire*—which states that “only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b)” and that “this provision has no bearing on this case”—is mere dictum. *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore*, 6941 (9th Cir. 2010). But even if dictum, the Court’s observation that § 1333(b) is subject to a “situs requirement” is not “unconsidered” or “unpersuasive.” The Court was addressing the very issue before us: whether OCSLA’s coverage is determined by *situs* or *status*. The footnote reflects the Court’s understanding that the outer continental shelf is the *situs* of coverage, and that injuries occurring elsewhere—on land or on waters above the shelf—fall outside the statute’s scope.

This interpretation is further supported by *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), where the Court held that a worker injured on a platform located in state waters was not entitled to LHWCA benefits because the platform was not on the outer continental shelf. Id. at 422–24. The Court emphasized that “the outer continental shelf is a distinct geographic area,” and that “Congress intended to limit the application of the LHWCA to injuries occurring on the shelf itself.” Id. at 423. The same logic applies here: if a worker injured on a platform in state waters is not covered, then a worker injured onshore—three miles from the shelf—cannot be covered under § 1333(b).

### III. THE ONSHORE FACILITY WAS NOT A MARITIME SITUS UNDER THE LHWCA

Even if § 1333(b) were interpreted to extend coverage beyond the shelf, the injury did not occur on a maritime situs. The LHWCA requires that an injury occur “upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978), “functional relationship” test, which considers whether the site is “used as an integral part of longshoring operations.” Id. at 141.
