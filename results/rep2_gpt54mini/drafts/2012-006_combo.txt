The court of appeals erred in placing on petitioner the burden to disprove continued membership in the conspiracy after the limitations period. The government, not the defendant, bears the burden to prove every element of the charged conspiracy within the limitations period beyond a reasonable doubt. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (constitutional due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) (“the burden is on the government to prove every element of the offense charged”). A conspiracy is a continuing offense, but continuing-offense doctrine does not invert the ordinary burden of proof. See Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912) (conspiracy “continues until the accomplishment or abandonment of the purpose of the conspiracy”); United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910) (conspiracy may persist so long as its “purpose” remains active).

Long before the modern limitations cases, this Court held that when the government proceeds on a continuing offense, it must show the offense persisted during the relevant time. In United States v. Cook, 17 Wall. 168, 179-80 (1872), the Court explained that the statute of limitations is a matter the prosecution must overcome when the indictment reveals timeliness is at issue; the government must prove the offense was committed within the statutory period. See also United States v. Scharton, 285 U.S. 518, 521-22 (1932) (limitations is a “matter of defense” only in the sense that the indictment need not anticipate it, but where the issue is raised the government must meet it); Biddinger v. Commissioner of Police, 245 U.S. 128, 135-36 (1917) (limitations bars prosecution unless the government can bring the charge within the permissible period).

That rule applies with full force to conspiracy. The government must prove the conspiracy existed during the limitations period and that the accused was a member of it during that time. See Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; Brown v. Elliott, 225 U.S. 392, 400-01 (1912) (conspiracy is a continuing agreement, and liability depends on participation in the unlawful combination); Clune v. United States, 159 U.S. 590, 595-96 (1895) (government must prove the agreement and defendant’s participation). If a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal before the limitations period, that evidence negates the government’s proof of continuing membership; it does not create a new affirmative burden on the defense to disprove an essential element. See Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640, 646-47 (1946) (co-conspirator liability rests on continued association with the conspiracy until withdrawal); United States v. Manton, 107 F.2d 834, 839-40 (2d Cir. 1939) (withdrawal ends conspiratorial liability going forward).

The court of appeals’ approach also conflicts with the settled rule that the prosecution bears the burden to prove timeliness when the statute of limitations is implicated. In United States v. Cook, 17 Wall. at 179-80, the Court held that although an indictment need not negative limitations in its pleading, the government must prove the offense occurred within the period once the issue is raised. Scharton, 285 U.S. at 521-22, reiterated that limitations is not a mere technicality but a substantive protection the government must satisfy. That principle cannot be nullified by recasting the defendant’s withdrawal evidence as an affirmative burden. Where withdrawal is shown, the government must prove either that the defendant remained in the conspiracy or that the withdrawal was ineffective. See Hyde, 225 U.S. at 369-70; Pinkerton, 328 U.S. at 646-47.

Nor does due process permit shifting the burden to the accused to prove he was not a conspirator during the limitations period. In Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432, 453-60 (1895), the Court recognized the “presumption of innocence” as a rule of evidence requiring the government to establish guilt. Davis, 160 U.S. at 488, likewise rejected any regime that would relieve the prosecution of proving essential facts. The court below’s rule effectively requires the defendant to prove non-membership or withdrawal, even though membership during the limitations period is part of the government’s case. That is incompatible with Winship, 397 U.S. at 364, and with the longstanding rule that reasonable doubt must be resolved in favor of the accused. See Coffin, 156 U.S. at 460.

The proper rule is straightforward: once a defendant puts withdrawal before the limitations period in issue, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period. That conclusion follows from Cook, Scharton, Hyde, Kissel, Brown, Clune, Manton, Davis, and Coffin. The judgment should be reversed.
