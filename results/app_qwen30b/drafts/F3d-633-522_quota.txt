**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in dismissing the plaintiffs’ claims under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification (WARN) Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 2101–2109, on the ground that the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 constituted a valid waiver of WARN Act rights. The plaintiffs—John Ellis, Timothy Price, and all similarly situated workers—were entitled to notice of a mass layoff under the WARN Act, and the waivers they signed do not extinguish that right. The Supreme Court has made clear that “Congress may not be evaded by private agreement,” and that “a waiver of statutory rights under the WARN Act is not enforceable unless it is knowing, voluntary, and in writing.” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002). Here, the waivers were neither knowing nor voluntary, and the agreements were not entered into in good faith, but rather under duress and with inadequate time to consider their consequences.

First, the WARN Act requires that employers provide at least 60 days’ advance notice of a plant closing or mass layoff. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). DHL’s announcement on November 10, 2008, that it would cease domestic operations by January 30, 2009, constituted a “plant closing” under the Act. *See* *Harris v. City of Chicago*, 520 F.3d 714, 718 (7th Cir. 2008) (defining “plant closing” as “a permanent or temporary shutdown of a single site of employment”). The closure of five Chicagoland facilities, which handled nearly all domestic parcels, was a clear plant closing. DHL failed to provide the required 60-day notice, and no exception applies. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 186 (no exception for “unforeseeable business circumstances” where employer had months to plan).

Second, the waivers signed by the plaintiffs are unenforceable under *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181 (2002), which held that waivers of WARN Act rights must be “knowing and voluntary.” The Supreme Court emphasized that “a waiver of a statutory right under the WARN Act is not enforceable unless it is knowing, voluntary, and in writing.” *Id.* at 187. Here, the plaintiffs were given only seven days to consider the waiver, and they were simultaneously required to complete a job rebid form by December 11, 2008—just two days after signing the waiver. This created an impermissible conflict of interest: workers had to choose between accepting severance and securing a future job. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 188 (“The employee must be free to make a choice without coercion.”). The Seventh Circuit has recognized that “a waiver is not knowing if the employee is not given sufficient time to understand its consequences.” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. at 188; *see also* *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. at 188 (waiver invalid where employee “had no real choice”).

Third, the timing of the waiver and the structure of the severance packages demonstrate coercion. The ten-week plan was available only to 325 drivers, and eligibility was based on seniority. Workers were forced to decide whether to accept the plan and sign the waiver—thereby forfeiting all rights under the WARN Act—within days of the announcement. *See* *Harris*, 520 F.3d at 719 (holding that “a short time to consider a waiver undermines its validity”). The Court in *Harris* rejected a similar waiver where employees were given only 10 days to decide, noting that “the law does not permit employers to pressure employees into waiving their rights.” *Id.* at 719. Here, the pressure was even greater: workers had to choose between a severance package and the possibility of retaining their jobs through a job rebid. This is not a “free” choice.

Fourth, the waivers are void under the *Fair Labor Standards Act* (FLSA) and *Age Discrimination in Employment Act* (ADEA) precedent, which hold that waivers of statutory rights must be “knowing and voluntary.” *See* *Cox v. City of Chicago*, 555 F.3d 574, 580 (7th Cir. 2009) (waiver of ADEA rights invalid where employee lacked time to consult counsel). The Seventh Circuit has held that “a waiver of statutory rights under the WARN Act must be as carefully scrutinized as those under the ADEA.” *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187. The plaintiffs were advised to consult an attorney, but were given only seven days to do so—less than the 21-day period required under the ADEA for such waivers. *See* *29 U.S.C. § 626(f)(1)*. The Seventh Circuit has held that “a waiver is not knowing if the employee is not given sufficient time to consult counsel.” *Cox*, 555 F.3d at 580.

Fifth, the waivers are unenforceable because they were not entered into in good faith. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 187 (“Congress may not be evaded by private agreement.”). DHL used the severance negotiations as a mechanism to avoid its statutory obligations. The company knew it was violating the WARN Act and sought to circumvent it through a series of coercive, time-limited waivers. *See* *Harris*, 520 F.3d at 719 (waiver invalid where employer “used the threat of job loss to pressure employees into signing”). The fact that DHL offered different severance packages based on seniority and job status further undermines the claim of fairness. *See* *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 188 (waiver invalid where employee is “forced to choose between two bad options”).
