**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s systematic and continuous contacts with Montana support the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford in this case.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the defendant’s contacts with the forum state are such that the defendant “purposefully avails itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State.” *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this standard and concluded that Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts, advertising, and the presence of authorized dealerships—demonstrate that Ford “purposefully availed” itself of the Montana market. *See Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Jud. Dist. Ct.*, 443 P.3d 407, 420 (Mont. 2019).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that a defendant’s deliberate targeting of a forum state’s market, even if not directly causing the plaintiff’s injury, suffices to establish specific jurisdiction. *See Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014) (“[T]he defendant’s relationship with the plaintiff or the plaintiff’s rights . . . must arise out of contacts that the defendant himself creates with the forum State.”). Here, Ford’s nationwide distribution of replacement parts and its advertising in Montana—activities that are not merely “fortuitous” or “attenuated”—clearly satisfy this requirement. *See Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Ct. of Cal., San Francisco Cty.*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017) (distinguishing between “purposeful availment” and “random, fortuitous, or attenuated” contacts).

Moreover, the Montana Supreme Court correctly rejected Ford’s argument that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle at issue was not originally sold in Montana. *Ford Motor Co.*, 443 P.3d at 421. This Court has made clear that a defendant’s forum-state contacts need not be tied to the specific transaction giving rise to the lawsuit. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984) (holding that jurisdiction may be based on “continuous and systematic” contacts with the forum, even if unrelated to the plaintiff’s claims). Ford’s extensive business activities in Montana—including the sale of parts and service support—demonstrate that it purposefully availed itself of the Montana market, and thus specific jurisdiction is proper.

**II. The Montana Supreme Court’s holding is consistent with this Court’s precedents on specific jurisdiction.**

This Court has repeatedly upheld jurisdiction where a defendant’s forum-state activities are sufficiently related to the plaintiff’s claims, even if the defendant’s conduct did not directly cause the injury. *See, e.g., Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 788-89 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum-state activities were “purposefully directed” at the forum and caused harm there). In *Calder*, the Court held that jurisdiction was proper because the defendant’s intentional tortious acts were aimed at the forum state, even though the defendant did not physically enter the state. *Id.* at 789.

Similarly, in *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Jud. Dist. Ct.*, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this principle. The court found that Ford’s nationwide distribution of replacement parts and its advertising in Montana were sufficiently related to the plaintiff’s claims, as the defective tire that caused the accident was a Ford product sold in the stream of commerce. *Ford Motor Co.*, 443 P.3d at 421. This reasoning aligns with *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), which held that jurisdiction is proper where the defendant’s forum-state activities are “purposefully directed” at the forum and give rise to the plaintiff’s claims.

**III. Ford’s reliance on *BMS* is misplaced.**

Ford argues that *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Ct. of Cal.*, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), precludes jurisdiction in this case. However, *BMS* is inapposite. In *BMS*, the Court held that jurisdiction was improper because the defendant’s forum-state contacts were not sufficiently related to the plaintiffs’ claims, as the defendant had not purposefully availed itself of the California market in connection with the specific drug at issue. *Id.* at 1781. Here, by contrast, Ford’s contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts and advertising—are directly related to the plaintiff’s claims, as the defective tire was a Ford product sold in the stream of commerce. *See Ford Motor Co.*, 443 P.3d at 421.

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford’s systematic and continuous contacts with Montana support the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford in this case. Ford’s reliance on *BMS* is misplaced, and its argument that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle at issue was not originally sold in Montana is unavailing. The Court should affirm the judgment below.
