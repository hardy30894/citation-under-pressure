# ARGUMENT

**A grant of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.**

## I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Satisfies § 1255(a)’s Admission Requirement.

Congress provided that once an alien is granted TPS, “the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that this language does real work: because nonimmigrant status by definition requires admission, deeming a TPS recipient to be “in . . . lawful status as a nonimmigrant” necessarily deems that recipient to have satisfied the antecedent requirement of admission. *Flores v. U.S. Citizenship & Immigration Servs.*, 718 F.3d 548, 553–55 (6th Cir. 2013). As the Ninth Circuit later explained, “[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.” *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). Nonimmigrant status categories under 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) universally presuppose admission; there is no such thing as nonimmigrant status untethered from an admission event. Section 1254a(f)(4) borrows that concept deliberately, and this Court should give the words their ordinary, technical meaning within the INA’s scheme. *See Robinson v. Shell Oil Co.*, 519 U.S. 337, 341 (1997) (statutory language must be read “in [its] context and with a view to [its] place in the overall statutory scheme”).

The statutory definition of “admission”—“the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer,” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A)—does not foreclose this reading. Congress is free to specify, as it did in § 1254a(f)(4), that a particular status determination shall be treated as satisfying that definition. Nothing in the text limits § 1254a(f)(4)’s operation to aliens who were inspected at the border; to the contrary, Congress wrote the provision to apply to “the alien,” without regard to the alien’s manner of entry. Had Congress intended to exclude aliens who entered without inspection from the benefit of § 1254a(f)(4), it could easily have said so, just as it excluded certain classes of aliens elsewhere in the INA. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.”). Congress imposed no such limitation here.

## II. The Statutory Structure and Purpose of the TPS Program Confirm That Congress Intended TPS to Operate as an Admission.

TPS was designed to permit aliens who cannot safely return to their home countries to remain and to build a life in the United States, including, where eligible, the ability to adjust status. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) as petitioners urge gives full effect to every word of both provisions without rendering any language superfluous. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (a statute should be construed so that “no part will be inoperative or superfluous”). Section 1255(a) requires an alien to have been “inspected and admitted or paroled”; § 1254a(f)(4) simply identifies TPS as one route by which that threshold requirement is met, just as parole under § 1182(d)(5) satisfies the same requirement through an entirely different administrative mechanism unconnected to the alien’s physical entry. Treating a TPS grant as an admission does not collapse the distinct concepts of “admission” and “status” identified by the Third Circuit; rather, it recognizes that Congress created a legal fiction converting an alien’s status into the functional equivalent of admission for the limited purpose of adjustment eligibility—no more anomalous than the fiction of parole, which likewise satisfies § 1255(a) without any inspection at a port of entry accompanying an unauthorized entry.

Nor does this reading render § 1254a(h)’s supermajority requirement superfluous. Section 1254a(h) addresses special congressional legislation adjusting the status of entire classes of TPS beneficiaries by statute; it says nothing about the ordinary case-by-case eligibility determination under § 1255(a) that Congress separately authorized through § 1254a(f)(4). The two provisions serve different purposes and coexist without conflict.

## III. The Sixth and Ninth Circuits’ Reasoning Should Control, Not the Contrary View Adopted Below.

The panel below rejected the Sixth and Ninth Circuits’ interpretation, but its reasoning does not withstand scrutiny. The panel reasoned that TPS “cannot be an ‘admission’ because § 1254a requires an alien to be present in the United States to be eligible for TPS.” But presence in the United States is equally a precondition for adjustment of status generally, see 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a) (“alien whose status . . . is present in the United States”), and does not logically preclude Congress from deeming a later legal event—the TPS grant itself—to function as an admission for purposes of that later application. The panel’s reliance on the general/specific canon, *see Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432, cuts the other way: Congress specifically legislated in § 1254a(f)(4) to address adjustment eligibility for TPS recipients, and that specific provision should control over the general admission requirement of § 1255(a). *See Long Island Care at Home, Ltd. v. Coke*, 551 U.S. 158, 170 (2007) (specific statutory provisions govern over general ones).

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a grant of TPS satisfies § 1255(a)’s requirement that an alien be “inspected and admitted or paroled,” and reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit.
