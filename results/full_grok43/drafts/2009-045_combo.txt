The district court acted within its discretion in enhancing the fee award to account for the exceptional quality of representation and results obtained. Courts of equity have long recognized that compensation for counsel must reflect the character of the services rendered and the benefits secured for the class. Trustees v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527 (1881); Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116 (1885). This principle rests on the inherent authority of the court to ensure that those who create a common fund or achieve substantial relief receive remuneration commensurate with the value of their efforts. Winton v. Amos, 255 U.S. 373 (1921); Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co., 259 U.S. 101 (1922).

The same considerations govern the measure of a reasonable fee when success is extraordinary. Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161 (1939). In determining what is reasonable, courts examine the skill displayed, the difficulties overcome, and the magnitude of the outcome. Oelrichs v. Spain, 82 U.S. 211 (1872); Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. 363 (1851). An award that merely multiplies hours by an ordinary hourly rate fails to capture these elements when counsel’s performance far surpasses the norm and produces results of lasting public importance. Stewart v. Sonneborn, 98 U.S. 187 (1878).

Because the lodestar calculation begins with market rates that assume ordinary competence and ordinary success, an enhancement remains available in the rare case where specific findings demonstrate that those assumptions do not hold. The district court’s detailed observations supplied the necessary predicate, and the judgment should be affirmed.
