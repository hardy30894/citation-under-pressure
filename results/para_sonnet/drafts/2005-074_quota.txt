**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens, Like Petitioner, Who Reentered the United States Before the Statute's 1997 Effective Date**

This Court's two-step retroactivity framework governs. First, courts ask "whether Congress has expressly prescribed the statute's proper reach," and if not, "whether the new statute would have retroactive effect." *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994). Because "congressional enactments…will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result," *Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988), silence favors the alien. Section 241(a)(5) contains no date restriction and no clause stating that it reaches reentries predating its enactment. That silence is telling, particularly because Congress elsewhere in the same statutory scheme expressly specified effective dates and retroactive scope when it intended them. *See Lindh v. Murphy*, 521 U.S. 320, 330 (1997) (drawing a negative inference from Congress's specification of retroactivity in one provision but not another within the same Act). Under *Lindh*'s interpretive method, Congress's failure to specify that Section 241(a)(5) reaches pre-1997 reentries "implies" that it does not.

This reading is reinforced by *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001), which held that IIRIRA's elimination of discretionary relief did not apply to aliens whose relevant conduct preceded the statute's enactment. The Court there invoked "the longstanding principle of construing any lingering ambiguities in deportation statutes in favor of the alien," quoting *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 449 (1987). *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 320. That canon resolves any doubt here: Section 241(a)(5)'s temporal silence must be construed against reinstating an order and stripping relief based on conduct—a 1982 reentry—that occurred fifteen years before the statute existed.

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Petitioner's Pre-1997 Reentry Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if the text could be read to reach Petitioner, that application would have an impermissible retroactive effect because it "would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party's liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Petitioner's reentry in 1982 was a completed act. At that time, no statute barred him from ever seeking adjustment of status based on marriage to a citizen; that categorical bar did not exist until 1997. Applying Section 241(a)(5) now "attaches new legal consequences" to that 1982 conduct and to nineteen years of settled life built afterward—precisely the harm *Landgraf* forbids. *Id.* at 270.

*St. Cyr* is directly analogous. There, the Court refused to apply IIRIRA's elimination of § 212(c) relief to aliens who pleaded guilty before the statute's enactment, reasoning that aliens "almost certainly relied upon" the continued availability of relief when deciding how to proceed with their lives, and that retroactive application would attach "a new disability" to their past decisions. 533 U.S. at 321–23. Petitioner's reliance is equally, if not more, substantial: he remained in the United States, married a United States citizen, and pursued adjustment of status precisely because such relief was available to him at the time of his conduct and for years thereafter. The Tenth Circuit's demand that Petitioner show a "protectable expectation" akin to a vested right misapplies *St. Cyr*, which required only reasonable reliance on the pre-existing legal landscape, not a guarantee of a particular outcome. *Id.* at 325.

The retroactivity presumption is not limited to procedural rules or to statutes that operate on primary conduct alone; it extends to statutes that alter the legal significance of completed transactions, including changes to substantive entitlements and remedies. *See Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 950 (1997) (refusing to apply an expanded qui tam liability provision to pre-enactment conduct because doing so would "attach[] new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment"); *Miller v. French*, 530 U.S. 327, 344–45 (2000) (reaffirming that the presumption against retroactivity guards substantive rights, not merely procedural expectations). Here, the "substantive right" at stake—the statutory eligibility to seek adjustment of status—was extinguished by operation of a rule enacted long after Petitioner's reentry, and the elimination reaches back to erase relief for conduct that could not have been guided by a rule that did not yet exist.

Functionally, as *Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 357–59 (1999), instructs, courts must examine "the point at which the relevant activity occurs" rather than the date of the reinstatement order itself. The relevant activity here—illegal reentry—occurred in 1982. Fixing the retroactivity inquiry instead on the 2001 reinstatement order, as the Tenth Circuit did, allows the government to bootstrap a present-day enforcement action into cover for erasing rights tied to decades-old, completed conduct.

Because Section 241(a)(5) neither clearly reaches pre-enactment reentries nor may permissibly be applied to erase relief attached to Petitioner's 1982 reentry and subsequent reliance, the judgment below should be reversed.
