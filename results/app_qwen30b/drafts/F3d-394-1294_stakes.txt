**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that the amended trust established by Ralph H. Davis does not qualify for a marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7) of the Internal Revenue Code. The trust, as amended by the April 9, 1996 Amendment to the Declaration of Trust, creates a qualifying income interest for life in Evelyn L. Davis, the surviving spouse, and satisfies all statutory requirements for a Qualified Terminable Interest Property (QTIP) deduction. The Commissioner’s contrary determination rests on a misreading of the trust’s terms and an erroneous application of the “no power to appoint” requirement under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii). This Court should reverse.

**I. The Trust Conferred Upon Evelyn Davis an Unrestricted, Lifetime Income Interest in All Trust Income, Satisfying the “Qualifying Income Interest for Life” Requirement**

Under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii), a surviving spouse has a “qualifying income interest for life” if he or she is “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” The Amendment to the Declaration of Trust unambiguously grants Evelyn Davis “all of the net income from the trust estate” during her lifetime, payable in “quarter annual or more frequent installments.” This language is not subject to a discretionary limitation. The trust provides that the trustee “shall pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse” all such income, and the trustee’s discretion is limited to determining “what is proper for the health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare of the surviving spouse in accordance with the surviving spouse’s accustomed manner of living.” This standard does not permit the trustee to withhold income; rather, it governs the *timing* and *method* of distribution, not the *amount* or *existence* of the income interest.

This Court has held that a surviving spouse’s interest is “qualifying” when the trust provides for the payment of all income to the spouse, even if the trustee has discretion to determine the timing or purpose of distributions. *In re Estate of Hensley*, 988 F.2d 1008, 1011 (9th Cir. 1993) (holding that a trust providing for “income for the health, education, maintenance, and support” of the surviving spouse satisfied the income interest requirement, even where the trustee had discretion to determine the amount). Similarly, in *Estate of Hensley*, the court emphasized that “the key is whether the spouse is entitled to all the income, not whether the trustee has discretion to determine how much to distribute.” Id. at 1012. Here, the trust does not permit the trustee to reduce or deny the income interest; it only permits the trustee to determine how best to apply the income to the spouse’s needs. The interest is therefore “all the income,” payable at least quarterly, and thus satisfies the statutory requirement.

**II. The Trust Does Not Confer a Power to Appoint on Any Third Party, Satisfying the “No Power to Appoint” Requirement**

The Commissioner argues that the trust violates § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii) because Evelyn Davis, as trustee, has a power to appoint trust principal to others. This argument misapprehends the nature of the trustee’s authority. The trust grants Evelyn Davis the power to serve as *successor trustee*, not as a beneficiary with a power to appoint. As trustee, she has no authority to appoint trust assets to anyone other than herself. The trust explicitly provides that she may invade principal *for her own benefit* only if her own income and assets are insufficient to maintain her health, support, and maintenance. This is not a power to appoint to third parties; it is a limited power to access principal for her own support, which is consistent with the purpose of a marital trust.

The Supreme Court has made clear that a surviving spouse’s power to invade principal for her own benefit does not violate the “no power to appoint” rule. In *Estate of Bowers v. Commissioner*, 462 U.S. 559 (1983), the Court held that a trust allowing the surviving spouse to invade principal for her own support did not defeat the QTIP status, so long as the spouse had no power to appoint the property to others. The Court emphasized that “the power to invade principal for the spouse’s own benefit is not a power to appoint to another.” Id. at 567. The same principle applies here. Evelyn Davis’s authority to invade principal is limited to her own needs and is not a power to transfer assets to third parties. The trust contains no provision allowing her to direct distributions to anyone else, including her daughters or their descendants.

Moreover, the trust’s successor trustees are the daughters, Carol Tawney Pencke and Mary Martha Bennett, or their survivor. But they are not beneficiaries of the trust during Evelyn Davis’s lifetime; they are merely successors to administer the trust after her death. Their role is administrative, not distributive. The trust does not grant them any power to appoint trust assets to others during Evelyn Davis’s lifetime. Thus, no person other than the surviving spouse has a power to appoint any part of the property to any person other than the surviving spouse.

**III. The Tax Court’s Ruling Contradicts Established Precedent and Creates an Unwarranted Disincentive to Marital Trust Planning**

The Tax Court’s conclusion that the trust fails the QTIP test because the trustee may consider the spouse’s “other income or resources” is legally untenable. The trust’s provision in Section 4—that the trustee may consider “any other income or resources of the beneficiary known to the trustee”—is a standard and permissible feature of marital trusts. It does not alter the spouse’s entitlement to all trust income. In *Estate of Hensley*, this Court upheld a trust that allowed the trustee to consider the spouse’s other resources in determining distributions, noting that such considerations “do not diminish the spouse’s entitlement to all income.” 988 F.2d at 1012. The same applies here.

To hold otherwise would render many carefully structured marital trusts ineligible for the QTIP deduction, undermining Congress’s intent to allow spouses to retain control over trust assets while minimizing estate tax liability.
