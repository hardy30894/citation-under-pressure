ARGUMENT

I. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I Fix the Sole Standards for Membership in Congress, and No State May Add to Them.

Article I, Section 2 and Section 3 enumerate the qualifications of age, citizenship, and inhabitancy for Representatives and Senators. This Court has already held that these enumerated qualifications are exhaustive even as against Congress itself. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the House of Representatives could not exclude a duly elected member for reasons beyond the standing qualifications "prescribed in the Constitution," reasoning that the Framers intended the qualifications set out in the text to be fixed and complete. If the body that sits in Congress lacks power to supplement those qualifications, it follows a fortiori that a single State—one of fifty jurisdictions whose voters send representatives to a national legislature—lacks any greater authority. Amendment 73's disqualification of otherwise eligible candidates based on prior terms served is precisely the sort of additional qualification Powell forecloses.

II. The National Character of Congress, Recognized Since the Founding, Precludes State-by-State Alteration of Eligibility for Federal Office.

The Constitution created "a government of the people" whose members "are chosen directly by the people" of the whole nation, not creatures of the several states. Ex parte Yarbrough, 110 U.S. 651 (1884). Congress is the institution through which the People of the United States, not the People of Arkansas alone, govern themselves nationally. Because congressional elections implicate a federal, not merely local, interest, this Court has consistently protected the integrity and uniformity of that process against local interference. In United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court recognized that the right to select members of Congress "is a right established and guaranteed by the Constitution," subject to federal, not state, definition. Uniform, national eligibility standards are indispensable if the House and Senate are to remain, as intended, representative of the whole nation rather than a patchwork of fifty different eligibility regimes.

This national-uniformity principle finds further support in cases addressing the states' role in federal constitutional machinery generally. In Hawke v. Smith, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), this Court struck down an attempt by a state to impose a referendum requirement on the ratification of a federal constitutional amendment, holding that the state's function in that process was a federal function that "transcends any limitations sought to be imposed by the people of a state." Leser v. Garnett, 258 U.S. 130 (1922), reaffirmed that a state cannot invoke its own constitutional processes to add conditions to functions assigned by the Federal Constitution. The same logic applies here: eligibility for federal office is a matter defined by the Federal Constitution, and Arkansas cannot invoke its reserved powers under Amendment 7 to impose an additional condition never sanctioned by the Framers.

III. Arkansas's Attempt to Disguise an Additional Qualification as a "Ballot Access" Restriction Cannot Save Amendment 73.

The State's characterization of Section 3 as a mere ballot-access rule, rather than an outright disqualification, does not alter its substance. This Court has long recognized that restrictions denying a class of candidates access to the ballot function as substantive barriers to office, not neutral procedural regulations. In Williams v. Rhodes, 393 U.S. 23 (1968), the Court struck down ballot-access restrictions because "the right to form a party for the advancement of political goals means little if a party can be kept off the election ballot." The same reasoning applies to individual candidates: a rule that forbids placing an otherwise qualified candidate's name on the ballot is functionally indistinguishable from disqualification. The Elections Clause, Article I, Section 4, grants states authority only over the "Times, Places and Manner" of holding elections—procedural details—not the substantive eligibility of candidates. Smiley v. Holm, 285 U.S. 355 (1932), confirms that this state power is limited to procedural regulation of the electoral mechanism, not the qualifications of those who may be elected.

IV. Permitting States to Add Qualifications Would Undermine the Equal, National Character of Representation That This Court Has Repeatedly Protected.

The constitutional design contemplates equal representation and equal opportunity to serve, uninfluenced by parochial state limitations. In Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), this Court explained that "[n]o right is more precious in a free country than that of having a voice in the election of those who make the laws under which, as good citizens, we must live," underscoring that congressional representation is a matter of national, not merely state, concern. Similarly, in Gray v. Sanders, 372 U.S. 368 (1963), the Court held that constitutional equality principles reduce to a single command: "one person, one vote." Allowing Arkansas to bar experienced legislators from the ballot—while a citizen of a neighboring state faces no such bar—would fracture this uniformity, making a Representative's eligibility depend on the happenstance of state boundaries rather than the fixed constitutional text.

V. The Supremacy of the Federal Constitution Forecloses State Modification of Its Terms.

Finally, the structural principle that state law must yield when it conflicts with the Constitution's assignment of federal power is foundational. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), established that states may not exercise power in a manner that trenches upon exclusively federal constitutional functions. And as Chief Justice Marshall observed in Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), "[i]t is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." The law here is plain: the Qualifications Clauses set fixed, exclusive standards for congressional eligibility. Amendment 73's Section 3 cannot stand.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed insofar as it invalidates Section 3 of Amendment 73.
