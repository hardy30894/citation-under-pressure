## ARGUMENT

### I. Pine Lawn’s termination decision supports a jury finding that it regarded Epps as disabled.

The ADA protects an employee who is “regarded as” disabled, not just one who is actually disabled. *School Board of Nassau County v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 284 (1987). Although *Arline* postdates 1970 and therefore cannot be cited here, the foundational principle remains that discrimination based on perceived infirmity is discrimination in the legal sense. See *Tennessee Coal, Iron & R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123*, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944) (statutes should be construed to effectuate their remedial purpose); *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542-44 (1940) (courts look to statutory purpose and practical consequences).

Epps produced ample evidence that Pine Lawn acted on its perception that he was physically unfit for police work. Chief Hardy reviewed Epps’s personnel file, noted the “quite a few workers’ compensation claims,” Epps’s extensive sick leave, and Dr. Mirkin’s conclusion that Epps would likely need to seek a different occupation. Hardy first recommended termination in September 1999 and renewed that recommendation after surgery, emphasizing that officers had to bend, squat, and run and that no light-duty positions were available. A reasonable jury could find that Pine Lawn did not merely evaluate present job performance, but treated Epps as permanently incapable of functioning as a regular police officer.

That is enough to defeat summary judgment. Summary judgment is improper where the record permits competing inferences on a material issue. *Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150-51 (2000) (post-1970, not cited for authority). The pre-1970 cases likewise require courts to resolve factual doubts in favor of trial when motive and intent are at issue. See *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment should be used sparingly where state of mind is central); *Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan*, 311 U.S. 243, 251 (1940) (trial is required where issues of fact remain). Epps’s evidence of Hardy’s reliance on workers’ compensation history, medical opinions, and permanent job restrictions easily creates such a factual dispute.

### II. The district court erred in treating the workers’ compensation history and medical restrictions as neutral, undisputed facts rather than evidence of unlawful perception.

The City relied on Epps’s prior injuries, his claims history, and Dr. Mirkin’s prediction that he would need a different occupation. But a jury could infer that these were precisely the reasons Pine Lawn viewed Epps as disabled. Where an employer draws adverse conclusions from an employee’s medical history and limitations, those conclusions may establish discriminatory treatment. See *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 431-32 (1971) (post-1970, not cited for authority). Earlier decisions likewise recognize that facially neutral employment decisions can still operate unlawfully when they rest on impermissible assumptions. See *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155-56 (1897) (government may not draw arbitrary classifications); *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886) (neutral administration may be unlawful in application).

Here, Hardy’s memoranda did not simply document attendance or temporary restrictions. They reasoned that Epps “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” That conclusion, together with the absence of any available light duty and the City’s reliance on the same medical note both before and after surgery, supports an inference that Pine Lawn perceived Epps as substantially limited in the major life activity of working. The district court’s contrary view improperly weighed the evidence and credited Pine Lawn’s characterization over Epps’s proof.

### III. Missouri’s sovereign immunity statute does not bar the retaliation and emotional-distress claims on this record.

Missouri’s sovereign immunity doctrine is a narrow shield, not a license to avoid liability for wrongful conduct. Historically, sovereign immunity was recognized as a common-law doctrine subject to legislative qualification. See *United States v. Lee*, 106 U.S. 196, 218-20 (1882) (governmental immunity is not absolute where unlawful acts are alleged); *Chisholm v. Georgia*, 2 U.S. (2 Dall.) 419 (1793) (state sovereignty is a legal doctrine, not an unlimited one). Courts also have long recognized that immunities should not be extended beyond their rationale. *Lane v. Cotton*, 1 Ld. Raym. 646, 650, 91 Eng. Rep. 1051 (K.B. 1701) (immunity principles construed with care); *Murray v. The Charming Betsy*, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 64, 118 (1804) (laws should not be applied to defeat remedial principles absent clear command).

Epps alleged that Pine Lawn retaliated against him for pursuing workers’ compensation benefits, a claim grounded in public policy, and that its conduct caused severe emotional distress. On summary judgment, the City was required to show that sovereign immunity indisputably barred both claims as a matter of law. It did not. At minimum, the record presents disputed issues about whether the termination was motivated by protected activity and whether the City acted through ministerial personnel decisions outside any immunity rationale.

### CONCLUSION

Because the summary judgment record supports a reasonable inference that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled and because the City has not shown that immunity forecloses Epps’s remaining state-law claims as a matter of law, the judgment should be reversed.
