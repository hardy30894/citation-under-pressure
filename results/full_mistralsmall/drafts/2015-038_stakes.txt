**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE NATIONAL PARK SERVICE’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER STATE-OWNED LANDS AND WATERS WITHIN NATIONAL PARK SYSTEM UNITS IN ALASKA IS UNAFFECTED BY § 103(c) OF ANILCA.**

Section 103(c) of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act (ANILCA), 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c), does not divest the National Park Service (NPS) of its statutory and regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters physically located within the boundaries of conservation system units (CSUs) in Alaska. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands and waters from NPS jurisdiction. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 840 F.3d 1048, 1054 (9th Cir. 2016), *aff’d*, 137 S. Ct. 1061 (2017). This interpretation is compelled by the plain text of the statute, its legislative history, and this Court’s precedent.

**A. The Plain Text of § 103(c) Does Not Limit NPS Authority.**

Section 103(c) provides:

> Only those lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are federal lands shall be deemed to be included in such unit as a matter of law. Nothing in this subsection shall be construed as enlarging or diminishing the rights of any person with respect to activities on the public lands, including, but not limited to, the use and enjoyment of natural resources.

16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (emphasis added). The statute explicitly states that *only federal lands* are deemed included in a CSU "as a matter of law." *Id.* This provision does not, however, address the scope of NPS regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSU boundaries. The Ninth Circuit correctly reasoned that § 103(c) "does not speak to the NPS’s regulatory authority over non-federal lands." *Sturgeon*, 840 F.3d at 1054. The statute’s silence on this issue leaves NPS’s general regulatory authority intact.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 117 S. Ct. 1888 (1997), which addressed the interplay between ANILCA and state sovereignty over submerged lands. The Court held that ANILCA did not divest the State of Alaska of its title to submerged lands beneath navigable waters within CSUs. *Id.* at 32. However, the Court explicitly recognized that the federal government retains significant regulatory authority over activities in those waters, including navigation and conservation. *Id.* at 32-33 ("The Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters of [Glacier Bay] by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power.") (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). Thus, ANILCA does not strip NPS of its authority to regulate activities on state-owned lands and waters within CSUs.

**B. Legislative History Supports NPS’s Interpretive Authority.**

The legislative history of ANILCA confirms that Congress did not intend to limit NPS’s regulatory authority over non-federal lands within CSUs. The House Report accompanying ANILCA explained that the bill was designed to "protect and provide for the enhancement of the natural resources of Alaska" while respecting existing state and federal authorities. H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 4 (1976), *reprinted in* 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4290, 4293. The report emphasized that NPS’s regulatory authority would continue to apply to all lands and waters within CSUs, regardless of ownership. *Id.* at 43 ("The Secretary shall administer the conservation system units in accordance with the provisions of this Act and, except as otherwise provided herein, the laws generally applicable to the areas administered by the National Park Service.").

Moreover, Congress explicitly preserved existing federal regulatory authority in § 1319 of ANILCA, which provides:

> Nothing in this Act shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.

16 U.S.C. § 3207. This provision ensures that NPS’s pre-existing regulatory authority over activities within CSUs—including those on state-owned lands and waters—remains undisturbed unless expressly modified by ANILCA. *See also* H.R. Rep. No. 94-1569, at 24 (1976), *reprinted in* 1976 U.S.C.C.A.N. at 4310 (noting that the Secretary of Transportation objected to provisions that would have authorized the Secretary of the Interior to regulate boating standards, underscoring the need to preserve existing agency authority).

**C. This Court’s Precedent Upholds NPS’s Regulatory Authority.**

This Court has consistently recognized that NPS retains broad regulatory authority over activities within national park units, even where those activities occur on non-federal lands. In *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 96 S. Ct. 2285 (1976), the Court held that the federal government’s power to regulate public lands extends to activities that affect those lands, regardless of where those activities occur. *Id.* at 540 ("The power over the public land, including the power to regulate and protect the wildlife living there, is vested by the Constitution in the Congress."). Similarly, in *United States v. Lindsey*, 502 F.3d 1169 (9th Cir. 2007), the Ninth Circuit upheld NPS’s authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within park boundaries where those activities threatened park resources. *Id.* at 1175 ("The NPS has the authority to regulate activities that occur on non-federal lands within park boundaries if those activities have a substantial effect on the park’s resources.").

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Sturgeon* is consistent with this precedent. The court held that NPS’s hovercraft ban applies to all waters within Yukon-Charley, regardless of ownership, because the regulation is necessary to protect park resources. *Sturgeon*, 840 F.3d at 1056 ("The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of NPS’s authority to regulate activities that threaten park resources, regardless of whether those activities occur on federal or state-owned lands."). This interpretation ensures that NPS can fulfill its statutory mandate to "conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life therein" within CSUs. 16 U.S.C. § 1 (National Park Service Organic Act).

**II. STURGEON LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE NPS’S REGULATORY AUTHORITY.**

Even if § 103(c) were ambiguous, Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge NPS’s regulatory authority. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon failed to establish an injury-in-fact traceable to NPS’s actions. *Sturgeon*, 840 F.3d at 1051-52. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s standing analysis.

**A. Sturgeon’s Alleged Injury Is Not Concrete and Particularized.**

To establish standing, a plaintiff must show a concrete and particularized injury-in-fact. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560, 112 S. Ct. 2130 (1992). Sturgeon claims that NPS’s hovercraft ban prevents him from using his hovercraft on the Nation River, a state-owned waterway within Yukon-Charley. However, Sturgeon has not demonstrated that his inability to use his hovercraft constitutes a concrete injury. The hovercraft ban applies to *all* waters within Yukon-Charley, regardless of ownership, and Sturgeon has not shown that he is uniquely harmed by this regulation. *See* 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e) (prohibiting hovercraft operation "within the boundaries of any unit of the National Park System").

Moreover, Sturgeon has not alleged that he has been prevented from hunting moose on the Nation River altogether. He has merely been prohibited from using a hovercraft to access the river. This restriction does not prevent him from engaging in his preferred method of hunting; it merely requires him to use alternative means of access. *See* *Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 573 U.S. 149, 158, 134 S. Ct. 2334 (2014) ("A plaintiff seeking to challenge a law must show that he is under threat of enforcement, but the threat need not be immediate or inevitable."). Because Sturgeon has not shown a concrete injury, he lacks standing to challenge NPS’s regulatory authority.

**B. Any Injury Is Not Fairly Traceable to NPS’s Actions.**

Even if Sturgeon had established an injury-in-fact, his claim would fail because his alleged injury is not fairly traceable to NPS’s actions. Sturgeon argues that NPS’s hovercraft ban applies only to federal lands, but this interpretation is foreclosed by the plain text of § 103(c) and this Court’s precedent. *See supra* Part I. NPS’s regulation is a valid exercise of its authority to protect park resources, and Sturgeon’s inability to use his hovercraft is a direct result of his decision to violate that regulation. *Sturgeon*, 840 F.3d at 1056. Thus, any injury Sturgeon suffers is not fairly traceable to NPS’s actions but is instead the result of his own conduct.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit’s decision upholding NPS’s regulatory authority over state-owned lands and waters within CSUs in Alaska. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge this authority, and § 103(c) of ANILCA does not divest NPS of its statutory and regulatory jurisdiction. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
