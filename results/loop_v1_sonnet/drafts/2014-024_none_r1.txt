**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Presumption of Scienter Requires Construing §875(c) to Demand Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

Section 875(c) says nothing about the defendant's required state of mind, but that silence does not license a strict-liability or purely objective reading. This Court has long recognized that the requirement of a culpable mental state "is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Congressional silence as to intent is ordinarily read as an invitation to import the common-law mens rea requirement, not to abandon it. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with full force here. Section 875(c) does not belong to the narrow category of "public welfare" offenses in which Congress may dispense with scienter because the conduct regulated is inherently dangerous or heavily supervised. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922). A prosecution for words transmitted in ordinary speech, carrying felony penalties and lasting stigma, is precisely the type of "true crime" for which this Court has insisted that intent remain an element. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260. Where a statute is ambiguous, moreover, this Court has cautioned that criminal liability should not be extended to punish conduct the defendant did not know was unlawful, construing "willfully" in an analogous federal statute to require that the defendant act with a specific intent to violate a known right rather than through mere carelessness or misjudgment. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101–107 (1945). The same interpretive care compels reading a subjective-intent requirement into §875(c), rather than allowing conviction on proof of what a reasonable listener might have felt.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Turns on the Speaker's Intent, Not a Listener's Reaction.**

Even apart from ordinary statutory construction, the Constitution independently requires proof of the speaker's intent before expression may be punished as a "true threat." Only a narrow category of speech that is "no essential part of any exposition of ideas" falls outside the First Amendment altogether, and such categories must be defined with precision to avoid sweeping in protected expression. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). This Court's own treatment of a threat prosecution in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), confirms that the analysis cannot rest solely on how an offended or frightened listener might construe ambiguous words. Even though the petitioner's statement that "the first man I want to get in my sights is L.B.J." could have alarmed some listeners taken literally, this Court held that, "[t]aken in context, and regarding the expressly conditional nature of the statement and the reaction of the listeners, we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by petitioner fits within that statutory term." *Id.* at 708. The Court's focus was on the speaker's evident meaning, not merely on the fear the words produced.

An objective, listener-focused standard threatens to chill vast amounts of protected expression—hyperbole, dark humor, venting, artistic lyrics—because speakers cannot know in advance how an unseen and unknown audience will receive ambiguous words. This Court has repeatedly held that First Amendment freedoms "need breathing space to survive," *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963), and that statutes lacking a scienter requirement impermissibly burden protected expression because a cautious speaker "will tend to restrict" himself far more than the Constitution requires, out of fear of liability for words he never intended as unlawful. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959). The uncertainty inherent in an objective standard "inevitably cause[s]" speakers to "steer far wider of the unlawful zone than if the boundaries of the forbidden areas were clearly marked." *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958). Where advocacy or expression is at stake, this Court has likewise required proof that the speech was intended to bring about the proscribed harm before criminal liability may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective Standard Punishes Carelessness, Not Wrongdoing, and Offends Due Process.**

Dispensing with subjective intent converts §875(c) into a negligence offense—punishing defendants not for what they meant to convey, but for failing to foresee how their words might be received by strangers online. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where fair notice of what is forbidden is essential to due process. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952). A defendant should not be convicted of a serious federal felony because a jury, applying hindsight, concludes that a reasonable person might have read his words as threatening; the statute must instead be interpreted so that its coverage is no broader than its text plainly requires. *United States v. Cardiff*, 344 U.S. 174, 176–177 (1952). Because the jury here was instructed that it need only find that a reasonable person would view Elonis's statements as threatening—without any finding that Elonis himself intended them as threats—the conviction cannot stand. This Court should hold that §875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten, and should reverse the judgment below.
