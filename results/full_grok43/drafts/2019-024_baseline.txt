**ARGUMENT**

The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars respondent’s claims. That provision precludes any action under 29 U.S.C. § 1104 commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit held that “actual knowledge” requires subjective awareness both that the challenged investments occurred and that they were imprudent. That construction is irreconcilable with this Court’s and the circuit’s precedents.

Section 1113(2) is triggered by knowledge of the transaction constituting the alleged violation, not by knowledge that the transaction violated ERISA. Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 992 (9th Cir. 1985) (“[T]he statute of limitations is triggered by the [claimant’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.”). The same rule was applied in Meagher v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), and Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990). In each case the plaintiff’s awareness of the underlying facts, not an appreciation of their legal consequences, started the limitations clock.

The disclosures Intel furnished to respondent supplied precisely that factual knowledge. Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notices described the asset allocations, the inclusion of alternative investments, and the performance consequences of those allocations. Once those documents were provided, respondent possessed “actual knowledge of the breach or violation” within the meaning of § 1113(2). His subsequent failure to read the materials or inability to recall having read them does not erase the knowledge the statute attributes upon receipt.

The Ninth Circuit’s additional requirement—that the plaintiff must have known the investments were imprudent—imports an element the statute does not contain and that this Court has never endorsed. Tibble v. Edison International, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1828 (2015), recognized that the duty of prudence is measured by an objective standard; nothing in § 1113(2) converts that objective duty into a subjective scienter requirement for limitations purposes. Requiring proof that the plaintiff subjectively appreciated imprudence would effectively revive the constructive-knowledge provision Congress repealed in 1987, contrary to the text and history of the statute.

Because respondent received the disclosures more than three years before filing suit, § 1113(2) bars his claims. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
