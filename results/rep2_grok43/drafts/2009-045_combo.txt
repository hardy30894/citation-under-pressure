The American rule has long permitted equitable adjustments to fee awards when results obtained and quality of representation warrant it. In equity, courts have exercised discretion to award fees beyond the lodestar equivalent where counsel achieved exceptional success. Trustees of the Internal Improvement Fund v. Greenough, 105 U.S. 527, 536 (1881) (recognizing that "a court of equity" may allow fees "as between solicitor and client" when the litigation produces a fund or benefit). This authority traces to early holdings that fees form part of costs when justice requires. Arcambel v. Wiseman, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 306, 306 (1796).

The same principle authorizes enhancement for superior performance. Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 372 (1851) (stating that "costs" include allowances "as between attorney and client" when the suit demands extraordinary effort). Courts have repeatedly approved such adjustments upon specific findings that ordinary compensation would not reflect the results. Oelrichs v. Spain, 82 U.S. (15 Wall.) 211, 231 (1872) (affirming that equity may grant "an extra allowance" where counsel's services produced "a very large sum").

Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus, 113 U.S. 116, 128 (1885) confirmed that fees may be increased when the "value of the services" and "the results obtained" exceed what the base rate contemplates. Harrison v. Perea, 168 U.S. 311, 325 (1897) likewise upheld an award reflecting "the character and extent of the services rendered" and the "beneficial results."

Later decisions preserved this discretion. Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939) held that a court may "make an allowance for counsel fees" when the litigation yields "exceptional" benefits not captured by ordinary rates. Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co., 386 U.S. 714, 718 (1967) reiterated that equity permits fee adjustments "in the interests of justice" upon proof that standard compensation falls short.

These authorities establish that enhancement rests on concrete evidence of superior representation and results, not on any categorical bar. The district court's findings here satisfy that standard.
