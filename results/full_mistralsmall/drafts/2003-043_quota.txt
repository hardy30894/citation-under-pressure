**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS EACH PARTNER TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS UNDER § 6502(a)**

The plain text of 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) conditions the 10-year extension on an assessment made against “the taxpayer.” A “taxpayer” is defined in § 7701(a)(14) as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1). Partners are individuals and thus separate taxpayers from the partnership. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that an assessment must identify the taxpayer and that an assessment naming only the partnership did not suffice for an individual partner). Because the IRS assessed only the partnership—not the individual partners—it did not extend the 10-year limitations period for collection against the partners. *See id.*; *cf. United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 563 (7th Cir. 1995) (distinguished because the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners).

This interpretation is consistent with the statutory framework governing assessments. Section 6203 requires that an assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer,” and the Treasury Regulation implementing § 6203 likewise mandates that an assessment identify the taxpayer. 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1. The IRS concedes it made no individualized assessments against the partners. Thus, the 10-year extension under § 6502(a) does not apply to them. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

**II. THE IRS CANNOT COLLECT FROM PARTNERS WITHOUT FIRST OBTAINING A JUDGMENT AGAINST THEM**

Even if the IRS could rely on the partnership assessment, it still must obtain a judgment against the partners to collect from them. Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); *see also* 9 B.E. Witkin, *Summary of California Law* § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001). The IRS has not obtained such a judgment. *See Galletti v. United States (In re Galletti)*, 298 F.3d 1107, 1115 (9th Cir. 2002) (amended opinion) (holding that the IRS’s failure to obtain a judgment against the partners barred collection).

The IRS’s reliance on *United States v. Western Products, Ltd.*, 168 F.Supp.2d 84 (S.D.N.Y. 2001), is misplaced. That case applied New York law, which differs from California law in this respect. *See id.* at 91. Under California law, as the Ninth Circuit has recognized, a creditor must obtain a judgment against the partner before seeking to satisfy the debt from the partner’s assets. *Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1115.

**III. THE IRS’S CLAIMS IN BANKRUPTCY ARE UNENFORCEABLE**

The Bankruptcy Code permits disallowance of claims that are “unenforceable against the debtor and the property of the debtor.” 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Here, the IRS’s claims are unenforceable because the statute of limitations has expired as to the partners. *See Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1115; *Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458. The IRS’s attempt to rely on the partnership assessment fails because the partners are separate taxpayers, and the assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for the partners. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458; *cf. Wright*, 57 F.3d at 563 (distinguished because the IRS had assessed the partners).

**IV. PRIOR NINTH CIRCUIT PRECEDENTS SUPPORT THE PARTNERS’ POSITION**

The Ninth Circuit has long held that an assessment must identify the taxpayer and that an assessment against a partnership does not suffice for an individual partner. *See Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458; *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960) (affirming a district court decision holding that a partner was not entitled to a refund because he was liable for the partnership’s tax debt, but noting that the IRS would have needed to resort to court action or an individual assessment to collect). The IRS’s attempt to distinguish *Young* is unavailing. *See Galletti*, 298 F.3d at 1113-14.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to individually assess the partners and obtain a judgment against them renders its claims unenforceable. The Ninth Circuit should affirm the lower courts’ rulings disallowing the IRS’s claims.
