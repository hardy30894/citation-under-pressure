### Argument

Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), announced a new substantive rule that must apply retroactively on collateral review. Under Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), a rule is retroactive if it is substantive rather than procedural. Substantive rules “alter[] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes,” and they apply retroactively because they “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal” or is serving a punishment that the law does not permit. Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 351-52 (2004); Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 200-01 (2016).

Johnson fits that description. It did not merely change the method by which courts decide ACCA status. It invalidated the ACCA residual clause itself as void for vagueness. Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591, 597-602 (2015). That holding “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act, altering ‘the range of conduct or the class of persons that the [Act] punishes.’” Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120, 129 (2016) (quoting Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353). Once the residual clause fell, defendants whose sentences depended on it could no longer lawfully receive ACCA’s fifteen-year mandatory minimum. Johnson therefore did not regulate procedure; it changed the law’s permissible punishment. See Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 206 (“A conviction or sentence imposed in violation of a substantive rule is not just erroneous but contrary to law and, as a result, void.”).

The government may argue that Johnson is merely “procedural” because it speaks to how courts classify prior convictions. But this Court has already rejected that characterization. In Welch, the Court held that Johnson is retroactive because “by striking down the residual clause as void for vagueness, Johnson changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act.” 578 U.S. at 129. The Court explained that Johnson “affected the reach of the underlying statute rather than the judicial procedures by which the statute is applied.” Id. at 130. That is the hallmark of a substantive rule. Schriro, 542 U.S. at 353.

Nor does the fact that Johnson arose from vagueness doctrine make it procedural. This Court has made clear that “the Teague principle is not inconsistent with retroactive application of a rule that is substantive, even though the rule is ‘based on a new interpretation’ of a statute or a constitutional provision.” Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 200. A rule invalidating a sentencing provision because it is too vague to authorize punishment is substantive because it eliminates one category of punishment for a class of defendants. See Welch, 578 U.S. at 129-30; Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-21 (1998).

That conclusion is reinforced by the structure of retroactivity doctrine. New substantive rules apply to cases on collateral review because they “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant” has been subjected to punishment the law cannot impose. Schriro, 542 U.S. at 352. Johnson created exactly that risk: prisoners sentenced under the residual clause received mandatory minimums that the Constitution forbids. See Johnson, 576 U.S. at 597, 606. A sentence imposed under a clause later held void is not a matter of procedure corrected for future cases only; it is a sentence imposed without lawful authority. Montgomery, 577 U.S. at 200-01.

Welch’s case illustrates the point. His ACCA sentence depended on the residual clause’s inclusion of offenses that “otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). After Johnson, that clause cannot constitutionally support an ACCA enhancement. The consequence is substantive and immediate: Welch no longer qualifies for the fifteen-year mandatory minimum on the basis of a clause the Court has held invalid. Welch, 578 U.S. at 130-31.

This Court should therefore hold that Johnson announced a retroactive substantive rule and that Welch is entitled to collateral relief. Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120, 129-31 (2016); Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 351-53 (2004); Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 200-01 (2016).
