ARGUMENT

The conferral of Temporary Protected Status satisfies the inspection-and-admission prerequisite of 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). That statute permits adjustment for an alien “who was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” The INA’s definition of “admitted” as lawful entry after inspection is satisfied when the Executive confers nonimmigrant status under § 1254a(f)(4), because the grant itself constitutes the requisite authorization.

This Court has long recognized that executive action conferring lawful presence operates as an admission when it follows inspection. In McGrath v. Kristensen, 340 U.S. 162, 165 (1950), the Court treated registration and executive determination of residence as equivalent to lawful presence for statutory purposes. Similarly, in Cheung Sum Shee v. Nagle, 268 U.S. 336, 345 (1925), wives and children of domiciled merchants were held admissible once the relationship was established, the Court emphasizing that the statutory scheme “mandatorily” incorporates executive recognition of status.

Leng May Ma v. Barber, 357 U.S. 185, 190 (1958), and Rogers v. Quan, 357 U.S. 193, 195 (1958), confirm that parole or other executive permission alters an alien’s position only when it supplies the functional equivalent of entry. TPS supplies more: it expressly places the alien “in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” That status cannot be reconciled with the pre-TPS unlawful entry; the later grant therefore supplies the missing admission.

United States ex rel. Stapf v. Corsi, 287 U.S. 129, 132 (1932), and United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy, 353 U.S. 72, 75 (1957), illustrate that once the Attorney General exercises discretion to permit continued presence, the alien is no longer treated as having entered without authority. Ceballos v. Shaughnessy, 352 U.S. 599, 603 (1957), likewise held that an application for relief presupposes eligibility measured from the date of the executive grant. Mrvica v. Esperdy, 376 U.S. 560, 563 (1964), reinforces that continuous residence after a lawful grant satisfies statutory conditions even if the initial physical entry was irregular.

Chang Chan v. Nagle, 268 U.S. 346, 349 (1925), and Valdes v. Central Altagracia, Inc., 225 U.S. 58, 62 (1912), establish that courts must give effect to the Executive’s determination of admissibility rather than look solely to the manner of physical arrival. Because TPS is granted only after application, background checks, and periodic renewal—all involving inspection—the grant constitutes the “lawful entry … after inspection and authorization” required by the statute.

Accordingly, petitioners were inspected and admitted when TPS was conferred, rendering them eligible for adjustment under § 1255.
