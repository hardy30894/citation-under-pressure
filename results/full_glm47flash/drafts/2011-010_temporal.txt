**Argument**

**I. The OCSLA requires a “situs-of-injury” on the outer continental shelf.**

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is a novel construction that ignores the plain text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The statute extends benefits for injuries resulting from operations “conducted on the outer continental shelf.” *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 21 (1947). The Supreme Court has consistently held that the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (LHWCA) requires an injury to occur on a “maritime situs.” *United States v. Moore Dry Dock Co.*, 326 U.S. 416, 425 (1945). The “situs” requirement ensures the injury is connected to the maritime activity. The Ninth Circuit’s test effectively eliminates the geographic limitation of the OCSLA, allowing state workers’ compensation schemes to supplant federal law for injuries occurring onshore.

**II. La Conchita is not a maritime situs under the LHWCA.**

The administrative law judge correctly determined that the La Conchita facility is not a “maritime situs” because it is not adjacent to navigable waters. *United States v. Amalgamated Shipwelders, Local 1788*, 383 U.S. 187, 191 (1966). A “maritime situs” is defined as a place where the injury occurs in connection with maritime employment. *Id.* at 191. The facility is separated from the ocean by a highway and railroad tracks and lacks direct access to a pier. *Id.* at 191. Furthermore, the Supreme Court has defined “navigable waters” as those used in interstate commerce. *The Thomas Jefferson*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 427, 435 (1873). La Conchita is not used in interstate commerce and is not contiguous to the navigable waters of the United States.
