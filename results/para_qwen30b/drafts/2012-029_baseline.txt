**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that involve patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary focus of the case—requires a careful application of the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence on federal-question jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has made clear that federal jurisdiction does not automatically attach merely because a federal issue is present in a state-law claim. Rather, exclusive federal jurisdiction arises only when four conditions are met: (1) resolution of a federal issue is necessary to the outcome; (2) the federal issue is actually disputed; (3) the federal issue is substantial; and (4) federal adjudication will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314, 125 S.Ct. 2363, 162 L.Ed.2d 257 (2005). The Texas Supreme Court erred in concluding that all four elements are satisfied here, thereby dismissing the case for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction.

**I. THE PATENT ISSUE IS NOT NECESSARY TO RESOLUTION OF THE MALPRACTICE CLAIM**

Petitioner’s legal malpractice claim rests on the core elements of duty, breach, causation, and damages—all governed by Texas common law. To prevail, Minton must prove that Gunn owed him a duty of care, breached that duty by failing to raise the experimental use exception, and that this breach proximately caused his loss. *Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex.2004). The central dispute is not whether the '643 Patent is valid or infringed, but whether Gunn’s failure to assert a defense that was legally and factually unavailable at the time of litigation constitutes professional negligence.

The experimental use exception was not a viable defense under the facts known to Gunn at the time. Minton himself admitted that Stark used TEXCEN for commercial purposes—specifically, to open brokerage accounts and execute trades for clients. The lease agreement explicitly permitted Stark to derive revenue from using TEXCEN, and Minton received payments based on Stark’s gross revenues. There is no evidence that the purpose of the lease was experimental. Indeed, Minton failed to disclose to Stark that he intended to use the software for experimental purposes, undermining any claim of good faith experimentation. Under *Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Tramp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed.Cir.2005), the exception applies only where the sale is “primarily” experimental—not incidental or speculative. Here, the record shows the opposite: the transaction was commercial from inception.

Thus, the patent law issue is not “necessary” to the malpractice claim. As the Supreme Court held in *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 809, 108 S.Ct. 2166, 100 L.Ed.2d 811 (1988), a federal issue must be “a necessary element of one of the well-pleaded claims.” That standard is not met here. Minton’s claim can be resolved entirely under Texas law without resolving whether the experimental use exception applies. The trial court correctly concluded that there was no evidence of causation because Gunn had no duty to raise a defense that was not legally viable. The mere possibility that the exception might have been successful—had it been timely raised—is insufficient to establish a causal link under Texas law.

**II. THE FEDERAL ISSUE IS NOT SUBSTANTIAL OR ACTUALLY DISPUTED**

Even if the patent issue were relevant, it is not substantial or actually disputed in the context of this malpractice suit. The Federal Circuit’s decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed.Cir.2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed.Cir.2007), rely on a broad reading of § 1338(a) that ignores the Supreme Court’s caution against opening the “arising under” door too widely. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313, 125 S.Ct. 2363 (“A suit does not so arise unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends.”).

Here, the patent issue is not genuinely contested. Minton never argued before the district court that the lease was experimental. He only raised the issue after losing the case. The Federal Circuit affirmed the denial of reconsideration precisely because the exception was not timely asserted. *Minton v. Nat’l Ass’n of Sec. Dealers, Inc.*, 336 F.3d 1373, 1379–81 (Fed.Cir.2003). Thus, the issue is not “actually disputed”—it was not raised in the underlying litigation, nor was it ever properly litigated. Moreover, the issue is not substantial in the sense required by *Grable*. The resolution of whether the experimental use exception applies would not affect the validity of the patent in a way that threatens national uniformity or federal interests. The patent was already invalidated under the on-sale bar—a doctrine rooted in public policy to prevent patentees from gaining monopoly rights after commercializing their inventions. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). The question of whether an exception applies to a prior sale is not a matter of national importance when the sale itself was commercial.
