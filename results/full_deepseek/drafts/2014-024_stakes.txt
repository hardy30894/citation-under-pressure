# ARGUMENT

The conviction of Anthony Elonis must be reversed because the jury was instructed on an objective standard for "true threats," whereas the First Amendment and the proper construction of 18 U.S.C. §875(c) require proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

## I. The First Amendment Requires Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten

The true threat exception to First Amendment protection is a narrow carve-out for speech that the speaker intends as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. In *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), the Court explained that "true threats" encompass those statements where the speaker "means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group." This language—"means to communicate"—plainly describes a subjective intent requirement. The Court further distinguished cross burning with intent to intimidate, which is a true threat, from cross burning as a statement of ideology, which is protected. *Id.* at 365-66. The distinction turns on the speaker's purpose.

The Third Circuit's objective standard—asking whether a "reasonable speaker would foresee" the statement would be interpreted as a threat—cannot be squared with *Black*. The Court in *Black* did not ask whether a reasonable observer would understand the statement as a threat; it asked whether the speaker "intend[ed] to place the victim in fear of bodily harm or death." *Id.* at 360. This subjective inquiry is constitutionally required because the First Amendment protects speech that may be perceived as threatening but is actually bluster, hyperbole, or artistic expression. *See Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969) (per curiam) (holding that political hyperbole is not a threat, even if it "might seem ominous" to a reasonable listener).

Every court of appeals to address the question after *Black* has held that subjective intent is required. *See United States v. Cassel*, 408 F.3d 622, 633 (9th Cir. 2005) (en banc) ("After *Black*, we must conclude that the First Amendment requires a subjective intent to threaten"); *United States v. Jeffries*, 692 F.3d 473, 478 (6th Cir. 2012) (same); *United States v. White*, 670 F.3d 498, 507 (4th Cir. 2012) (same). The Third Circuit's contrary holding is an outlier that chills protected speech.

## II. Section 875(c) Requires Proof of Subjective Intent as a Matter of Statutory Interpretation

Even if the First Amendment did not demand subjective intent, the statute itself requires it. 18 U.S.C. §875(c) criminalizes the transmission of "any communication containing any threat." The term "threat" in a criminal statute carries a common-law meaning that includes a mental state element. *See Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723, 734 (2015) (interpreting §875(c) and holding that the statute requires proof of "the mental state with which the defendant acted"—specifically, that the defendant knew the threatening nature of his communication). The Court in *Elonis* rejected the government's argument that an objective standard sufficed, noting that "wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal." *Id.* at 735 (quoting *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 252 (1952)).

The Third Circuit's instruction allowed the jury to convict Elonis without finding that he intended his Facebook posts as threats. The district court told the jury that a true threat exists when "a defendant intentionally makes a statement in a context or under such circumstances that a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat." This instruction omitted any requirement that the defendant himself subjectively intended the statement to threaten. The jury could convict even if Elonis was merely venting, writing rap lyrics, or engaging in dark humor, so long as a reasonable person would have foreseen the threatening interpretation.

## III. The Objective Standard Chills Protected Speech

The objective standard fails to protect speakers from prosecution for speech they never intended to be threatening. The First Amendment requires that speakers have fair warning of what speech is criminal. *See Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 19 (1971) (protecting emotionally provocative speech because the government cannot "cleanse public debate" by punishing speech that offends). Under the objective standard, a speaker who posts a violent fantasy or a rap lyric can be convicted even if he had no intent to communicate a threat. This chilling effect is particularly acute for marginalized groups, such as rappers and political protesters, whose speech is often perceived as threatening by a reasonable listener. *See Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 535 U.S. 234, 245 (2002) (striking down statute that "prohibits speech that records no crime and creates no victims").

Elonis testified that his posts were therapeutic rap lyrics and that he did not intend them as threats. The jury was never instructed that it must find that Elonis subjectively intended to threaten. The conviction therefore rests on a legally erroneous standard and must be reversed.

## Conclusion

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with proper instructions requiring proof of subjective intent.
