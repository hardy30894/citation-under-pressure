**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Code exempts only funds that function as retirement savings for the debtor at the time the petition is filed.**

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempt "retirement funds" held in tax-favored accounts. Whether an asset qualifies for that exemption is measured by its character *at the commencement of the case*, not by what it once was in someone else's hands. This Court has long held that the status of exempt property is fixed as of the filing date. In *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310 (1924), the Court explained that the bankrupt's exemptions "are to be determined as of the date of the filing of the petition," because the Code looks to the estate as it actually exists when bankruptcy begins. *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294 (1903), likewise held that exemption depends on the property's actual legal character under the law governing it when the petition is filed, not its provenance. Applying that rule here, the inherited IRA must be evaluated as what it is in the Clarks' hands on the petition date—a fund that must be drawn down within a fixed term, without regard to Heidi Heffron-Clark's retirement. It is not, and cannot become, a retirement account "of" the debtor merely because it once belonged to her mother.

**II. The functional character of the asset, not its label, controls.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that the treatment of an asset in bankruptcy turns on its actual function, not its formal name. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court looked past the formal characterization of a tax loss-carryback refund and examined how the asset actually operated in deciding whether it belonged to the estate. The same approach governs the disposition of estate property more generally: in *Sampsell v. Imperial Paper & Color Corp.*, 313 U.S. 215 (1941), the Court looked through the formal ownership arrangement to the substance of the transaction in determining what belonged to the estate for creditors' benefit. The word "IRA" in "inherited IRA" is a similarly formal label; it does not change the fact that, functionally, the account no longer serves a retirement purpose for its current holder. The inherited IRA should receive the same functional scrutiny: it is, in economic reality, a time-limited, freely spendable fund that must be distributed on an accelerated schedule wholly divorced from Heidi's own retirement date.

This Court's older insurance-exemption cases confirm that the "protective" character of an exempt fund depends on whether it continues to serve its protective purpose in the debtor's hands, not on its origin. In *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202 (1905), the Court tied the exemption of a life-insurance policy to its ongoing function of providing for dependents. Here, by contrast, the inherited IRA serves no analogous ongoing retirement-protective function for the Clarks; the very tax rules governing such accounts compel current, not future, consumption.

**III. Exempting inherited IRAs would contradict, not serve, the purpose of the bankruptcy exemption scheme.**

Exemptions exist to secure a fresh start for the debtor who has fallen on hard times—not to shelter windfalls from creditors. This Court has long recognized that the object of the bankruptcy exemption laws is to relieve the honest debtor of financial burdens and permit a new start, while leaving assets that do not serve that protective function available to creditors. See *Wetmore v. Markoe*, 196 U.S. 68 (1904). That purpose is served by protecting funds a debtor has actually set aside, through sacrifice, for his or her own retirement. It is not served by shielding an inheritance that the debtor never contributed to, has no obligation to preserve, and must currently spend down by statute.

Moreover, this Court has cautioned against reading exemption and bankruptcy provisions so broadly that they defeat the balance Congress struck between debtor relief and creditors' rights. In *Stellwagen v. Clum*, 245 U.S. 605 (1918), the Court examined the purposes underlying the bankruptcy exemption scheme and the balance it draws between debtor protection and the interests of creditors, rather than reading any single exemption in isolation from that balance. And where a literal or mechanical reading of statutory text would produce a result inconsistent with the statute's evident design, this Court has looked to the statute's purpose rather than a formalistic parsing of isolated words. See *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470 (1917). Treating an inherited IRA as exempt "retirement funds" would extend the exemption beyond its purpose, sheltering ordinary inherited wealth from legitimate creditor claims simply because the funds once resided in a retirement account belonging to someone else.

For these reasons, the inherited IRA held by the Clarks is not "retirement funds" within the meaning of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
