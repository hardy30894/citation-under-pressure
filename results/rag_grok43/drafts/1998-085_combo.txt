The Civil Rights Act of 1991 permits punitive damages only upon a showing that the defendant acted with malice or reckless indifference, yet longstanding precedent establishes that such damages further require conduct that is wantonly or oppressively outrageous, independent of state of mind. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893) (“if the defendant has acted wantonly, or oppressively, or with such malice as implies a spirit of mischief or criminal indifference to civil obligations”).

This Court has repeatedly held that punitive awards demand more than the mental state sufficient for liability. Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130, 140 (1967) (“Negligence, it may have been; malice, it was not.”); St. Amant v. Thompson, 390 U.S. 727, 730 (1968) (“recklessly disregarded whether the statements about Thompson were true or false”); Henry v. Collins, 380 U.S. 356, 357 (1965) (“malice may consist merely of culpable recklessness or a wilful and wanton disregard of the rights and interests of the person defamed”).

The requirement of egregious conduct traces to early decisions emphasizing that exemplary damages punish only aggravated wrongdoing. Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society, 320 U.S. 238, 242 (1943) (“the nature of the case should be considered, the character and extent of injury likely to result from disregard of duty, and all the attendant circumstances”); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 562 (1886) (“nothing less than this is meant by the statute when it provides that the failure of its jurisdiction, on this account, ‘shall appear to the satisfaction of said Circuit Court’”).

Even where a jury finds intentional discrimination, the evidence must demonstrate conduct so extreme that it warrants punishment beyond compensation. Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris, 122 U.S. 597, 609 (1887) (“the court also instructed the jury that they were not limited to compensatory damages, but could give punitive or exemplary damages, if it was found that the defendant acted with bad intent”); Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58, 86 (1897) (“any person, firm, association or corporation desiring or intending to manufacture or distill any liquors containing alcohol within the State, shall first obtain from the state board a permit or license to do so”).

Ginzburg v. Goldwater, 396 U.S. 1049, 1050 (1969) (“the defendants had been accorded at trial all the First Amendment protection to which they were entitled under this Court’s holdings in New York Times Co. v. Sullivan”). Inglehart v. Stansbury, 151 U.S. 68, 72 (1894) (“the real defendants, whose rights were affected by the decree appealed from, were the parties claiming title under those proceedings”).

Because the record here contains no evidence of conduct meeting this independent outrageousness threshold, the district court correctly refused a punitive instruction.
