The Ninth Circuit's holding that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the underlying breach or violation to trigger ERISA's three-year statute of limitations is inconsistent with this Court's precedent and the plain meaning of the statute. The statute requires "actual knowledge of the breach or violation," 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), not merely knowledge of the underlying transaction. While the Ninth Circuit correctly noted that knowledge of illegality under ERISA is not required, it erred in holding that knowledge of the transaction alone is sufficient.

This Court has not directly addressed the meaning of "actual knowledge" under § 1113(2), but the lower courts have developed a body of law interpreting this provision. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the Ninth Circuit held that the statute is triggered by the plaintiff's knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law. However, this holding was later nuanced in Waller v. Blue Cross of California, 32 F.3d 1337 (9th Cir. 1994), which suggested that knowledge of the transaction alone may not be sufficient.

Other circuits have taken a more nuanced approach, requiring that the plaintiff have knowledge of the essential facts underlying the breach or violation. See, e.g., Larson v. Northrop Corp., 21 F.3d 1164, 1172-73 (D.C. Cir. 1994) (holding that "actual knowledge" requires knowledge of the essential facts underlying the breach); Gluck v. Unisys Corp., 960 F.2d 1168, 1177 (3d Cir. 1992) (same). This approach is consistent with the plain meaning of the statute and ensures that the limitations period is triggered only when the plaintiff has sufficient knowledge to bring a claim.

The Ninth Circuit's holding in this case is also inconsistent with its own precedent. In Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990), the court held that the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the ERISA violation when the insurance company informed them of the relevant facts. Similarly, in Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509 (9th Cir. 1991), the court held that actual knowledge is measured from the "earliest date" on which the plaintiff knew of the breach.

In this case, the Ninth Circuit erred in reversing the district court's grant of summary judgment to Intel. The district court correctly applied the substantive law, and the Ninth Circuit's holding that a dispute of material fact existed over Sulyma's actual knowledge is unfounded. As the court noted in Curley v. City of North Las Vegas, 772 F.3d 629, 631 (9th Cir. 2014), summary judgment is appropriate when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact.

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's grant of summary judgment to Intel should be reinstated. The plain meaning of § 1113(2) and the weight of circuit authority support the conclusion that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the breach or violation, not merely knowledge of the underlying transaction. See, e.g., Brown v. Owens Corning Investment Review Committee, 622 F.3d 564, 572-73 (6th Cir. 2010); In re Unisys Corp. Retiree Medical Benefit "ERISA" Litigation, 242 F.3d 497, 506-07 (3d Cir. 2001).
