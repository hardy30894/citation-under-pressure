# ARGUMENT

## I. The Prison Litigation Reform Act Permits a Population Limit When, As Here, the Statutory Prerequisites Are Satisfied.

The PLRA does not categorically forbid population caps; it conditions them on findings this three-judge court made after years of litigation and extensive evidentiary hearings. Congress required that prospective relief be "narrowly drawn," extend "no further than necessary to correct the violation," and be "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). Where a court finds "that crowding is the primary cause of the violation" and "no other relief will remedy the violation," a population limit is expressly authorized. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). Petitioners do not dispute that the statute permits such relief in the abstract; they contend only that the findings below were inadequate. That contention fails against the record.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy proven constitutional violations in institutions, including prisons. In *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678 (1978), the Court upheld a district court's order limiting the duration of confinement in punitive isolation as a permissible remedy for Eighth Amendment violations, explaining that "the ordered remedy was well within the bounds of the court's discretion." *Id.* at 687. The same principle undergirds *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), which recognized that overcrowding can itself inflict "genuine privations and hardship over an extended period of time" sufficient to violate the Eighth Amendment when it results in the deprivation of "the minimal civilized measure of life's necessities." *Id.* at 347. Where, as here, overcrowding is not incidental but causal, a remedy addressing population directly follows the violation to its source.

Petitioners' suggestion that the PLRA displaces this traditional equitable authority misreads the statute and this Court's precedent on institutional remedies. In *Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367 (1992), the Court reaffirmed that courts overseeing institutional reform litigation retain flexibility to adjust relief to "changed circumstances," and it rejected a rigid, formalistic approach to prospective relief in the corrections context. *Id.* at 383. Likewise, in the school-desegregation context, this Court held in *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education*, 402 U.S. 1 (1971), that once a constitutional violation is established, "the scope of a district court's equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad, for breadth and flexibility are inherent in equitable remedies." *Id.* at 15. *Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267 (1977), confirmed that remedial decrees must be tailored to the nature and extent of the violation, but it did not require courts to select the remedy petitioners prefer merely because it is less comprehensive. *Id.* at 280–81. The PLRA imports this same violation-tailored logic; it does not forbid systemic remedies for systemic violations.

## II. The Three-Judge Court's Findings Satisfy Section 3626(a)(3)(E).

The Eighth Amendment prohibits deliberate indifference to serious medical needs, *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104 (1976), and to conditions posing a substantial risk of serious harm, *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 828 (1994). The three-judge court found, after extensive hearings, that chronic overcrowding was the "primary cause" of California's continuing failure to provide constitutionally adequate medical and mental health care—the very finding the PLRA requires before a population limit may issue. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i). That finding did not emerge in a vacuum; it followed years of failed remedial efforts, including the stipulated injunctions and the receivership discussed in the proceedings below. *See Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d 1088 (9th Cir. 2009) (per curiam), affirming that the district court's earlier orders arose from a documented "history of constitutional violations and a failure of California to comply with remedial orders." The record thus establishes precisely the causal nexus between overcrowding and the constitutional deprivation that Congress demanded.

Petitioners' own conduct confirms the necessity finding. As this Court recognized analogously in *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343 (1996), remedial orders in the prison context must be grounded in an actual, ongoing violation rather than speculation, *id.* at 349–50; here, the three-judge court's findings rest on a decade of documented noncompliance, not speculation. California's failure to fund the receiver's remedial construction plans, chronicled in *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 603 F.3d at 1090–92, itself demonstrates that "no other relief" short of population reduction would remedy the violation, satisfying § 3626(a)(3)(E)(ii).

## III. The Order Reflects the Least Intrusive Means and Gives Substantial Weight to Public Safety.

Finally, the PLRA requires that a court "shall give substantial weight to any adverse impact on public safety." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). The three-judge court did so, crediting expert testimony and adopting a phased, two-year compliance schedule that afforded the State substantial discretion in selecting the means of reduction—parole reform, diversion, and construction—rather than mandating release of any particular class of prisoners. This measured approach mirrors the deference to state implementation choices this Court approved in *Rufo*, 502 U.S. at 392, and confirms that the order is the least intrusive means available consistent with correcting the violation.

For these reasons, the order below fully complies with the Prison Litigation Reform Act.
