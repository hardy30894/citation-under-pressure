The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial here. The Constitution provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that the Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Once the State has failed to prove an allegation necessary to increase punishment, it may not make a second attempt at the same issue.

That principle applies with particular force where the State has chosen to make the prior-conviction allegation an element-like fact that must be found in a formal proceeding. In California, the prosecution alleged Monge’s prior conviction to secure a harsher sentence, the court bifurcated the issue, the State introduced evidence, and the court made a true finding. When the Court of Appeal held the evidence insufficient, that ruling was the functional equivalent of an acquittal on the sentencing allegation. The State should not get “another bite at the apple” simply because the allegation affects punishment rather than the substantive offense.

This Court’s precedents do not support exempting such a proceeding from the Double Jeopardy Clause. In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court addressed a jury’s sentencing recommendation in a capital case, but it did not hold that retrial is always permissible whenever punishment is at issue. To the contrary, the Court’s double jeopardy cases have repeatedly focused on the danger of repeated efforts by the sovereign to convict or punish after an adverse adjudication. See Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904); United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896). Those decisions teach that finality attaches when the government has had one full and fair opportunity to prove the fact it must establish.

Nor does the historical treatment of sentencing eliminate double jeopardy protection. It is true that judges historically have enjoyed broad discretion to consider information at sentencing. See Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949). But Williams concerned ordinary individualized sentencing, not a separate adversarial proceeding to determine the truth of a specific, outcome-determinative allegation. The Court there sustained a sentencing judge’s use of extra-record information and emphasized the traditional difference between guilt adjudication and sentencing. Id. at 245-251. That case does not authorize the State to litigate a sentencing allegation, lose for lack of proof, and then try again. Williams addressed the scope of information a judge may consider; it did not decide whether the government may relitigate a failed enhancement allegation after an insufficiency ruling.

Indeed, when the Court has considered proceedings that resemble trials in all but name, it has treated them as subject to constitutional trial protections. In In re Oliver, 333 U.S. 257 (1948), the Court explained that due process requires the essential safeguards of a fair hearing where the State seeks to impose punishment through a formal adjudicatory process. Likewise, in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), the Court held that double jeopardy principles prevent the government from making repeated attempts to obtain a conviction once the factfinder has resolved the issue in the defendant’s favor. The same principle governs here. The State elected to create a formal, bifurcated proceeding to prove Monge’s prior conviction allegation. Having failed to prove it, the State may not try again.

The practical reasons behind the Double Jeopardy Clause also support petitioner. The Clause is meant to protect against the “embarrassment, expense and ordeal” of repeated prosecutions and to preserve the finality of judgments. Green, 355 U.S. at 187-188. That concern is no less real where the State seeks to double a prison term based on a prior conviction allegation. Monge has already endured one contested hearing; forcing him to defend the same allegation again would subject him to exactly the kind of repeated governmental effort the Clause forbids.

The State will argue that sentencing is different because it concerns punishment rather than guilt. But this Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause disappears whenever the government labels a proceeding “sentencing.” The constitutional inquiry turns on substance, not nomenclature. Where the State creates a separate adjudication, alleges a specific fact, and bears the burden of proof, the constitutional protection against a second attempt after failure must apply.

Because the Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to sustain the prior-conviction allegation, the Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial. The judgment allowing a second proceeding on the enhancement should be reversed.
