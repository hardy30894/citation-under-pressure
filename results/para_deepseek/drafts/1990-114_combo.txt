**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Displace Missouri’s Constitutional Retirement Provision for State Judges.**

The tenure and qualifications of state judicial officers have always been matters of state law. This Court has repeatedly recognized the plenary authority of the States over the structure of their own judiciaries. *See Dreyer v. Illinois*, 187 U.S. 71, 84 (1902) (the State’s control over its judicial tribunals is not subject to federal intervention); *Taylor v. Beckham*, 178 U.S. 548, 570–71 (1900) (the selection and tenure of state officers are for the State to determine). A federal statute will not be read to displace such a traditional state arrangement absent a clear expression of congressional purpose. In *Ex parte Crow Dog*, 109 U.S. 556, 571–72 (1883), this Court refused to construe a federal criminal statute to reach conduct that a treaty had committed to tribal adjudication, because Congress had not plainly and unmistakably expressed an intention to make so radical a change. Likewise, in *Parker v. Brown*, 317 U.S. 341, 362–63 (1943), the Court held that a federal marketing act would not be construed to supersede a State’s agricultural regulatory program unless Congress had made clear its intention to do so.

The ADEA, enacted in 1967, contains no such clear statement regarding state judges. Its definition of “employee” nowhere refers to the judiciary, and its legislative history is silent on the subject. In a field so central to state sovereignty as the composition of state courts, this Court should not presume that Congress silently aimed the ADEA at judges sitting on state benches.

Even if the ADEA were applicable, appointed state judges fall squarely within the statutory exclusion for “appointee[s] on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The judicial function is the exercise of judgment and discretion of the highest order. *Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335, 347 (1871). Judges do not mechanically apply settled rules; in deciding hard cases they necessarily resolve unsettled questions and articulate standards that guide the future conduct of litigants, the bar, the other branches of government, and the public. That is the essence of policymaking. Missouri’s appointed judges also exercise supervisory and rulemaking authority over the administration of the state court system, responsibilities that are themselves plainly policymaking in character. Because petitioners are appointees on the policymaking level, they are excluded from the ADEA’s coverage.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Satisfies Equal Protection.**

The provision mandating retirement of judges at age seventy burdens no suspect class and no fundamental right. It therefore must be upheld if any rational basis for it can be conceived. State legislation carries a strong presumption of validity, and every reasonable intendment must be made in its favor. *Metropolitan Cas. Ins. Co. v. Brownell*, 294 U.S. 580, 584 (1935). The burden is on the challenger to negative every conceivable basis for the classification. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78–79 (1911) (“if any state of facts reasonably can be conceived that would sustain it, the existence of that state of facts at the time the law was enacted must be assumed”).

Rational bases abound. Missouri may rationally conclude that the faculties demanded by judicial office—mental acuity, stamina, and the capacity to absorb and process complex facts and law—tend to erode with age. It may also rationally conclude that a fixed retirement age spares the public and the judiciary the expense, delay, and embarrassment of case-by-case removal of judges of failing competence, and that a predictable retirement age keeps the bench representative of the population it serves. Any one of these justifications supplies the necessary rational basis.

The Equal Protection Clause does not require the State’s classification to be perfect; it need only bear a reasonable relation to a legitimate end. *Royster Guano Co. v. Virginia*, 253 U.S. 412, 415 (1920). A State may address a problem one step at a time, “addressing itself to the phase of the problem which seems most acute to the legislative mind.” *Goesaert v. Cleary*, 335 U.S. 464, 466 (1948). A classification is unconstitutional only if it “rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State’s objective.” *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425 (1961). The age-based classification here is squarely relevant to Missouri’s legitimate objective of maintaining a competent and respected judiciary. Mandatory retirement of judges at seventy is not invidious discrimination; it is a considered exercise of the State’s constitutional authority to structure its own courts. *See Dreyer*, 187 U.S. at 84.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
