**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because genuine disputes of material fact exist as to whether Burnett provided adequate notice of his serious health condition under the FMLA and ADA. Summary judgment is a drastic remedy that should be granted only when the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law and no reasonable jury could find for the non-movant. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962); *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Here, the record, viewed in the light most favorable to Burnett, reveals multiple communications that could reasonably be construed as notice.

**I. Burnett Provided Sufficient Notice of His Medical Condition**

The FMLA and ADA require an employee to provide notice of a serious health condition, but the notice need not be explicit or use legal terminology. Burnett repeatedly informed his supervisor, Sergio Polo, of his medical difficulties. In October 2003, Burnett told Polo he had a “weak bladder” and was seeing a doctor. In December 2003, he gave Polo a doctor’s order for blood testing and explained he had high PSA and cholesterol levels. He further stated he needed to see a urologist. On December 16, Burnett told Polo and other managers that he had been “sick” and compared his condition to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. In January 2004, he provided a document describing a prostate biopsy and ultrasound, which explicitly referenced “elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA).”

These facts, taken together, are sufficient to create a jury question on notice. The question of whether an employer has received adequate notice is ordinarily one of fact for the jury. *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). A jury could reasonably infer that Habitat knew or should have known that Burnett suffered from a serious medical condition. *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946) (holding that when evidence supports conflicting inferences, the jury must resolve the dispute). Burnett’s statements about his bladder, PSA levels, and prostate biopsy were not ambiguous; they directly alerted Habitat to a potentially serious prostate condition.

**II. The District Court Improperly Weighed Evidence and Drew Inferences Against Burnett**

The district court concluded that Burnett failed to explain the significance of a high PSA level and that no one asked him to do so. But the law does not require an employee to provide a medical diagnosis. An employer cannot ignore facts that would put a reasonable employer on notice. *Tiller v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co.*, 318 U.S. 54, 67 (1943) (employer’s knowledge may be inferred from employee’s reports of dangerous conditions). Here, Burnett gave Polo a document that explicitly linked elevated PSA to prostate cancer screening. Even if Polo denied receiving it, the jury could credit Burnett’s testimony that he gave it to another supervisor, Mitch Hehr, who under company policy should have forwarded it. *Bailey v. Central Vermont Ry.*, 319 U.S. 350, 353 (1943) (jury may draw reasonable inferences from circumstantial evidence).

Moreover, the district court disregarded Burnett’s repeated statements about his health and his need for medical appointments. Habitat itself acknowledged Burnett’s condition by granting sick leave for his appointments on January 6 and 8, 2004. That acknowledgment creates a genuine issue as to whether Habitat had actual knowledge. *Wilkerson v. McCarthy*, 336 U.S. 53, 57 (1949) (summary judgment improper where reasonable minds could differ on employer’s knowledge).

**III. The Employer Had a Duty to Inquire Further**

When an employee provides information that suggests a serious health condition, the employer has a duty to inquire further rather than terminate the employee. *Brady v. Southern R. Co.*, 320 U.S. 476, 479 (1943) (employer’s failure to investigate known risks may constitute negligence). Here, Burnett told Polo he was “sick,” had high PSA, and was undergoing a prostate biopsy. A reasonable employer would have asked for clarification or medical documentation before disciplining Burnett. Instead, Habitat issued multiple reprimands and effectively terminated him. The jury should decide whether Habitat’s actions were reasonable.

**Conclusion**

Because genuine issues of material fact exist regarding notice, the district court’s grant of summary judgment must be reversed. The case should be remanded for trial.
