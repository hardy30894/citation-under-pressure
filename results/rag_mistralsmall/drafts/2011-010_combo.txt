**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE OCSLA’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION PROVISION REQUIRES THAT THE INJURY OCCUR ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.**

The OCSLA extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (“disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf”). This Court has long recognized that the OCSLA’s coverage is geographically limited to the Shelf itself. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 356 (1969) (“The Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States are extended to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and to all artificial islands and fixed structures which may be erected thereon”); *United States v. Louisiana*, 363 U.S. 1, 8 (1960) (OCSLA extends federal jurisdiction to submerged lands “seaward of the low-water mark”).

The Fifth Circuit correctly applied this situs requirement in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), holding that an OCSLA claimant must show the injury occurred on the Shelf or its waters. This interpretation is consistent with the statute’s plain text and the Supreme Court’s guidance. *Pennsylvania R.R. v. O’Rourke*, 344 U.S. 334, 337 (1953) (statutory coverage turns on “locale”); *Nogueira v. New York, N.H. & H.R.R.*, 281 U.S. 128, 134 (1930) (LHWCA’s coverage is “manifestly broad” but tied to navigable waters).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—requiring only a “substantial nexus” between the injury and Shelf operations—ignores the OCSLA’s geographic limitation. *Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co.*, 375 U.S. 34, 40 (1963) (federal maritime law is “geographically confined” to the Shelf). The court’s reliance on *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), is misplaced; the Supreme Court’s footnote on § 1333(b) was dictum and did not address the situs requirement. *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2.

**II. THE LHWCA’S MARITIME SITUS REQUIREMENT IS NOT SATISFIED HERE.**

The LHWCA covers injuries “occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that La Conchita—a facility 250–300 feet from the shore, separated by a highway and railroad tracks—qualifies as a maritime situs. *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978) (adjoining area must be “used as an integral part of longshoring operations”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the LHWCA’s coverage is tied to navigable waters. *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251, 254 (1940) (“injury occurring upon the navigable waters”); *Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc.*, 314 U.S. 244, 246 (1942) (death must occur “upon navigable waters”). La Conchita’s distance from the water and lack of direct access to maritime facilities place it outside the statute’s scope. *Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson*, 396 U.S. 212, 215 (1969) (pier injuries are compensable only if on navigable waters).

**III. THE DECEDENT WAS NOT ENGAGED IN MARITIME EMPLOYMENT.**

Even if La Conchita were a maritime situs, the decedent’s work—consolidating scrap metal for eventual sale—was not maritime employment. The Supreme Court has held that maritime employment involves “intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation.” *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 83 (1979). Here, the scrap metal was transported by third parties and stored for years before sale, severing any nexus to maritime activity. *Northern Coal & Dock Co. v. Strand*, 278 U.S. 142, 145 (1928) (longshore work must be “maritime” in nature).

**CONCLUSION**

The OCSLA and LHWCA require both a maritime situs and maritime employment. The Ninth Circuit erred in relaxing these requirements. This Court should reverse and remand with instructions to deny benefits.
