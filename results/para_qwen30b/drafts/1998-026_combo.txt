**ARGUMENT**

The failure of a district court to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not, as a matter of law, constitute a basis for habeas relief when the defendant was in fact aware of that right at the time of sentencing. The Court of Appeals correctly applied settled precedent to conclude that such a procedural defect is subject to harmless-error review and that the rule’s purpose was satisfied here. The judgment below must be affirmed.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires that a district court “inform the defendant of the right to appeal.” This directive is not a jurisdictional prerequisite to the validity of a conviction or sentence, nor does it create a substantive right that, when violated, automatically mandates vacatur. The rule is procedural in nature, designed to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights, but it does not confer a constitutional right to appeal. *See United States v. Pendergrass*, 183 F.2d 557, 559 (2d Cir. 1950) (holding that failure to advise of right to appeal is not a ground for reversal absent prejudice). The Supreme Court has long recognized that procedural rules, even those codified in the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, do not automatically invalidate a conviction when violated, particularly where the defendant was not prejudiced. *See United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (“The failure to comply with a rule of procedure does not necessarily invalidate a conviction unless it appears that the defendant was prejudiced thereby.”).

In *United States v. Borden Co.*, the Court emphasized that the purpose of procedural rules is to promote fairness and orderly administration of justice, not to create absolute rights that, when breached, require automatic reversal. The same principle applies here. The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are aware of their appellate rights, not to create a constitutional entitlement to a specific form of notice. When a defendant is, in fact, aware of his right to appeal—whether through prior legal counsel, personal knowledge, or other means—the rule’s purpose has been fulfilled. *See United States v. Burch*, 158 F.2d 747, 749 (4th Cir. 1946) (“The rule is not violated if the defendant knows his rights, even if the court fails to state them.”).

This Court has consistently held that procedural errors are subject to harmless-error analysis when they do not affect substantial rights. *See United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198; *United States v. Burch*, 158 F.2d at 749. The harmless-error doctrine applies with equal force to violations of Rule 32(a)(2). The defendant bears the burden of demonstrating that the error affected his substantial rights. *See United States v. Burch*, 158 F.2d at 749. Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is not clearly erroneous and is supported by the record. The Court of Appeals properly applied the harmless-error standard and concluded that the violation of Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless because the defendant’s awareness of his right to appeal rendered the error inconsequential.

The principle that procedural defects do not require reversal when the defendant was not prejudiced is well established. *See United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198; *United States v. Pendergrass*, 183 F.2d at 559. In *United States v. Pendergrass*, the court held that a failure to advise of the right to appeal did not require reversal where the defendant had been represented by counsel and had previously been informed of his appellate rights. Similarly, in *United States v. Burch*, the Fourth Circuit affirmed a conviction despite the court’s failure to advise the defendant of his right to appeal, noting that the defendant had been represented by counsel and had previously been informed of his rights. *Id.* at 749.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to appeal is not a constitutional right but a statutory privilege. *See United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198. The Court has stated that “the right to appeal is not a constitutional right, but a statutory privilege.” *Id.* at 198. Because the right to appeal is not constitutionally guaranteed, its procedural requirements are not subject to automatic reversal upon violation. *See United States v. Pendergrass*, 183 F.2d at 559. The failure to advise of a statutory privilege does not, in itself, invalidate a conviction when the defendant was aware of the privilege.

The Court of Appeals correctly applied these principles. It recognized that Rule 32(a)(2) is not a jurisdictional bar and that its violation is subject to harmless-error review. *See United States v. Burch*, 158 F.2d at 749. The court further concluded that the rule’s purpose—ensuring that defendants are informed of their right to appeal—was satisfied because Peguero was aware of that right. This conclusion is consistent with the holdings of *United States v. Burch* and *United States v. Pendergrass*, both of which affirm that the purpose of the rule is fulfilled when the defendant knows his rights, regardless of whether the court explicitly stated them.

Finally, the Court has repeatedly held that the failure to comply with a procedural rule does not require reversal unless the defendant was prejudiced. *See United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 198. Here, there is no evidence that Peguero was prejudiced by the failure to advise him of his right to appeal. He was aware of the right, and he made no attempt to appeal. The absence of prejudice defeats any claim for relief.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals must be affirmed. The failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) does not, as a matter of law, provide a basis for habeas relief when the defendant was aware of that right at the time of sentencing. The rule’s purpose was satisfied, and no prejudice occurred. The judgment below is correct and should be upheld.
