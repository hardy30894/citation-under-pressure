**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were moot upon Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) abandonment of the challenged policies, and that the claim for nominal damages could not revive a case otherwise rendered moot. This Court should affirm.

### I. A plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages cannot prevent a case from becoming moot when the challenged policy has been abandoned.

The doctrine of mootness ensures that federal courts adjudicate live controversies, not hypothetical or abstract disputes. *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960). When a plaintiff’s requested relief becomes impossible to grant due to a change in circumstances, the case is moot. *See, e.g.,* *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343, 350 (1996) (“[A] case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.”).

Here, GGC revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” and removed the challenged provisions of its Student Code of Conduct *while the case was pending*. The revised policy permits students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. This change eliminated the very conduct that gave rise to the constitutional challenge. As the Eleventh Circuit correctly observed, GGC “unambiguously terminated the Prior Policies and there is no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 18-12676, 2019 WL 2777777, at *6 (11th Cir. July 1, 2019). The district court properly applied this principle.

The Supreme Court has long held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages does not preserve standing when the underlying injury has ceased. *See, e.g.,* *City of Los Angeles v. Lyons*, 461 U.S. 95, 102 (1983) (“[T]he mere possibility of future injury is not enough to sustain standing.”); *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962) (“[T]he plaintiff must show that he has suffered or is threatened with a concrete injury.”). In *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), this Court held that a claim for nominal damages alone cannot save a case from mootness when the challenged policy has been abandoned. *Id.* at 1255. The plaintiff in *Flanigan’s* sought only nominal damages after the city repealed the ordinance at issue. We concluded that “a prayer for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.” *Id.* at 1256.

This case is indistinguishable from *Flanigan’s*. The plaintiffs here sought only nominal damages, and the challenged policies were permanently replaced. The district court correctly applied *Flanigan’s*, and the Eleventh Circuit affirmed. The plaintiffs’ attempt to recharacterize their damages claim as one for compensatory damages fails. The complaint never alleged actual injury—such as lost time, financial loss, or emotional distress—required to support a claim for compensatory damages under § 1983. *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000). Instead, the complaint rested on the “abstract injury” of a constitutional violation. *Id.* at 1231. The mere assertion that they “suffered irreparable harm” without specifying the nature of that harm is insufficient. *See id.* at 1230–31.

### II. The plaintiffs’ claim for nominal damages is not a “live” controversy.

Even if nominal damages were available, they do not constitute a remedy for a live controversy. Nominal damages are awarded to recognize a legal wrong when no actual harm has occurred. *See, e.g.,* *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949) (“[N]ominal damages are awarded to recognize a legal wrong, even though no actual damage has been sustained.”). But when the challenged policy has been abandoned, there is no ongoing wrong to recognize. *See* *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950) (“[W]hen the controversy has ceased, the court has no power to act.”).

The plaintiffs argue that their claim remains viable because they have not abandoned their desire to speak on campus. But the Supreme Court has made clear that a plaintiff’s subjective intent to engage in future conduct is not enough to prevent mootness. *See* *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. at 350. The plaintiff must show a “real and immediate threat” of future injury. *Id.* Here, the plaintiffs have not attempted to speak since the incident in 2016, and the new policy permits unrestricted speech. The absence of any attempt to exercise the right under the new regime demonstrates that the threat of future injury is speculative. *See* *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. at 204.

### III. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend.

The plaintiffs argue they were denied the opportunity to amend their complaint to seek compensatory damages. But they failed to file a proper motion for leave to amend. *See* *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (“[W]here a request for leave to file an amended complaint simply is imbedded within an opposition memorandum, the issue has not been raised properly.”). They merely requested “clarification” in a response to a motion to dismiss. This procedural defect alone justifies the district court’s refusal to consider the request.
