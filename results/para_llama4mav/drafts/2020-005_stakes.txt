The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff's claims "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's activities within the state. Ford Motor Company v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court, et al., presents the question of whether a state court may exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when none of the defendant's contacts with the state caused the plaintiff's claims.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford Motor Company is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana because it has "purposefully availed" itself of the privilege of doing business in the state. By advertising and selling parts within Montana, Ford has established a connection with the state that is sufficient to support specific jurisdiction. This Court has long recognized that a defendant's contacts with a forum state can give rise to specific jurisdiction, even if those contacts did not directly cause the plaintiff's claims. See International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945) (holding that specific jurisdiction is proper when a defendant's "operations within a state are... so substantial and of such a nature as to justify suit" on claims that are "related to" those operations).

In Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California, San Francisco County, 137 S. Ct. 1773 (2017), this Court reaffirmed that specific jurisdiction requires a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue. However, the Court did not establish a bright-line rule requiring that the defendant's in-state contacts be the direct cause of the plaintiff's claims. Rather, the Court emphasized that the "relationship between the defendant and the forum... [must] arise out of contacts that the defendant himself creates with the forum State." Id. at 1780 (quoting Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985)).

Here, Ford's activities in Montana, including its advertising and sales of parts, demonstrate a significant connection with the state. While the specific vehicle involved in the accident was not sold in Montana, Ford's overall presence in the state is substantial. The claims at issue - design defect, failure to warn, and negligence - relate to Ford's conduct in designing and manufacturing the vehicle, which is connected to its broader activities in Montana.

The Due Process Clause does not require that a defendant's in-state contacts be the direct cause of the plaintiff's claims in order to establish specific jurisdiction. See Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall, 466 U.S. 408, 414 n.8 (1984) (noting that specific jurisdiction can be based on a defendant's "continuous and systematic" contacts with the forum state, even if those contacts are not directly related to the plaintiff's claims). Ford's purposeful availment of Montana's market and its significant connections with the state are sufficient to support the exercise of specific jurisdiction.

In light of Ford's substantial presence in Montana and the connection between its activities and the claims at issue, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in the state. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
