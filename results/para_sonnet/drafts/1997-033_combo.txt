**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Mandatory Text of Section 1407(a) Forecloses Use of Section 1404(a) to Retain a Transferred Case for Trial**

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." That command is unqualified. Nothing in Section 1407(a) authorizes a transferee judge to convert pretrial custody into permanent custody by invoking the general venue-transfer provision of 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The Ninth Circuit's approval of that maneuver rests not on text but on accumulated practice, beginning with the Second Circuit's decision in Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord—a course of decisions this Court should not perpetuate where, as here, the statutory language is plain.

**II. The Canon Against Implied Repeal and the Rule That Specific Statutes Govern General Ones Bar the Result Below**

This Court has long held that "repeals by implication are not favored," and that "the intention of the legislature to repeal must be clear and manifest." Posadas v. National City Bank of New York, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936). Section 1407(a) and Section 1404(a) can coexist without any need to read the general transfer statute as silently overriding the specific remand mandate that Congress enacted for multidistrict proceedings. Where Congress has addressed a particular subject—here, the temporal limits of a transferee court's authority over consolidated cases—through a specific statutory scheme, that scheme is not "controlled or nullified" by a general provision enacted for different purposes. United States v. Borden Co., 308 U.S. 188 (1939).

This Court applied precisely that principle in Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222 (1957), holding that the specific patent-venue statute was not to be supplemented or enlarged by the general venue provisions of the Judicial Code, even though both statutes nominally addressed venue. The same logic controls here. Section 1407(a) is the specific statute governing the scope and duration of a transferee court's authority over cases assigned to it by the Panel; Section 1404(a) is the general venue-transfer statute of nationwide application. Absent a clear indication that Congress intended the general statute to swallow the specific one—and none exists—Section 1404(a) cannot be read to erase the remand obligation that is the centerpiece of Section 1407(a).

**III. Section 1404(a)'s Purpose as a Convenience-Based "Housekeeping" Provision Confirms It Cannot Be Used to Annex Permanent Authority Over a Case Committed Only for Pretrial Purposes**

This Court has described Section 1404(a) as "a federal judicial housekeeping measure," Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 636–37 (1964), enacted to facilitate convenient litigation among districts where an action could otherwise have been brought—not to expand a court's dispositive power beyond what the statute conferring its jurisdiction over the case allows. In Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), the Court likewise treated Section 1404(a) as an ordinary tool for shifting venue between districts of proper jurisdiction, not as a vehicle for a court to acquire authority it would not otherwise possess. And in Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19 (1960), the Court confirmed that Section 1404(a) transfers serve efficiency and convenience within the ordinary transferor-transferee relationship that statute contemplates—a relationship distinct from the specialized, time-limited custody that Section 1407(a) creates for pretrial coordination alone.

This Court's refusal in Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335 (1960), to stretch Section 1404(a) beyond its terms is instructive. There, the Court held that a district court may transfer an action under Section 1404(a) only to a district where the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff, rejecting the argument that the defendant's consent could enlarge the statute's reach. Blaski teaches that Section 1404(a)'s language must be honored strictly, not stretched to accommodate convenience or litigant preference. The same fidelity to text requires rejecting respondent Milberg's attempt to use Section 1404(a) to override the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

**IV. Petitioner's Choice of Forum, Preserved by Section 1407(a)'s Remand Requirement, Is Entitled to Substantial Weight**

Section 1407(a)'s remand mandate protects the plaintiff's original choice of forum once the limited purpose of coordinated pretrial proceedings has been served. This Court has long recognized that "unless the balance is strongly in favor of the defendant, the plaintiff's choice of forum should rarely be disturbed." Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947). Lexecon chose the Northern District of Illinois. It litigated there until the Panel—exercising authority limited by statute to pretrial coordination—transferred the case to Arizona. Nothing in that transfer, or in Section 1404(a), permitted the Arizona court to convert its temporary custody into a permanent one for trial.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded to the Northern District of Illinois as Section 1407(a) requires.
