The rule announced in Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 258 (2015), regarding what constitutes a violent offense under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson held that the residual clause of the ACCA, which defined a violent felony as one that "otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," was unconstitutionally vague. This holding was later made retroactive to cases on collateral review in Welch v. United States, 578 U.S. 120 (2016), but the Court's reasoning in Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and subsequent cases, supports the retroactive application of the Johnson rule.

The Teague Court established a framework for determining whether a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. Under Teague, a new rule applies retroactively if it is a substantive rule or a "watershed rule of criminal procedure." A substantive rule is one that "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). The Johnson rule is substantive because it narrows the scope of the ACCA's residual clause, thereby altering the range of conduct that is punishable under the statute. See Montgomery v. Louisiana, 577 U.S. 190, 198 (2016) (holding that a new substantive rule applies retroactively).

The Eleventh Circuit's decision in this case, which held that Welch's Florida strong arm robbery conviction was a violent felony under the ACCA, was based on the now-invalidated residual clause. The court's analysis relied on cases such as United States v. Lockley, 632 F.3d 1238 (11th Cir. 2011), and United States v. Alexander, 609 F.3d 1250 (11th Cir. 2010), which applied the residual clause to determine whether a prior conviction qualified as a violent felony. However, in light of Johnson, these decisions are no longer good law.

The Johnson Court's holding that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague is grounded in the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, which requires that a penal statute be sufficiently clear to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punishes. See United States v. Harriss, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954). The Court's decision in Johnson is consistent with its earlier holdings in cases such as James v. United States, 550 U.S. 192 (2007), and Begay v. United States, 553 U.S. 137 (2008), which emphasized the importance of clarity and predictability in the application of the ACCA.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's decision in Sykes v. United States, 564 U.S. 1 (2011), which was overruled in part by Johnson, demonstrates the need for clarity and consistency in the application of the ACCA. The Sykes Court's holding that certain crimes, such as vehicular flight, presented a serious potential risk of physical injury to another, was later called into question by the Johnson Court's holding that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague.

In United States v. Chitwood, 676 F.3d 971 (11th Cir. 2012), the Eleventh Circuit applied the residual clause to determine whether a prior conviction for burglary qualified as a violent felony under the ACCA. However, in light of Johnson, this decision is no longer good law. The Chitwood court's analysis relied on the now-invalidated residual clause, and its holding is therefore no longer binding.

The Florida robbery statute under which Welch was convicted in 1996 is not categorically a violent felony under the ACCA, because it does not have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another. See Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1); McCloud v. State, 335 So.2d 257, 258 (Fla. 1976). The statute's requirement of "force, violence, assault, or putting in fear" is not sufficient to satisfy the ACCA's elements clause, because Florida law at the time of Welch's conviction allowed for a conviction based on a mere snatching, which is not necessarily forceful enough to satisfy the federal statute. See Goldsmith v. State, 573 So.2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d Dist. Ct. App. 1991).

In conclusion, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, and Welch's sentence under the ACCA should be vacated because his Florida strong arm robbery conviction is not categorically a violent felony under the statute. The Eleventh Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for resentencing.
