**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt who collected those debts on its own behalf, was not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA. The statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion.

### I. THE FDCPA REGULATES DEBT COLLECTORS, NOT CREDITORS—AND SANTANDER ACTED AS A CREDITOR

The FDCPA’s core purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). It does not regulate creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The Act distinguishes between “debt collectors” and “creditors” based on the nature of the relationship between the entity and the debt. A “creditor” is a person to whom a debt is owed. *Id.* A “debt collector” is a person who collects debts on behalf of another or whose principal business is debt collection. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6).

The definition of “debt collector” is structured in two parts: first, a list of persons *included* in the term; second, a list of persons *excluded*. The relevant inclusion is found in § 1692a(6)(2): “any person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” This provision applies only when a person collects debts *for another*. The key phrase is “owed or due another”—not “originally owed another,” but “owed or due another” *at the time of collection*. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (“Our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection”).

Here, Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011. After that date, the debts were owed to Santander, not to CitiFinancial. The complaint itself alleges that Santander “acquired” the debts “for a few cents on the dollar” and that it “began communicating with [the plaintiffs] ... in an attempt to collect on the alleged debts.” The complaint does not allege that Santander collected on behalf of CitiFinancial after the sale. Indeed, it is axiomatic that once a debt is sold, the right to collect passes to the purchaser. *See* *Barnes v. CitiMortgage, Inc.*, 755 F.3d 1253, 1257 (11th Cir. 2014) (“A purchaser of a debt becomes the creditor and may enforce the debt in its own name.”). Thus, when Santander collected the debts, it did so as a creditor, not as a debt collector.

### II. THE DEFAULT STATUS OF A DEBT IS IRRELEVANT TO THE “DEBT COLLECTOR” DETERMINATION

The petitioners’ argument—that a purchaser of defaulted debt is automatically a “debt collector”—rests on a fundamental misreading of the statute. They claim that because the debts were in default when Santander acquired them, Santander must be a debt collector. But the FDCPA does not define “debt collector” by the default status of the debt. Instead, it hinges on whether the entity collects debts *for another*.

The petitioners rely on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was *not in default* when obtained. But this exclusion only applies *if* the person would otherwise qualify as a debt collector. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314. The exclusion does not create a new category of debt collector; it merely carves out an exception. The petitioners’ argument inverts the statutory logic: they assume that because a debt was in default, the purchaser must be a debt collector, but the statute does not say that. On the contrary, Congress intended that a person who buys defaulted debt and collects it for itself is a creditor, not a debt collector.

The legislative history confirms this. The Senate Report explains that the exclusion for nondefaulted debts was meant to protect “mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. This shows Congress was concerned with *servicing* nondefaulted debts, not with the status of the debt at the time of purchase. The Act’s structure makes clear that the default status of a debt is not a threshold determinant of whether a person is a debt collector.

### III. THE PETITIONERS’ “LOOPHOLE” ARGUMENT IS CONTRARY TO CONGRESSIONAL INTENT

The petitioners argue that allowing Santander to escape FDCPA regulation after purchasing defaulted debt would create a “loophole” that enables debt collectors to avoid liability by buying the debts they are collecting. But this argument misunderstands the FDCPA’s scope. The Act targets *abusive conduct by third-party collectors*, not the conduct of creditors collecting their own debts. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293. If every creditor who occasionally collects for others were subject to the FDCPA when collecting for itself, the Act would regulate nearly every financial institution. Congress did not intend such a broad sweep.
