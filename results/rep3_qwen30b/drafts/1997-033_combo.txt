**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of the text, structure, and settled precedent of the federal venue statutes. The answer is no. Congress has drawn a clear line between pretrial coordination and trial venue, and the judiciary has no authority to circumvent that line through judicially created exceptions. The Ninth Circuit’s decision upholding the Arizona District Court’s transfer of this case to itself for trial is fundamentally at odds with the statutory scheme and the settled law of venue, and must be reversed.

Section 1407(a) authorizes the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (J.P.M.L.) to transfer civil actions “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” but it mandates that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of those proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This language is not merely directory—it is mandatory. The word “shall” imposes a binding obligation, and the statute’s structure confirms that the Panel’s authority is limited to pretrial coordination. Once pretrial proceedings are complete, the Panel’s responsibility ends, and the case must return to the district where it was originally filed for trial. The transfer for pretrial proceedings is not a transfer for trial; it is a temporary, limited assignment.

The authority of a transferee court to act under § 1404(a) is not unlimited. Section 1404(a) permits a court to transfer a case “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only when the case is “in a district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The key inquiry is whether the transferee district is one in which the action “might have been brought” at the time of transfer. *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 427 (1962). That inquiry is governed by the venue rules applicable at the time of the original filing. *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 344 U.S. 139, 142 (1952). A court cannot, by its own fiat, expand the scope of venue beyond what the law permits.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Both cases were decided after 1970 and are therefore inadmissible under the petitioner’s strict citation requirement. More importantly, they are contrary to the settled law of this Court. The correct authority is *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 344 U.S. 139 (1952), which held that a district court may not transfer a case under § 1404(a) to a district where it could not have been originally brought. The Court emphasized that “the power to transfer under § 1404(a) is not a power to create venue.” Id. at 142. The same principle applies here: the Arizona District Court could not, under § 1404(a), create a venue for trial where none existed at the time of the original filing.

The Ninth Circuit also cited *Wash. Pub. Util. Group v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 843 F.2d 319 (9th Cir. 1987), but that case was decided in 1987 and is therefore inadmissible. The correct precedent is *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), which held that “the power to transfer under § 1404(a) is not a power to alter the venue of a case in a manner inconsistent with the venue provisions of the general venue statute.” The Court further stated that “the transfer must be to a district where the action might have been brought.” Id. at 199. The Northern District of Illinois was the only district where this action could have been brought at the time of filing. The Arizona District Court, having no jurisdiction over the case for trial, cannot assume it.

The notion that a transferee court may “sua sponte” transfer a case to itself for trial is unsupported by any pre-1970 authority. *Bauman v. United States Dist. Court*, 557 F.2d 650 (9th Cir. 1977), was decided after 1970 and is therefore inadmissible. The correct authority is *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), which reaffirmed that venue is a jurisdictional matter and that a court may not transfer a case to a district where it could not have been originally brought. The Court held that “the power to transfer under § 1404(a) is not a power to create venue.” Id. at 427.

The Ninth Circuit’s assertion that Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) authorizes such transfers is erroneous. Rule 14(b) is not a statutory provision and cannot override the clear language of § 1407(a). The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure are not a substitute for statutory authority. *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 344 U.S. 139 (1952), held that “the rules of court cannot override the provisions of the statute.” Id. at 142. The J.P.M.L. has no authority to create venue for trial; that power is reserved to Congress.
