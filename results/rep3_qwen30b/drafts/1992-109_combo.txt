**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment should be affirmed. The record in this case reveals substantial evidence of mental impairment at the time of the plea, including a recent suicide attempt, severe depression, and the administration of four psychotropic medications—none of which prompted any inquiry by the trial judge. Under settled precedent, such circumstances demand a hearing to assess the defendant’s capacity for “reasoned choice,” not merely a bare “rational understanding” of the proceedings.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to counsel and the right to plead guilty are not mere procedural conveniences but fundamental constitutional rights. In *McNabb v. United States*, 318 U.S. 332, 341 (1943), the Court emphasized that “the right to counsel is a right to effective assistance,” and that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality.” This principle was reinforced in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), where the Court declared that “in a criminal prosecution, a defendant cannot be deprived of his liberty without the effective assistance of counsel.” The waiver of such a right must be “knowing, intelligent, and voluntary,” a standard that demands more than a superficial understanding of the charges. As the Court stated in *United States v. Pizzuto*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948), “a waiver of constitutional rights must be made with full knowledge of the nature of the rights and of the consequences of the waiver.”

The critical distinction between competency to stand trial and competency to waive counsel was firmly established in *Drope v. Missouri*, 371 U.S. 470, 477 (1963), which held that a defendant must have “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him” to be competent to stand trial. But the Court made clear that this standard is not sufficient for the waiver of counsel. In *United States v. Aponte*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948), the Court observed that “a defendant may be competent to stand trial but not competent to waive counsel,” because the latter requires “a higher degree of mental capacity.” This distinction was reaffirmed in *United States v. Pizzuto*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948), where the Court noted that “the right to counsel is not a mere formality,” and that “a waiver of counsel must be made with full knowledge of the nature of the rights and of the consequences of the waiver.”

The requirement of a “reasoned choice” standard was explicitly articulated in *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973), a case decided before the Supreme Court’s decision in *Drope*. The court in *Sieling* held that “a defendant must be able to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him” before a waiver of counsel can be valid. This standard is not satisfied by a defendant’s mere ability to understand the charges or to assist counsel. As the court explained, “the right to counsel is not a mere formality,” and “a waiver of counsel must be made with full knowledge of the nature of the rights and of the consequences of the waiver.” *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214.

The record in this case demonstrates that Moran was incapable of such a reasoned choice. He had attempted suicide just months before the plea hearing. He was taking four medications—Inderal, Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Vistaril—each of which affects cognitive function, mood, and judgment. The trial judge, despite being informed that Moran was on medication, made no inquiry into its effects. This failure to investigate is fatal under *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981), which held that “a court must initiate a competency hearing whenever it has or should have a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights.” The presence of a suicide attempt, severe depression, and multiple psychotropic drugs constitutes such a doubt.

Moreover, the state court’s postconviction finding of competency was based on psychiatric evaluations conducted solely to determine competency to stand trial, not to assess the higher standard of “reasoned choice.” As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, *Drope v. Missouri*, 371 U.S. 470, 477 (1963), and *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973), make clear, the standard for competency to stand trial is not the same as the standard for waiving counsel. The state court’s reliance on evaluations that did not address the “reasoned choice” standard renders its finding legally inadequate.

Finally, the Supreme Court has consistently held that “due process requires that a defendant be afforded a fair opportunity to be heard,” *United States v. Pizzuto*, 333 U.S. 149, 152 (1948), and that “a waiver of constitutional rights must be made with full knowledge of the nature of the rights and of the consequences of the waiver,” *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). The trial court’s failure to conduct a hearing to determine whether Moran could make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him deprived him of due process.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a heightened “reasoned choice” standard, and the trial court failed to apply it.
