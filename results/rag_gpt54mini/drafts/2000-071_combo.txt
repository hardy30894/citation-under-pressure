Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club violated the Free Speech Clause, and the asserted Establishment Clause concern does not justify the exclusion. This Court has repeatedly held that when the government opens its property to private speech on a nonpublic basis, it may not discriminate against speech because of its religious viewpoint. In Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), the Court explained that there is “no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’” and become unprotected “worship.” The Second Circuit’s decision conflicts with that principle by converting the Club’s religious perspective on morality into a different “subject” altogether.

That error is especially clear under the Court’s earlier forum and religion cases. In Fowler v. Rhode Island, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), the Court held that it is not for government to treat one religious service differently from another, because that “amounts to the state preferring some religious groups over this one.” Similarly, in Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), the Court invalidated denial of access to public property because public officials may not discriminate against religious speech based on disfavor toward the speaker’s views. Milford’s refusal here was triggered by the Club’s Christian content, not by any neutral limit on time, place, or manner.

The forum here was also opened to a range of community uses. Under Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952), the Court recognized that the State may accommodate religious instruction without establishing religion. Zorach emphasized that the public schools may not themselves teach religion, but may permit religious activity where the State remains neutral. Milford’s policy, however, did the opposite: it singled out the Club because its speech was religious. The Establishment Clause does not compel that discrimination. To the contrary, Illinois ex rel. McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), addressed a program in which religious teachers “came weekly into the school buildings during the regular hours set apart for secular teaching.” That is a far cry from the after-hours, private, community use at issue here. Likewise, Abington School District v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203 (1963), involved mandatory Bible reading and prayer “at the opening of each public school on each school day,” again a direct public-school exercise. Milford’s after-hours access for private speakers bears no resemblance to those coercive in-school practices.

This Court has also protected private religious speech in public settings rather than requiring exclusion. In Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court recognized that permit schemes cannot be administered to deny religious meetings a fair opportunity to speak. And in Board of Education of Central School District No. 1 v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236 (1968), the Court upheld aid to parochial students where the benefit ran to the children and not to the religious schools themselves. The same principle applies here: allowing children to attend a private club after school would not make the school the speaker, nor would it transform the school into an establishment of religion.

Milford’s decision also cannot be defended as ordinary subject-matter discrimination. The Court has made clear that when government opens a forum to private speakers, it may not exclude speech simply because the viewpoint is religious. In Cochran v. Louisiana State Board of Education, 281 U.S. 370 (1930), the Court sustained a program benefiting schoolchildren even though some attended religious schools, because the aid was directed to the children. That reasoning underscores that neutrality toward religion, not hostility, is the constitutional requirement. Likewise, Merion Cricket Club v. United States, 315 U.S. 42 (1942), shows that legal classifications turn on what is actually being conferred, not on labels. Here, Milford was not being asked to sponsor worship; it was being asked to open a community-use forum on equal terms.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on the notion that the Club sought “religious instruction” rather than “values” is exactly the sort of judicial classification the First Amendment forbids. As Fowler held, courts may not “approve, disapprove, classify, regulate, or in any manner control sermons delivered at religious meetings.” 345 U.S. at 70. Milford’s exclusion did just that. Because the Club sought access to a forum otherwise available to civic and youth groups, the denial was viewpoint discrimination.

Nor does the Establishment Clause require exclusion. The School’s concern was only that permitting the Club’s meetings might appear to endorse religion. But the Court has long recognized that mere accommodation of private religious activity is not establishment. Zorach, 343 U.S. 306; Allen, 392 U.S. 236. The proper constitutional course is neutrality, not suppression. Milford could have allowed the Club on the same terms as other community groups and thereby avoided any endorsement problem. Its choice to exclude religious speech was therefore both unnecessary and unconstitutional.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
