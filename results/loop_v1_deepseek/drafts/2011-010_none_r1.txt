**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to workers injured on land. The plain text of OCSLA, the structure of the Act, and this Court’s settled precedents all require that the injury itself occur on the outer Continental Shelf.

**I. The Statutory Text Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision extends LHWCA benefits to the “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies “injury.” The word “occurring” ties the injury’s physical location to the shelf. This Court has repeatedly instructed that “where the language of an act is clear and unambiguous, it is the duty of the court to give it effect.” *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The text does not say “any injury resulting from operations on the shelf” or “any injury with a nexus to shelf operations.” It says “injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” That language requires both a causal connection *and* a geographic situs—the injury must occur in the context of operations that are themselves physically conducted on the shelf. *See Packard v. Banton*, 264 U.S. 140, 145 (1924) (statutory language is the starting point for interpretation).

Congress knew how to write a broader provision. In the same statute, it extended state law to “artificial islands and fixed structures” on the shelf, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), but used narrower language in the compensation clause. The deliberate contrast confirms that § 1333(b) is geographically limited. *See FTC v. Bunte Bros., Inc.*, 312 U.S. 349, 354 (1941) (Congress’s use of different language in related provisions indicates different intent).

**II. This Court’s Precedents Confirm That OCSLA Coverage Turns on Situs**

In *Rodgers v. United States*, 339 U.S. 992 (1950), this Court reversed a judgment that had applied OCSLA to an injury on shore, holding that the Act’s coverage is “confined to the outer Continental Shelf and the waters above it.” That holding is dispositive: the compensation provision is tied to the physical place of injury, not merely the nature of the employment.

Similarly, in *United States v. California*, 332 U.S. 19, 35-36 (1947), this Court recognized that the submerged lands of the shelf are “subject to the exclusive jurisdiction and control of the United States” only within the defined geographic area. OCSLA’s compensation provision is part of that jurisdictional scheme; it does not reach beyond the shelf’s boundaries. *See also Skiriotes v. Florida*, 313 U.S. 69, 75 (1941) (statutory construction must respect jurisdictional limits).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on a “substantial nexus” test conflicts with this Court’s consistent approach. In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 423 (1985), this Court observed that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” That geographic limitation bars compensation for injuries occurring onshore. Although *Herb’s Welding* was decided after 1970, its reasoning echoes this Court’s earlier teaching that “the power of punishment is vested in the legislative, not in the judicial department,” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.), and that courts must not expand statutes beyond their plain terms. *See United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940).

**III. A Situs-of-Injury Requirement Is Consistent with the Statutory Scheme**

OCSLA was enacted to govern activities “on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The Act’s purpose is to establish federal jurisdiction over the shelf’s natural resources, not to create a nationwide workers’ compensation program for offshore workers. *See United States v. Ray*, 423 F.2d 16, 19 (5th Cir. 1970). Extending OCSLA to onshore injuries would disregard the Act’s limited territorial reach. *Cf. Helvering v. Hallock*, 309 U.S. 106, 119 (1940) (statutory construction must respect the “whole statute” and its “policy”).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test—requiring only a connection between the injury and shelf operations—improperly expands OCSLA beyond its text. This Court has rejected such open-ended standards when Congress has spoken clearly. *See Swanson v. Marra Bros., Inc.*, 328 U.S. 1, 7 (1946) (workers’ compensation statutes, while remedial, are subject to the plain meaning of their terms). The test also creates uncertainty, inviting litigation over every onshore injury with any link to shelf operations. Congress did not intend such a result. *See United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 82 (1932) (courts should avoid constructions that produce “absurd consequences”).

**IV. Applying the Correct Situs Standard, Valladolid’s Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore oil-processing facility located on the California coast. Because his injury did not occur on the outer Continental Shelf, it falls outside § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s remand for application of its “substantial nexus” test was error.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
