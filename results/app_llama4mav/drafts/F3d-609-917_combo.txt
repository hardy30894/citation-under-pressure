ARGUMENT

I. RAY'S STATE TORT CLAIMS ARE PREEMPTED BY FEDERAL AVIATION LEGISLATION

The Airline Deregulation Act of 1978's preemption provision, although enacted after the events in question, has its roots in earlier federal aviation legislation. The Federal Aviation Act of 1958, Pub. L. 85-726, 72 Stat. 731, granted the Federal Aviation Agency broad authority to regulate aviation safety and services. As the Supreme Court noted in City of Burbank v. Lockheed Air Terminal Inc., 411 U.S. 624, 633 (1973) (citing 49 U.S.C. § 1301 et seq. (1964 ed., Supp. V)), the Federal Aviation Act "pervasively regulates" the aviation industry. The Court has long recognized that federal regulation of aviation is "complete and exclusive," as stated in Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. Minnesota, 322 U.S. 292, 303 (1944).

The preemption doctrine has its roots in the Supremacy Clause, U.S. Const. art. VI, cl. 2. As the Court held in Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. 1, 211 (1824), "the laws of the United States ... are the supreme law of the land." When Congress exercises its authority under the Commerce Clause, as it did in the Federal Aviation Act, state laws that conflict with federal law are preempted. See Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941).

In the context of aviation, the Court has consistently held that state laws are preempted when they conflict with federal regulations or statutes. See, e.g., Rosenhan v. United States, 131 F.2d 932, 934 (10th Cir. 1942) (citing 49 U.S.C. § 40101 et seq.), cert. denied, 318 U.S. 790 (1943); cf. Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss, 302 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1937) (upholding state regulation where no conflicting federal regulation existed).

Ray's claims for false imprisonment and negligence arise from American's decision to keep passengers on the plane during the tarmac delay. However, the decision to keep passengers on board was closely related to the airline's operations and services, which are subject to federal regulation. As the Court held in Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co., 272 U.S. 605, 613 (1926), state laws that "constitute an unreasonable burden on interstate commerce" are preempted.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the regulation of airline services, including the treatment of passengers, is a matter of federal concern. See, e.g., Terminal R. Ass'n of St. Louis v. Brotherhood of R. Trainmen, 318 U.S. 1, 8 (1943) (noting the "comprehensive" nature of federal regulation of railroads, which is analogous to the regulation of airlines).

In light of the pervasive federal regulation of the aviation industry, Ray's state tort claims are preempted. The district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of American should be affirmed on this alternative ground.

II. RAY FAILED TO RAISE A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT

As the Court held in Dyer v. MacDougall, 201 F.2d 265, 269 (2d Cir. 1952) (L. Hand, J.), a party opposing summary judgment must present more than mere allegations or denials to raise a genuine issue of material fact. Ray failed to present evidence that American's actions were negligent or that she was falsely imprisoned. The record shows that American provided passengers with opportunities to deplane, and Ray chose to remain on the plane.

The district court's denial of Ray's Rule 56(f) motion was proper, as she failed to demonstrate that additional discovery would raise a genuine issue of material fact. See, e.g., Engl v. Aetna Life Ins. Co., 139 F.2d 469, 472-73 (2d Cir. 1943) (affirming denial of continuance where party failed to show additional discovery would be material).

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's grant of summary judgment in favor of American should be affirmed.
