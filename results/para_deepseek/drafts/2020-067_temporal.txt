**ARGUMENT**

The Eleventh Circuit properly considered the entire record, including Gregory Greer’s prior convictions, to determine that the *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness of his trial. This Court has long held that an appellate court reviewing for plain error may examine matters outside the trial evidence to assess whether an error caused prejudice. The plain-error standard, rooted in *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), permits an appellate court to notice errors “affecting substantial rights” even if not raised below, but only to prevent “a miscarriage of justice.” *Id.* at 160. In making that determination, the court must consider the whole record, not merely the evidence submitted to the jury.

**I. The Appellate Court May Consider the Entire Record to Determine Prejudice**

This Court has consistently instructed that harmless-error and plain-error review require examination of the full record. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764–65 (1946), the Court explained that an appellate court must “review the whole record” to decide whether an error “had substantial influence” on the outcome. Similarly, in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 23–24 (1967), the Court held that even constitutional errors may be harmless if the reviewing court can “declare a belief that [the error] was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt” after “examining the record.” These cases establish that the appellate court’s inquiry is not confined to the evidence presented at trial; it may consider any part of the record that sheds light on whether the error actually prejudiced the defendant.

The same principle governs plain-error review. In *Atkinson*, the Court recognized that an appellate court may notice plain error “to prevent a miscarriage of justice,” and in doing so, it must evaluate the error in the context of the entire case. 297 U.S. at 160. The Court has never limited that evaluation to the trial evidence alone. To the contrary, in *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 83 (1942), the Court stated that an appellate court must “examine the entire record” to determine whether an error affected substantial rights. That examination includes facts that are undisputed and part of the case file, such as a defendant’s admissions in a plea agreement or stipulations at sentencing.

**II. Greer’s Prior Convictions Were Part of the Record and Properly Considered**

Greer pleaded guilty and expressly agreed that he was “punishable as an Armed Career Criminal” based on five prior aggravated burglary convictions. That stipulation, along with the presentence report and the district court’s findings, became part of the record. The Eleventh Circuit did not “go outside” the record; it considered evidence that Greer himself placed before the court. This Court has long held that an appellate court may take judicial notice of facts that are not in dispute and are established by the record. *See, e.g., United States v. Jones*, 174 U.S. 96, 100 (1899) (court may consider “facts of common knowledge” and those “appearing in the record”). Moreover, in *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 506 (1943), the Court approved an appellate court’s reliance on a stipulation in the record to assess prejudice, even though the stipulation was not presented to the jury.

Greer’s argument that the appellate court must blind itself to his admitted prior convictions would require the court to ignore reality. The *Rehaif* error—the failure to instruct the jury that Greer must have known he was a felon—could not have prejudiced him because the record conclusively demonstrates that he knew he was a felon. He had been convicted of five aggravated burglaries, each a felony. He admitted those convictions in open court. No rational jury could have found otherwise. Under *Kotteakos*, an error is harmless if it did not “substantially influence” the verdict. 328 U.S. at 765. Here, the error had no influence at all because the undisputed facts foreclosed any possibility of acquittal.

**III. Plain-Error Review Does Not Require a Retrial of Undisputed Facts**

Greer seeks to transform plain-error review into a rigid rule that an appellate court may consider only the evidence the jury heard. That approach finds no support in this Court’s precedents. In *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), the Court held that harmless-error review requires the court to “consider the record as a whole,” including matters that were not before the jury but were part of the case. The Court reasoned that the purpose of harmless-error analysis is to determine whether the error “affected the verdict,” and that determination necessarily involves the entire factual context. *Id.* at 87. Similarly, in *Atkinson*, the Court emphasized that plain-error review exists to prevent “a miscarriage of justice,” not to enforce procedural technicalities. 297 U.S. at 160. If the record shows that the defendant suffered no actual prejudice, the error cannot be said to affect substantial rights.

Greer’s reliance on a narrow view of the “trial record” is misplaced. The record in a criminal case includes all documents filed with the court, including plea agreements, stipulations, and sentencing materials. This Court has long recognized that an appellate court may consider such materials. *See, e.g., United States v. Bufalino*, 285 U.S. 209, 214 (1932) (appellate court may examine “the entire record” to determine whether error was harmless). The Eleventh Circuit did exactly what this Court’s precedents require: it examined the whole record to determine whether the *Rehaif* error actually prejudiced Greer. Because the record conclusively showed that Greer knew he was a felon, the error did not affect his substantial rights, and the court properly declined to vacate his conviction.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this Court’s long-standing precedent that an appellate court reviewing for plain error may consider the entire record, including matters outside the trial evidence, to determine whether an error affected substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings. The judgment should be affirmed.
