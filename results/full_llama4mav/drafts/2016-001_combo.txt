The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones.

The design patent statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that an infringer shall be liable "to the extent of his total profit" from the sale of the infringing article. However, this provision must be interpreted in light of the principle that damages for patent infringement should be commensurate with the value of the infringing feature. As the Supreme Court held in Garretson v. Clark, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884), "the patentee ... must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features".

In Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co., 225 U.S. 604, 614-15 (1912), the Court held that when an infringing device contains both patented and unpatented components, the infringer's profits should be apportioned to reflect the value of the patented feature. Similarly, in Dowagiac Mfg. Co. v. Minnesota Moline Plow Co., 235 U.S. 641, 646 (1915), the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be limited to the profits attributable to the infringing feature.

The principle of apportionment is also reflected in the Court's decision in Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480, 489 (1854), where it held that a patentee is entitled to recover "the actual damages sustained" as a result of the infringement. In Coupe v. Royer, 155 U.S. 565, 582 (1895), the Court held that the infringer's profits should be calculated based on the "net gain" from the sale of the infringing article, but only to the extent that the gain is attributable to the infringing feature.

In Tilghman v. Proctor, 125 U.S. 136, 146 (1888), the Court held that a patentee is entitled to recover damages for infringement, but only to the extent that the infringement caused the patentee harm. Similarly, in Root v. Railway Co., 105 U.S. 189, 194 (1882), the Court held that damages for patent infringement should be limited to the "actual loss" sustained by the patentee.

The Federal Circuit's decision in this case is inconsistent with these principles. By affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, the Federal Circuit effectively allowed Apple to recover damages for features that were not infringing. This result is contrary to the principle of apportionment established by the Supreme Court in Garretson, Westinghouse, and Dowagiac.

Furthermore, the Federal Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the principle that damages for patent infringement should be limited to the value of the infringing feature. As the Court held in Suffolk Co. v. Hayden, 70 U.S. 315, 320 (1866), "the profits recoverable ... are limited to those made upon the manufacture, use, and sale of the patented improvement". In Providence Rubber Co. v. Goodyear, 76 U.S. 788, 804 (1869), the Court held that the infringer's profits should be calculated based on the "profits made on the patented article".

In conclusion, the Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits from the sale of its smartphones. Damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the component that infringes the design patent. The Court should reverse the Federal Circuit's decision and remand the case for a determination of damages based on the principles established in Garretson, Westinghouse, Dowagiac, and other cases.
