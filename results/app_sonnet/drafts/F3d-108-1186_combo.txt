## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Resubmit the Ambiguous Verdict to the Jury Before Discharge.

A jury's verdict must be given a construction that carries out the jury's actual intent, and where two readings are possible, the court must adopt the one that renders the jury's findings consistent rather than one that nullifies its evident purpose. Gallick v. Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co., 372 U.S. 108, 119 (1963) (where "there is a view of the case that makes the jury's answers to the special questions consistent, they must be resolved that way"). This duty to reconcile, rather than defeat, a jury's findings applies with particular force where, as here, the verdict forms invited overlapping computations of the same damages. See Atlantic & Gulf Stevedores, Inc. v. Ellerman Lines, Ltd., 369 U.S. 355, 364 (1962) (courts must examine the interrelationship of jury findings before treating them as irreconcilable).

The jury's award on Form 1—$70,768.02—is not an isolated number; it is the product of the jury doing exactly the arithmetic the instructions asked of it: subtracting the cost of correcting the pipe defects from the $157,262.36 in unpaid invoices. That the jury also completed Form 3, awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30—the very same cost of repair—reflects confusion in the verdict forms, not a considered intent to deny Unit any recovery at all. Where separate issues submitted to a jury are, in the words of the Supreme Court, "so interwoven ... that [they] cannot be submitted to the jury independently ... without confusion and uncertainty," the resulting ambiguity is a defect in the trial mechanism, not evidence of a deliberate jury decision. Gasoline Products Co. v. Champlin Refining Co., 283 U.S. 494, 500 (1931). The remedy for such interwoven ambiguity is clarification, not the imposition of the interpretation least favorable to the prevailing party on liability.

The district court's speculation that the jury meant to award Enron a full, independent set-off on top of a separate damages reduction is precisely the kind of guesswork the courts have refused to indulge when a verdict's meaning is unclear. As the Supreme Court has cautioned, courts should not resolve genuine ambiguity in a verdict by assuming the jury intended a result inconsistent with its own reasoning. Dunn v. United States, 284 U.S. 390, 393 (1932) ("Consistency in the verdict is not necessary."). Dunn's teaching cuts against, not for, the district court's approach: rather than treating apparent tension between verdict forms as fatal, the court should have gone to the source—the jury itself, before it was discharged—to remove the ambiguity. Because the jury remained available and willing to answer, the district court's refusal to ask a clarifying question was error, and it left the judgment resting on the court's own conjecture rather than the jury's actual finding. See Galloway v. United States, 319 U.S. 372, 407 (1943) (Black, J., dissenting) (emphasizing that the jury's role is to find the facts, and courts should not supplant that finding by inference).

### II. The District Court Erred in Construing the Verdict to Award Unit Nothing.

Even without resubmission, the verdict, read as a whole in light of the instructions actually given, compels the conclusion that the jury intended Unit to recover $70,768.02. The instructions told the jury that if it found Unit had only substantially, rather than fully, performed, it was to award "the amount of the unpaid invoices, less the cost to Enron of correcting any defects." The jury's Form 1 figure is arithmetically identical to that formula: $157,262.36 minus $86,494.30 equals $70,768.02. The most natural, and indeed the only reasonable, explanation for the jury's separate notation of the same $86,494.30 figure on the set-off form is that the jury—following a confusing set of forms—simply recorded the offset twice rather than intending Enron to receive it twice. See Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent, 230 N.Y. 239, 244 (1921) (Cardozo, J.) (recognizing that where performance is substantial, the proper remedy is an allowance for the cost of the defect, not forfeiture of the entire contract price).

Appellate courts owe deference to a jury's assessment of damages, but that deference presumes the verdict reflects the jury's actual arithmetic rather than a court-imposed double subtraction. Fairmount Glass Works v. Cub Fork Coal Co., 287 U.S. 474, 481 (1933) (damages verdicts are peculiarly for the jury and should not be supplanted by the court's independent judgment absent genuine irreconcilability). Likewise, a trial court's discretion to interpret or set aside a verdict must be exercised to protect, not defeat, the substance of the jury's decision. Montgomery Ward & Co. v. Duncan, 311 U.S. 243, 251 (1940). Where, as here, a straightforward and internally consistent reading of the verdict is available—one that gives full effect to both the liability finding and the damages calculation the jury was instructed to perform—the court is not free to adopt a contrary reading that nullifies the jury's finding for Unit. Kennon v. Gilmer, 131 U.S. 22, 30 (1889) (a verdict should be molded to accord with the manifest intention of the jury, not construed to defeat it).

The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02.
