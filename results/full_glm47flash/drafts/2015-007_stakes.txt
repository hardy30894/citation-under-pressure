**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE SIXTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE A UNANIMOUS JURY RECOMMENDATION FOR DEATH OR A JURY DETERMINATION OF MENTAL RETARDATION AS AN ABSOLUTE BAR.**

**A. *Ring* Did Not Require a Unanimous Jury Recommendation for Death.**

In *Ring v. Arizona*, the Supreme Court held that the fact-finding function of determining aggravating circumstances is a "critical stage" of the trial requiring a jury determination. 536 U.S. at 609. However, the Court explicitly declined to require a unanimous jury recommendation for the death sentence. Id. at 610. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment does not require unanimity in the recommendation of a sentence, only in the finding of the elements of the offense. Id. at 610-11. The Court noted that the jury’s role is to recommend a sentence, not to impose it, and that the judge retains the authority to impose the sentence based on the jury’s findings. Id. at 611.

Following *Ring*, the Florida Supreme Court held that the decision did not require a unanimous jury recommendation for death or a judge’s finding of aggravators based on a jury verdict. *Steele v. State*, 921 So. 2d 543, 548 (Fla. 2006). The Court explained that *Ring* only required that the jury find the existence of the aggravating factor, not that the jury vote unanimously to impose the death penalty. Id. at 548-49. Similarly, in *Coday v. State*, the Court reaffirmed that *Ring* did not require a unanimous jury finding of the sentence. 946 So. 2d at 1024. Therefore, the trial court’s reliance on the jury’s non-unanimous recommendation to find the statutory aggravating factors and impose the death penalty does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

**B. *Ring* Did Not Require a Jury Determination of Mental Retardation as an Absolute Bar.**

*Atkins v. Virginia* held that the execution of mentally retarded persons is cruel and unusual punishment. 536 U.S. at 321. However, the Court left the manner of determining mental retardation to the states. Id. at 317. Florida has established a statutory scheme to determine mental retardation, which the trial court applied here. The determination of whether a defendant is mentally retarded is a statutory element, not an aggravating factor, and is therefore properly determined by the judge. *Steele*, 921 So. 2d at 548. The judge’s finding that Hurst is not mentally retarded is a valid application of Florida law and does not violate the Sixth Amendment. The State is not required to submit the determination of mental retardation to the jury as an element of the offense, as *Ring* only mandated jury findings for aggravating circumstances.

**II. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE IMPOSITION OF THE DEATH PENALTY UNDER FLORIDA’S STATUTORY SCHEME.**

**A. *Atkins* Permits State Determination of Mental Retardation.**

As noted, *Atkins* left the determination of mental retardation to the states. 536 U.S. at 317. Florida’s statutory scheme is a valid method of compliance. The trial court’s application of the three-prong test to find that Hurst is not mentally retarded is a valid exercise of state authority and does not constitute cruel and unusual punishment.

**B. The Lack of Unanimity Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment.**

The Eighth Amendment requires that the death penalty be imposed only after a reliable determination that the defendant is eligible for the death penalty. *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991). While the Eighth Amendment demands reliability, it does not mandate unanimity in the jury’s recommendation of the sentence. *Steele*, 921 So. 2d at 549. The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that the lack of unanimity in the recommendation of the sentence does not violate the Eighth Amendment. *Coday*, 946 So. 2d at 1024. The reliability of the sentence is ensured by the judge’s independent review of the record and the statutory requirements for finding aggravating factors. Therefore, the imposition of the death penalty in this case does not violate the Eighth Amendment.
