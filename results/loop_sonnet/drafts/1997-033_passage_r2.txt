# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) commands that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That command must be enforced as written.

In *Caminetti v. United States*, this Court held that where statutory language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

In *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, this Court likewise explained that it will follow the plain meaning of statutory text: "In such cases we have followed their plain meaning." 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Only when plain meaning produces absurd or futile results has this Court looked beyond the statutory text to purpose. Nothing about remanding a case once pretrial coordination concludes is absurd; it is precisely what Congress directed, since Section 1407(a) exists to coordinate pretrial matters among related actions, not to relocate trials permanently.

Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute. Under *Caminetti* and *American Trucking*, courts have no license to depart from that mandatory command merely because departure might serve efficiency.

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision, applicable to ordinary civil litigation, designed to "protect litigants, witnesses and the public against unnecessary inconvenience and expense." *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964). It operates only where the transferee forum is one in which the action "might have been brought" by the plaintiff in the first instance—a limitation this Court has strictly enforced even against arguments grounded in convenience or party consent. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343–44 (1960). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions, governed by the "convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice" standard applicable to venue transfers generally, but expressly bounded by the Panel's remand command. *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960).

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. As this Court has explained, "however inclusive may be the general language of a statute, it 'will not be held to apply to a matter specifically dealt with in another part of the same enactment.'" *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957) (quoting *Rodgers v. United States*, 185 U.S. 83, 87–89 (1902)). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer. It cannot be displaced by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a), a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all.

This Court has likewise confirmed that Section 1404(a) operates according to its own terms and does not license departure from requirements Congress imposed elsewhere in the transfer scheme. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949). Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance is inconvenient.

Under *American Trucking*, courts must follow plain statutory meaning absent absurdity. 310 U.S. at 543. Under *Caminetti*, a court's task when statutory language is plain is to enforce it according to its terms. 242 U.S. at 485.

Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois. The statute states that remand must occur "at or before the conclusion of" pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command. This conclusion follows from the reasoning of *Fourco Glass*, 353 U.S. at 228–29, *Van Dusen*, 376 U.S. at 616, *Hoffman*, 363 U.S. at 343–44, and *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. at 61. The judgment below should be reversed.
