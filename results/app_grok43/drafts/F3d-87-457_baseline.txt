ARGUMENT

I. The Disclosure Requirements Are Facially Overbroad and Chill Protected Political Speech.

A statute that compels disclosure of expenditures made in connection with lobbying “must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest.” See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64 (1976) (per curiam). Chapter 93-121 requires lobbyists and principals to report every expenditure by category and to identify the source of funds for each, without regard to whether the expenditure is de minimis or unrelated to any attempt to influence legislation. Because the statute “sweeps within its ambit a substantial amount of protected” expression, it is facially invalid. See Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601, 612 (1973).

The administrative-opinion procedure does not cure the overbreadth. A lobbyist who must “refrain from engaging in protected First Amendment activity or risk civil sanction” faces the precise dilemma that renders a facial challenge ripe. See Abbott Labs. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53 (1967). The League therefore need not await an enforcement action or an advisory opinion before mounting its challenge. See United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987) (recognizing that a facial claim succeeds when “no set of circumstances exists under which the Act would be valid”).

II. The Contingency-Fee Prohibition Cannot Survive the Heightened Scrutiny Required for Restrictions on Political Advocacy.

The ban on fees “contingent on legislative outcome” directly restricts the terms on which citizens may retain advocates to petition the government. Such a restriction “is a burden on political speech” that must satisfy strict scrutiny. See Meyer v. Grant, 486 U.S. 414, 425 (1988). The State has identified no interest that is both compelling and narrowly tailored to justify prohibiting compensation arrangements that have long been used in other forms of legal representation. See, e.g., Bates v. State Bar of Ariz., 433 U.S. 350, 384 (1977) (invalidating blanket prohibition on attorney advertising because less restrictive means were available).

Although older authority once sustained analogous limits, more recent decisions have recognized that “the First Amendment protects political advocacy” even when it takes the form of paid persuasion. See Citizens Against Rent Control v. City of Berkeley, 454 U.S. 290, 299 (1981). The district court therefore erred in upholding the prohibition on the basis of superseded precedent.

III. Because the Statute Is Facially Invalid, the Judgment Must Be Reversed.

The League has shown that “a substantial number of [the statute’s] applications are unconstitutional.” See New York State Club Ass’n v. City of New York, 487 U.S. 1, 14 (1988). Summary judgment for the State was therefore improper, and the case should be remanded with instructions to enter judgment for the League.
