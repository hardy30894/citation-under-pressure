**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Moll’s hostile work environment claim. The court improperly limited its analysis to only “sexually offensive acts” and failed to consider the totality of the circumstances, including facially sex-neutral conduct that contributed to the same unlawful environment. This legal error requires reversal.

**I. Hostile Work Environment Claims Require Consideration of All Circumstances, Including Facially Sex-Neutral Conduct**

A hostile work environment exists “[w]hen the workplace is permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment.” *Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The inquiry is holistic: courts must consider “the totality of the circumstances,” including the frequency and severity of the conduct, whether it is physically threatening or humiliating, and whether it unreasonably interferes with an employee’s work performance. *Id.* at 23.

This Court has repeatedly held that facially sex-neutral incidents may be considered when evaluating a sex-based hostile work environment claim. In *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002), we explained that “a plaintiff may rely on facially neutral incidents” to support a hostile work environment claim, because “the accumulation of such incidents can, over time, reinforce the harassment and create a hostile environment.” *See also Raniola v. Bratton*, 243 F.3d 610, 620 (2d Cir. 2001) (considering both sexual and non-sexual acts as part of a single hostile environment). The district court’s exclusive focus on “sexually offensive acts” ignored this binding precedent.

**II. The District Court’s Temporal Limitation Was Legally Erroneous**

The district court dismissed Moll’s hostile work environment claim because it found no “sexually offensive acts” within the applicable statute of limitations. But a hostile work environment claim is “composed of a series of separate acts that collectively constitute one ‘unlawful employment practice.’” *National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101, 117 (2002). Under *Morgan*, a plaintiff may file a timely charge “as long as an act contributing to the claim occurs within the filing period.” *Id.* at 120. The district court, however, looked only at the most recent sexually explicit acts—Irving’s note mentioning showering (March 2001) and the hotel room call (1999)—and ignored the many acts that occurred within the limitations period.

Moll filed her EEOC charge on September 19, 2003. Acts occurring after September 19, 2001, are within the limitations period. The record includes numerous such acts: Irving’s refusal to allow Moll to work from home while male colleagues continued to do so (May 2002); the denial of her vacation request (July 2002); the exclusion of women from work-related social events (ongoing); and the transfer to Syracuse in December 2004. These facially sex-neutral acts were part of the same pattern of gender-based hostility that began with Irving’s sexually explicit notes. See *Alfano*, 294 F.3d at 375 (noting that “a series of separate acts” may constitute a hostile environment even if none individually is severe). The district court’s failure to consider these timely acts as part of the continuing violation was clear error.

**III. The “Sham Issue of Fact” Doctrine Does Not Bar Consideration of the Testimony of Non-Party Witnesses**

Verizon may argue that certain testimony contradicts prior statements, invoking the “sham issue of fact” doctrine. That doctrine, however, prohibits a party from contradicting its own prior deposition testimony to create a material dispute of fact. *See, e.g., Brown v. Henderson*, 257 F.3d 246, 252 (2d Cir. 2001). It does not apply to non-party witnesses. The district court’s order did not rely on this doctrine, and Verizon cannot use it to exclude evidence that supports Moll’s claim.

**IV. Conclusion**

The district court’s judgment on Moll’s hostile work environment claim must be vacated. The court improperly limited its analysis to sexually explicit conduct and ignored the timely, facially sex-neutral acts that, together with the earlier acts, created a hostile work environment. This Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings consistent with the totality-of-the-circumstances standard.
