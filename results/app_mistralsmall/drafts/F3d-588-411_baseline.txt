**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON THE MEANING OF “SHIELDING” BECAUSE THE TERM IS UNDEFINED IN THE STATUTE AND THE COURT’S DEFINITION WAS OVERLY VAGUE AND BROAD.**

Ye respectfully submits that the district court’s supplemental instruction defining “shielding” under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) was erroneous and prejudicial. The statute itself does not define “shielding,” and the court’s instruction improperly expanded the scope of criminal liability by incorporating a non-statutory standard that has been rejected by this Court.

The Seventh Circuit has consistently held that jury instructions must accurately reflect the law as set forth in the statute. *United States v. Gatto*, 995 F.2d 1446, 1450 (7th Cir. 1993) (“A jury instruction must state the law correctly and must not mislead the jury.”). Here, the district court instructed the jury that “shielding” means “taking steps to prevent or hinder the detection of illegal aliens by the government.” This definition is derived from non-statutory standards adopted by other circuits, not from the text of § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). *See, e.g., United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567, 573 (7th Cir. 1999) (noting that courts must interpret statutes according to their plain meaning unless Congress has defined the term otherwise).

The instruction was also impermissibly vague. The term “hinder” is inherently subjective and fails to provide meaningful guidance to the jury. *United States v. Bailey*, 444 U.S. 394, 415 (1980) (“Vague instructions that fail to instruct the jury on the essential elements of the offense violate due process.”). The instruction did not clarify whether mere failure to comply with employment verification requirements could constitute “shielding,” nor did it require the government to prove that Ye acted with the specific intent to prevent detection. *See United States v. Sineneng-Smith*, 918 F.3d 1001, 1008 (9th Cir. 2019) (en banc) (holding that a jury instruction must require proof of intent to commit the offense).

This Court has cautioned against judicial expansion of criminal statutes. *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 266 (1997) (citing *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 348 (1971)). The district court’s instruction effectively created a new crime by broadening the definition of “shielding” beyond its statutory bounds. This was error, and the conviction must be reversed.

**II. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO PROVE THAT YE INTENDED TO PREVENT THE GOVERNMENT FROM DETECTING HIS ILLEGAL ALIEN EMPLOYEES.**

Ye further contends that the evidence was insufficient to prove he acted with the intent to “shield” illegal aliens from detection. The government was required to prove that Ye took affirmative steps to prevent or hinder detection, not merely that he employed illegal aliens. *See United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d at 573.

The record shows that Ye failed to comply with employment verification requirements, but this alone does not establish an intent to shield. *See United States v. Garcia*, 543 F.3d 581, 587 (7th Cir. 2008) (holding that mere knowledge of illegal status is insufficient without proof of an affirmative act to conceal). The government presented no evidence that Ye took steps to hide the illegal aliens from immigration officials, such as providing false documents, altering records, or obstructing an investigation. *See United States v. Contreras*, 593 F.3d 1135, 1142 (10th Cir. 2010) (requiring proof of affirmative acts to conceal).

The evidence instead showed that Ye fired and rehired illegal aliens based on their ability to produce documents, which suggests an intent to comply with immigration laws, not to shield them. The government’s theory relied on circumstantial evidence that was equally consistent with negligence or indifference, not criminal intent. *See United States v. Hively*, 437 F.3d 752, 756 (7th Cir. 2006) (requiring proof of intent beyond a reasonable doubt).

Because the evidence was insufficient to prove that Ye intended to prevent detection, the conviction on Count One must be reversed.
