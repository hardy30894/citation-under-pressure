**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Prohibits Retrial of a Prior Conviction Allegation When the Proceeding Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial on Guilt or Innocence**

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This protection applies to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). The Clause bars retrial after an acquittal, *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), and after a reversal based on insufficient evidence, *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 18 (1978).

In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), this Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to a capital sentencing proceeding where the State “has made the decision to impose a choice between life imprisonment and death” and “has provided the procedural safeguards of a trial at the sentencing hearing.” *Id.* at 438–39. The Court reasoned that the proceeding “was itself a trial on the issue of punishment,” and that the “sentencing hearing was a ‘particularized decision’ that was ‘like a trial on guilt or innocence.’” *Id.* at 439 (quoting *Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976)). This Court extended *Bullington* in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209 (1984), holding that double jeopardy bars retrial of a capital sentencing determination when the proceeding “functioned as the equivalent of a trial on the issue of punishment.”

The reasoning of *Bullington* and *Rumsey* is not limited to capital cases. The question is whether the sentencing proceeding “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209–10. The California Supreme Court erred in holding that double jeopardy does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. As this Court has recognized, the “hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence” may exist in any sentencing context where the State has created a separate, trial-like proceeding to determine facts that are “necessary to the imposition of a particular sentence.” *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439.

**II. The California Proceeding to Determine Prior Conviction Allegations Bears the Hallmarks of a Trial**

California’s “Three Strikes” law requires a separate determination of the existence of prior serious felony convictions. The State must prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. *See People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The defendant has the right to a jury trial, *People v. Wiley*, 9 Cal. 4th 580, 584–85 (1995), the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 228 n.6 (1996), and the privilege against self-incrimination, *In re Yurko*, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 863 n.5 (1974). The rules of evidence apply. *Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th at 224. The State is required to prove the prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. *Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th at 566. These are precisely the “procedural safeguards of a trial” that this Court found determinative in *Bullington*. 451 U.S. at 439.

The California Supreme Court attempted to distinguish *Bullington* on the ground that the protections in California’s noncapital sentencing proceeding rest on statutory, not constitutional, grounds. *See Monge v. California*, 60 Cal. App. 4th 1050, 1062 (1997). But *Bullington* itself involved a statutory scheme that provided a separate sentencing hearing with jury trial and a beyond-a-reasonable-doubt standard. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 434–37. The Court did not require that the protections be constitutionally mandated. It looked to the “characteristics” of the proceeding. *Id.* at 439. Here, California has chosen to create a trial-like proceeding for prior conviction allegations. The State cannot now claim that this proceeding is merely a sentencing matter immune from double jeopardy.

**III. Retrial After a Reversal for Insufficient Evidence Is Barred**

The California Court of Appeal reversed the trial court’s true finding on the prior conviction allegation because the evidence was insufficient to establish that petitioner personally used a deadly weapon. *See Monge*, 60 Cal. App. 4th at 1065. Under *Burks*, 437 U.S. at 18, the Double Jeopardle Clause bars retrial after a court has determined that the evidence was insufficient to sustain a conviction. The same principle applies to a sentencing determination that “functioned as the equivalent of a trial on the issue of punishment.” *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209. The California court’s sufficiency-of-evidence ruling is a final determination that the State failed to prove its case. To allow retrial would subject petitioner to a second jeopardy for the same “offense” of the prior conviction allegation.

**IV. The State’s Reliance on *Williams v. New York* Is Misplaced**

The California Supreme Court relied on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), for the proposition that sentencing proceedings need not be adversarial. *Williams* held only that the Due Process Clause did not require a judge to have a hearing when imposing a sentence based on a presentence report. *Id.* at 251. *Williams* did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause, and it did not involve a statutory scheme that provided a separate trial on specific sentencing allegations. Here, California has gone far beyond the minimal due process required by *Williams*. It has created a trial-like proceeding, and that proceeding must be subject to the double jeopardy protection that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* afford.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed. The Double Jeopardy Clause precludes retrial of the prior conviction allegation in this case.
