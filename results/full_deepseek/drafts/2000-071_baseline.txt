**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because the Club’s Activities Constituted Religious Instruction and Prayer, Not Viewpoint-Based Speech on a Permissible Subject.**

Milford Central School created a limited public forum by opening its facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” *See* New York Educ. Law § 414. Within such a forum, the government may restrict speech based on subject matter so long as the restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Rosenberger v. Rector of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). Here, Milford reasonably excluded the Good News Club because its proposed activities—including Bible lessons, prayer, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—constituted religious instruction and worship, a subject the school validly chose not to include.

The Club’s claim that Milford engaged in unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination fails because the school did not permit discussion of morals and character from secular viewpoints while prohibiting the same subject from a religious perspective. Rather, Milford excluded the Club’s activities because their *subject matter* was religious, not because the Club offered a religious *viewpoint* on a secular topic. As the Second Circuit correctly observed, “the Club’s activities are quintessentially religious,” involving “religious instruction and prayer,” not “pure ‘moral and character development.’” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). The Club did not seek to discuss character development from a religious viewpoint; it sought to conduct a religious service. The distinction between subject matter and viewpoint is dispositive. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (upholding exclusion of advocacy organizations from a limited public forum because the forum was limited to charitable fundraising, not political advocacy).

The Club’s lesson plans confirm this conclusion. They included “challenge and invitation” segments urging unsaved children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved,” and asking children to pray, “Dear Lord Jesus, I do believe You died for me.” *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 508. These activities are not discussions of moral values; they are invitations to religious conversion. Accordingly, Milford’s decision to exclude the Club was a permissible subject-matter restriction, not viewpoint discrimination. *See id.* at 510–11.

**II. Permitting the Good News Club’s Activities Would Have Violated the Establishment Clause.**

Even if Milford’s exclusion of the Club constituted a First Amendment free speech violation, the exclusion was justified by Milford’s compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation. The school’s reasonable concern that allowing the Club’s explicitly religious activities—including prayer, Bible lessons, and proselytizing—would appear to endorse religion is sufficient to justify the restriction. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981) (recognizing that a state’s interest in complying with the Establishment Clause may justify content-based restrictions on speech). In *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612–13 (1971), this Court held that government action must have a secular purpose, must not have the primary effect of advancing or inhibiting religion, and must not foster excessive government entanglement with religion. Milford’s policy of excluding religious worship serves the secular purpose of avoiding religious endorsement; permitting the Club’s meetings—which include prayer and invitations to accept Christ—would have the primary effect of advancing religion, particularly given the impressionable age of the children involved. *See Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (recognizing the heightened risk of coercion when public schools are involved with religious exercises).

Although the Club argues that the Establishment Clause does not require exclusion because the school opens its facilities to a wide range of groups, the presence of other groups does not eliminate the risk of endorsement. *See Sch. Dist. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 250 (1990) (plurality opinion) (noting that “secondary school students are mature enough” to understand equal access, but acknowledging that younger students may perceive endorsement). Here, the Club targeted elementary-aged children, increasing the likelihood that they would perceive school sponsorship of a religious meeting as official endorsement. *See id.* at 249. Milford’s decision to exclude the Club was therefore a reasonable and constitutionally required measure to avoid violating the Establishment Clause.

**III. The Second Circuit Correctly Affirmed Summary Judgment Because the Undisputed Facts Show That the Club’s Activities Were Religious Worship, Not Protected Speech.**

The Club stipulated to the facts describing its meetings, which included prayer, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 507–08. On these undisputed facts, Milford was entitled to summary judgment because the Club’s activities fell outside the scope of the limited public forum. *See id.* at 510. The Club cannot transform its request to conduct religious worship into protected viewpoint discrimination merely by characterizing it as “moral instruction from a religious perspective.” *Id.* at 511–12 (Jacobs, J., dissenting) (noting that the majority’s distinction between subject matter and viewpoint was “falla[cious]” because the Club’s “religious viewpoint on morality looks very like religion itself”). But the majority correctly held that the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious,” not “pure ‘moral and character development.’” *Id.* at 510. The Club’s proposed use was not speech on a permissible subject; it was worship, a subject the school validly excluded.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
