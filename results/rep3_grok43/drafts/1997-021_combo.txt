The National Labor Relations Act requires that the Board's rules on employer polling be rational and consistent with the statutory scheme. Ray Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 103 (1954). An incumbent union's majority status is rebuttable, and an employer may test employee sentiment when objective facts create genuine doubt. Id. at 104. The Board's insistence on the same evidentiary threshold for polling as for withdrawal of recognition is therefore irrational, for it renders the poll a useless formality. See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 613-14 (1969).

The Act protects both employee free choice and stable bargaining relationships. Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 797-98 (1945). When an employer possesses substantial objective evidence that support has eroded, a secret-ballot poll conducted under neutral safeguards serves the statutory purpose without coercion. NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co., 351 U.S. 105, 113 (1956). Requiring proof sufficient to justify outright withdrawal before any poll may occur inverts this balance and chills legitimate inquiry. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 706 (1944).

The Board's factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked reasonable doubt likewise fails substantial-evidence review. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Multiple unit employees stated they no longer desired representation, and the successor employer inherited a unit materially smaller than its predecessor. NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939). Such statements, coupled with the reduced complement, supplied objective grounds for doubt. NLRB v. Katz, 369 U.S. 736, 743 (1962).

The Board may not disregard competent evidence of employee disaffection simply because it was voiced during hiring interviews. NLRB v. Exchange Parts Co., 375 U.S. 405, 409 (1964). Nor may it demand direct testimony from every employee whose views were reported by coworkers when the reports are uncontradicted. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 490. The record as a whole therefore compels the conclusion that the employer possessed the requisite doubt. Columbian Enameling, 306 U.S. at 301.

Because the polling standard is irrational and the evidentiary finding unsupported, the judgment enforcing the bargaining order should be reversed.
