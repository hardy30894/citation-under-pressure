**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION PROVIDE THE EXCLUSIVE RULES FOR ELIGIBILITY TO CONGRESS, AND THE STATES MAY NOT ALTER THEM.**

The Constitution establishes the qualifications for service in the United States Congress with precision and finality. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be at least twenty-five years old, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. Article I, Section 3, Clause 3 provides that a Senator must be at least thirty years old, nine years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the State when elected. These provisions represent the complete and exclusive set of qualifications that may be imposed on candidates for Congress. This Court has long recognized that "[t]he office, if it be properly called an office, is created by that Constitution and by that alone. It also declares how it shall be filled, namely, by election." *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58, 63 (1900). Because the Constitution itself creates the office and specifies the qualifications for holding it, those qualifications are exclusive and may not be supplemented by the States.

The history of the Qualifications Clauses confirms this understanding. During the Constitutional Convention, the Framers debated and rejected proposals that would have allowed States to impose additional eligibility requirements. The decision to enumerate specific qualifications reflects a deliberate choice to ensure uniformity across the Nation. As this Court observed, Members of Congress are "part of the national team" whose duties affect all States, and "[u]niformity of qualifications is paramount, and individual states are not free to engraft variations." *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 816 (1995) (citing *Wiley*, 179 U.S. at 63). Because Arkansas's Amendment 73 adds a qualification—term limits—not found in the Constitution, it is invalid.

**II. THE STATES’ POWER TO REGULATE THE "TIMES, PLACES, AND MANNER" OF ELECTIONS DOES NOT INCLUDE THE POWER TO ADD QUALIFICATIONS FOR CANDIDATES.**

The States possess authority under the Elections Clause, Article I, Section 4, Clause 1, to prescribe the "Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections for Senators and Representatives." But this power is limited to procedural regulations; it does not extend to altering the substantive qualifications for office. This Court has drawn a clear distinction between permissible election regulations and impermissible qualifications. For example, a State may impose reasonable signature requirements for ballot access, *Brockington v. Rhodes*, 396 U.S. 41, 41-42 (1969) (per curiam), but it may not erect eligibility barriers that effectively exclude a class of candidates from the ballot.

Amendment 73 does not merely regulate the "manner" of elections; it disqualifies otherwise eligible candidates based on their prior service. The Amendment provides that any person who has served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate "shall not be certified as a candidate and shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot." This is not a procedural requirement—it is an outright prohibition on candidacy. Because it adds a qualification beyond those enumerated in the Constitution, it exceeds the States' authority under the Elections Clause. *See Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221, 227 (1920) (holding that the Constitution's method of amendment is exclusive and that "the people" of a State cannot alter it). By analogy, the Constitution's exclusive method for setting qualifications cannot be altered by state initiative.

**III. THE POWER TO JUDGE QUALIFICATIONS RESIDES IN EACH HOUSE OF CONGRESS, NOT IN THE STATES.**

The Constitution vests each House of Congress with the power to "be the Judge of the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members." U.S. Const. art. I, § 5, cl. 1. This power is exclusive. As this Court recognized in *Barry v. United States ex rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 613 (1929), when the Senate exercises this power, it "acts as a judicial tribunal." The Constitution thus reserves to Congress the final determination of whether a member-elect satisfies the Constitution's qualifications. If the States could add qualifications through ballot-access restrictions, they would effectively circumvent this structural protection and usurp the judgment that the Constitution assigns to each House. *See Newberry v. United States*, 256 U.S. 232, 257 (1921) (McReynolds, J.) (recognizing the "exclusive power" of each House over its members' qualifications). Arkansas's Amendment 73 impermissibly intrudes upon this exclusive federal power.

**IV. THE RESULTING PATCHWORK OF STATE-IMPOSED QUALIFICATIONS WOULD UNDERMINE THE UNIFORMITY REQUIRED BY THE CONSTITUTION'S FRAMEWORK.**

The Constitution's design contemplates a uniform system for the election of Representatives and Senators. If each State were free to impose additional qualifications, the Nation would face a "hodge-podge of qualifications" that would undermine the national character of Congress. As the Arkansas Supreme Court recognized, "[c]ongressional officeholders partake of the same national character as the President of the United States," and "[t]he rules which govern their qualifications are contained in the Constitution of the United States." *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill*, 316 Ark. 251, 262, 872 S.W.2d 349, 356 (1994). This Court has consistently rejected state efforts to fragment the national electoral process. *See, e.g., Fortson v. Morris*, 385 U.S. 231, 233 (1966) (upholding a state procedure only because it did not conflict with any federal constitutional provision); *Hughes v. WMCA, Inc.*, 379 U.S. 694, 695 (1965) (per curiam) (requiring prompt state compliance with federal reapportionment mandates). The uniformity required by the Qualifications Clauses would be destroyed if each State could impose its own term-limit regime.

**V. THE STATE'S ARGUMENT THAT AMENDMENT 73 IS A BALLOT-ACCESS RESTRICTION, NOT A QUALIFICATION, FAILS.**

The proponent of Amendment 73 contends that the measure does not add a qualification because it merely restricts ballot access, not eligibility *per se*. This argument elevates form over substance. The Amendment provides that a person who has served the specified number of terms "shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot." As a practical matter, this is a complete bar to election, because no candidate can win without appearing on the ballot. This Court has long recognized that procedural barriers can be tantamount to disqualification. *See United States v. Bathgate*, 246 U.S. 220, 225 (1918) (conspiracy to deprive a candidate of ballot access); *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 488 (1917) (same). The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly observed that "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp." *Hill*, 316 Ark. at 264, 872 S.W.2d at 357. Because the practical effect of Amendment 73 is to disqualify a class of otherwise eligible candidates, it is an impermissible qualification, not a permissible regulation of elections.

**CONCLUSION**

The Constitution's Qualifications Clauses are exclusive. The States may not add to them, whether directly or through ballot-access restrictions. Arkansas's Amendment 73 is therefore unconstitutional. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
