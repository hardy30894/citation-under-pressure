**ARGUMENT**

The Missouri Constitution’s mandatory retirement provision for state court judges—requiring judges to retire at age seventy—does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA) or the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. This Court has long recognized that the judiciary is a co-equal branch of government, entrusted with the independent and impartial administration of justice, and that the structure and tenure of state judges are matters of profound state concern, traditionally left to state constitutional design. The ADEA does not override this fundamental state authority unless Congress has clearly and unequivocally intended to do so. Moreover, the mandatory retirement age of seventy bears a rational relationship to legitimate state interests in preserving judicial competence, ensuring the efficient administration of justice, and maintaining public confidence in the judiciary. The judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges Because They Are “Appointees on the Policymaking Level”**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person ... appointed by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The term “policymaking level” is not limited to executive or legislative officials; it applies to any individual whose duties involve the exercise of discretion and judgment in shaping the law or the operation of government. This Court has consistently held that judicial decision-making is inherently policymaking in nature. In *United States v. Rock Royal Co-op*, 307 U.S. 533, 540 (1939), this Court affirmed that “the function of the judiciary is not merely to apply the law, but to interpret and shape it in the light of changing conditions.” The decisions of judges, particularly in the absence of clear statutory guidance, necessarily involve the creation of legal principles that govern future conduct. As this Court observed in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “the courts are not mere mechanical instruments for applying the law; they are organs of government charged with the duty of interpreting and applying it in the light of justice and public policy.”

The role of a state judge is not merely to “decide cases” in a mechanical sense, as the petitioners suggest. Rather, judges are entrusted with the power to interpret constitutions, statutes, and common law, to resolve ambiguities, and to develop legal doctrine. In *Harrison v. United States*, 317 U.S. 139, 145 (1942), this Court emphasized that “the judiciary is the final arbiter of the meaning of the Constitution and of the laws,” and that “its decisions are not mere applications of rules, but are themselves rules of law.” This function is inherently policymaking. The decisions of state judges, particularly those of appellate courts, establish binding precedent that governs the conduct of individuals, businesses, and public officials. As this Court held in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953), “the courts are not mere passive instruments of the law; they are active participants in the development of legal standards.”

The Missouri judges in this case are not mere clerks or administrative officers. They are appointed by the Governor under the Missouri Non-Partisan Court Plan and serve in a system where they exercise substantial supervisory authority over the state’s judicial administration. The Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, the Chief Judges of the Court of Appeals, and Presiding Judges in the circuit courts have general supervisory powers over court operations, rule-making, and internal judicial discipline. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4. The Missouri Supreme Court has the authority to adopt rules governing the professional conduct of the bar and to resolve disputes within the judicial hierarchy. In *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo.1985), the Supreme Court intervened to restore order in a circuit court that had fractured into factions, demonstrating the court’s role in maintaining institutional integrity. These responsibilities are not administrative; they are fundamentally policymaking in character.

The petitioners’ argument that judges are not “on the policymaking level” because they are not “elected” is unavailing. The ADEA’s exception is not limited to elected officials. The legislative history of the ADEA, while not controlling, confirms that Congress intended the “policymaking level” exception to apply broadly to individuals whose duties involve significant discretion and judgment. See *Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746*, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The phrase “policymaking level” was meant to encompass “persons with comparable responsibilities at the local level,” including those in the judiciary. The fact that judges are appointed rather than elected does not diminish the policymaking nature of their duties.

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Is Rationally Related to Legitimate State Interests**

Even if the ADEA were to apply, the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. The Fourteenth Amendment permits states to classify individuals based on age when the classification is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. In *Baldwin v. G. A. F. C. Co.*, 283 U.S. 579, 584 (1931), this Court held that “the power of the state to regulate the conditions of employment includes the power to fix the age at which a person may be required to retire.” The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring that judges remain mentally and physically capable of performing their duties. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), this Court recognized that “the state may reasonably conclude that the physical and mental faculties necessary for the performance of certain duties decline with age.”

The judiciary is not immune to the effects of aging. The ability to concentrate, to analyze complex legal issues, and to manage the demands of a heavy caseload may diminish over time.
