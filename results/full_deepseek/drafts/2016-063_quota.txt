**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously transferred this case to the Federal Circuit. Under the plain text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702 and this Court’s decision in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), judicial review of a mixed case dismissed on jurisdictional grounds lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary precedent in *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), has been effectively overruled by *Kloeckner*.

**I. The Statutory Scheme and *Kloeckner* Require District Court Review for All Mixed Cases**

A “mixed case” arises when an employee “has been affected by an action which such employee may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and “alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The statute unambiguously provides that judicial review of such cases “shall be filed” in district court. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 44. This Court held that “the forum for judicial review of a mixed case is a district court, not the Federal Circuit, regardless of how the Board disposes of the case.” *Id.* at 46 (emphasis added). The Court expressly rejected the notion that the proper forum depends on whether the Board reached the merits or dismissed on a procedural ground. *Id.* at 55.

The critical statutory phrase is “action which such employee may appeal to the Board.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). That language focuses on the *nature of the action*, not the Board’s ultimate jurisdictional ruling. An action is “appealable” if it is the type of adverse employment action listed in 5 U.S.C. § 7512—removal, suspension for more than 14 days, reduction in grade or pay, or furlough of 30 days or less. *See* 5 C.F.R. § 1201.3(a). Here, Perry’s suspension and retirement are precisely such actions. The Board’s later determination that the settlement was voluntary does not change the fact that the *actions themselves* were appealable. *Kloeckner* made clear that the employee need only “allege[] that the action is appealable,” not that the Board ultimately agrees. 568 U.S. at 52.

**II. *Powell*’s Distinction Between Procedural and Jurisdictional Dismissals Is Inconsistent with *Kloeckner***

In *Powell*, the D.C. Circuit held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case for lack of jurisdiction, review lies in the Federal Circuit. 158 F.3d at 599-600. But *Kloeckner* “eviscerated” that distinction. *See Dellums v. U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Comm’n*, 863 F.2d 968, 978 n.11 (D.C. Cir. 1988). The *Kloeckner* Court repeatedly described the issue as “whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or in a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds.” 568 U.S. at 44. But the Court’s reasoning applies with equal force to jurisdictional dismissals. The Court emphasized that the statutory definition of a mixed case turns on whether the employee “may appeal” the action, not on whether the Board actually exercises jurisdiction. *Id.* at 52-53. A jurisdictional dismissal, like a procedural dismissal, does not alter the fact that the action was appealable in the first instance.

The D.C. Circuit below attempted to distinguish *Kloeckner* by noting that the Court cited *Harms v. IRS*, 321 F.3d 1001 (10th Cir. 2003), and *Downey v. Runyon*, 160 F.3d 139 (2d Cir. 1998), both of which recognized a procedural/jurisdictional distinction. But *Kloeckner* did not adopt that distinction; it simply noted the circuit split. The Court’s holding was categorical: “the proper forum for judicial review of a mixed case is a district court.” 568 U.S. at 55. The Court did not carve out an exception for jurisdictional dismissals. Indeed, the Court observed that the Board’s characterization of its dismissal as “procedural” or “jurisdictional” is not dispositive. *Id.* at 53.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit itself has acknowledged that *Kloeckner* “may have cast doubt” on the jurisdictional/procedural distinction. *Conforto v. MSPB*, 713 F.3d 1118, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013). But it declined to revisit the issue. This Court should resolve the conflict.

**III. The Board’s Jurisdictional Determination Here Is Inextricably Intertwined with the Merits of the Discrimination Claim**

Perry’s claim of coercion—that his retirement and suspension were involuntary due to discrimination—goes to the heart of his discrimination allegations. The Board’s jurisdictional ruling necessarily required it to assess the voluntariness of the settlement, which overlaps with the merits of Perry’s discrimination and retaliation claims. *See Garcia v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (voluntariness is a jurisdictional question). When the jurisdictional issue is so intertwined with the discrimination claim, district court review is particularly appropriate. *See Ballentine v. MSPB*, 738 F.2d 1244, 1247 (Fed. Cir. 1984) (noting that jurisdictional and merits questions may overlap). The statutory scheme contemplates that district courts, with their expertise in discrimination law, should resolve such mixed questions. *See Sloan v. West*, 140 F.3d 1255, 1260 (9th Cir. 1998).

**IV. The Statutory Deadlines Confirm That Forum Must Be Determined at the Outset**

The statute imposes strict deadlines for seeking judicial review. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 7702(e)(1)(B) (30 days to file in district court); § 7703(b)(1) (60 days to petition the Federal Circuit). If the proper forum depends on the Board’s later jurisdictional ruling, employees will be forced to guess or file protective appeals in both courts. *Kloeckner* rejected such uncertainty, holding that the employee’s initial characterization controls. 568 U.S. at 52-53. Perry properly filed in district court (via the D.C. Circuit’s transfer authority), and that is where his case belongs.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed, and the case should be remanded with instructions to transfer to the appropriate district court.
