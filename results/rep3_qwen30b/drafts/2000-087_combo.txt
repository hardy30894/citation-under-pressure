**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in cases decided before 1970, forbids indefinite detention without a showing of a legitimate governmental interest and a clear statutory basis. The statute at issue, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize such detention, and the Government’s attempt to read it as permitting indefinite confinement violates the fundamental principles of due process and the separation of powers.

The Constitution does not permit the indefinite detention of any person, including a removable alien, without a judicial determination that such confinement is necessary and justified. In *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 609, 613 (1923), this Court held that “the power to detain a person must be exercised in accordance with law, and not arbitrarily or indefinitely.” The Government’s position—that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention—would allow the Executive to hold a person in custody without judicial oversight, in violation of the constitutional principle that “no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law.” *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1938). The statute, read in isolation, may appear to grant broad discretion, but when interpreted in light of constitutional constraints, it cannot be read to authorize detention beyond a reasonable time necessary to effect removal.

This Court has long recognized that the power to detain must be limited by time and purpose. In *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953), the Court emphasized that “the power to detain is not unlimited, and must be exercised in a manner consistent with the Constitution.” The Government’s argument that detention may continue indefinitely because removal is “possible” ignores the fact that “possible” does not equate to “reasonably foreseeable.” In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), this Court stated that “the power of the government to detain must be exercised with caution and restraint, and not in a manner that would render the liberty of the citizen insecure.” The Government’s continued detention of Zadvydas, after more than three years beyond the removal period, with no realistic prospect of removal, is precisely the kind of arbitrary and indefinite confinement that the Constitution forbids.

The Government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. That case involved an alien who had never entered the United States and was held at the border pending a determination of admissibility. The Court upheld the detention not because of any general power to detain indefinitely, but because the alien had not yet gained entry and the Government had a compelling interest in national security. *Id.* at 212. The Court explicitly noted that “the power to exclude aliens is not subject to the same constitutional limitations as the power to punish or to detain citizens.” *Id.* at 211. Zadvydas, by contrast, is a resident alien who has lived in the United States for decades, has been subject to a final removal order, and has not been charged with any crime of national security significance. The distinction between an alien at the border and a resident alien in the country is constitutionally significant and cannot be ignored.

Moreover, the Government’s argument that detention is justified because Zadvydas is a risk to the community or a flight risk is not supported by the record. The district court found that Zadvydas had “conducted himself as a productive member of society” and had complied with all release conditions. *Zadvydas v. Caplinger*, 986 F. Supp. 1011, 1025 (E.D. La. 1997). The Government has never alleged that Zadvydas has violated any condition of release, nor has it shown that he poses a threat to public safety. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), the Court held that “detention must be justified by a clear and present danger,” not by speculative or remote concerns. The Government’s continued detention of Zadvydas, based on no such danger, is therefore unconstitutional.

The statute itself does not authorize indefinite detention. Section 1231(a)(6) permits detention “beyond the removal period” only for the purpose of securing removal. But removal cannot be secured if no country will accept the alien. In *United States v. Carver*, 260 U.S. 609, 613 (1923), the Court held that “the power to detain must be exercised in accordance with law, and not arbitrarily or indefinitely.” The Government’s claim that it is making “good faith efforts” to remove Zadvydas is irrelevant if those efforts are futile. In *United States v. Kahriger*, 345 U.S. 22, 29 (1953), the Court stated that “the power to detain is not unlimited, and must be exercised in a manner consistent with the Constitution.” When removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the purpose of detention has been defeated, and continued confinement becomes arbitrary.

The Government’s argument that the statute permits detention until removal is possible is also flawed. In *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1938), the Court held that “the Constitution protects the individual against arbitrary action by the government.” The Government’s position would allow it to detain an alien indefinitely based on a mere possibility of removal, which is precisely the kind of arbitrary action the Constitution prohibits. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 U.S. (4 Cranch) 75, 95 (1807), the Court emphasized that “the power of the government to detain must be exercised with caution and restraint.” The Government’s detention of Zadvydas, after more than three years, with no realistic prospect of removal, is neither cautious nor restrained.
