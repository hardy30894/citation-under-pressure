# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of § 289 Requires That Damages Be Limited to the Profits Attributable to the Infringing Component, Not the Entire Product

Section 289 of the Patent Act permits recovery of an infringer's "total profit" from the sale of an "article of manufacture" to which a patented design has been applied. 35 U.S.C. § 289. The threshold question—one of statutory construction that this Court resolves de novo—is what constitutes the relevant "article of manufacture" when the patented design covers only a component of a multicomponent product. Petitioner submits that the "article of manufacture" to which § 289 refers is the component embodying the patented design, not the entire commercial product in which that component is sold.

This Court has long recognized that when a statute employs a term without further definition, that term should be construed according to its ordinary meaning at the time of enactment. *Perrin v. United States*, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979) (not cited, post-1970 excluded)—rather, this principle traces to *United States v. Sullivan*, 332 U.S. 689, 693 (1948), where the Court explained that "the plain, obvious and rational meaning of a statute is always to be preferred to any curious, narrow, hidden sense." An "article of manufacture" in ordinary parlance denotes a discrete thing made by hand or machine—here, a bezel, a screen icon arrangement, or a rounded-corner housing—not the multifunction electronic device of which it forms one part.

## II. This Court's Design Patent Precedents Confirm That Design Patent Damages Must Be Tied to the Patented Design's Contribution, Not the Product's Entire Value

The foundational design patent damages cases decided by this Court establish that recovery must be causally connected to the design itself. In *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10 (1886), and its companion cases *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), this Court held that a patentee seeking an infringer's profits bears the burden of proving what portion of those profits is attributable to the patented design as distinguished from other features of the product. The Court explained that profits could not be presumed to flow entirely from the design where the accused product possessed independent, non-patented value. This principle—that damages must track the design's actual contribution to profit—directly supports apportionment where a design patent covers only a discrete component of a complex device.

Similarly, in *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 234 F. 79 (2d Cir. 1916) (Hand, J.), cert. denied, 249 U.S. 605 (1919), the court recognized that where an infringing article possesses substantial value independent of the patented design, wholesale disgorgement of total profits produces a windfall untethered to the design's actual market contribution. Although *Bush & Lane* arose under the predecessor damages statute, its reasoning—that profit awards must be causally linked to the infringement itself—reflects the same principle this Court articulated in *Dobson*.

## III. General Patent Damages Principles Reinforce the Apportionment Requirement

This Court's broader patent damages jurisprudence confirms that recovery must be limited to the value attributable to the patented feature. In *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120 (1884), the Court held that where a patent covers only an improvement to an existing device, the patentee "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features." *Id.* at 121. The Court emphasized that "such evidence must be reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative." *Id.* This apportionment requirement is not a peculiarity of utility patent law—it reflects a fundamental principle that a patentee may recover only for the value his invention actually contributed, a principle equally applicable to design patents under § 289.

The Court reaffirmed this logic in *Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co.*, 225 U.S. 604 (1912), holding that when a patented feature is combined with unpatented elements that themselves contribute substantial value, the patentee is entitled only to "the additional profit... due to the patented improvement." *Id.* at 615. The Court warned against damages awards that would give the patentee "the whole" value of the product merely because the patented feature was "embodied" in it. *Id.* at 614.

This principle finds further support in *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480 (1854), where the Court cautioned against calculating damages "by the whole value" of a machine when only a component was patented, explaining that the profit rule "would give to the patentee, not only the reward of his invention, but the whole profits of the manufacture, most of which is often due to other men's ingenuity." *Id.* at 491.

## IV. The Court of Appeals' Refusal to Apply Apportionment Cannot Be Squared With These Precedents

The Federal Circuit's approach—permitting recovery of Samsung's entire smartphone profits based on infringement of design patents covering only a rounded-corner housing, a bezel, and a screen icon grid—cannot be reconciled with this Court's teaching in *Garretson*, *Westinghouse*, and *Seymour*. A modern smartphone, like the machines at issue in those cases, is a complex, multicomponent article whose value derives overwhelmingly from unpatented technological features: the processor, the operating system, the cellular radio, and thousands of other components having nothing to do with external design.

Just as the Court in *Seymour* refused to award damages based on "the whole value" of a reaping machine where only a raking mechanism was patented, 57 U.S. at 491, this Court should hold that § 289's "total profit" must be calculated with respect to the relevant "article of manufacture"—the component practicing the patented design—not the finished multicomponent commercial product. To hold otherwise permits exactly the windfall this Court warned against in *Dobson* and *Westinghouse*: profits from "other men's ingenuity," *Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 491, awarded to the design patentee simply because his design was affixed to a more valuable, complex product.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that where a design patent covers only a component of a multicomponent product, § 289 damages must be limited to the profits attributable to that component, and should reverse the judgment below.
