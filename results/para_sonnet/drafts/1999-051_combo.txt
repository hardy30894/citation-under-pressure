# ARGUMENT

## I. Federal habeas corpus exists to safeguard against unremedied constitutional violations, and forfeiture of that remedy must rest on the petitioner's own considered choice, not on counsel's error.

This Court has long recognized that "the writ of habeas corpus is the fundamental instrument for safeguarding individual freedom against arbitrary and lawless state action," and that procedural default should bar relief only where the prisoner himself has made a deliberate, informed choice to forgo a state remedy. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963). In Fay, this Court held that a habeas petitioner forfeits review only if he "understandingly and knowingly forwent the privilege of seeking to vindicate his federal claims in the state courts," and that such forfeiture requires that the prisoner have "deliberately bypassed the orderly procedure of the state courts." Id. at 438-39. The Court emphasized that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake," and that federal courts must not visit upon a prisoner the consequences of another's default. Id. at 424.

Carpenter did not deliberately bypass anything. The default here arose from his appellate counsel's failure to raise a meritorious sufficiency claim, and, at most, from post-conviction counsel's late filing of the application to reopen — decisions made by lawyers, not by Carpenter. Under Fay's deliberate-bypass standard, that is not the kind of forfeiture that forecloses federal review.

## II. Because the underlying default was caused by the very deprivation of effective counsel that the Constitution guarantees, requiring the ineffectiveness claim itself to clear an independent procedural hurdle creates an untenable bootstrap.

The right to counsel on a criminal appeal is not an empty formality; it is, as this Court has held, "a right so basic to a fair trial that it must be applied retroactively." Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 355 (1963). This Court there held that a State may not condition meaningful appellate review on the fortuity of retained counsel, because indigent and non-indigent defendants alike are entitled to counsel's assistance on their first appeal as of right. Id. at 357-58. The right to counsel recognized in Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), and traced to Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 68-69 (1932) — where the Court explained that "the right to be heard would be, in many cases, of little avail if it did not comprehend the right to be heard by counsel" — is a right to the effective assistance of counsel, not merely his nominal presence. See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 462-63 (1938) (the Sixth Amendment "embodies a realistic recognition of the obvious truth that the average defendant does not have the professional legal skill to protect himself").

It follows that when appellate counsel's ineffectiveness causes a procedural default, the very harm the Constitution sought to prevent has occurred. To then require that the ineffectiveness claim itself survive an independent procedural gauntlet before it can excuse the underlying default is to ask the prisoner to overcome, unaided, the same structural deficiency — the absence of effective counsel — that produced the default in the first place. This Court's decision in Anders v. California, 386 U.S. 738, 744 (1967), recognized that appellate counsel's obligation is to function as an "active advocate," and that where counsel fails that duty the resulting proceeding cannot be treated as though the defendant had a fair opportunity to be heard. The same logic forecloses treating a defaulted ineffectiveness claim as though it were an ordinary defaulted claim requiring its own showing of cause.

## III. This Court's abuse-of-the-writ jurisprudence counsels against mechanical forfeiture rules untethered from a prisoner's own conduct.

In Sanders v. United States, 373 U.S. 1, 17-18 (1963), this Court held that successive or belated habeas claims should be barred only where "the ends of justice would not be served by reaching the merits," and warned against denying relief merely because a claim was not raised at the earliest opportunity, where there was no deliberate withholding "for strategic purposes." The Court's teaching in Sanders — reinforced by Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 311-12 (1963), which broadly construed federal habeas jurisdiction to ensure "full and fair" consideration of constitutional claims — reflects a consistent policy: procedural bars in habeas are equitable in nature and must yield when rigid application would insulate constitutional violations from any review. Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 465 (1953), likewise established that state prisoners are entitled to have their federal constitutional claims heard on the merits in federal court notwithstanding state procedural rulings, so long as no deliberate forfeiture is shown.

## IV. Conclusion

Under Fay, Sanders, Townsend, and Brown, the touchstone is the prisoner's own deliberate choice, not the happenstance of a missed deadline by counsel. And under Douglas, Gideon, Johnson v. Zerbst, Powell, and Anders, the right at stake — effective appellate counsel — is precisely the right whose deprivation produced the default. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim could serve as cause for the default of his sufficiency claim without first independently satisfying a separate procedural gauntlet. This Court should affirm.
