**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s definition of “violent felony” is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This Court has consistently held that new substantive rules, which place conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law, must be given retroactive effect. *Johnson* announced precisely such a rule: it narrowed the scope of the ACCA by rendering the residual clause void, thereby prohibiting the enhanced sentences that clause previously authorized. Petitioner Gregory Welch was sentenced under that invalid clause, and he is entitled to relief.

**I. The *Teague* Framework Requires Retroactivity for Substantive Rules**

Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review unless it falls within one of two narrow exceptions. But the *Teague* analysis begins with a threshold question: whether the rule is substantive or procedural. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). “Substantive rules” include “decisions that narrow the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms,” and such rules “apply retroactively because they necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal.” *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998) (internal quotation marks omitted). *See also Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 207 (2016) (substantive rules “must be applied retroactively because they ‘place certain primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe’”) (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307).

**II. *Johnson* Announced a Substantive Rule That Narrowed the ACCA**

The ACCA’s residual clause defined a violent felony as one that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). In *Johnson*, this Court held that clause void for vagueness, because it required courts to assess “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version of the crime,” leading to “unpredictable and arbitrary application.” 576 U.S. at 597, 600. By striking down the residual clause, *Johnson* “changed the substantive reach of the Armed Career Criminal Act,” rendering it impossible to sentence a defendant under that clause. *Welch v. United States*, 578 U.S. 120, 128–29 (2016). The rule is “substantive” because it “alters the range of conduct that the statute punishes” and “prohibits a category of sentences that the Constitution could not tolerate.” *Id.* at 129–30.

Importantly, *Johnson* does not announce a procedural rule. It does not regulate the manner in which a court determines guilt or imposes sentence; it eliminates a category of punishment altogether. *Compare Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353 (rule requiring jury finding of aggravating factors is procedural because it “alter[s] only the method of determining the defendant’s culpability”). Because *Johnson* “place[s] a class of persons beyond the State’s power to punish by death,” *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 207, it must apply retroactively.

**III. *Johnson* Satisfies the Second *Teague* Exception in Any Event**

Even if *Johnson* were classified as a procedural rule, it would satisfy *Teague*’s second exception for “watershed rules of criminal procedure” that are “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Whorton v. Bockting*, 549 U.S. 406, 417 (2007) (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311). The *Johnson* Court condemned the residual clause as “hopelessly indeterminate” and “nearly impossible to apply.” 576 U.S. at 601. A rule that “seriously diminish[es] the likelihood of obtaining an accurate determination” of a sentence that is “plainly unconstitutional” qualifies as a watershed rule. *See Bockting*, 549 U.S. at 417.

**IV. Welch Was Sentenced Under the Residual Clause and Is Entitled to Relief**

Welch’s ACCA enhancement relied on a Florida robbery conviction that did not satisfy the “elements clause” requiring the “use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force.” *Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010). The Eleventh Circuit upheld the enhancement based solely on the residual clause, concluding that Florida strong-arm robbery “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury.” *See* opinion below. That clause is now void. Under settled retroactivity principles, Welch must be resentenced without the ACCA enhancement. *See Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974) (retroactivity of statutory interpretation that narrows the reach of a criminal statute).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should hold that *Johnson* applies retroactively to cases on collateral review and that Welch is entitled to resentencing.
