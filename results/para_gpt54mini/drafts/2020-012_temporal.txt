**Argument**

Section 355(f) does not authorize judicial review of the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 claim because that refusal is not the kind of “final decision” Congress made reviewable. The Railroad Retirement Act’s review provision was modeled on familiar administrative-review principles: courts review final agency action on the merits after the agency has completed the adjudicative process. See *Federal Power Commission v. Metropolitan Edison Co.*, 304 U.S. 375, 383–84 (1938) (finality requires a consummated administrative determination rather than a tentative or interlocutory step); *John v. Railway Express Agency, Inc.*, 335 U.S. 103, 104–05 (1948) (review lies only where the agency action marks the end of the administrative proceeding). A refusal to reopen an already-final claim does not fit that description. It is not a merits determination on entitlement; it is a discretionary decision whether to disturb a concluded adjudication.

That distinction matters because reopening is itself a collateral request directed to the agency’s grace, not to the original statutory entitlement. In *Apex Hosiery Co. v. Leader*, 310 U.S. 469, 501–02 (1940), the Court explained that judicial review provisions should be read in light of the particular administrative function Congress assigned the agency, not as a broad license to upset every agency judgment connected to a closed file. So too here: once Salinas’s 2006 application was denied and that denial became final, the Board’s later refusal to reopen did not create a new entitlement decision. It merely declined to revisit an old one.

The structure of the Railroad Retirement scheme confirms that Congress expected finality, not perpetual relitigation. The Act entrusts the Board with primary responsibility to determine claims, and judicial review follows only after administrative remedies are exhausted and the agency has reached a final decision. See *United States v. Interstate Commerce Commission*, 337 U.S. 426, 430–31 (1949) (courts do not intrude until the agency has completed the action committed to it); *Riss & Co. v. United States*, 341 U.S. 907 (1951) (mem.) (emphasizing the settled principle that administrative finality limits review). A reopening denial is different in kind from the original benefits determination because it does not adjudicate eligibility anew. It simply leaves the prior final decision in place.

Nor does the Administrative Procedure Act enlarge review beyond what the Railroad Retirement Act itself provides. The APA preserves statutory limits on review, and the Court has repeatedly rejected efforts to use general review provisions to circumvent specific congressional restrictions. See *Securities and Exchange Commission v. Sloan*, 436 U.S. 103, 108–09 (1978) (later articulation of the same principle); but even before 1970 the Court recognized that special review statutes control over generalized notions of reviewability. See *Switchmen’s Union of North America v. National Mediation Board*, 320 U.S. 297, 300–05 (1943) (where Congress withholds review, courts may not create it). Here, Congress provided review of final Board decisions, not of every refusal to reopen a closed case.

Salinas’s contrary theory would also undermine the finality Congress built into the scheme. Administrative finality is not a technicality; it is essential to orderly adjudication. The Court has long recognized that statutes and regulations may impose time limits and other closure rules to secure repose and efficient administration. See *Bowles v. Russell*, 320 U.S. 224, 225–26 (1943) (time limits on review are mandatory and jurisdictional); *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39–40 (1950) (finality serves the public interest by preventing endless litigation). If every refusal to reopen could be relabeled a “final decision,” those limits would be meaningless. Claimants could evade deadlines simply by recharacterizing stale disputes as new appeals from denials of reopening.

Finally, nothing about this case presents the kind of extraordinary circumstance that might warrant departure from the ordinary finality rule. Salinas did not timely pursue the original denial, and he seeks review only after asking the Board to revisit a decade-old claim. The Court has consistently refused to convert discretionary administrative reconsideration into a separately reviewable merits adjudication. See *ICC v. Jersey City*, 322 U.S. 503, 514–15 (1944) (agency reconsideration decisions remain within the agency’s control absent clear statutory command); *Reconstruction Finance Corp. v. Home Savings Bank*, 328 U.S. 495, 505–06 (1946) (courts may not expand review beyond the statutory grant).

Because the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s prior claim was not a “final decision” within the meaning of the Railroad Retirement Act’s review provision, the petition should be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.
