**Argument**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 reentry absent a clear congressional command.**

Retroactivity analysis begins with the presumption that statutes do not attach new legal consequences to completed conduct unless Congress has spoken with clarity. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 280 (1994). Courts first ask whether Congress has “prescribed the statute’s proper reach.” *Ibid.* If not, the question is whether applying the new law would have retroactive effect by impairing rights, increasing liability, or imposing new duties on past conduct. *Id.* at 269-70.

Section 241(a)(5) contains no clear statement that it reaches aliens who reentered before the provision took effect. The statute provides that if the Attorney General finds that an alien “has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed or having departed voluntarily, under an order of removal,” the prior order “is reinstated” and the alien “is not eligible and may not apply for any relief under this chapter.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). The provision speaks in the present perfect—“has reentered”—but that phrasing does not unambiguously reach pre-enactment reentries. Ambiguity must be resolved against retroactive application. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 265; *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001).

This Court’s immigration retroactivity cases confirm the point. In *St. Cyr*, the Court held that the repeal of § 212(c) relief could not be applied to aliens who had pleaded guilty before the repeal, because they likely relied on the availability of discretionary relief in deciding to forgo trial. 533 U.S. at 321-23. Likewise, in *Martin v. Hadix*, the Court rejected an application of a new fee cap to work already performed, emphasizing that statutes are not applied retroactively when they “affect substantive rights.” 527 U.S. 343, 359-60 (1999). And in *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, the Court held that even a jurisdictional statute may not be applied retroactively if it creates “new legal consequences” for prior conduct. 520 U.S. 939, 947-48 (1997).

Here, Fernandez-Vargas reentered in 1982, long before § 241(a)(5) became effective in 1997. At that time, he could seek to regularize his status through marriage to a citizen and adjustment of status, subject to the INA’s then-existing restrictions and forms of relief. Section 241(a)(5) later eliminated that avenue entirely. Applying it to him would do more than regulate future conduct; it would strip away a path to lawful status based on conduct that had already occurred and had long since accrued settled legal significance. That is classic retroactive effect under *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr*.

The government will rely on the statutory command that reinstatement applies when an alien “is found in the United States” after illegal reentry. But the statute’s operation still depends on the antecedent illegal reentry, and Congress did not clearly state that past reentries would trigger the new disability. Where Congress intends such sweeping retroactivity, it knows how to say so. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 316-17; *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280.

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Fernandez-Vargas would be impermissibly retroactive because it attaches new disabilities to past conduct and frustrates settled expectations.**

A law is impermissibly retroactive when it “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. That is exactly what happened here. Before 1997, Fernandez-Vargas’s unlawful presence did not categorically foreclose all relief; after 1997, § 241(a)(5) made him “not eligible” for any relief under the INA. 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). This Court has repeatedly held that statutes eliminating relief can be retroactive when they alter the legal consequences of prior acts. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-23; *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 394 F.3d 881, 884-86 (10th Cir. 2005), rev’d, 548 U.S. 30 (2006).

The Tenth Circuit reasoned that Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting status. But *St. Cyr* rejected the notion that retroactivity turns only on a vested right; reliance and settled expectations matter even where relief was discretionary. 533 U.S. at 323. And *Martin*, 527 U.S. at 360, and *Hughes Aircraft*, 520 U.S. at 947-48, both teach that retroactivity analysis focuses on changed legal consequences, not merely whether a right had matured into an entitlement.

This Court’s cases involving immigration remedies also recognize that changes in eligibility can have retroactive consequences when they upset the legal landscape existing at the time of the relevant conduct. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 326; *Vartelas v. Holder*, 566 U.S. 257, 266-67 (2012) (holding that a new rule burdening lawful permanent residents’ brief trips abroad could not be applied to pre-enactment convictions because it attached a new disability to past conduct). The same principle applies here. Fernandez-Vargas’s pre-1997 reentry was complete before Congress enacted § 241(a)(5), and he remained in the country for years under the prior regime. To impose the new reinstatement bar on that past entry is to rewrite the legal consequences of conduct already finished.

Nor does the fact that Fernandez-Vargas later married a U.S. citizen make the statute nonretroactive. His marriage and adjustment application were attempts to invoke relief still available under the pre-1997 framework as he had lived under it for fifteen years. Denying that opportunity based on a statute enacted after the reentry that made him removable would impose a new disability on a completed act. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70; *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321-23.

**Conclusion**

Section 241(a)(5) should not be construed to apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. And if it is so construed, the statute’s application to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive under *Landgraf*, *St. Cyr*, *Hughes Aircraft*, *Martin*, and *Vartelas*. The judgment below should be reversed.
