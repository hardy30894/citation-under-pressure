The Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy under Section 522 of the Bankruptcy Code. The plain language of Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts "retirement funds" to the extent they are in a tax-exempt account. However, the statute does not define "retirement funds." 

In interpreting this provision, the Seventh Circuit correctly considered the characteristics of an inherited IRA. Upon inheritance, the account's attributes change significantly. The beneficiary cannot make new contributions, roll over the account, or merge it with another account. 26 U.S.C. §408(d)(3)(C). Moreover, the beneficiary must begin distributing the assets within a year of the original owner's death, 26 U.S.C. §402(c)(11)(A), incorporating 26 U.S.C. §401(a)(9)(B), and complete payout in as little as five years. These characteristics demonstrate that an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund for the beneficiary.

The Seventh Circuit's interpretation is consistent with the purpose of the exemption, which is to protect retirement savings. As the court noted, treating an inherited IRA as exempt would shelter from creditors a pot of money that can be freely used for current consumption. Clark v. Rameker, 673 F.3d 706, 711 (7th Cir. 2013). This is evident when considering a hypothetical scenario where the original owner withdraws the funds, pays the penalty tax if necessary, and then gives the money to the beneficiary. In this case, the money is no longer considered "retirement funds." The same result should obtain when the money is inherited directly.

The Fifth Circuit's decision in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012), is distinguishable. The Chilton court focused on the fact that Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) do not explicitly require the retirement funds to be the debtor's. However, this omission does not imply that the funds need not be reserved for the debtor's retirement. The Seventh Circuit correctly noted that the statute's language should be interpreted in light of its purpose, which is to protect retirement savings. Clark, 673 F.3d at 712.

Furthermore, the Seventh Circuit's decision is consistent with the principle that exemptions should be interpreted narrowly. See In re Barker, 768 F.2d 191, 196 (7th Cir. 1985). The court's holding does not create an overly broad or narrow interpretation of the exemption, but rather a nuanced understanding that is consistent with the statute's purpose.

In conclusion, the Seventh Circuit correctly held that an inherited IRA is not exempt from a debtor's estate in bankruptcy. The characteristics of an inherited IRA, the purpose of the exemption, and the principles of statutory interpretation all support this conclusion. The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
