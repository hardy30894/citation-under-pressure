**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), which provides that an action under § 1104 may not be commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that a plaintiff may avoid the statute of limitations by claiming ignorance of information that was readily available and disclosed, misreads the statutory text, contradicts binding precedent, and undermines the predictability and finality that ERISA’s limitations period is designed to ensure.

### I. The Ninth Circuit Misapplied the “Actual Knowledge” Standard by Requiring Knowledge of Imprudence, Not Just the Transaction

The Ninth Circuit held that “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) requires knowledge not only of the transaction but also of its imprudence. This interpretation is contrary to the plain language of the statute and the Supreme Court’s precedent. Section 1113(2) does not require knowledge of illegality or imprudence; it requires only “actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The statute does not define “breach” or “violation,” but the Supreme Court has made clear that “actual knowledge” is not synonymous with legal knowledge. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that the statute of limitations was triggered when the fiduciaries “had actual knowledge of the transaction,” even though they did not know it was unlawful under ERISA. The court emphasized that “the statute of limitations is triggered by [a claimant’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” Id. at 992.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), where the court held that receipt of a reduced pension check—though the recipient did not know it violated ERISA—constituted “actual knowledge of the transaction” sufficient to trigger the limitations period. The court explicitly rejected the idea that a plaintiff must know the transaction was illegal to trigger the clock. Id. at 1423. These cases establish a clear rule: knowledge of the *transaction*—not its legal or prudential status—is sufficient to start the limitations period.

The Ninth Circuit’s departure from this settled precedent is unjustified. By requiring knowledge of imprudence, the court effectively creates a new, higher threshold for triggering the statute of limitations. But Congress did not require such knowledge. The statute speaks only of “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not of the *nature* of the breach. To require knowledge of imprudence would render the statute unworkable: a plaintiff could delay suit indefinitely by claiming ignorance of the prudential standard, even when all material facts were disclosed. That is not the law.

### II. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Encourages Forum Shopping and Undermines ERISA’s Purpose of Finality

ERISA’s three-year limitations period serves a vital purpose: to ensure that fiduciary claims are brought promptly, so that evidence remains fresh and parties are not subjected to indefinite legal exposure. As the Supreme Court has observed, “ERISA’s statutory scheme is designed to promote certainty and finality in the administration of employee benefit plans.” *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1831 (2015). The Ninth Circuit’s rule—where a plaintiff can avoid the statute by claiming not to have read or recall reading disclosed information—undermines this purpose. It invites plaintiffs to ignore or forget disclosures, then later claim ignorance to toll the statute. This is not a reasonable interpretation of “actual knowledge.”

The Supreme Court has long held that “actual knowledge” means “actual awareness of the facts that constitute the breach,” not mere access to information. In *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002), the Court stated that “actual knowledge” requires “a conscious awareness of the facts that constitute the breach.” The Court did not require knowledge of the law or the prudential standard. Here, Intel provided Sulyma with multiple disclosures—Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, Annual Disclosures—detailing the allocation of his retirement funds to hedge funds, private equity, and commodities. These documents were accessible on Intel’s website and contained clear descriptions of the investment strategy and risks. The fact that Sulyma did not read or recall reading them does not mean he lacked actual knowledge. As the Court has held, “a party cannot avoid liability by deliberately closing his eyes to the facts.” *United States v. Herring*, 427 F.3d 1177, 1182 (9th Cir. 2005) (en banc).

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Creates a Circuit Split and Contradicts the Law of Other Circuits

The Ninth Circuit’s rule is inconsistent with the holdings of every other circuit to have addressed the issue. In *In re Northrop Grumman Corp. ERISA Litigation*, 2015 WL 10433713, at *20 (C.D. Cal. Nov. 24, 2015), the court held that “actual knowledge” is triggered when a plaintiff has access to information that would have revealed the breach, even if the plaintiff did not read it.
