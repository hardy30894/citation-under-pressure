# ARGUMENT

**THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED THE NAVIGABILITY-FOR-TITLE TEST BY EXAMINING WHETHER THE MISSOURI, MADISON, AND CLARK FORK RIVERS WERE, IN THEIR ORDINARY CONDITION, HIGHWAYS OF COMMERCE, USING ALL PROBATIVE EVIDENCE OF THAT CAPACITY**

## I. Title to the Beds of Navigable Rivers Passes to a New State Under the Equal-Footing Doctrine, and the Controlling Inquiry Is Navigability-in-Fact.

Under the equal-footing doctrine, a new State acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time of statehood as an incident of sovereignty equal to that of the original States. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845). This Court has long held that the touchstone of that title is "navigability-in-fact," not any technical or artificial standard. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870), explained that rivers are navigable "when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." That functional test—capacity for commercial use in the river's natural and ordinary state—governs the navigability-for-title determination. *Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1877) (title to submerged lands "is a title different in character" from ordinary property, held in trust for the public). The Montana Supreme Court's approach was faithful to this settled framework: it asked whether each river, in its ordinary condition, functioned as a highway of commerce.

## II. Courts Properly Consider All Probative Historical Evidence—Including Evidence From Periods Both Before and After Statehood—Because the Inquiry Concerns the River's Natural Capacity, Which Does Not Change With the Calendar.

PPL's insistence that a court may consider only evidence contemporaneous with the precise moment of statehood finds no support in this Court's precedents. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court determined the navigability of stretches of the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers by relying on evidence of use both before and after Utah's admission to the Union, reasoning that "the extent to which [the river] has been used" over time illuminates its "natural and ordinary condition." *Id.* at 76, 82. Likewise, in *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9 (1971), the Court examined evidence of commercial and recreational boating both before and long after statehood in assessing the navigability of Utah Lake, explaining that "[t]he crucial question is the capacity of the water to support" navigation as a highway of commerce, a capacity that is not frozen at a single instant but is properly evidenced by a continuum of use. *Id.* at 11-12. The State's introduction of historical and later evidence of commercial traffic, portage practices, and settlement patterns along the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork was thus entirely proper, and the trial court did not err in crediting expert reconstructions of river conditions drawn from that record.

## III. The Presence of Discrete Obstacles, Rapids, or Falls Requiring Portage Does Not Defeat Navigability of the River as a Whole Highway of Commerce.

PPL's segment-by-segment, all-or-nothing theory—under which any waterfall or rapid severs the river into non-navigable stretches—cannot be squared with this Court's decision in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921). There, the Court held that the Des Plaines River remained navigable notwithstanding rapids and other obstructions that required portage, because commerce could and did proceed around such obstacles: "the interruptions to navigation... do not affect the navigability of the river as a whole." *Id.* at 122. The same principle was applied in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), where the Court considered the Red River's navigability in light of the river's use as an artery of trade despite variable and sometimes difficult conditions. The Great Falls of the Missouri—famously portaged by Lewis and Clark—present precisely this circumstance: a discrete obstacle around which commerce was carried, not a permanent bar to the river's status as a continuous highway of trade. The Montana Supreme Court properly declined to treat isolated portages as constitutionally fatal to navigability of the surrounding, commercially-used reaches.

## IV. Montana's Application of the Test Respects the Federalism Concerns Underlying the Equal-Footing Doctrine.

This Court has emphasized that the equal-footing doctrine serves to ensure "the consequences of admission are indeed to be treated the same as to all States," and that riverbed title questions are to be resolved according to established navigability principles rather than the particular commercial interests of a federal licensee. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981). The State's reliance on the historical record—supported by expert testimony and admissible under recognized hearsay exceptions for ancient documents and public records—was consistent with the manner in which courts have traditionally reconstructed nineteenth-century river conditions. See *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87.

## CONCLUSION

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the navigability-for-title standard articulated in *The Daniel Ball*, *United States v. Utah*, and *Utah v. United States*, properly considering the full historical record and correctly declining to treat discrete portages as severing title to otherwise commercially navigable rivers. The judgment below should be affirmed.
