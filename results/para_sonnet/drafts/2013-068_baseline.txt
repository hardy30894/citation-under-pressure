# ARGUMENT

## I. THE MASSACHUSETTS ACT IS A CONTENT-NEUTRAL TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION THAT SATISFIES INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY

The First Circuit correctly held that Massachusetts General Laws chapter 266, § 120E½ is a valid time-place-manner regulation. This Court's framework for such regulations, established in *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781 (1989), requires that the regulation be content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Id.* at 791. The Act satisfies each requirement.

### A. The Act Is Content-Neutral on Its Face

The Act does not single out any topic, viewpoint, or speaker for restriction. It applies equally to sidewalk counselors, protesters, media, and passersby alike within the thirty-five-foot zone, subject only to narrow, generally applicable exemptions for clinic employees, patients, and those merely passing through. This Court's test for content neutrality asks whether a regulation "restricts expression because of its message" — and the Act does not. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. As this Court held in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), a strikingly similar Colorado statute regulating access to healthcare facilities was content-neutral precisely because "[i]t applies to all demonstrators, regardless of viewpoint." *Id.* at 723. The same logic governs here.

Petitioners' reliance on *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. Those cases addressed content-based and speaker-based restrictions in entirely different contexts—corporate political speech and pharmaceutical marketing data—not generally applicable buffer zones around sensitive medical facilities. As the First Circuit correctly recognized, those decisions articulate "conventional First Amendment principles" that do not disturb the settled framework governing facility-access regulations. Nothing in this Court's recent jurisprudence suggests any intent to overturn *Hill*'s content-neutrality analysis.

### B. The Act Serves Significant Governmental Interests

Massachusetts has compelling interests in "protecting the health and safety of patients" and in ensuring "unobstructed access" to healthcare facilities. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. This Court has consistently recognized that "the government's legitimate content-neutral interests" in public safety and unimpeded ingress and egress justify reasonable buffer zones. *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997) (upholding fixed buffer zones around clinic entrances as serving the State's interest in public safety and preventing obstruction).

The history underlying this Act—including the well-documented pattern of clinic blockades, harassment, and violence that prompted Massachusetts's original 2000 statute, upheld in *McGuire v. Reilly*, 260 F.3d 36 (1st Cir. 2001)—confirms that the legislature's interests are substantial and well-grounded, not speculative.

### C. The Act Is Narrowly Tailored

Narrow tailoring under intermediate scrutiny does not require the least restrictive means; it requires only that the regulation not "burden substantially more speech than is necessary." *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. A fixed thirty-five-foot zone is a modest, readily identifiable buffer that leaves speakers ample room to communicate just beyond its boundary. This Court upheld a comparable—indeed broader—one-hundred-foot floating buffer zone in *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726, and an eight-foot no-approach zone within a fixed fifteen-foot buffer in *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 380. Massachusetts's fixed, clearly marked zone is no less tailored than the schemes this Court has already approved.

### D. Ample Alternative Channels of Communication Remain

The trial record—which petitioners do not meaningfully dispute—demonstrates that petitioners retain robust means of communication: oral speech (with or without amplification), literature distribution, signage, prayer, and religious expression, all conducted at the boundary of the buffer zone. Petitioner McCullen herself testified to persuading approximately eighty women against abortion during the operative period—hardly evidence of a communicative blackout. As this Court observed in *Hill*, a regulation need not guarantee the "most effective" means of communication; it need only leave open channels that are adequate. 530 U.S. at 726–27. The record here confirms exactly that.

## II. *HILL v. COLORADO* WAS CORRECTLY DECIDED AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

Petitioners' invitation to overrule *Hill* ignores this Court's exacting standard for departing from precedent. *Stare decisis* "promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles." *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991). Overruling precedent demands "special justification," *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000), not present here.

*Hill* was correctly decided because it faithfully applied *Ward*'s time-place-manner framework to the unique challenges presented by healthcare-facility access. Nothing in the fourteen years since *Hill* has undermined its reasoning. To the contrary, *Schenck*—decided before *Hill* but employing identical principles—confirms that buffer zones around clinics are a well-established, constitutionally sound tool.

Petitioners' argument that intervening cases like *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), require overruling *Hill* fails on inspection. *Snyder* addressed tort liability for offensive speech on public issues; it did not address, let alone disturb, the distinct question of physical buffer zones protecting access to medical facilities. Absent any genuine doctrinal conflict, "the burden borne by the party advocating the abandonment of an established precedent is greater" when, as here, no changed circumstances justify departure. *Vasquez v. Hillery*, 474 U.S. 254, 266 (1986).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the First Circuit.
