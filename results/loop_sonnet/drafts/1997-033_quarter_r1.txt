# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain, mandatory text of Section 1407(a) forecloses any transferee court's use of Section 1404(a) to retain a case for trial.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Panel "shall" remand each transferred action once pretrial proceedings conclude. That word is not a suggestion; Congress's use of mandatory language must be enforced according to its terms. As this Court explained in Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917), "It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain ... the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." This Court has likewise recognized that the word "shall," when Congress uses it to impose a duty rather than confer a discretion, must be read as mandatory rather than permissive. Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947).

Nothing in Section 1407(a) qualifies the remand duty for cases in which a transferee judge concludes, under the separate discretionary standard of Section 1404(a), that retention would be convenient. In United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), this Court emphasized that a statute's own words are the best evidence of the duty Congress intended to impose, and that courts may not supply exceptions Congress did not enact. Respondent's countermotion asked the District of Arizona to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a permanent one by invoking Section 1404(a)'s general convenience standard. But Congress did not leave the duration of a Panel transfer to the discretion of the transferee judge; it fixed that duration by statute, and courts have no license to nullify a mandatory command because departure from it might serve efficiency.

## II. Because Congress specifically restricted multidistrict transfer to pretrial proceedings, the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a) cannot be used to override that specific limitation.

Sections 1404(a) and 1407(a) serve different purposes and were drafted with different scope. Section 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision applicable to ordinary civil litigation, designed to permit transfer for the convenience of parties and witnesses and in the interest of justice. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964); Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960). This Court has cautioned that Section 1404(a) transfers, even when they serve efficiency, must respect the statutory limits Congress placed on that authority and may not be used to defeat the litigant's chosen forum without satisfying the statute's own criteria. Norwood v. Kirkpatrick Co., 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955). Section 1407(a), by contrast, is a specialized mechanism enacted for the narrow purpose of coordinating pretrial proceedings among related actions pending in different districts, with the Panel's authority expressly bounded by the remand command.

Where a specific statutory provision addresses a particular subject, it is not displaced by a general provision addressing the same general subject matter. This Court has explained that however inclusive the general language of a statute may be, it will not be held to override a matter specifically addressed elsewhere in the same statutory scheme. Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222, 228–29 (1957). Section 1407(a) specifically and exclusively governs the temporal scope of a Panel-ordered transfer; it cannot be controlled or nullified by resort to the general transfer authority of Section 1404(a), a statute of general applicability that says nothing about multidistrict litigation at all.

Nor can an administrative rule promulgated by the Panel expand that authority beyond what the statute allows. Just as the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure cannot be applied to abridge, enlarge, or modify any substantive right created by Congress, Sibbach v. Wilson & Co., 312 U.S. 1, 13–14 (1941), a Panel rule permitting self-transfer under Section 1404(a) cannot expand the transferee court's authority beyond the pretrial period Congress specified in Section 1407(a). An administrative rule is no substitute for statutory text, and it cannot create power that Congress withheld.

Lexecon selected the Northern District of Illinois as its forum. Nothing in Section 1404(a)'s convenience calculus permits a court sitting by virtue of the Panel's pretrial assignment to substitute its own trial-venue preference for the plaintiff's chosen forum, particularly when doing so directly contradicts the specific remand command of Section 1407(a).

## III. The Ninth Circuit erred in treating the conflict as unresolved.

The court below acknowledged "what appears to be a clear statutory mandate" yet declined to enforce it, resting instead on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) and non-binding circuit precedent. But an agency rule cannot rewrite the words Congress chose, and no court may relax a mandatory statutory command because compliance is inconvenient. Under American Trucking, 310 U.S. at 543, and Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 485, the function of a reviewing court when statutory language is plain is to enforce it—not to weigh policy tradeoffs Congress itself resolved. Because Section 1407(a) required remand of this action to the Northern District of Illinois once pretrial proceedings concluded, and because Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to override that specific command, the judgment below should be reversed.
