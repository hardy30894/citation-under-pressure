**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT GUAM’S CERCLA § 107 COST-RECOVERY CLAIM IS TIMELY BECAUSE THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT TRIGGER § 113(F)(3)(B).**

CERCLA § 107(a) authorizes “any person” who has incurred response costs to seek full recovery from “any other person” liable under the statute. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). By contrast, § 113(f)(3)(B) permits contribution only where a party has “resolved its liability . . . in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The D.C. Circuit has recognized that these provisions are “distinct remedial avenues,” *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 167 (2004), and that a party may pursue § 107 unless its liability was resolved in a qualifying settlement. *See United States v. District of Columbia*, 858 F.3d 1056, 1062 (D.C. Cir. 2017) (holding that § 113(f)(3)(B) is not triggered unless the settlement “resolves liability”).

Here, the 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for response costs. The decree explicitly states that it was entered “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree at 3, *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam 2004). Courts have repeatedly held that such disclaimers preclude a finding that liability was resolved. *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding no resolution where the decree disclaimed liability); *Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1113, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (same). The D.C. Circuit has cautioned that “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” *Refined Metals Corp. v. Doe Run Resources Corp.*, 937 F.3d 923, 931 (D.C. Cir. 2019). Thus, the absence of an admission of liability in the decree is fatal to the government’s argument that § 113(f)(3)(B) was triggered.

Moreover, the decree did not resolve Guam’s liability because it did not impose a final, binding determination of responsibility for the contamination. *See United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 61 (1998) (explaining that liability under CERCLA requires a showing of causation). The decree merely required Guam to close the landfill and install a cover system—obligations that did not allocate responsibility for the underlying contamination. *See Consent Decree* at 5–12. Courts have distinguished such remedial obligations from settlements that resolve liability. *See, e.g., United States v. Davis*, 260 F.3d 1226, 1234 (10th Cir. 2001) (holding that a decree requiring cleanup did not resolve liability where it did not address the merits of the government’s claims). Because the decree did not resolve Guam’s liability, § 113(f)(3)(B) was not triggered, and Guam’s § 107 claim remains timely.

II. **THE GOVERNMENT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD UNFAIRLY SHORTEN GUAM’S RECOVERY PERIOD AND CONTRADICTS CERCLA’S STRUCTURE.**

The government argues that any settlement with EPA, regardless of its terms, triggers § 113(f)(3)(B). This interpretation would render § 107’s six-year limitations period a nullity for parties who enter settlements, as § 113(g)(3) imposes a mere three-year limitations period for contribution claims. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). Such a result would contravene CERCLA’s structure, which Congress amended in 1986 to provide two distinct remedies with different limitations periods. *See Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167 (explaining that § 113 was added to provide contribution rights).

The government’s interpretation also leads to absurd results. As the district court noted, if a party could trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) simply by entering a settlement, parties would be forced to litigate rather than settle, undermining CERCLA’s policy of encouraging settlements. *See Guam v. United States*, 341 F. Supp. 3d 74, 89 (D.D.C. 2018). The D.C. Circuit has recognized this concern, cautioning that “[a]llowing the [Clean Water Act] and [Consent Decree] at issue here to trigger contribution rights equates to silently extinguishing the property interest of anyone who might have a potential claim against a settling party without due process of law.” *Id.* at 23. Thus, the government’s interpretation must be rejected.

III. **GUAM’S CLAIMS ARE NOT BARRED BY THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.**

The government argues that even if § 107 applies, Guam’s claim is time-barred because the contamination occurred decades ago. However, CERCLA’s limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff discovers or should have discovered the injury. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B) (providing that the limitations period runs from “the date of discovery of the release or threatened release”). Guam did not learn of the full extent of the contamination until after the EPA’s 2004 Consent Decree, and it filed this action in 2017—well within six years of discovery. *See* *Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. at 61 (holding that the limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff has sufficient information to bring a claim).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the district court’s denial of the government’s motion to dismiss. The 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability, and thus § 113(f)(3)(B) was not triggered. Guam’s § 107 claim is timely, and the government’s interpretation of CERCLA would lead to absurd and unfair results.
