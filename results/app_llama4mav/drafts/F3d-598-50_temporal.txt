The District Court's evidentiary errors and incorrect jury instructions warrant reversal and remand for a new trial. 

I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Prejudicial and Improper Testimony from the Assistant District Attorneys and Police Lieutenant.

The testimony of the two Assistant District Attorneys and the police lieutenant was improperly admitted, as it vouched for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, provided legal conclusions, and interpreted evidence that the jury was equally capable of analyzing. This Court has long held that such testimony is inadmissible. See, e.g., United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 140-41 (2d Cir. 1948) (holding that it is "improper and prejudicial" for a witness to vouch for the credibility of another witness); Marx & Co. v. Diners' Club, Inc., 405 F.2d 477, 480 (2d Cir. 1969) (stating that a witness may not provide a legal conclusion). The admission of this testimony was not harmless, as it directly addressed the most contested issues in the case: the credibility of Ramos and Rivera and the existence of probable cause.

II. The District Court Should Have Instructed the Jury on the Lawful Arrest Requirement for Obstructing Governmental Administration.

Under New York law, a person cannot be guilty of obstructing governmental administration unless the obstructed officers were engaged in a lawful arrest. See People v. Lupinacci, 191 N.Y.S.2d 354, 357 (N.Y. App. Div. 1959) (citing People v. Kotsch, 282 N.Y. 408, 411 (1940)). The District Court's failure to so instruct the jury was error. As this Court has held, a trial court's failure to provide a proper instruction on a material issue can be grounds for reversal. See, e.g., Smith v. United States, 230 F.2d 935, 939 (2d Cir. 1956).

III. The District Court Erred in Failing to Provide a Punitive Damages Instruction.

Appellants' version of events, if believed by the jury, would have allowed a finding of malice on the part of Ramos and Rivera. Under the law, a jury instruction on punitive damages is warranted when there is evidence from which a jury could infer malice. See, e.g., Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893) (holding that punitive damages are available when a defendant's actions are "wanton, or oppressive, or ... accompanied by fraud or malice"). The District Court's failure to provide such an instruction was error.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial.
