**Argument**

The question here is whether Congress, by granting Temporary Protected Status under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a, also deemed a noncitizen “admitted” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The answer is yes. Section 1254a(f)(4) directs that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for adjustment purposes. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). That statutory command places the recipient in the very immigration posture Congress required in § 1255: someone who may seek to adjust status because the government has treated the person as lawfully present and inspected for immigration purposes.

1. **TPS’s text supplies the necessary adjustment predicate.**  
Section 1255(a) permits adjustment for an alien who was “inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States.” 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). But Congress did not leave TPS recipients to the ordinary consequences of their original entry. It expressly provided that, once TPS is granted, the alien “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Congress’s choice to deem TPS recipients in lawful nonimmigrant status for adjustment purposes is powerful evidence that it meant to supply an adjustment predicate, not merely a label. See **Burgess v. United States, 553 U.S. 124, 129-30 (2008)** (courts must apply statutory text according to Congress’s chosen words); **Duncan v. Walker, 533 U.S. 167, 172-73 (2001)** (courts give effect to every word Congress uses).

The Government’s contrary reading collapses the statute’s operative language. Congress knew how to require “admission” when it wanted to do so, but in § 1254a(f)(4) it chose a different formulation: “lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). Under settled interpretive principles, that difference matters. See **Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983)**; **Corley v. United States, 556 U.S. 303, 314 (2009)**.

2. **Section 1254a must be read in harmony with § 1255.**  
This Court has long recognized that the INA must be read as a coherent whole. See **INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987)**. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) as a nullity would make Congress’s special TPS protection largely meaningless for precisely the people Congress sought to protect—those already present in the United States whose home countries have become unsafe. See **Matter of Sosa Ventura, 25 I. & N. Dec. 391, 395 (B.I.A. 2010)** (TPS is a protection-based status tied to presence in the United States). TPS exists to allow covered noncitizens to remain here lawfully despite unsafe conditions abroad. **Mejia Rodriguez v. U.S. Department of Homeland Security, 562 F.3d 1137, 1143-44 (11th Cir. 2009)**.

Congress also knew how to make TPS a narrow, temporary protection. But it did not say TPS recipients remain forever barred from adjustment unless they depart and reenter. Where Congress wanted to condition adjustment on a formal admission, it said so expressly elsewhere in the INA. See **In re H-G-G-, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 637-38 (A.A.O. 2019)** (describing the distinct statutory treatment of admission and status); **Matter of Arrabally and Yerrabelly, 25 I. & N. Dec. 771, 778-79 (B.I.A. 2012)** (recognizing the INA’s separate treatment of admission-related concepts).

3. **Agency and judicial precedent support petitioner’s reading.**  
The Ninth Circuit held that TPS recipients are treated as admitted for adjustment purposes. **Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 958-62 (9th Cir. 2017)**. The Sixth Circuit reached the same result in the context of a TPS recipient seeking adjustment. **Flores v. United States Citizenship & Immigration Services, 718 F.3d 548, 553-56 (6th Cir. 2013)**. Those decisions correctly recognized that § 1254a(f)(4) is not an empty status label; it changes the recipient’s immigration posture in a way Congress intended to matter for adjustment. See also **Matter of Sosa Ventura, 25 I. & N. Dec. at 396**.

4. **The Government’s contrary reading defeats Congress’s evident design.**  
The Government’s construction would force TPS recipients to undertake a pointless legal fiction: they would be deemed lawfully present, yet still treated as though Congress had given them no path to regularization. That is not how statutes work. **King v. Burwell, 576 U.S. 473, 486 (2015)** teaches that courts must read statutory terms in their context and with a view to Congress’s overall scheme. Here, the scheme is plain: TPS protects eligible noncitizens from removal and preserves their ability to adjust status when Congress has deemed them lawfully present as nonimmigrants.

Because Congress conferred that lawful nonimmigrant status by operation of § 1254a(f)(4), TPS recipients satisfy the adjustment framework of § 1255. The judgment below should be reversed.
