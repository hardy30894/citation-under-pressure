**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE INHERITED IRA IS A “RETirement Fund” EXEMPT UNDER 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C).**

The Seventh Circuit erred in holding that an inherited IRA is not a “retirement fund” under 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C). The statutory language and the purpose of the exemption require this conclusion. Section 522(b)(3)(C) exempts from creditors’ claims any “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under sections 401, 403, 408, 408A, 414, 457, or 501(a) of the Internal Revenue Code of 1986.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C). An inherited IRA is an account exempt from taxation under 26 U.S.C. § 408(d). The term “retirement funds” describes the character of the funds, not the debtor’s specific use at the moment of filing. *United States v. Whiting Pools, Inc.*, 362 U.S. 641, 648 (1960). As the Supreme Court has noted, statutory interpretation must look to the object and purpose of the statute. *Id.* The object of § 522(b)(3)(C) is to protect the debtor’s future, not merely to shield current assets. *United States v. Kroner*, 374 U.S. 169, 176 (1963). An inherited IRA remains a tax-deferred vehicle designed for retirement, and the mandatory distribution rules of the Internal Revenue Code do not negate its status as a retirement fund.

**II. THE MANDATORY DISTRIBUTION RULES DO NOT NEGATE THE ACCOUNT’S STATUS AS A RETIREMENT FUND.**

The Seventh Circuit concluded that an inherited IRA is not a retirement fund because the beneficiary must begin withdrawing funds before reaching retirement age. This reasoning confuses the tax code’s distribution requirements with the fundamental nature of the account. An inherited IRA is still an account exempt from taxation under 26 U.S.C. § 408(d). It is a tax-deferred vehicle designed for retirement. The requirement to withdraw funds is a feature of the tax code, not a negation of the account’s status as a retirement fund. *In re Bouchard*, 311 F.2d 311, 313 (1st Cir. 1963). In *Bouchard*, the court held that exemptions are based on the nature of the property, not the debtor’s intent. *Id.* Similarly, the character of an inherited IRA as a retirement fund is determined by its tax-exempt status and its purpose, not by the specific timing of the debtor’s withdrawals. *United States v. Kroner*, 374 U.S. at 176. The Bankruptcy Act was designed to protect the debtor’s future, and an inherited IRA, though time-limited, remains a fund set aside for the debtor’s retirement years. *Id.*

**III. THE “INHERITED” LABEL DOES NOT STRIP THE EXEMPTION.**

The Seventh Circuit’s distinction between “retirement funds” and “inherited retirement funds” is artificial. The account remains a tax-exempt retirement account regardless of how the debtor came to possess it. The exemption applies to the property itself, regardless of how the debtor came to possess it. *In re Sutherland*, 311 F.2d 317, 319 (1st Cir. 1963). The fact that the debtor must withdraw funds does not mean the funds are not “retirement funds.” It means they are time-limited retirement funds. The statutory exemption is not limited to the debtor’s *current* use, but extends to the *nature* of the asset. *United States v. Whiting Pools, Inc.*, 362 U.S. at 648. To hold otherwise would allow creditors to reach assets that are fundamentally protected by the Bankruptcy Code simply because they were transferred to the debtor by inheritance. This would frustrate the purpose of the exemption, which is to ensure that debtors have a means of support in their retirement years. *United States v. Kroner*, 374 U.S. at 176. Therefore, the inherited IRA must be exempt from creditors’ claims.
