**ARGUMENT**

I. **The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts statute as a valid, content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation.**

The First Amendment permits reasonable, content-neutral regulations of speech in traditional public fora so long as they are narrowly tailored to serve significant governmental interests and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791-802 (1989); *Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 481-88 (1988). Massachusetts’s 35-foot buffer zone readily satisfies that standard. The State enacted the law to protect clinic access, patient safety, and public order at healthcare facilities—interests this Court has repeatedly recognized as substantial and, indeed, compelling. *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768-70 (1994); *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 376-77 (1997).

The First Circuit properly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which upheld a law limiting certain speech-related conduct near healthcare facilities. *Hill* rejected the argument that a buffer-zone law is content based merely because it turns on the speaker’s purpose or applies in a clinic-access context. Id. at 719-25. Massachusetts’s statute is even more modest than the law sustained in *Hill*: it creates a fixed 35-foot zone, exempts entry, exit, employment, emergency, and passage activities, and leaves petitioners free to converse, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and engage pedestrians outside the zone. That is the sort of “reasonable” time, place, and manner regulation this Court has long permitted. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

Petitioners argue the law is not narrowly tailored because Massachusetts could instead rely on ordinary laws against trespass, obstruction, and harassment. But narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. It requires that the regulation not burden substantially more speech than necessary. Id. The Court upheld similar access-protective measures in *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 769-70, and *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 380-81, because “fixed” restrictions around clinic entrances were justified by demonstrated obstruction and intimidation. The record here, as the First Circuit found, likewise shows that Massachusetts adopted the statute to address persistent interference with access and effective clinic operations.

Petitioners’ challenge also fails under *United States v. Grace*, 461 U.S. 171, 177-80 (1983), which makes clear that public sidewalks are traditional public fora but permits content-neutral limits protecting other important interests. The First Circuit’s decision faithfully applied that framework. The statute does not foreclose petitioners’ message; it regulates where they may stand while speaking. That is constitutionally permissible. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726-30; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802.

II. **The Court should not overrule Hill v. Colorado.**

Stare decisis is at its strongest in statutory and First Amendment cases where legislatures and lower courts have long relied on controlling precedent. *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 212 (1984); *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991). *Hill* has been on the books for more than a decade and has guided courts evaluating clinic-access regulations nationwide. Petitioners have not shown the “special justification” required to overturn precedent. *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443 (2000); *Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pa. v. Casey*, 505 U.S. 833, 854-69 (1992).

Nor is *Hill* inconsistent with the Court’s later speech decisions. *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), all involved markedly different regulatory settings: political expenditures, data use, and hateful picketing on private land. None addressed the State’s power to protect access to healthcare facilities through neutral spatial regulation. This Court has repeatedly distinguished *Hill* rather than undermined it. See *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 U.S. 464, 484-85 (2014) (describing *Hill* as controlling precedent while invalidating Massachusetts’s statute on other grounds).

If anything, the proper course is to reaffirm that *Hill* remains good law where a clinic-access law is content neutral and appropriately tailored. The doctrine of stare decisis counsels strongly against overruling precedent merely because petitioners disagree with its reasoning. *Janus v. American Federation of State, County, and Municipal Employees, Council 31*, 585 U.S. 878, 917-18 (2018); *Horne v. Flores*, 557 U.S. 433, 448-49 (2009). Petitioners identify no workability problem, no doctrinal erosion sufficient to compel reversal, and no reliance interests favoring overruling. *Ramos v. Louisiana*, 590 U.S. 83, 107-08 (2020).

III. **At minimum, Hill should be limited to uphold clinic-access buffers like Massachusetts’s law.**

Even if the Court were to revisit *Hill*, it should limit—not discard—its rule. A content-neutral buffer zone designed to secure unobstructed access to medical care fits comfortably within the line of cases permitting carefully drawn, place-based restrictions in public fora. *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 484-85; *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 768-70; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 380-81; *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 799. The First Circuit’s judgment should therefore be affirmed.
