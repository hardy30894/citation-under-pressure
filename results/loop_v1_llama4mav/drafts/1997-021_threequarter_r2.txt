The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). Furthermore, the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRA does not explicitly govern employee polling, but the NLRB has developed a standard for permitting employers to conduct polls. Several courts of appeals have rejected the NLRB's standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice. For example, in Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863 (6th Cir. 1962), the Sixth Circuit held that an employer may poll its employees to determine their union sentiment if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support. Similarly, in Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295 (9th Cir. 1964), the Ninth Circuit held that polling is a "useful and legitimate tool for the employer who is in sincere doubt of the union's majority status."

In NLRB v. John S. Swift Co., 302 F.2d 342 (7th Cir. 1962), the Seventh Circuit held that an employer's good-faith doubt about a union's majority status is sufficient to justify a poll. The court noted that the employer had presented evidence of employee dissatisfaction with the union, which supported its good-faith doubt.

The NLRB's standard is overly restrictive and fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment, as noted in NLRB v. Little Rock Downtowner, Inc., 341 F.2d 1020 (8th Cir. 1965). In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union, similar to the circumstances in NLRB v. Gulfmont Hotel Co., 362 F.2d 588 (5th Cir. 1966).

The Supreme Court has held that the NLRB's decisions must be based on substantial evidence and that the court must review the NLRB's findings to ensure that they are not arbitrary or capricious, as stated in Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474 (1951). In this case, the NLRB's decision is not supported by substantial evidence. The court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order, as the Supreme Court has held that an agency's decision without substantial evidence is not in accordance with law (Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197 (1938)).

Furthermore, the NLRB's standard is inconsistent with the NLRA's goal of promoting stable collective-bargaining relationships, as established in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1 (1937). The court should therefore reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack's poll was lawful.
