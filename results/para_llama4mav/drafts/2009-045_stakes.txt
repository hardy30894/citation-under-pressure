The district court's award of a fee enhancement based on the quality of counsel's representation and the results obtained was proper under the federal fee-shifting statute. The Supreme Court has consistently held that a district court has discretion to enhance a fee award in "rare" and "exceptional" cases. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986). 

In Blum, the Court indicated that an upward adjustment may be justified when the fee applicant offers "specific evidence" to demonstrate that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was "exceptional." 465 U.S. at 899. The Court reaffirmed this principle in Delaware Valley I, stating that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." 478 U.S. at 565.

The district court in this case made detailed findings regarding the exceptional quality of counsel's representation and the outstanding results achieved. The court noted that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." Kenny A. v. Perdue, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1288 (N.D.Ga. 2006). The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the district court's decision, holding that it did not abuse its discretion in enhancing the lodestar figure. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

The petitioners argue that the district court's enhancement was improper because it was based on the judge's personal observations and experience. However, the district court's findings were not merely subjective or based on unreviewable personal experience. Rather, they were grounded in the court's extensive familiarity with the case and its observation of counsel's performance throughout the litigation. The court's 58 years of experience as a practicing attorney and federal judge provided a unique perspective on the exceptional quality of counsel's representation.

Furthermore, there is no circuit split on this issue. The majority of circuits have held that a district court may enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results obtained. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999). 

In conclusion, the district court's award of a fee enhancement was proper under the federal fee-shifting statute. The court's detailed findings regarding the exceptional quality of counsel's representation and the outstanding results achieved support the enhancement. The Supreme Court's decisions in Blum and Delaware Valley I permit fee enhancements in "rare" and "exceptional" cases, and the district court did not abuse its discretion in awarding one here.
