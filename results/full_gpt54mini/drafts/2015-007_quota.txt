Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, as applied to Timothy Hurst, violates the Sixth Amendment because it permits a judge, not a jury, to make the critical factual findings necessary to impose death. It also raises serious Eighth Amendment concerns because it authorizes the ultimate punishment without the heightened reliability the Court has long required in capital cases. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.

I. Florida’s scheme conflicts with Ring and Apprendi because a judge, not a jury, found the aggravating facts necessary for death.

The Sixth Amendment requires that any fact increasing the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000). In Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002), the Court applied that rule to capital sentencing and held that “[c]apital defendants, no less than noncapital defendants, are entitled to a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in their maximum punishment.” Arizona’s scheme, like Florida’s, allowed a judge to find the aggravating circumstance that made the defendant death-eligible. Id. at 603-09.

Florida’s system does the same. Under the scheme applied here, the jury gave only an advisory recommendation by a 7-5 vote, while the judge independently found the aggravating circumstances and imposed death. That structure cannot survive Ring. The Florida Supreme Court had previously attempted to distinguish Florida’s statute on the theory that the jury’s role was substantial enough. See, e.g., Bottoson v. Moore, 833 So.2d 693 (Fla. 2002); King v. Moore, 825 So.2d 243 (Fla. 2002). But Ring did not turn on labels; it turned on who makes the finding that exposes the defendant to death. Ring, 536 U.S. at 602-09.

Nor can Florida rely on Hildwin v. Florida, 490 U.S. 638 (1989), or Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), because Ring expressly overruled the reasoning of those cases to the extent they permitted judicial factfinding of aggravation. Ring, 536 U.S. at 603-04. A sentencing judge may not independently find the facts that “operate as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense.” Apprendi, 530 U.S. at 494 n.19. That is what happened here.

II. Florida’s nonunanimous jury recommendation does not satisfy the Sixth Amendment.

The Sixth Amendment’s jury-trial guarantee is not honored by a divided advisory vote. The jury’s role must be real, not illusory. In Ramos v. Louisiana, 590 U.S. 83, 100-01 (2020), the Court reaffirmed that the jury-trial right requires unanimity in serious criminal cases. Even before Ramos, the Court had recognized that capital sentencing requires especially careful adherence to jury factfinding. See Caldwell v. Mississippi, 472 U.S. 320, 329-30 (1985); Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 278-82 (1993).

Here, the jury did not unanimously recommend death. A 7-5 vote cannot substitute for a unanimous verdict on the facts necessary to impose the death penalty. Florida’s scheme therefore departs from the Sixth Amendment’s core guarantee that a jury, not a judge, must make the decisive finding. Ring, 536 U.S. at 609; Apprendi, 530 U.S. at 490.

III. Florida’s refusal to treat mental retardation as a jury-determined bar to execution also conflicts with constitutional principles.

Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 321 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of persons with intellectual disability and left to the states the task of enforcing that prohibition. But when a state creates procedures that permit the sentencer to deny that protection without jury factfinding, those procedures must still comport with the Constitution. See Schriro v. Smith, 546 U.S. 6, 7 (2005) (per curiam). Florida’s refusal to allow the jury to determine intellectual disability as an absolute bar to execution, while leaving the issue to the court, is difficult to reconcile with Atkins’s command of categorical exemption from death for a constitutionally protected class.

IV. The Eighth Amendment independently condemns a system that authorizes death without the reliability demanded in capital cases.

The Court has repeatedly said that “death is different.” Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976). Because of that difference, the Eighth Amendment demands “heightened reliability” in death sentencing. Beck v. Alabama, 447 U.S. 625, 637-38 (1980); Lockett v. Ohio, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978); Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72-73 (1987). Florida’s scheme falls short because it authorizes a death sentence based on a nonunanimous advisory vote and judicial factfinding of aggravation. That structure creates an unacceptable risk of arbitrary and unreliable death sentences. Furman v. Georgia, 408 U.S. 238, 239-40 (1972) (per curiam); Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349, 358 (1977).

The Court should therefore hold that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, as applied here, violates the Sixth Amendment, and that the absence of unanimous jury factfinding and jury-centered protection against execution of the intellectually disabled also renders the scheme inconsistent with the Eighth Amendment’s demand for reliability in capital punishment.
