**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Employees Engaged in Protected Activity Under 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C).**

The Federal Railroad Safety Act prohibits retaliation against an employee who “notifies . . . the railroad carrier . . . of a work-related personal injury or a work-related hazardous condition.” 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). The employees’ written and recorded statements—describing Moore’s fall and the bridge’s missing side rails and lighting—plainly constitute protected notification. *See Araujo v. N.J. Transit Rail Operations, Inc.*, 708 F.3d 152, 158 (3d Cir. 2013) (holding that reporting a hazardous condition is protected activity under FRSA). The district court erred if it treated the employees’ statements as mere “routine” accident reports; the record shows they specifically identified an ongoing safety hazard—the unlit, unrailed bridge—that had been previously reported to BNSF. That is precisely the kind of “hazardous condition” Congress intended to protect.

**II. The Employees Suffered Adverse Actions.**

Discipline—including Foster’s termination, Kline’s thirty-day record suspension, Snyder’s formal reprimand, and the one-year review periods—constitutes adverse action. *See Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 790 (8th Cir. 2014) (applying Title VII’s adverse-action standard to FRSA claims). The district court’s focus on the Public Law Board’s later reinstatement and record-clearing misses the point: the employees still suffered the stigma, risk, and financial consequences of the discipline, and the Board’s decision does not erase the retaliatory motive. *Cf. Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000) (a plaintiff may prove retaliation even if the adverse action is later rescinded).

**III. A Genuine Dispute of Material Fact Exists on Causation.**

FRSA retaliation claims are evaluated under the McDonnell Douglas burden-shifting framework. *Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 790. The employees established a prima facie case by showing protected activity, adverse action, and a causal connection. The timing here is telling: the employees gave their statements on April 2 and April 5, 2012; BNSF announced its investigation on April 6; and the employees were subjected to increased operations testing while the hearing was pending. *See Kachmar v. SunGard Data Sys., Inc.*, 109 F.3d 173, 178 (3d Cir. 1997) (temporal proximity can support an inference of causation). Moreover, the employees’ testimony that they were told to change crews west of the bridge—directly contradicting the company’s witnesses—creates a credibility dispute that must be resolved by a jury, not the court. *See Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150.

**IV. BNSF’s Proffered Reasons Are Pretextual.**

Once the employees made a prima facie showing, the burden shifted to BNSF to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for the discipline. *Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981). BNSF claimed the employees violated operating rules—failing to blow the whistle, failing to use flashlights, improperly turning off the headlight, and fouling the track. But the employees presented evidence that these “violations” were disputed, that the crew-change location itself was the subject of conflicting testimony, and that BNSF’s own witnesses were sequestered and contradicted. A reasonable jury could find that BNSF’s stated reasons were not the real reasons for the discipline. *See St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993) (a plaintiff may survive summary judgment by showing the employer’s reasons are unworthy of credence).

The Public Law Board’s decision to reinstate Foster, remove Snyder’s reprimand, and remove Kline’s suspension further undermines BNSF’s justification. *See BNSF Ry. Co. v. U.S. Dep’t of Labor*, 816 F.3d 628, 638 (10th Cir. 2016) (arbitration board’s rejection of the railroad’s discipline supports a finding of pretext). The Board’s ruling demonstrates that the discipline was not based on legitimate rule violations but on retaliation for the employees’ protected reports.

**V. The District Court Erred by Crediting BNSF’s Version of the Facts.**

Summary judgment is improper when the record contains conflicting evidence on material facts. *Reeves*, 530 U.S. at 150. Here, the parties dispute where the crew change was supposed to occur, whether the bridge was unsafe, and whether the employees committed the alleged rule infractions. The district court resolved these disputes in BNSF’s favor, but that is the jury’s role. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 804 (1973) (the trier of fact must decide whether the employer’s explanation is pretextual). The employees’ testimony, the prior reports of the bridge’s danger, and the timing of the investigation and discipline create genuine issues of material fact that preclude summary judgment.

**CONCLUSION**

The employees engaged in protected activity, suffered adverse actions, and presented sufficient evidence of causation and pretext to survive summary judgment. The district court’s judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
