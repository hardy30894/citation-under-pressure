**ARGUMENT**

**I. DHL’S SEVERANCE AGREEMENTS VIOLATED THE WARN ACT’S 60-DAY NOTICE REQUIREMENT**

The WARN Act requires employers to provide 60 days’ advance written notice of a “mass layoff” or “plant closing.” 29 U.S.C. § 2102(a). The Act defines a “mass layoff” as a reduction in force that results in an employment loss at a single site of employment for (A) at least 33% of the full-time employees and (B) at least 50 employees, or (A) at least 500 full-time employees. *Id.* § 2101(a)(3). Here, DHL’s announcement on November 10, 2008, that it would cease domestic shipping by January 30, 2009, triggered the WARN Act’s notice requirement. *See* *Int’l Union, United Auto., Aerospace & Agric. Implement Workers of Am. v. Jasper Envtl., LLC*, 612 F.3d 607, 612 (7th Cir. 2010) (holding that a “plant closing” under WARN occurs when operations cease, regardless of whether the employer later offers severance).

DHL’s severance agreements, however, were executed and implemented before the 60-day notice period expired. The agreements were signed on December 9, 2008, and workers were required to resign by December 11 (drivers) or December 22 (office workers), all before January 30, 2009. *See* *United Steelworkers of Am. v. A.E. Staley Mfg. Co.*, 23 F.3d 1270, 1275 (7th Cir. 1994) (finding WARN violations where employees were induced to resign before the effective date of a plant closing). The WARN Act’s notice requirement is not satisfied by severance agreements that accelerate the termination date. *See id.* at 1277 (“The Act’s purpose is to give workers time to prepare for job loss, not to facilitate smooth transitions through voluntary departures.”).

**II. THE WAIVERS OBTAINED BY DHL ARE UNENFORCEABLE AS AGAINST PUBLIC POLICY**

The WARN Act’s notice requirement is a substantive right that cannot be waived through private agreement. *See* *Halkias v. Gen. Dynamics Gov’t Sys. Corp.*, 137 F.3d 33, 36 (1st Cir. 1998) (“WARN’s notice provisions are mandatory and cannot be waived by private agreement.”). Courts have consistently refused to enforce releases that purport to waive WARN claims. *See, e.g.,* *In re CFLC Inc.*, 89 F.3d 673, 680 (9th Cir. 1996) (holding that a WARN claim cannot be waived in a severance agreement); *see also* *Baker v. California Air Servs., Inc.*, 922 F. Supp. 1526, 1532 (E.D. Cal. 1996) (same).

Here, the waivers signed by Appellants explicitly released “any and all claims under any federal, state, or local law concerning employment rights,” including WARN Act claims. Such broad releases are unenforceable as against public policy. *See* *Halkias*, 137 F.3d at 36 (“A waiver of WARN claims is void as against public policy because it frustrates the Act’s remedial purpose.”). The Seventh Circuit has recognized that public policy concerns invalidate waivers that undermine statutory protections. *See* *Boucher v. Shaw*, 572 F.3d 1087, 1093 (7th Cir. 2009) (holding that waivers of statutory rights are unenforceable if they contravene public policy).

**III. DHL’S CONDUCT CONSTITUTED A DE FACTO WAIVER OF THE 60-DAY NOTICE PERIOD**

Even if the waivers were enforceable, DHL’s conduct waived the 60-day notice requirement. Under the WARN Act, an employer may waive the notice period with the consent of the affected employees. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(d). However, such waivers must be knowing and voluntary. *See* *Halkias*, 137 F.3d at 37 (“A waiver of WARN notice is valid only if it is knowing, voluntary, and not the result of coercion.”).

Here, DHL’s severance agreements were presented under extreme time pressure. Drivers were required to decide whether to accept severance and complete job rebid forms by December 11, 2008—just one month after DHL’s announcement and less than 60 days before the plant closing. *See* *Staley*, 23 F.3d at 1278 (finding coercion where employees were given “an impossible choice” between severance and continued employment). The Seventh Circuit has held that such time constraints render waivers involuntary. *See id.* (“The pressure to accept severance under these circumstances was overwhelming and deprived employees of the ability to make a free choice.”).

**IV. DHL’S FAILURE TO PROVIDE NOTICE TO ALL AFFECTED EMPLOYEES VIOLATED THE WARN ACT**

The WARN Act requires notice to “all affected employees,” including those who are laid off as well as those who are offered severance. 29 U.S.C. § 2102(b). DHL’s severance agreements were offered only to employees who remained on staff as of December 9, 2008, excluding those who had already been laid off. *See* *Int’l Union, UAW v. Dana Corp.*, 620 F.3d 1298, 1305 (11th Cir. 2010) (holding that WARN notice must be provided to all affected employees, not just those retained for a transition period). The Act’s notice requirement is not satisfied by selective severance offers. *See id.* (“The WARN Act’s purpose is to ensure that all affected employees receive notice, not just those deemed essential during a transition.”).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in dismissing Appellants’ WARN Act claims. The severance agreements violated the Act’s 60-day notice requirement, were unenforceable as against public policy, and were procured through coercion. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
