**ARGUMENT**

The question presented requires this Court to determine whether punitive damages under Title VII demand proof of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct independent of the employer’s state of mind. Respondent submits that the statute’s text—requiring “malice or reckless indifference” to federally protected rights—incorporates the settled common-law principle that punitive damages are reserved for conduct that is not merely intentional, but wanton, oppressive, or accompanied by a spirit of mischief. The court of appeals erred in suggesting that intentional discrimination alone suffices.

**I. Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct exceeding mere intentional discrimination.**

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 permits punitive damages where a plaintiff demonstrates discrimination “with malice or with reckless indifference.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That standard echoes the common-law rule: exemplary damages lie only when the defendant has acted “wantonly, or oppressively, or with such malice as implies a spirit of mischief or criminal indifference to civil obligations.” *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893). This Court has consistently required more than proof of intentional injury. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 13 How. 363, 371 (1851), the Court instructed that punitive damages may be awarded when the defendant has acted “with bad intent, and in pursuance of an unlawful purpose.” *Denver & Rio Grande Railway v. Harris*, 122 U.S. 597, 610 (1887), reaffirmed that instruction, holding that the jury could consider punitive damages if it found that the defendant acted “with bad intent, and in pursuance of an unlawful purpose.” *Accord The Amiable Nancy*, 3 Wheat. 546, 558-559 (1818).

The same principle governs in libel cases involving public figures, where “actual malice” demands “knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not.” *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254, 280 (1964). In *St. Amant v. Thompson*, 390 U.S. 727, 731 (1968), the Court clarified that “reckless disregard” requires evidence that the defendant in fact entertained serious doubts as to the truth of his publication. Mere negligence or failure to investigate is insufficient. *See also Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130, 155 (1967) (requiring highly unreasonable conduct constituting an extreme departure from the standards of investigation and reporting ordinarily adhered to by responsible publishers).

**II. The common law’s “egregiousness” standard is incorporated by § 1981a.**

Nothing in § 1981a’s language suggests Congress intended to depart from these settled common-law limits. When Congress uses common-law terms, it “is understood to adopt the settled judicial interpretation of those terms.” *Bell v. Preferred Life Assurance Society*, 320 U.S. 238, 241 (1943) (applying Alabama’s rule that punitive damages “bear proportion to the actual damages sustained” and rest in the jury’s “discretion,” but not “unbridled discretion”). The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act confirms that punitive damages demand “an even higher standard” than intentional discrimination. 137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991) (Interp. Memo of Rep. Edwards) (describing standard as “the same as that applied under 42 U.S.C. § 1981”). That standard, developed in the lower federal courts, universally requires “something more than a bare showing of intentional discrimination,” such as “outrageousness or egregiousness of the defendant’s conduct.” *See, e.g., Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy*, 816 F.2d 1104, 1107-1108 (6th Cir. 1987) (pre-1970 § 1981 case requiring “willful, wanton, or malicious” conduct).

**III. Petitioner’s reliance on a bare “evil motive” standard is misplaced.**

Petitioner argues that any intentional discriminator necessarily acts with “malice.” That conflates the *mens rea* required for liability (intent to discriminate) with the additional *conduct* required for punitive damages. But this Court has long distinguished between simple intent and the “spirit of mischief” necessary to support punitive damages. *Prentice*, 147 U.S. at 107. In *Barry v. Edmunds*, 116 U.S. 550, 564 (1886), the Court recognized that a “legal discretion” must be “controlled by fixed rules of law,” not by a mere “personal conviction” that the defendant acted badly. So too, here: the jury cannot award punitive damages based solely on a finding that the employer intentionally discriminated; it must find that the employer’s conduct was so indifferent to rights as to be “reckless” in the lay sense—i.e., highly unreasonable and an extreme departure from ordinary standards. *St. Amant*, 390 U.S. at 731; *Butts*, 388 U.S. at 155.

**IV. This construction respects § 1981a’s cap and purpose.**

Congress capped punitive damages at $300,000, signaling that not every meritorious Title VII case warrants such awards. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3). The caps would be meaningless if punitive damages were automatic upon a liability finding. *Cf. Scott v. Donald*, 165 U.S. 58, 98-99 (1897) (upholding state’s limitation on profits to encourage domestic industry, but not addressing punitive damages directly). The purpose of punitive damages is punishment and deterrence, not routine compensation. *See Henry v. Collins*, 380 U.S. 356, 358 (1965) (per curiam) (reversing libel verdict where jury was instructed that malice could be inferred from falsity alone, because that standard failed to require “actual malice” under *New York Times*). The “egregiousness” requirement ensures that punitive awards are reserved for extraordinarily egregious cases, as Senator Dole explained. 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991).

**Conclusion**

The judgment below should be reversed. This Court should hold that punitive damages under Title VII require proof of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct independent of the employer’s state of mind—i.e., conduct that is wanton, oppressive, or so reckless as to imply a conscious disregard for federally protected rights.
