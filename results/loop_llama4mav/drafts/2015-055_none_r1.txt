The issue before this Court is whether the rule announced in Johnson v. United States regarding what constitutes a violent offense applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. To resolve this question, we must examine the principles governing the retroactive application of Supreme Court decisions and the constitutional underpinnings of the vagueness doctrine.

The Supreme Court has long held that its decisions construing federal statutes apply retroactively. See United States v. Schooner Peggy, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801) (establishing that a court must apply the law as it exists at the time of its decision). This principle is rooted in the idea that the judiciary's role is to declare what the law is, rather than to make new law. See Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137, 177 (1803) ("It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.").

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) imposes a minimum sentence of 15 years for defendants with three prior convictions for violent felonies or serious drug offenses. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e). The ACCA defines a "violent felony" as a crime that has as an element the "use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another," or that is "burglary, arson, or extortion, involves use of explosives, or otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The Supreme Court has held that a statute is void for vagueness if it fails to provide adequate notice of the conduct it prohibits. See Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (stating that a penal statute must be "sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties"); Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926) ("That the terms of a penal statute creating a new offense must be sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties is a well-recognized requirement.").

In Johnson v. United States, the Court held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide clear guidance on what constitutes a violent felony. The Court's decision in Johnson was based on the principle that a statute must provide fair notice of the conduct it prohibits, as well as the concern that vague statutes allow for arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement. See Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445, 465 (1927) ("A statute which either forbids or requires the doing of an act in terms so vague that men of common intelligence must necessarily guess as to its meaning and differ as to its application, violates the first essential of due process of law.").

The decision in Johnson applies retroactively to Welch's case because it is a substantive rule that alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the ACCA punishes. The rule announced in Johnson is substantive because it changes the definition of a violent felony under the ACCA. As the Court has held, new substantive rules generally apply retroactively. See United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81, 89-90 (1921) (striking down a statute as unconstitutionally vague and applying the decision retroactively).

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that a new rule applies retroactively if it is a substantive rule that narrows the scope of a criminal statute. See Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 112-13 (1945) (holding that a new interpretation of a statute that narrows its scope applies retroactively). The rule announced in Johnson is a substantive rule that narrows the scope of the ACCA by invalidating the residual clause, and therefore, it applies retroactively to Welch's case.

In light of the above authorities, the rule announced in Johnson v. United States applies retroactively to Gregory Welch's case. The residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague, and Welch's prior conviction for strong arm robbery does not qualify as a predicate offense under the ACCA. Therefore, Welch's sentence should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing.

Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81 (1921); Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945); and Winters v. New York, 333 U.S. 507 (1948).
