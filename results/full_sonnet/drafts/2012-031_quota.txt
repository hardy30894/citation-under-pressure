# ARGUMENT

## I. RULE 23(B)(3) REQUIRES ADMISSIBLE EVIDENCE THAT DAMAGES ARE SUSCEPTIBLE OF MEASUREMENT ON A CLASS-WIDE BASIS, AND THE THIRD CIRCUIT FAILED TO ENFORCE THIS REQUIREMENT

Rule 23 is not a mere pleading standard. This Court has made clear that a party seeking class certification must "affirmatively demonstrate his compliance with the Rule," *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011), and that certification is proper "only if the trial court is satisfied, after a rigorous analysis," that Rule 23's prerequisites are met, *id.* (quoting *General Telephone Co. of Southwest v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982)). Critically, this rigorous analysis "will frequently entail overlap with the merits of the plaintiff's underlying claim." *Id.* at 351. The Third Circuit itself has recognized that a district court "may delve beyond the pleadings to determine whether the requirements for class certification are satisfied," and that "[a]n overlap between a class certification requirement and the merits of a claim is no reason to decline to resolve relevant disputes when necessary." *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 316-17 (3d Cir. 2008).

Rule 23(b)(3) demands that "questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members." Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). Where, as here, damages are an element of the underlying antitrust claim, predominance requires that plaintiffs demonstrate—through admissible, reliable evidence—that damages can be proven on a class-wide basis using a methodology that actually matches the theory of liability accepted for certification. This Court has long insisted that expert methodologies must be reliable and tied to the facts of the case. *See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993) (holding that expert testimony must be grounded in a "reliable foundation" and be "relevant to the task at hand"); *General Electric Co. v. Joiner*, 522 U.S. 136, 146 (1997) (rejecting expert opinions where there is "too great an analytical gap" between the data and the conclusion). These evidentiary principles do not evaporate at the certification stage merely because a district court incants the word "predominance."

The court below abdicated its gatekeeping role. Plaintiffs' expert, Dr. James McClave, presented a regression model designed to calculate damages arising from *four* distinct theories of anticompetitive conduct. The district court, however, rejected three of those four theories and certified the class solely on the theory that Comcast's clustering deterred "overbuilder" competition. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 190 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Despite discarding three-quarters of the theoretical bases for the model, the district court accepted the same aggregate damages figure—generated by a model that made no attempt to isolate damages attributable to the single surviving theory—as sufficient "common evidence" to satisfy predominance. This is precisely the kind of "analytical gap" this Court condemned in *Joiner*, 522 U.S. at 146.

## II. A DAMAGES MODEL THAT DOES NOT ISOLATE DAMAGES ATTRIBUTABLE TO THE ACCEPTED THEORY OF LIABILITY CANNOT SATISFY PREDOMINANCE

This Court's antitrust jurisprudence requires that damages be tied with reasonable precision to the specific anticompetitive conduct found to be unlawful. In *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 114 n.9 (1969), this Court explained that a plaintiff must provide a basis for a jury to make a "fair and reasonable estimate" of damages flowing from the *proven* violation—not from conduct never adjudicated as unlawful. Similarly, in *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946), the Court permitted approximation of damages only where the "uncertainty as to the amount rather than the fact of damage" existed—necessarily presupposing that the fact of damage from the specific violation has been established.

Here, the district court's own findings undermine any claim of predominance. The court acknowledged that McClave's model calculated damages assuming four separate theories of harm operated in combination, yet the court certified only one theory for trial. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 191. The Third Circuit brushed aside this discrepancy, reasoning that Comcast's attack "goes to the merits of the methodology" rather than to predominance. Comcast Corp. v. Behrend, 655 F.3d 182, 206 (3d Cir. 2011). But this reasoning collapses the distinction between the fact of common impact and the *method* of proving damages—a method that must itself be common to the class to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3). As Judge Jordan recognized in dissent below, a model that cannot "attribute[] damages resulting from the reduced level of competition attributable to the deterrence of overbuilders" alone fails to demonstrate that damages are provable through evidence common to the class. *Id.* at 218 (Jordan, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part).

The Third Circuit's approach effectively holds that any damages model touching on antitrust injury suffices for certification, regardless of whether it is capable of isolating damages caused by the specific theory of liability that will proceed to trial. That approach contravenes *Hydrogen Peroxide*'s own admonition that "findings [at the certification stage] must be made by a preponderance of the evidence," 552 F.3d at 320, and it renders the predominance inquiry a nullity. If plaintiffs may proceed with an admittedly overinclusive damages model, individual questions concerning what portion of aggregate damages is attributable to lawful versus unlawful conduct will inevitably swamp the litigation, defeating the very efficiency rationale of Rule 23(b)(3). *See Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997) (predominance test is meant to test whether "proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
