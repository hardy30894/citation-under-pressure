### Argument

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use school premises for an after-hours film series violated the First Amendment. Once the District opened its facilities for community use, it could not exclude speech because it was religious or because it presented a religious viewpoint on an otherwise permissible subject. That kind of discrimination is exactly what the Free Speech Clause forbids. *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160-61 (1939); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 310-11 (1940).

First, the District created a forum for expressive activity. When government property is opened for public discourse or assembly, the government may regulate only in a manner consistent with the First Amendment. *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946); *Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413, 416-17 (1943); *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 537 (1945). The government cannot permit expressive use while reserving discretion to exclude a disfavored subject or viewpoint. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949); *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315, 320-21 (1951). The constitutional rule is one of neutrality, not suppression. *West Virginia State Bd. of Educ. v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943).

The District’s policy failed that rule. It did not bar Lamb’s Chapel because the film series was disruptive, unsafe, or untimely. It barred access because the films were “church related.” A denial on that ground is content-based, and more specifically viewpoint-based, because the District permitted community use for analogous secular purposes but excluded a religious perspective on family values and child rearing. The First Amendment does not allow government to favor one side of a debate over another. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 551-55 (1965); *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 559, 563-65 (1965). Nor may officials suppress speech merely because they dislike the ideas expressed. *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 235 (1963); *Street v. New York*, 394 U.S. 576, 592 (1969). Those decisions reflect the older rule that government may not silence speech because it is controversial, unpopular, or offensive to official preferences.

The subject matter here underscores the constitutional violation. Lamb’s Chapel sought to show films about family life and child rearing—topics plainly within the realm of public discussion. Speech on social and moral issues is core First Amendment expression. *Thomas*, 323 U.S. at 537; *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 310-11. Religious speech does not lose protection because it is religious. *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 111-12 (1943), held that dissemination of religious views is protected expression, and *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04, recognized that the Constitution protects the right to advocate religion as well as nonreligion. Excluding Lamb’s Chapel because its film series presented a Christian view of family values is thus discrimination against the viewpoint of the speaker, not a neutral time, place, or manner restriction. *Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 160-61; *Jamison*, 318 U.S. at 416-17.

The District cannot salvage its policy by characterizing the facilities as a nonpublic forum. Even where the government has not opened property for all speech, restrictions must be reasonable and not a disguise for viewpoint hostility. *Marsh*, 326 U.S. at 506-09; *Murdock*, 319 U.S. at 111-12. Here, the District allowed after-hours use by community groups but singled out religious use for exclusion. That is not a reasonable access rule; it is censorship by label. The Constitution does not permit the government to say that a film about family values may be shown, but the same film may not be shown if it is presented from a Christian perspective. *Barnette*, 319 U.S. at 642; *Street*, 394 U.S. at 592.

The Establishment Clause does not justify the exclusion either. Neutrality, not hostility to religion, is the constitutional command. *Everson v. Board of Educ.*, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947). The Court has explained that government may not favor one religion over another or religion over nonreligion. *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 303-04; *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 18. Excluding Lamb’s Chapel because its speech is religious does not avoid establishment; it imposes a denominational and viewpoint preference for secular expression. The First Amendment does not require that result.

Accordingly, the District’s refusal to permit Lamb’s Chapel to use the premises for its film series violated the Free Speech Clause, and the judgment should be reversed. *Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515-16; *Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 310-11; *Barnette*, 319 U.S. at 642.
