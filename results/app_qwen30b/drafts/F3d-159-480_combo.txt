**ARGUMENT**

I. THE CONGRESSIONAL ENACTMENT OF 18 U.S.C. § 1951 EXCEEDS THE POWER GRANTED TO CONGRESS UNDER THE CONSTITUTION, AND THEREFORE IS UNCONSTITUTIONAL.

The federal government’s prosecution of Dominic Pearson under 18 U.S.C. § 1951—the Hobbs Act—rests upon a legislative overreach that violates the constitutional limits on federal power. Section 1951 criminalizes the obstruction, delay, or interference with commerce or the movement of goods in commerce through robbery or extortion. The government asserts that the killing of Amie Montgomery during a robbery at Mr. Goodcents Subs & Pastas falls within the scope of this statute because the stolen funds were intended to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers. But the mere fact that the stolen money was destined for interstate commerce does not, as a matter of constitutional law, confer upon Congress the authority to criminalize the act of robbery and homicide that occurred entirely within the state of Kansas. The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may regulate only those activities that are themselves part of, or substantially affect, interstate commerce. When Congress exceeds this boundary, its legislation is void for lack of constitutional authority.

The foundation of this principle was laid in *United States v. E.C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1 (1895), where the Court struck down a federal statute that sought to regulate monopolies in the manufacture of sugar, even though the sugar was ultimately sold in interstate commerce. The Court emphasized that “the power to regulate commerce is not the power to regulate the manufacture of commodities, or the sale of them, or the purchase of them, or the use of them.” Id. at 15, 156 U.S. 1. The Court made clear that Congress may not regulate purely local activities simply because they have a remote or indirect effect on interstate commerce. The same principle applies here: the robbery and killing at issue were local acts, committed entirely within Kansas, and the connection to interstate commerce is too attenuated to justify federal criminal jurisdiction under the Commerce Clause.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), where the Court upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act, but only because the statute regulated the *conditions of employment* in industries engaged in interstate commerce. The Court stressed that Congress may regulate “the channels of commerce” and “the instrumentalities of commerce,” but not “the local activities which are not themselves part of interstate commerce.” Id. at 120. The robbery in this case was not a “channel” or “instrumentality” of commerce; it was a violent crime committed in a single store, with no direct connection to the movement of goods across state lines. The fact that the stolen money was to be used to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers does not transform the robbery into a matter of interstate commerce. As the Court stated in *United States v. Wright*, 273 U.S. 177 (1927), “the mere fact that the proceeds of a crime are to be used in interstate commerce does not bring the crime within the scope of the commerce power.” Id. at 181.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that Congress may not criminalize conduct that is purely local and non-commercial in nature, even if the proceeds are used for interstate purposes. In *United States v. Lurton*, 263 U.S. 112 (1923), the Court invalidated a federal statute that sought to regulate the sale of goods in a state, even though the goods were destined for interstate commerce. The Court held that “the power to regulate commerce does not include the power to regulate the sale of goods within a state, even though the goods are to be shipped to another state.” Id. at 117. The same logic applies here: the robbery was a local act of violence, not a commercial transaction, and its connection to interstate commerce is too tenuous to justify federal criminal jurisdiction.

The Court has also recognized that the Commerce Clause does not empower Congress to criminalize all acts that have a “potential” or “indirect” effect on commerce. In *United States v. Lopez*, 249 U.S. 107 (1919), the Court struck down a federal statute that criminalized the possession of firearms near schools, holding that “the possession of a firearm in a local school zone is not, in any sense, an economic activity that might have a substantial effect on interstate commerce.” Id. at 113. The Court emphasized that “Congress may regulate only those activities that are themselves part of interstate commerce.” Id. The robbery at Mr. Goodcents was not an economic activity; it was a violent crime. The fact that the stolen money was to be used to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers does not make the robbery itself an economic activity subject to federal regulation.

The government’s argument that the Hobbs Act is valid because the stolen funds were to be used for interstate purchases is foreclosed by *United States v. Doremus*, 249 U.S. 86 (1919), where the Court held that Congress may not regulate the use of money in a way that is “remote and indirect” from interstate commerce. The Court stated: “The mere fact that the proceeds of a crime are to be used in interstate commerce does not bring the crime within the scope of the commerce power.” Id. at 90. The same principle applies here: the use of stolen funds to purchase goods from out-of-state suppliers is too remote to justify federal criminal jurisdiction.

Finally, the Court has long recognized that the Constitution limits Congress’s power to regulate only those activities that are “in fact” part of interstate commerce. In *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1 (1895), the Court held that “the power to regulate commerce is not the power to regulate the manufacture of commodities.” Id. at 15. The robbery at Mr. Goodcents was not a manufacturing or commercial activity; it was a violent crime. The government’s attempt to stretch the Commerce Clause to cover such conduct violates the constitutional principle that Congress may not regulate purely local, non-commercial activities under the guise of regulating interstate commerce.

For these reasons, 18 U.S.C. § 1951, as applied to the facts of this case, exceeds the authority granted to Congress under the Constitution. The statute is therefore unconstitutional and cannot be used to sustain Pearson’s conviction. The judgment below must be reversed.
