ARGUMENT

The district court erred in concluding that the commercial-activity exception, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2), does not confer subject-matter jurisdiction. A foreign state loses immunity when the claim is “based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state” or upon an act outside the United States “in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere” that “causes a direct effect in the United States.” Id.

The nature of the conduct, not its purpose, controls. Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc., 504 U.S. 607, 614 (1992). Pakistan’s dispatch of an emissary to the United States to negotiate and tender substitute real property constituted a commercial transaction. See id. at 616 (issuance of bonds was commercial because it was “the type of action by which a private party engages in trade”); Texas Trading & Milling Corp. v. Federal Republic of Nigeria, 647 F.2d 300, 309 (2d Cir. 1981) (contract to purchase cement was commercial). The subsequent refusal to convey clear title or pay compensation flowed directly from that transaction and produced a direct effect in the United States by depriving a U.S. resident of the bargained-for value. See Weltover, 504 U.S. at 618–19; Hanil Bank v. PT. Bank Negara Indonesia (Persero), 148 F.3d 127, 131–32 (2d Cir. 1998).

Expropriation unaccompanied by payment is not invariably sovereign. Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina, 965 F.2d 699, 708–09 (9th Cir. 1992); de Sanchez v. Banco Central de Nicaragua, 770 F.2d 1385, 1393 (5th Cir. 1985). When the foreign state later engages in commercial dealings to resolve the claim, those dealings independently satisfy the exception. Practical Concepts, Inc. v. Republic of Bolivia, 811 F.2d 1543, 1549 (D.C. Cir. 1987); Asociacion de Reclamantes v. United Mexican States, 735 F.2d 1517, 1524 (D.C. Cir. 1984).

The Supreme Court of Pakistan’s invitation to pursue further remedies in Pakistan does not negate the prior commercial contacts in the United States. Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria, 461 U.S. 480, 490 (1983) (FSIA exceptions are to be applied “in every civil action” against a foreign state). Because the complaint alleges facts that, if proven, establish commercial activity causing a direct effect here, the district court possessed jurisdiction. See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson, 507 U.S. 349, 356–57 (1993); Foremost-McKesson, Inc. v. Islamic Republic of Iran, 905 F.2d 438, 449 (D.C. Cir. 1990).

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
