# ARGUMENT

## I. WOODARD HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST IN CLEMENCY PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE

Executive clemency has never been a matter of right. From the Nation's beginning, the pardon power has been understood as an act of grace lying wholly within executive discretion, not a stage of adjudication to which the judiciary supplies procedural content. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150 (1833), this Court described a pardon as "the private, though official act of the executive magistrate," a characterization reaffirmed in *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), where the Court explained that a pardon "is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." That characterization forecloses any claim that an inmate possesses a constitutionally cognizable expectation of receiving clemency, or of receiving any particular procedure before it is granted or denied.

This Court has applied the same principle to analogous forms of executive leniency. In *Burns v. United States*, 287 U.S. 216, 220 (1932), the Court treated parole as "a matter of favor," not entitlement, and in *Escoe v. Zerbst*, 295 U.S. 490, 492 (1935), it held that "probation or suspension of sentence comes as an act of grace to one convicted of a crime," subject to whatever conditions the granting authority chooses to impose. If a convicted defendant has no constitutionally protected interest in probation or parole—dispositions that operate as extensions of the sentencing process itself—he a fortiori has none in clemency, a determination made by the Executive after the judicial process has run its course and the sentence has become final.

The Sixth Circuit's contrary holding rests on the theory that because clemency review is "an integral part" of Ohio's system for finally disposing of capital cases, it must be judged by the same due process standard applied to trial and appeal. That reasoning cannot be squared with this Court's teaching that due process protections attach only where the private interest at stake and the nature of the governmental function require them. As the Court explained in *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961), "consideration of what procedures due process may require under any given set of circumstances must begin with a determination of the precise nature of the government function involved as well as of the private interest that has been affected by governmental action." The nature of the clemency function—an act of grace committed to executive discretion—supplies the answer here: because the inmate has no entitlement to clemency, he has no liberty interest that the state's voluntary provision of a review procedure can convert into a right to judicially enforceable process. The State's decision to afford a review mechanism, on top of a power it need not exercise at all, does not itself create the kind of interest this Court has protected in cases involving the deprivation of a right the individual already possesses.

## II. THE OPTION OF A VOLUNTARY CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

Nor does Ohio's procedure offend the privilege against self-incrimination. The privilege bars only compelled testimony; it does not reach statements an individual chooses to give. As this Court stated in *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 478 (1966), "[v]olunteered statements of any kind are not barred by the Fifth Amendment." Ohio's regulation does no more than offer Woodard the opportunity to speak; it does not require him to answer any question, nor does it withhold clemency consideration if he declines the interview altogether. Because nothing compels participation, the privilege is simply not implicated. *See Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1 (1964) (privilege protects against compelled, not voluntary, disclosure); *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 438–439 (1956) (privilege applies only where a real hazard of compulsion exists).

Nor does the interview option impose an unconstitutional penalty on the exercise of the privilege. In *Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493, 497 (1967), the Court found compulsion where the choice was "between self-incrimination or job forfeiture"—a threat of concrete loss for remaining silent. No comparable threat exists here. Declining the interview carries no sanction: the clemency authority still considers the application, along with whatever affidavits, briefs, or other materials counsel chooses to submit. Because refusal to speak triggers no penalty, this case is far removed from *Garrity* and its companion decisions condemning the use of a threatened deprivation to force a choice between silence and loss. *See Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (privilege guards against "real and appreciable" hazards of compulsion, not speculative ones).

The Sixth Circuit's remand for further fact-finding rests on the "conceivable" possibility that a volunteering inmate might someday say something that could be used against him in some future, unspecified proceeding. That speculation cannot support a Fifth Amendment claim. The privilege protects against compelled self-incrimination in fact, not against the hypothetical consequences of a choice an inmate is free not to make.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
