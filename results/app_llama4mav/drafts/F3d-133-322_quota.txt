ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING JOANN A. PARKER'S MOTION TO DISMISS THE INDICTMENT BECAUSE SHE LACKED THE AUTHORITY TO GRANT OR DENY BENEFITS, AND THUS, COULD NOT HAVE COMMITTED PUBLIC BRIBERY UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).

The appellants argue that the indictment charging Joann A. Parker with conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery under 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) was deficient because she lacked the authority to grant or deny benefits. However, this argument is unpersuasive in light of the broad definition of "official act" under § 201(a)(3) and relevant case law.

Section 201(b)(2)(C) prohibits public officials from being induced to do or omit any act in violation of their official duty. The statute does not require that the public official have the authority to perform the act in question. Rather, it is sufficient that the act is related to the official's duties and is done in their official capacity. See United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914) (holding that "duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery").

In United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983), the Sixth Circuit held that a public official's actions can be considered "official acts" even if they are not within the official's specific authority. Similarly, in United States v. Carson, 464 F.2d 424, 433 (2d Cir. 1972), the Second Circuit held that a public official's corruption of their office can be considered a violation of their official duty, even if the specific act in question is not within their authority.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has held that the term "official act" is to be interpreted broadly. See Dixson v. United States, 465 U.S. 482, 496 (1984) (holding that the term "public official" under § 201(a)(1) should be interpreted broadly to include individuals who occupy positions of public trust). The Court has also noted that Congress intended to "include any decision or action taken by a public official in his capacity as such." S.Rep. No. 87-2213, (1962), reprinted in 1962 U.S.C.C.A.N. 3852, 3856.

In this case, Joann A. Parker's actions, although not within her specific authority, were related to her duties as a clerk to ALJ Aronson and were done in her official capacity. She had access to the office's computer system and was involved in the processing of benefits claims. Her actions, including approving benefits and forging ALJ Aronson's signature, were in violation of her official duty and can be considered "official acts" under § 201(a)(3). See United States v. Holley, 23 F.3d 902, 910 (5th Cir. 1994) (holding that a public official's actions can be considered "official acts" if they are related to their duties and are done in their official capacity).

The appellants' reliance on United States v. Valdes, 949 F.2d 306 (9th Cir. 1991), is misplaced. In Valdes, the Ninth Circuit held that a public official must have the authority to perform the act in question to be guilty of public bribery under § 201(b)(2)(C). However, this holding is not supported by the majority of case law, including Gjieli and Carson.

In light of the broad definition of "official act" under § 201(a)(3) and the relevant case law, the district court did not err in denying Joann A. Parker's motion to dismiss the indictment.

II. THE CONVICTIONS OF RALPH PARKER SHOULD BE REVERSED BECAUSE THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT HIS CONVICTION FOR CONSPIRACY TO COMMIT PUBLIC BRIBERY AND PUBLIC BRIBERY.

Ralph Parker's convictions should be reversed because the evidence was insufficient to support his conviction for conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery. To convict Ralph Parker of conspiracy, the government must prove that he knowingly and willfully participated in the conspiracy. See United States v. Hernandez-Palacios, 838 F.2d 1346, 1348 (5th Cir. 1988). The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that Ralph Parker was aware of the conspiracy or that he participated in it.

The government's reliance on the videotape of Ralph Parker taking $500 from Simmons is not sufficient to prove his involvement in the conspiracy. See United States v. Tolliver, 780 F.2d 1177, 1183 (5th Cir. 1986) (holding that mere presence at the scene of a crime is not sufficient to prove participation in a conspiracy). The government must prove that Ralph Parker had knowledge of the conspiracy and intentionally participated in it. See United States v. Garcia, 762 F.2d 1222, 1225 (5th Cir. 1985).

In this case, the evidence presented at trial was insufficient to prove that Ralph Parker had knowledge of the conspiracy or that he intentionally participated in it. Therefore, his convictions for conspiracy to commit public bribery and public bribery should be reversed.

In conclusion, the district court did not err in denying Joann A. Parker's motion to dismiss the indictment, and her convictions should be affirmed. However, Ralph Parker's convictions should be reversed due to insufficient evidence.
