**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Erdman Failed to Accumulate the 1,250 FMLA Hours Required for Eligibility**

The FMLA confers eligibility on employees who have worked for an employer for at least 12 months and have accumulated at least 1,250 hours of service during the prior 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(i)-(ii). The statute does not define “hours of service,” but the Department of Labor’s regulations provide that “[a]n employee’s ‘hours of service’ include ... all time during which an employee is required or allowed to perform ... work for the employer.” 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c) (2008). The Third Circuit has not addressed whether unapproved overtime may be counted toward the 1,250-hour threshold, but this Court has long held that an employer may not avoid its statutory obligations by disavowing work that it permitted or encouraged. *See, e.g., Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery Co.*, 328 U.S. 680, 687 (1946) (“The Portal-to-Portal Act did not relieve employers of their duty to compensate employees for all work actually performed.”); *United States v. Klinghoffer Bros.*, 285 F.2d 777, 782 (3d Cir. 1960) (“An employer cannot escape liability by asserting that the work was not authorized or requested.”).

Here, the record shows that Nationwide permitted and compensated Erdman for extra hours worked outside her scheduled part-time and four-day schedules. Patty Sarno, Erdman’s supervisor, testified that she regularly authorized payment for these hours or allowed Erdman to use them as “comp” time. Even after Getgen became Erdman’s supervisor, she did not object to Erdman’s use of “comp” time until February 10, 2003—well after Erdman had already accrued the hours in question. Getgen’s belated admonition cannot retroactively erase the hours Erdman worked with Nationwide’s tacit approval. *See Anderson*, 328 U.S. at 687 (“The fact that the employer does not want the work does not mean that the employee has not performed it.”).

Nationwide’s argument that Erdman’s extra hours were “unapproved” is unavailing. The Third Circuit has long recognized that an employer may not avoid liability by labeling work as “unauthorized” when it knew or should have known of the work and permitted it to continue. *See Klinghoffer Bros.*, 285 F.2d at 782 (“The employer’s knowledge and acquiescence in the work performed are sufficient to establish liability.”). Here, Nationwide’s own policies and practices—including its payment of extra hours and allowance of “comp” time—demonstrate that it knew of and permitted Erdman’s extra work. The District Court therefore erred in disregarding these hours when calculating Erdman’s eligibility.

**II. The District Court Improperly Weighed the Evidence and Drew Inferences Against Erdman**

Summary judgment is appropriate only if there are no genuine issues of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). In reviewing the grant of summary judgment, this Court must view the facts and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. *Id.* The District Court failed to do so here.

The record contains disputed facts regarding whether Erdman’s extra hours were “allowed” or “authorized.” Erdman testified that she worked extra hours with Sarno’s approval and that Getgen did not object to her use of “comp” time until after the fact. Nationwide, by contrast, claims that Erdman’s extra hours were unapproved. These disputes are material to the FMLA eligibility question and must be resolved by a jury. *See Anderson*, 328 U.S. at 687 (“The question of whether certain work is compensable is generally a question of fact.”).

Moreover, the District Court improperly resolved credibility disputes in Nationwide’s favor. Credibility determinations are the province of the jury, not the court at summary judgment. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 150 (2000) (citing *United States v. Yellow Cab Co.*, 338 U.S. 338, 341 (1949)). Here, the District Court resolved conflicting testimony in Nationwide’s favor without explanation, effectively weighing the evidence and drawing inferences against Erdman. This was error.

**III. The District Court’s Ruling Creates a Circuit Split and Undermines the FMLA’s Protections**

The FMLA was enacted to balance the demands of the workplace with the needs of families. *See S. Rep. No. 103-3, at 10 (1993)*. The Department of Labor’s regulations make clear that employers may not avoid their obligations by disavowing work they permitted or encouraged. 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). The District Court’s ruling—disregarding hours worked with the employer’s knowledge or acquiescence—undermines these protections and creates a circuit split. *See, e.g., Brungart v. BellSouth Telecomm., Inc.*, 231 F.3d 791, 797 (11th Cir. 2000) (holding that unapproved overtime may count toward FMLA eligibility if the employer knew or should have known of the work).

This Court should align with the Eleventh Circuit and hold that hours worked with the employer’s knowledge or acquiescence count toward FMLA eligibility. *See Klinghoffer Bros.*, 285 F.2d at 782 (employer’s knowledge and acquiescence sufficient to establish liability). Doing so would effectuate the FMLA’s remedial purpose and ensure consistent application of the statute.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claim. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
