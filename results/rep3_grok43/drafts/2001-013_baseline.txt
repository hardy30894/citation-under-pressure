**ARGUMENT**

Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act provides that the Act does not apply to “working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies … exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard has not exercised such authority over the conditions that produced the explosion on Rig 52.

In *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), this Court held that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” *Id.* at 1122. The vessel there was subject to the Coast Guard’s comprehensive inspection regime, and the Court relied on the agency’s “regulations … governing the safety and health of persons working on vessels.” *Ibid.* *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), reaffirmed the same principle for “vessels in navigation” already under active Coast Guard oversight, emphasizing that “a single, uniform set of rules should govern the maritime workplace.” *Id.* at 829.

Neither decision addressed an uninspected vessel on which the Coast Guard has promulgated only the limited requirements enumerated in 46 U.S.C. § 4102 and 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq., 26.01 et seq. The statutory text requires actual exercise of authority over the particular working conditions, not the mere existence of some regulatory power. Because the Coast Guard’s investigation of the Rig 52 casualty resulted in no citation or safety standard directed at the blowout-prevention procedures at issue, section 4(b)(1) does not displace OSHA’s jurisdiction.

The court of appeals therefore erred in extending *Clary* and *Donovan* to preclude OSHA regulation of uninspected vessels subject only to minimal Coast Guard oversight.
