**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension in patent law: the balance between protecting the patentee’s exclusive rights and ensuring that damages are proportionate to the actual value contributed by the patented feature. The Federal Circuit’s decision, which affirmed the award of Samsung’s entire profits on the basis of design patents covering only the appearance of a smartphone’s front face, violates settled principles of patent damages and the statutory framework of 35 U.S.C. § 284. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Violates the Core Principle of Apportionment in Patent Damages

The law has long recognized that a patentee is entitled to recover only the profits attributable to the patented invention, not the entire profit from the infringing product. As this Court held in *Westinghouse Electric & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Electric & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 615 (1912), “there are many cases in which the plaintiff’s patent is only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits.” In such cases, “he is only entitled to recover that part of the net gains” attributable to the patented feature. The patentee must “give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features, and such evidence must be reliable and tangible, and not conjectural or speculative.” Id. This principle applies with equal force to design patents.

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to require apportionment in this case—where the patented design covered only the front face of a smartphone, a component that is but one part of a complex, multi-component device—contradicts this foundational rule. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that “the entire value of the article” cannot be attributed to a single component unless the patented feature is the “entire value” of the product. *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885). In *Dobson*, this Court rejected an award of damages based on the entire profit from a carpet, where the patented design was only one element of the product. The Court held that “the entire profits as due to the figure or pattern, unless it is shown that the entire value of the article is properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” Id. The same logic applies here: the iPhone’s front face, while distinctive, is not the entire value of the smartphone. The device’s value arises from its processor, memory, operating system, camera, and other components—none of which were patented by Apple.

The Federal Circuit’s decision, which allowed Apple to recover nearly $300 million in profits from Samsung’s entire smartphone sales, effectively treats the design of a single component as if it were the entire product. This is not merely a procedural error—it is a substantive violation of the apportionment principle. The Court has long held that “the measure of damages for infringing a patented improvement on a machine in public use is the same as if the defendant had pirated the whole machine and every improvement on it previously made” only when the improvement is the “entire value” of the product. *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 495 (1854). That is not the case here. The patented design was not the sole or dominant source of value. The jury’s award, therefore, was not supported by substantial evidence and cannot stand.

### II. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Contradicts the Statutory Framework of 35 U.S.C. § 284

Section 284 of the Patent Act provides that a patentee “shall be entitled to recover damages adequate to compensate for the infringement.” 35 U.S.C. § 284. This is not a blank check. The statute does not authorize the recovery of “entire profits” without regard to the contribution of the patented feature. Rather, the Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to require a “reasonable” and “proportionate” award. *General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp.*, 461 U.S. 648, 654 (1983). In *Devex*, the Court emphasized that “the patentee must show the extent to which the patented feature contributed to the overall value of the product.” Id. at 655. This is not a mere formality—it is the essence of the damages remedy.

The Federal Circuit’s rule, which allows recovery of entire profits for a component design, renders § 284 meaningless. If a patentee can recover the entire profit from a product based on a design covering only a single component, then the statute becomes a tool for extracting windfall profits from infringers, regardless of the actual contribution of the patented feature. This is not the law. As this Court stated in *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 15 (1886), “the principle on which damages are to be computed in respect to a patent for a machine, or for an improvement in a machine, or for a process, is not applicable to a patent for a design, because, in a patent for a design, the result is patented, while in the other kind of patent the means are patented.” But even in design cases, the Court has insisted that damages must be apportioned. *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. at 445. The Federal Circuit’s decision ignores this distinction and elevates form over substance.

### III. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Encourages Patent Trolling and Distorts Innovation

The Federal Circuit’s rule creates a dangerous precedent that incentivizes patentees to assert design patents for minor, non-functional features and then seek entire-product profits. This is not innovation—it is rent-seeking. The Court has repeatedly warned against allowing patentees to “extort” excessive damages from infringers. *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390, 398 (1940). In *Sheldon*, this Court held that “there may be an apportionment so as to give to the owner of the copyright only that part of the profits found to be attributable to the use of the copyrighted material as distinguished from what the infringer himself has supplied.”
