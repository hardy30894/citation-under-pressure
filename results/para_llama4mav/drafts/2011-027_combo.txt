The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court properly applied the test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes. The equal footing doctrine, which grants states title to the beds of navigable rivers upon admission to the Union, is rooted in the principle that navigable waters are "held in trust for the people of the state." United States v. Holt State Bank, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926). To determine whether a river is navigable, courts have long applied the test set forth in The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557 (1870), which asks whether the river is "navigable in fact" and "used or susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce." Id. at 563.

The trial court's application of this test to determine the navigability of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers at the time of Montana's statehood in 1889 was proper. As the Supreme Court has held, the "navigability for title" test is a question of fact that requires consideration of the river's condition at the time of statehood. Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 86-87 (1922). The trial court's reliance on historical evidence, including reports and documents from the late 19th century, was therefore justified.

PPL's argument that the trial court erred in considering hearsay evidence on summary judgment is unpersuasive. As this Court has held, "ancient documents" may be admissible as evidence under certain circumstances. See M. R. Evid. 901(8); United States v. Pendell, 185 F. 601, 605 (C.C.D. Or. 1910). The State's expert, Foley, provided a proper foundation for the historical documents she submitted, and the trial court properly considered them.

The equal footing doctrine is a well-established principle of law, rooted in the Supreme Court's decisions in cases such as Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), and Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1 (1894). In Pollard's Lessee, the Court held that the original states held title to the beds of navigable waters, and that this title passed to the new states upon their admission to the Union. 44 U.S. at 228-29. In Shively, the Court reaffirmed this principle, holding that the equal footing doctrine is a "fundamental principle" of American law. 152 U.S. at 26.

The trial court's conclusion that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is supported by the evidence and consistent with the principles set forth in these cases. The Montana Supreme Court correctly affirmed the trial court's decision, and this Court should uphold that decision on appeal.

The principles set forth in cases such as The Montello, 87 U.S. 430 (1874), and Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), also support the trial court's conclusion. In The Montello, the Court held that a river is navigable if it is "used, or capable of being used, as a highway for commerce." 87 U.S. at 441. In Economy Light & Power Co., the Court reaffirmed this principle, holding that a river's navigability is determined by its "natural condition" and its "capacity for valuable floatage." 256 U.S. at 122-23.

In United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), the Court further clarified the test for navigability, holding that a river is navigable if it is "navigable in fact" and "used or susceptible of being used" for commerce. Id. at 14-15. The trial court's application of this test to the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers was proper, and its conclusion that these rivers were navigable at the time of statehood is supported by the evidence.

The decision of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
