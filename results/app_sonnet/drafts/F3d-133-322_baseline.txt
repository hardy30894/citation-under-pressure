# ARGUMENT

## I. THE INDICTMENT AND EVIDENCE FAILED TO ESTABLISH THAT MRS. PARKER COMMITTED A VIOLATION OF 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C) BECAUSE SHE LACKED AUTHORITY TO GRANT OR DENY BENEFITS

Section 201(b)(2)(C) criminalizes the receipt of anything of value by a public official in return for "being induced to do or omit to do any act in violation of the official duty of such official." 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). The government's theory necessarily requires proof that Mrs. Parker possessed an "official duty" capable of being violated. The uncontroverted evidence shows that Mrs. Parker's official duties were limited to assisting ALJ Aronson before and during hearings, taking notes, and typing and mailing his decisions. She was never authorized to approve, deny, or reinstate SSI benefits, nor was she authorized to sign ALJ Aronson's name to any document.

The government cannot escape this deficiency by pointing to the breadth of the statutory definition of "official act" in § 201(a)(3), which encompasses "any decision or action on any question, matter, cause, suit, proceeding or controversy" pending before an official "in such official's official capacity." 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). This provision defines the *scope* of matters that qualify as official acts; it does not eliminate the separate requirement that the defendant hold an official duty with respect to those matters. The Senate Report's explanation that Congress intended "to include any decision or action taken by a public official in his capacity as such," S.Rep. No. 87-2213 (1962), reprinted in 1962 U.S.C.C.A.N. 3852, 3856, confirms that the acts must be taken "in his capacity" as an official—that is, pursuant to some recognized authority, whether formal or informal.

This Court's own recitation of the governing law demonstrates the point. Official duties may be established not only by "statutes and written rules and regulations," but also by "established usage," because "duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery." United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). Birdsall thus requires *some* settled practice or usage vesting the official with authority over the matter—not the complete absence of authority that exists here. Mrs. Parker's forgery of ALJ Aronson's signature and her alteration of computer records to approve benefits were not acts taken pursuant to any "settled practice" recognized by the SSA; they were usurpations of authority that belonged exclusively to the ALJ. No evidence in the record suggests that clerks in the Office of Hearings and Appeals customarily approved or reinstated benefits, signed ALJs' names, or otherwise exercised decisional authority over claims. Absent such a showing, the government failed to establish the predicate "official duty" required by the statute.

The government's reliance on decisions such as United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968 (6th Cir. 1983), for the proposition that official acts violating an official duty are "not limited to those within the official's specific authority" does not salvage the conviction. Even accepting that formulation, there must be some nexus between the defendant's position and the corrupted act—some color of authority or established practice by which the act could plausibly be understood as being performed "in such official's official capacity." 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). Mrs. Parker's conduct here was not an abuse of discretion within a zone of ambiguous or informally expanded authority; it was outright forgery and fabrication wholly divorced from any duty, express or customary, ever entrusted to her. To stretch § 201(b)(2)(C) to cover such conduct—conduct indistinguishable from simple fraud committed by an employee with no decisional authority whatsoever—would read the "official duty" element out of the statute entirely and would contravene the statute's structure, which distinguishes bribery of officials exercising some measure of entrusted authority from garden-variety fraud by government employees.

Because the indictment charged, and the government's proof at trial established, only that Mrs. Parker performed acts categorically outside any duty—formal or customary—ever reposed in her by the SSA, the essential element of "official duty" under § 201(b)(2)(C) was not satisfied. The indictment was therefore deficient as charged, and the resulting convictions of both Mrs. Parker and Mr. Parker for conspiracy and substantive violations of § 201(b)(2)(C) cannot stand. See Birdsall, 233 U.S. at 231 (limiting bribery liability to acts within an official's actual or customary duties). This Court should reverse the convictions on Counts charging violations of § 201(b)(2)(C) and the related conspiracy count, as they rest on a legally insufficient theory of "official duty."
