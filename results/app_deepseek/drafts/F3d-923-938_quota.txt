**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erroneously denied Meruelo’s deduction by requiring a direct cash transfer from him to Merco, ignoring that the net transfers through his affiliates, when reclassified as loans, established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him. Such formalism contradicts the economic substance doctrine and this Circuit’s precedent.

**I. The Tax Court Erred in Requiring a Direct Transfer from Meruelo to Merco**

A shareholder’s basis in S corporation debt includes obligations on which the shareholder is the “true creditor.” *Underwood v. Comm’r*, 535 F.2d 309, 311 (5th Cir. 1976). The critical inquiry is whether the shareholder made an “actual economic outlay” and whether the debt runs directly to the shareholder. *Selfe v. United States*, 778 F.2d 769, 772 (11th Cir. 1985). The Tax Court focused on the form of the transfer—through affiliates rather than directly from Meruelo—but the substance demonstrates that Meruelo was the economic source.

In *Selfe*, this Court held that a shareholder’s guarantee of a corporation’s loan could give rise to basis if the shareholder ultimately paid the debt. *Id.* at 773. Here, the funds originated from Meruelo’s personal loan and from entities he controlled. The promissory note, annual charges, and netting of accounts payable transformed the affiliate advances into debt owed directly to Meruelo. *See Bolding v. Comm’r*, 117 F.3d 270, 272 (5th Cir. 1997) (recognizing that a shareholder’s indirect advances through related entities can create direct indebtedness when the arrangement is structured as a loan). The Tax Court’s demand for a “direct” cash transfer elevates form over substance.

**II. The Economic Substance of the Transactions Demonstrates the Debt Ran Directly to Meruelo**

The net transfers from Merco’s affiliates to Merco, recharacterized as loans from Meruelo, had real economic effect. *See Perry v. Comm’r*, 764 F.2d 891, 895 (1st Cir. 1985) (shareholder may obtain basis where he “is the ultimate source of the funds”). Meruelo’s ownership interests in the affiliates meant that the funds advanced were his economic capital. *Malone v. Comm’r*, 412 F.2d 1168, 1172 (5th Cir. 1969) (shareholder’s advances through a controlled entity treated as direct loans). The annual reconciliation and promissory note evidenced a debtor-creditor relationship between Merco and Meruelo, not between Merco and the affiliates. *See Gilday v. Comm’r*, 620 F.2d 1346, 1349 (5th Cir. 1980) (standard for debt includes “a real expectation of repayment”).

The Commissioner’s position ignores the step-transaction doctrine. *See Yates v. Comm’r*, 116 T.C. 476, 498 (2001), aff’d, 50 F. App’x 532 (5th Cir. 2002) (steps may be collapsed when they are “component parts of a single transaction”). Meruelo contributed funds to the affiliates, which then advanced them to Merco. The net effect was a direct loan from Meruelo. *Brown v. Comm’r*, 108 T.C. 94, 105 (1997) (substance controls over form when taxpayer’s actions “are integrated into a single plan”).

**III. Meruelo Made Actual Economic Outlays**

The Tax Court found that Meruelo failed to show an “actual economic outlay,” but the record demonstrates otherwise. Meruelo personally borrowed $4.9 million and transferred it to Akoya, which then sent it to Merco. The Commissioner conceded that this gave Meruelo basis. The remaining $9 million came from Meruelo’s affiliates, which he controlled and which had no independent economic substance. *See Miller v. Comm’r*, 115 T.C. 582, 590 (2000) (shareholder’s economic outlay includes funds advanced through a “wholly owned entity”). The promissory note and interest charges reflect Meruelo’s personal obligation to repay the affiliates if Merco defaulted—a genuine economic risk. *See Estate of Leavitt v. Comm’r*, 875 F.3d 230, 238 (4th Cir. 2017) (economic outlay exists where shareholder “parts with funds” in a manner that creates personal exposure). Meruelo’s guarantee of the affiliates’ advances, combined with their ultimate reclassification as loans to him, satisfied the economic outlay requirement.

**CONCLUSION**

The Tax Court imposed an artificial requirement that ignores the economic reality of Meruelo’s integrated business arrangements. This Court should reverse and hold that Meruelo had a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him in the amount of $9,085,000, entitling him to the full $13 million deduction.
