**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (1997), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries occurring before its enactment. In interpreting such provisions, courts apply the "well-established rule" that statutes are not to be given retroactive effect absent clear congressional intent. *United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co.*, 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928). This principle is rooted in the Due Process Clause, which prohibits retroactive legislation that impairs vested rights. *See id.* at 162; *United States v. Heinszen & Co.*, 206 U.S. 370, 390 (1907).

Here, the statute’s text and structure do not evince a clear intent to apply retroactively. The statute’s operative phrase—"has reentered"—is framed in the present perfect tense, which typically describes actions completed prior to the statute’s effective date. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Moreover, the statute’s legislative history is silent on retroactive application. *See* H.R. Rep. No. 104-828 (1996). Absent explicit language or clear evidence of congressional intent, the statute must be construed prospectively. *Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63.

The government’s contrary interpretation—that the statute applies to all illegal re-entries, regardless of when they occurred—would render the statute impermissibly retroactive. *See id.* at 162. Such an interpretation would impair the rights of aliens who re-entered before 1997 by subjecting them to a new deportation regime without fair warning. *See id.* The Due Process Clause forbids such retroactive application. *See id.*

**II. The reinstatement statute, as applied to pre-enactment re-entries, is impermissibly retroactive.**

Even if the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries, its application in this case violates the Due Process Clause. A statute is retroactive if it "takes away or impairs vested rights acquired under existing laws." *Heinszen & Co.*, 206 U.S. at 390. Here, Fernandez-Vargas re-entered the United States in 1982 and lived here for nearly two decades before the statute’s enactment. His prolonged residence and subsequent marriage to a U.S. citizen created a substantial reliance interest in remaining in the United States. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63.

The reinstatement statute retroactively strips Fernandez-Vargas of eligibility for adjustment of status—a form of relief that was available to him at the time of his re-entry. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1982). This deprivation of a previously available remedy is impermissibly retroactive. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63. The statute’s retroactive effect is further underscored by its denial of discretionary relief to aliens in Fernandez-Vargas’ position. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Such a sweeping deprivation of relief, applied to conduct predating the statute, violates due process. *See Heinszen & Co.*, 206 U.S. at 390.

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding—that Fernandez-Vargas had no "protectable expectation of being able to adjust his status"—is unsound. *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 428 F.3d 1198, 1205 (10th Cir. 2005). The Due Process Clause protects not only vested rights but also reasonable reliance interests. *See Magnolia Petroleum*, 276 U.S. at 162-63. Fernandez-Vargas’ long-term residence and family ties in the United States created a legitimate expectation of remaining here, which the reinstatement statute retroactively destroys. *See id.*

**Conclusion**

The reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date, and its application to such aliens is impermissibly retroactive. The judgment below should be reversed.
