ARGUMENT

The National Labor Relations Act protects employees' right to choose or reject representation while imposing on employers a duty to bargain only with a union enjoying majority support. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33 (1937). An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continued majority status, but that presumption yields to objective evidence of loss of support. Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 104 (1954). The Board's requirement that an employer possess "good-faith reasonable doubt" before polling therefore must be measured against the statutory command that representation questions be resolved by employee free choice rather than administrative fiat.

The Act nowhere mandates identical evidentiary thresholds for withdrawal of recognition, Board-conducted elections, and employer polls. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 706 (1944). Requiring the same quantum of proof for an internal poll as for unilateral withdrawal of recognition disregards the distinct risks each presents. An employer that polls under procedural safeguards does not coerce employees or destabilize bargaining; it merely obtains information the Board itself deems reliable when gathered by secret ballot. Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 797-798 (1945). By collapsing these distinctions, the Board has erected a barrier that effectively nullifies the employer's statutory right to test majority status without first risking an unfair-labor-practice charge.

The Board's factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked objective evidence of lost support likewise fails substantial-evidence review. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Multiple employees stated they no longer desired union representation; others expressed dissatisfaction with dues and services; still others conveyed the views of entire shifts. NLRB v. A.J. Tower Co., 329 U.S. 324, 330 (1946) (employer may rely on statements indicating employee sentiment). The Board discounted these expressions on grounds not required by the statute and unsupported by the record. When an employer has assembled such evidence without creating a coercive atmosphere, the Act permits a poll conducted under the safeguards of NLRB v. Babcock & Wilcox Co., 351 U.S. 105, 113 (1956).

Finally, the Board's rule cannot be reconciled with the principle that the Act favors employee expression over administrative presumption. NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 602 (1969). Because the standard applied here is neither rational nor consistent with the National Labor Relations Act, and because the record contains substantial evidence that Allentown Mack possessed a good-faith doubt, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
