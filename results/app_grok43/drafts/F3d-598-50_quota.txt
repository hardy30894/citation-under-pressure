I. The District Court Erred by Admitting the ADAs’ and Lieutenant’s Testimony.

The ADAs and lieutenant testified that Ramos and Rivera were credible, that probable cause existed, and that certain evidence strengthened or weakened appellants’ case. This testimony violated three settled rules.

First, witnesses may not vouch for the credibility of other witnesses. Nimely v. City of New York, 414 F.3d 381, 398 (2d Cir. 2005). Second, witnesses may not offer legal conclusions. United States v. Bilzerian, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991). Third, witnesses may not interpret evidence that jurors can evaluate unaided. Hynes v. Coughlin, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996). The testimony transgressed all three prohibitions.

The errors were not harmless. Reversal is required unless “it is highly probable that the error did not affect the outcome.” United States v. Grinage, 390 F.3d 746, 751 (2d Cir. 2004) (quoting Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946)). The testimony spoke directly to the two dispositive issues—officer credibility and probable cause—rendering the error prejudicial.

II. The Jury Instructions Were Erroneous.

A. Obstructing Governmental Administration.

Under New York law, a person commits obstructing governmental administration only when the officer is “engaged in a lawful official function.” People v. Greene, 5 N.Y.3d 171, 174, 833 N.E.2d 1254, 1256 (2005). The district court refused to instruct the jury that the underlying arrest must be lawful. This omission permitted the jury to find probable cause for Higgenbottom’s arrest even if Ramos and Rivera lacked probable cause to arrest Cameron. The instruction therefore misstated governing state law.

B. Punitive Damages.

A jury may award punitive damages in a § 1983 action when the defendant’s conduct is “motivated by evil motive or intent” or involves “reckless or callous indifference.” Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). Appellants’ evidence—that the officers fabricated charges after Cameron requested an incident report—permitted an inference of malice. The district court’s refusal to give a punitive-damages instruction therefore deprived the jury of the correct legal standard.

Because the evidentiary errors require a new trial and the instructional errors are likely to recur, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
