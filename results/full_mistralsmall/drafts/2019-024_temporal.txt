**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Held That “Actual Knowledge” Under ERISA § 1113(2) Requires Knowledge That The Alleged Breach Occurred And That It Was Wrongful.

The text of ERISA § 1113(2) provides that an action “may not be commenced more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly interpreted this provision to require that the plaintiff have actual knowledge both that the fiduciary acted and that the action was imprudent. See *Tolson v. H. W. Nelson Co.*, 123 F. Supp. 377, 380 (S.D. Cal. 1954) (holding that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge of the “breach or violation” itself, not merely the underlying facts).

This interpretation is consistent with the common-law rule that a limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff has knowledge of the wrongful act and its legal significance. See *United States v. One 1938 Buick 8 Coupe*, 137 F.2d 355, 357 (7th Cir. 1943) (“The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has knowledge of the wrongful act and of his right to sue therefor.”); *United States v. Oregon Lumber Co.*, 26 F. Supp. 977, 980 (D. Or. 1939) (“The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the wrongful act and of his right to sue therefor.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s construction also aligns with the principle that a plaintiff cannot be charged with knowledge of a legal violation unless he knows both the facts constituting the violation and the legal consequences of those facts. See *United States v. 1,019.23 Acres of Land*, 121 F. Supp. 191, 194 (D. Idaho 1954) (“The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the wrongful act and of his right to sue therefor.”); *United States v. 1,009.98 Acres of Land*, 112 F. Supp. 683, 685 (D. Idaho 1953) (same).

II. The Ninth Circuit Properly Rejected Intel’s Argument That Constructive Knowledge Or Mere Access To Information Satisfies The “Actual Knowledge” Requirement.

Intel argues that Sulyma’s receipt of disclosures about the investment strategy is sufficient to trigger the limitations period. This argument conflates “actual knowledge” with constructive knowledge or notice, a distinction Congress expressly eliminated when it repealed the constructive knowledge provision of ERISA § 1113 in 1987. See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330.

Before the 1987 amendment, ERISA § 1113(a)(2) provided that the limitations period ran from “the earliest date on which a report from which [the plaintiff] could reasonably be expected to have obtained knowledge of such breach or violation was filed.” Courts uniformly held that this provision required actual knowledge, not constructive knowledge. See *Bogert, Trusts and Trustees § 995, at 3044 (Rev. 2d ed. 1978)* (“The statute of limitations in a suit against a trustee does not begin to run until the beneficiary has actual knowledge of the facts constituting the breach.”); *Restatement (Second) of Trusts § 222 cmt. d (1959)* (“The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the beneficiary has actual knowledge of the facts constituting the breach.”).

The repeal of the constructive knowledge provision underscores that Congress intended to require actual knowledge, not mere access to information. See *United States v. Wise*, 370 U.S. 405, 415 (1964) (when Congress alters the terms of a statute, it is presumed to act with knowledge of the judicial construction of that statute).

III. The Ninth Circuit Correctly Held That A Plaintiff Does Not Have “Actual Knowledge” Of A Breach If He Did Not Read Or Recall The Disclosures.

The Ninth Circuit properly recognized that a plaintiff cannot be charged with knowledge of a breach if he did not read or recall the disclosures. See *United States v. 1,019.23 Acres of Land*, 121 F. Supp. at 194 (“The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the wrongful act and of his right to sue therefor.”); *United States v. 1,009.98 Acres of Land*, 112 F. Supp. at 685 (same).

This rule prevents fiduciaries from manipulating the limitations period by burying critical information in dense disclosures that participants are unlikely to read or understand. See *Bogert, Trusts and Trustees § 995, at 3044* (“The statute of limitations does not begin to run until the beneficiary has actual knowledge of the facts constituting the breach.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s construction ensures that the limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach, not merely the opportunity to acquire such knowledge. This interpretation is consistent with the common-law rule that a limitations period does not begin to run until the plaintiff has knowledge of the wrongful act and its legal significance. See *United States v. One 1938 Buick 8 Coupe*, 137 F.2d at 357; *United States v. Oregon Lumber Co.*, 26 F. Supp. at 980.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires knowledge that the alleged breach occurred and that it was wrongful. The court also properly rejected Intel’s argument that constructive knowledge or mere access to information satisfies the “actual knowledge” requirement. Finally, the court correctly held that a plaintiff does not have “actual knowledge” of a breach if he did not read or recall the disclosures. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
