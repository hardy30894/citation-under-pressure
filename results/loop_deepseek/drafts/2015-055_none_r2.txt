**ARGUMENT**

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review because the clause was void *ab initio* and because due process forbids imposing punishment under a statute that fails to give fair warning of the conduct it proscribes.

**I. A statute that is void for vagueness is void from its inception, and any sentence imposed under it is invalid.**

A statute that violates the Due Process Clause because it is impermissibly vague is void *ab initio*. This Court has long held that “an unconstitutional act is not a law; it confers no rights; it imposes no duties; it affords no protection; it creates no office; it is, in legal contemplation, as inoperative as though it had never been passed.” *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371, 376–77 (1879). Because the residual clause was void from the moment of its enactment, any sentence enhanced under that clause is invalid. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (holding that a law repugnant to the Constitution “is void” and that courts “must” disregard it). Welch’s prior conviction for Florida strong-arm robbery qualified as a violent felony only under the residual clause; the elements clause and the enumerated offenses do not apply. Accordingly, his enhanced sentence cannot stand.

**II. The void-for-vagueness doctrine requires fair notice, and a statute that fails to provide such notice cannot constitutionally be applied.**

The void-for-vagueness doctrine is rooted in the Due Process Clause and demands that laws “give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited.” *Connally v. General Construction Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926). A statute that “fails to give a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice that his contemplated conduct is forbidden” is unconstitutional. *Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). This requirement of fair notice “is a protection of the individual against the arbitrary and capricious exercise of the police power.” *Champlin Refining Co. v. Corporation Commission*, 286 U.S. 210, 243 (1932). The residual clause left courts to assess the “hypothetical risk” posed by an abstract generic version of a crime, giving no meaningful guidance. *See United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1876) (voiding a statute that “leaves the citizen to guess at his peril” as to whether his conduct is prohibited). Because the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague, it could not provide the fair notice required by the Fifth Amendment. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (a criminal statute must give “fair warning” of the conduct it punishes). Welch lacked any constitutionally adequate notice that his robbery conviction could trigger a 15-year mandatory minimum.

**III. A decision that a criminal statute is void for vagueness is a substantive ruling that must apply retroactively to correct sentences imposed under that void law.**

This Court has long recognized that a judicial decision that enlarges the scope of a criminal statute cannot be applied retroactively because it violates fair notice. *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 362–63 (1964) (holding that an unexpected judicial enlargement of a statute violates due process when applied retroactively). The principle underlying *Bouie* is that a defendant must have “fair warning” of the law’s commands. *Id.* at 353. Conversely, a decision that a statute is void for vagueness is a substantive ruling that the law never had legal effect. *See Siebold*, 100 U.S. at 376–77. Such a ruling necessarily applies retroactively because the statute was void from the start—it never provided the requisite notice. *See also United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625 (1954) (declining to apply a vagueness ruling retroactively only because the statute survived the challenge, not because a void statute could ever be applied). A substantive rule that invalidates a punishment applies to all cases, including those on collateral review. *Bouie* itself applied retroactively to correct a sentence imposed under an unconstitutionally enlarged statute. The same logic compels retroactive application of *Johnson*. Welch’s 15-year sentence depended on a clause that was void for vagueness at the time of his sentencing; he is therefore serving an illegal sentence.

**IV. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision must be reversed because Welch’s sentence rests on an unconstitutional provision.**

The court of appeals held that Florida strong-arm robbery qualifies as a violent felony under the residual clause, applying a standard that this Court has now declared unconstitutional. Because the residual clause was void *ab initio*, that holding cannot be sustained. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221. Welch is entitled to resentencing without the residual clause enhancement.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing consistent with *Johnson*.
