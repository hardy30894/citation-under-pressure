# ARGUMENT

## I. A Claim for Nominal Damages Does Not, by Itself, Present the "Definite and Concrete" Controversy That Article III Demands.

The judicial power of the federal courts extends only to genuine "cases" and "controversies," and this Court has consistently refused to adjudicate disputes that have lost their live, adversarial character. *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346 (1911). As this Court explained in *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227 (1937), "[t]he controversy must be definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests." *Id.* at 240-41. This Court further distinguished a justiciable dispute from an abstract or academic disagreement, emphasizing that a court's role is to resolve controversies admitting of specific relief rather than to render advice on hypothetical states of fact. *Id.* Once Georgia Gwinnett College rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged provision of the Student Code of Conduct, no live dispute remained over the validity of those provisions; petitioners no longer face, and cannot show any reasonable prospect of again facing, enforcement of rules that no longer exist. A bare demand for a nominal sum does not resurrect that extinguished controversy, because it identifies no injury, no continuing relationship between the parties, and no specific relief of the kind *Aetna* requires.

This limitation on judicial power is not a modern innovation. As early as *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), this Court held that "[t]he duty of this court, as of every other judicial tribunal, is limited to determining rights of persons or of property, which are actually controverted in the particular case before it." *Id.* at 653. Where, as here, the policy that gave rise to the dispute has been repealed and superseded, and where petitioners identify no actual, continuing injury flowing from that repealed policy, there is no "right actually controverted" left for a court to determine.

## II. This Court Has Long Held That Claims Too Insubstantial to Reflect a Genuine Stake in the Outcome Will Not Preserve Jurisdiction Once the Underlying Dispute Is Resolved.

In *California v. San Pablo & Tulare Railroad Co.*, 149 U.S. 308 (1893), the State sought to recover a small sum of back taxes from the railroad; while the suit was pending, the legislature released the claim entirely. This Court held the case moot notwithstanding the technically live claim for the residual sum, because the dispute no longer presented any matter of genuine consequence between the parties. *Id.* at 314. The same principle controls here: once GGC rescinded the Prior Policies, the only thing petitioners retained was a request for a token sum untethered to any pleaded actual injury—precisely the kind of residual, insubstantial claim that *San Pablo* held insufficient to sustain a federal court's continuing jurisdiction.

*United States v. Alaska Steamship Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920), confirms the point from the other direction: when the occurrence of subsequent events destroys the practical significance of the dispute, this Court has dismissed the case as moot even though some technical vestige of the original controversy survived on paper. *Id.* at 116. The repeal of the Prior Policies here is precisely such an intervening event; it eliminated any occasion for equitable relief and left only a nominal damages request incapable of restoring a live controversy over the constitutionality of provisions no longer on the books.

## III. The Voluntary Revision of the Challenged Policy Mooted This Case, and Petitioners Have Identified No Reasonable Expectation That the Same Controversy Will Recur.

Where a defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged conduct, and the plaintiff has no personal stake tied to concrete, continuing injury, the controversy is moot absent a reasonable expectation that the dispute will recur. *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969) (dismissing challenge to anonymous-handbill statute as moot where the legislator who had prompted the suit no longer held office and there was no reasonable expectation the same controversy would recur). The Eleventh Circuit correctly held—and petitioners do not dispute—that GGC's revision of the Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct mooted the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief. That same voluntary, complete abandonment of the challenged provisions likewise deprives the residual nominal-damages request of any definite and concrete controversy to attach to. *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 240.

*St. Pierre v. United States*, 319 U.S. 41 (1943), illustrates why a technical remnant of a claim cannot rescue an otherwise moot case: this Court dismissed a contempt appeal as moot once the sentence had been fully served, notwithstanding lingering technical consequences, because no concrete stake in the outcome remained. By contrast, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301 (1964), this Court found no mootness because a genuine, substantial monetary dispute over costs survived the completion of the enjoined construction project—an interest bearing directly on a real economic stake between the parties. *Id.* at 306 & n.3. Nominal damages, unmoored from any pleaded actual injury, present no comparable stake; they are the mirror image of the substantial cost dispute preserved in *Liner*, not its equivalent.

## Conclusion

Because the Prior Policies no longer exist, because petitioners allege no actual injury, and because a request for a token sum cannot substitute for the definite and concrete controversy Article III requires, *Aetna*, 300 U.S. at 240, the Eleventh Circuit correctly held this case moot.
