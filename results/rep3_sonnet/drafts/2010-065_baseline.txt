# ARGUMENT

## PETITIONER LACKS STANDING TO RAISE A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO HER FEDERAL CONVICTION

### A. This Court's Precedent Establishes That Private Parties Lack Standing to Assert Tenth Amendment Claims

The Tenth Amendment provides that powers not delegated to the federal government "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. By its text and structure, this Amendment protects the sovereign interests of States as governmental entities—not the individual liberty interests of private citizens. This Court's decision in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), forecloses Petitioner's claim.

In *Tennessee Electric*, private utility companies challenged the TVA's operations as exceeding federal power in violation of the Tenth Amendment. This Court rejected the claim on standing grounds, holding unambiguously that private parties, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. That holding was not incidental to the Court's analysis—it was a threshold determination controlling the disposition of the case, and it remains binding precedent.

The rationale underlying *Tennessee Electric* reflects a fundamental structural principle: because the Tenth Amendment's reservation of power runs to "the States," only the States themselves possess the requisite interest to invoke that reservation as a defense against federal overreach. This is consistent with the settled understanding that "[u]nder our federal system[,] the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). The corollary principle is that a State's sovereign prerogatives are vindicated by the State, not by private litigants purporting to assert them.

### B. The Weight of Circuit Authority Confirms That Private Parties Cannot Assert Tenth Amendment Claims

Consistent with *Tennessee Electric*, the overwhelming majority of circuits to consider this question have held that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment challenges. *See United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Services Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004).

The First Circuit's reasoning in *Medeiros* is particularly instructive. That court recognized that the Tenth Amendment protects state sovereignty as an institutional matter, and that if a State chooses not to object to federal legislation touching on its reserved powers, individual citizens have no independent right to raise that objection on the State's behalf. As the court explained, "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." 431 F.3d at 35. This is not a denial of remedy; it is a recognition that the remedy for Tenth Amendment concerns lies through the political process by which States assert or waive their own sovereign interests.

Although the Seventh and Eleventh Circuits have permitted private-party standing in some contexts, *see Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703–04 (7th Cir. 1999); *Atlanta Gas Light Co. v. U.S. Department of Energy*, 666 F.2d 1359, 1368 n.16 (11th Cir. 1982), even the Eleventh Circuit has since retreated from that position, denying standing where private parties assert only "an 'undifferentiated, generalized grievance.'" *Dillard v. Chilton County Commission*, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007). The minority position, moreover, did not engage with the controlling force of *Tennessee Electric*'s holding.

### C. Petitioner's Position Would Convert Every Federal Criminal Defendant Into a Federalism Litigant

Petitioner asks this Court to hold that any individual prosecuted under a federal criminal statute may challenge that statute as exceeding Congress's enumerated powers, regardless of whether the State whose "sovereignty" is allegedly infringed has raised any objection. This approach conflates two distinct constitutional inquiries: whether Congress possessed authority to enact a statute, and who may raise that objection in litigation.

This Court has consistently required that constitutional claims be raised by parties whose own rights are implicated. *See Warth v. Seldin*, 422 U.S. 490, 499 (1975) ("[T]he plaintiff generally must assert his own legal rights and interests, and cannot rest his claim to relief on the legal rights or interests of third parties."). The Tenth Amendment, unlike the individual-rights provisions of the Bill of Rights, protects a structural interest belonging to sovereign States. Permitting any criminal defendant to assert this interest—effectively standing in the shoes of the State—would eliminate the distinction between structural constitutional limitations and individual constitutional rights that this Court has long maintained. *See generally New York v. United States*, 505 U.S. 144 (1992) (analyzing Tenth Amendment claims brought by the State itself).

Nothing in this framework leaves federalism concerns unprotected. States retain full authority to challenge federal statutes they believe exceed Congress's enumerated powers, as they have done in cases such as *New York* and *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898 (1997). The absence of a private cause of action for individual defendants does not signal judicial abdication of federalism principles; it reflects the considered judgment, rooted in *Tennessee Electric* and confirmed by the weight of circuit authority, that the Tenth Amendment's institutional protections belong to institutions.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
