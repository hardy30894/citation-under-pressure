## ARGUMENT

### I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date

The Immigration and Nationality Act’s reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute existed. This Court has long held that “statutes are to be construed as having only a prospective operation unless a contrary intention is clearly expressed.” *Shwab v. Doyle*, 258 U.S. 529, 534 (1922). Nothing in Section 1231(a)(5)’s text manifests such a clear intent to reach prior reentries. The statute speaks in the present tense—it requires that the Attorney General find “that an alien has reentered”—and does not specify that it applies to reentries completed before its enactment. This ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the alien, for “deportation is a drastic measure,” and “statutes imposing it must be narrowly construed.” *Mahler v. Eby*, 264 U.S. 32, 40 (1924). See also *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 187 (1958) (deportation statutes “must be construed in favor of the alien when doubts remain”). Congress easily could have written “who reentered at any time” had it intended retroactive application. It did not. The statute therefore applies only to reentries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Applying the Reinstatement Statute Retroactively Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, such application would attach new legal consequences to past conduct, violating the principle that a statute “is not to be given retroactive effect unless that purpose is clearly expressed.” *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). See also *Hassett v. Welch*, 303 U.S. 303, 314 (1938) (presumption against retroactive application). Here, no clear expression exists.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the law permitted him to later apply for adjustment of status if he married a United States citizen. See *Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath*, 339 U.S. 33, 41 (1950) (recognizing that “the law accords the alien a full and fair hearing” and opportunities for relief). The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring any form of relief. This new bar alters the legal consequences of a reentry that was completed long before the statute’s enactment. Such a change is impermissibly retroactive because it “impairs rights a party possessed when he acted.” *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964) (quoting *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913)). Petitioner’s reentry in 1982 carried with it the prospect of eventual adjustment. The 1997 statute stripped that prospect away.

This Court has consistently held that deportation statutes must not be applied retroactively to attach new penalties to past conduct. In *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 300 (1944), the Court refused to apply a statute retroactively, stating that the law “must be construed to be prospective only unless a contrary intent is clearly expressed.” The same principle governs here. The reinstatement statute imposes a new, harsh consequence—foreclosing all relief—on petitioner’s pre-1997 reentry. As *Claridge v. Evans*, 137 F.2d 347, 349 (7th Cir. 1943), teaches, “it is a fundamental rule that statutes are to be construed as prospective only unless a contrary intention is clearly expressed.”

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner lacked a “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status. An alien who reenters illegally does not forfeit all future claims to relief. *See Leng May Ma*, 357 U.S. at 187 (the “grave nature of deportation” requires that “the statute be construed in favor of the alien”). The expectation of applying for adjustment under then-existing law was a legitimate right that the government could not retroactively extinguish without clear congressional command. *See Shwab*, 258 U.S. at 534. No such command appears in Section 1231(a)(5).

Retroactive application also offends due process by upsetting settled reliance interests. When petitioner returned in 1982, the legal landscape permitted him to later seek adjustment if he married a citizen. The 1997 statute retroactively destroyed that avenue, making his earlier conduct the basis for permanent exclusion from relief. This is precisely the kind of retrospective change the presumption against retroactivity is designed to prevent. *St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. at 3.

For these reasons, Section 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. Even if it did, applying it retroactively would be impermissible. The petition for review should be granted.
