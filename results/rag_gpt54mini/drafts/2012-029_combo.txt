The federal courts do **not** have exclusive jurisdiction merely because a case may require resolving a patent issue. Section 1338(a) gives federal courts exclusive jurisdiction only over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws, and the Supreme Court has long held that not every case that mentions or depends on patent doctrine falls within that grant. See 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988); Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

In Christianson, the Court held that a case arises under patent law only if the well-pleaded complaint shows either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that “the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808-09. That standard is demanding. The Court cautioned that jurisdiction cannot rest on hypothetical or defensive patent questions, and the inquiry must be driven by what appears on the face of the complaint. Id. at 809. Likewise, in Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, the Court rejected a broad construction of federal-question jurisdiction and explained that the Court must use “a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside.” 299 U.S. 109, 117-18 (1936).

That principle controls here. A state-law malpractice claim does not become exclusively federal simply because the plaintiff must prove that prior patent litigation would have been won but for counsel’s negligence. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated patent issues as incidental when they arise in a different cause of action. In Marsh v. Nichols, Shepard & Co., 140 U.S. 344 (1891), the Court refused to treat a dispute as one within federal patent jurisdiction where “the subject matter of this suit differed” from the earlier patent suit. Id. at 346. The same is true here: the subject matter of this suit is attorney negligence, not patent infringement or patent validity.

The older patent cases also show that federal jurisdiction has never been boundless. In St. Paul Plough Works v. Starling, the Court construed the patent jurisdictional statute as applying to a “case touching patent rights,” 127 U.S. 376 (1888), but that language described the then-existing appellate statute, not a rule that every state-law case involving patent analysis belongs exclusively in federal court. Id. at 381. And in Hoover Co. v. Coe, the Court emphasized that jurisdiction depends on the statute’s text, history, and judicial construction, concluding only that the particular suit before it fell within the patent statute. 325 U.S. 79 (1945). Hoover does not stand for a sweeping rule of exclusive federal jurisdiction over all patent-adjacent disputes. See id. at 84-85.

Nor do the public-use and on-sale cases transform this malpractice action into a federal patent suit. Andrews v. Hovey, 123 U.S. 267 (1887), and Andrews v. Hovey, 124 U.S. 694 (1888), addressed patent validity defenses in patent litigation. Root v. Third Avenue Railroad, 146 U.S. 210 (1892), similarly addressed whether a particular use was “substantially for experiment,” id. at 224, as a defense to patent validity. Those decisions govern the merits of patent disputes; they do not dictate forum jurisdiction over a state negligence claim.

The same is true of Ex Parte Frasch, 192 U.S. 566 (1904), and Kener v. La Grange Mills, 231 U.S. 215 (1913). Those cases concerned federal adjudication of rights created by federal law. See Ex Parte Frasch, 192 U.S. at 576-77; Kener, 231 U.S. at 218-19. But this malpractice action is created by state law. The federal patent issue is only one element in a state claim, and Christianson does not permit exclusive jurisdiction whenever a patent issue must be resolved along the way. 486 U.S. at 808-09.

Finally, the Court has long warned against expanding federal jurisdiction merely because a federal issue is embedded in a larger state dispute. Shulthis, 225 U.S. at 569; Gully, 299 U.S. at 117-18. That caution is especially apt here, where Texas malpractice law supplies the cause of action, the duty standard, the injury theory, and the remedy. See Christianson, 486 U.S. at 808-09. At most, federal patent law supplies one necessary analytical step in the “case within a case.” That is not enough to displace the state courts.

The judgment should be reversed.
