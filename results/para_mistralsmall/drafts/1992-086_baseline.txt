**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE SCHOOL DISTRICT’S FACILITIES WERE A “LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM” NOT OPEN TO RELIGIOUS SPEECH.**

The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the school facilities were a “limited public forum” not opened to religious speech by policy or practice is contrary to this Court’s decisions in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), and *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990). In *Widmar*, this Court held that a state university’s policy of accommodating student meetings created a forum generally open to student groups, and thus the university could not exclude religious speech. *Id.* at 267, 273. Similarly, in *Mergens*, the Court found that a high school’s allowance of noncurriculum-related student groups created a limited open forum, and thus the school could not deny access to a Christian club based on the content of its speech. *Id.* at 247.

Here, the District permitted a wide range of community groups to use its facilities, including secular organizations such as the Southern Harmonizers and Jerry Huck’s lecture series on parapsychology. *See* App. 45-47. As in *Widmar* and *Mergens*, the District’s policy of accommodating community use created a forum generally open to the public, and thus the exclusion of religious speech based on its content violates the First Amendment. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 267; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 247.

**II. THE DISTRICT’S EXCLUSION OF RELIGIOUS SPEECH VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT’S FREE SPEECH CLAUSE.**

The District’s policy of excluding religious speech while permitting secular speech violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause. This Court has held that “[a] regulation of speech that is content-based on its face is subject to strict scrutiny.” *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 382 (1992). The District’s policy is content-based because it excludes speech based on its religious viewpoint. *See Rosenberger v. University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 828-29 (1995) (holding that a university’s denial of funding to a religious student publication violated the Free Speech Clause because it discriminated based on viewpoint).

The District’s exclusion of religious speech cannot survive strict scrutiny. The District has not shown that its policy is necessary to serve a compelling state interest, nor is it narrowly tailored to achieve that interest. *See Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). The District’s reliance on *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602 (1971), to justify its exclusion is misplaced, as *Lemon* does not permit the suppression of speech based on its religious content. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 839-40.

**III. THE DISTRICT’S EXCLUSION OF RELIGIOUS SPEECH DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.**

The District argues that its exclusion of religious speech is justified by the Establishment Clause. However, this Court has held that “[t]he Free Speech Clause itself has the capacity to protect religious speech from viewpoint discrimination.” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 113 (2001). The District’s policy of excluding religious speech while permitting secular speech is not neutral with respect to religion, and thus it does not pass constitutional muster. *See id.* at 114.

Moreover, the District’s policy is not justified by the Establishment Clause because it does not have the “primary effect” of advancing or inhibiting religion. *See Lemon*, 403 U.S. at 612. The District’s exclusion of religious speech is not necessary to avoid the appearance of state endorsement of religion, as the District permits a wide range of secular speech in its facilities. *See Good News Club*, 533 U.S. at 114.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Second Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
