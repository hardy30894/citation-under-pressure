**Argument**

The school district violated the First Amendment when it denied Lamb’s Chapel access to school premises because its films were religiously oriented. The District had opened its facilities for after-hours community use, and once a government creates a forum for speech, it may not exclude expression because officials dislike its religious viewpoint. *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946); *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939); *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939).

This Court has long held that when government property is made available for expressive activity, the First Amendment forbids discrimination among speakers or messages absent a constitutionally sufficient justification. In *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501 (1946), the Court treated private-property-like control over a town’s streets as subject to First Amendment limits because speech cannot be suppressed merely by labeling a place private. In *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), the Court recognized that streets and parks are held open for assembly and discussion. And in *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), the Court rejected municipal efforts to bar leafleting in public spaces simply to keep order or avoid inconvenience. These decisions establish the foundational rule that once a place is available for public expression, censorship based on content is presumptively invalid.

The District’s exclusion here was plainly content based. Lamb’s Chapel sought to show films on family values and child rearing, but the District denied access because the films were “religious” or “church related.” A rule that allows the subject matter “family values” but disallows the same discussion when presented from a religious perspective is discrimination against speech because of its message. The First Amendment does not permit government to accept discussion of a topic from secular voices while excluding the same topic from a religious one. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1 (1949); *Feiner v. New York*, 340 U.S. 315 (1951); *Staub v. City of Baxley*, 355 U.S. 313 (1958).

The Court’s public-forum decisions confirm that the State may not open a forum to the public and then draw exclusions that suppress particular ideas. In *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516 (1945), the Court invalidated restrictions that burdened advocacy because the First Amendment protects not only politics but the means by which citizens communicate beliefs. In *Winters v. New York*, 333 U.S. 507 (1948), the Court held that vague censorship rules chill expression and cannot stand where they invite arbitrary suppression. In *Joseph Burstyn, Inc. v. Wilson*, 343 U.S. 495 (1952), the Court rejected official censorship of religiously charged film expression, recognizing that speech does not lose protection because it is “religious.” And in *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234 (1957), the Court warned that the freedom of speech protects the “vital process” of inquiry and debate. The District’s refusal to allow a religious perspective on family issues is inconsistent with those principles.

The District cannot justify its action by invoking its authority to regulate school property. Even where government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner rules, it may not favor secular ideas over religious ones. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), upheld neutral regulation of parade traffic, not viewpoint suppression. Likewise, *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290 (1951), struck down a permit system that vested officials with censorship discretion over religious speech in a public setting. And *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), invalidated denial of access to public park facilities where the government administered access in an arbitrary and discriminatory fashion. Those cases show that administrative control over public property cannot be used as a mask for exclusion of disfavored speech.

The District’s action also cannot survive the rule that government may not burden speech because it is offensive to some listeners. *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229 (1963), protected peaceful protest despite official hostility; *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536 (1965), likewise rejected suppression of expression based on anticipated offense or disruption. A religious film series about family life poses no greater danger to order than other permitted community uses. The District’s refusal therefore rests not on neutral administration but on the content and viewpoint of the message.

Finally, the Establishment Clause does not justify exclusion. This Court has made clear that the First Amendment does not require government to suppress religious expression from a forum open to others. *Burstyn*, 343 U.S. 495; *Sweezy*, 354 U.S. 234. To the contrary, equal access is the neutral course: permitting Lamb’s Chapel to speak on the same terms as other community groups avoids both discrimination against religion and any governmental sponsorship of it. The District’s policy instead singles out religious expression for disfavored treatment, the very harm the Free Speech Clause forbids.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
