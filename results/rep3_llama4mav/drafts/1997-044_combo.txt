## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are intended to provide a fresh start for honest debtors, but they are not a haven for those who commit fraud. The question presented here is whether individuals who commit fraud can discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded, under the Bankruptcy Code. The answer is no.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." This provision is rooted in the principle that a debtor who obtains property by fraud should not be allowed to discharge the debt arising from that fraud. As the Court held in Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877), "in all cases where fraudulent intent is a necessary element, it is a fraud in fact, and includes cases of false representations knowingly made with a view to deceive."

To prevail under § 523(a)(2)(A), a creditor must show that the debtor obtained money or property through a material misrepresentation, with knowledge of its falsity, and with intent to deceive. See Strang v. Bradner, 114 U.S. 555, 561 (1885) (holding that a debtor's false representation can render a debt non-dischargeable). The Court has long recognized that a debtor's silence or failure to disclose material information can constitute a misrepresentation. See Stewart v. Emerson, 52 N.H. 301, 314 (1872) (holding that a debtor's failure to disclose material information can be considered a fraudulent concealment).

In this case, the debtor, Edward S. Cohen, committed actual fraud by charging his tenants excessive rents in violation of the local rent control ordinance. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen made an implicit representation regarding the rent he charged, and that his silence coupled with the rental amount fixed constituted a representation that he was charging lawful rent. See In re Cohen, 185 B.R. 171, 172 (Bankr.D.N.J.1994). This finding is supported by the principle that a debtor's representation can be implied from their conduct. See Clements v. Moore, 73 U.S. 299, 312 (1867) (holding that a debtor's actions can imply a representation).

The damages awarded to the tenants, including treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, are also non-dischargeable. The Court has long recognized that damages arising from a debtor's fraudulent conduct are not dischargeable. See Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915) (holding that a debt arising from a debtor's fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable). The treble damages awarded in this case are a direct result of Cohen's fraudulent conduct and are therefore non-dischargeable.

Furthermore, the principle that a debtor who commits fraud should not be allowed to discharge their debts is supported by the Court's decision in Brown v. Bullock, 194 F. Supp. 207, 223 (S.D.N.Y. 1961), which held that a debtor's liability for damages arising from their fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable. Similarly, in Reisenberg v. Hankinson, 208 F. Supp. 701, 703 (E.D. Pa. 1962), the court held that a debt arising from a debtor's fraudulent conduct is not dischargeable.

The decision in Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177, 182 (1900), also supports the conclusion that a debtor who commits fraud should not be allowed to discharge their debts. In that case, the Court held that a debtor's fraudulent conduct can render a debt non-dischargeable. Additionally, the Court's decision in Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904), established that a debtor's intent to deceive can be inferred from their conduct.

In conclusion, the Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions do not permit individuals who commit fraud to discharge their debts, including damages awarded to those they defrauded. The Court's precedents, including Neal v. Clark, Strang v. Bradner, and Gleason v. Thaw, support this conclusion. Therefore, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
