**ARGUMENT**

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that only “public lands” within conservation system units are subject to the Act’s special rules, while “lands, waters, and interests therein” conveyed to the State, Native Corporations, or other nonfederal owners “shall be deemed to be excluded from such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The hovercraft prohibition, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is not a rule that “applies solely to public lands.” It governs the operation of hovercraft “within the boundaries of federally owned lands and waters administered by the National Park Service,” without regard to ownership of the submerged land beneath navigable waters. Because the regulation therefore operates on the geographic unit as a whole, it falls outside the limitation of § 103(c).

This reading follows from the statute’s text. Congress defined “public lands” to mean lands, waters, and interests therein to which the United States holds title. 16 U.S.C. § 3102(1)–(3). It simultaneously preserved the Service’s pre-existing authority to regulate activities on any lands within park boundaries. See 16 U.S.C. § 1a-2(h) (authorizing regulations “necessary or proper for the use and management of those areas”); 16 U.S.C. § 3207 (ANILCA does not “supersede[] … existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to … exercise … regulatory functions”). The Service’s hovercraft rule is an exercise of that retained authority.

Sturgeon’s contrary construction would require the Service to maintain two separate regulatory regimes inside a single park unit—an outcome the statute does not command. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the plain language of § 103(c) forecloses that result. Sturgeon v. Masica, 768 F.3d 1066, 1077–78 (9th Cir. 2014). The judgment should be affirmed.
