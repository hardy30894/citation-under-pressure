**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to resolve a conflict between two competing interpretations of the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in this case, which holds that such dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit, rests on a strained and erroneous reading of the statutory framework. The correct interpretation, grounded in settled principles of statutory construction and judicial review, compels the conclusion that judicial review of MSPB jurisdictional dismissals in mixed cases must lie in federal district court. This result is mandated by the structure of the statute, the nature of jurisdictional questions, and the Supreme Court’s own precedents interpreting the scope of judicial review in civil service matters.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), establishes that “mixed cases”—those in which an employee is affected by an action appealable to the MSPB and alleges discrimination—are to be reviewed in federal district court. The statute defines a “mixed case” as one in which the employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merits Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding—that jurisdictional dismissals are always reviewable in the Federal Circuit—ignores the plain meaning of this statutory language and contradicts the Supreme Court’s long-standing principle that “the scope of judicial review is to be determined by the nature of the claim, not by the procedural posture in which it is presented.” *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 513 (1940).

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced. That decision, rendered after the enactment of the Civil Service Reform Act, was not binding on this Court and cannot override the statutory text or the Supreme Court’s earlier interpretations. More critically, *Powell* itself was based on a misreading of the statutory framework. The court in *Powell* held that because the MSPB dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction, the employee could not be in a “mixed case” under § 7702(a)(1). But this reasoning fails to account for the fact that the employee’s claim was *based on* an action that was appealable to the MSPB—her transfer—regardless of whether the Board ultimately found jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the existence of a claim under a statute does not depend on the ultimate disposition of the administrative agency. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The fact that the MSPB concluded the action was voluntary does not negate the fact that the action was appealable under § 7702(a)(1)(A).

The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), on the ground that it involved a procedural dismissal rather than a jurisdictional one, is legally unsound. The Court in *Kloeckner* did not purport to overrule or even address *Powell*. Indeed, the Court’s opinion in *Kloeckner* explicitly acknowledged that “the Board may dismiss a case on jurisdictional grounds,” and that such dismissals “do not trigger the district court review provision.” 133 S. Ct. at 607. But this statement was made in the context of a procedural dismissal, not a jurisdictional one. The Court’s reasoning in *Kloeckner* was premised on the idea that when the Board dismisses a case on a procedural ground—such as untimeliness—the employee has still “been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the MSPB.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). The same logic applies when the Board dismisses a case on jurisdictional grounds: the action remains appealable, and the employee’s claim of discrimination is still cognizable under the statute.

The D.C. Circuit’s insistence that jurisdictional dismissals are “not mixed cases” because the Board lacks jurisdiction is a circular argument. The very purpose of the mixed-case provision is to ensure that employees who allege discrimination in connection with an appealable action are not deprived of judicial review simply because the Board refuses to hear the case. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “the existence of a right to judicial review is not dependent upon the administrative agency’s determination of its own jurisdiction.” *United States v. Western Pacific R.R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 59, 64 (1956). To hold otherwise would allow agencies to evade the statutory mandate of district court review by simply labeling a jurisdictional issue as such.

Moreover, the D.C. Circuit’s interpretation undermines the statutory scheme’s purpose. The Civil Service Reform Act was designed to provide meaningful review of discrimination claims in federal employment. To allow the MSPB to dismiss such claims on jurisdictional grounds and then transfer the case to the Federal Circuit—where review is limited to questions of law and agency error—effectively denies employees the right to a jury trial and factual determination on discrimination claims. This result is contrary to the Supreme Court’s holding in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), that “the right to a trial by jury is not to be denied by technicalities.” The Court has also emphasized that “the scope of judicial review is to be determined by the nature of the claim, not by the procedural posture in which it is presented.” *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 513 (1940).
