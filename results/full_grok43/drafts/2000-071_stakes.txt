**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club constituted permissible content discrimination in a limited public forum.**

Milford’s Community Use Policy opens school facilities to “social, civic and recreational meetings” while expressly barring “use for religious purposes.” This creates a limited public forum whose boundaries the State may define “by speaker identity or by subject matter.” *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 49 (1983). Restrictions “need only be reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum and . . . viewpoint neutral.” *Id.* at 49.

The Club’s proposed meetings consisted of prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Christ as personal Savior. These activities are not merely a “religious perspective” on morals; they are “religious instruction and prayer.” *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 265 n.2 (1981). Milford therefore applied a content-based exclusion that tracked the forum’s stated purpose. *See Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (distinguishing content from viewpoint discrimination).

The Club’s reliance on *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), is misplaced. There the proposed use was a film series discussing child-rearing from a religious viewpoint; here the meetings themselves constitute worship and proselytization. The Second Circuit correctly found that the Club’s program “fall[s] outside the bounds of pure ‘moral and character development.’” 202 F.3d at 510.

**II. Any viewpoint restriction was justified by the Establishment Clause.**

Even if the exclusion implicated viewpoint, Milford’s interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation supplies a compelling justification. *See Capitol Square Review & Advisory Bd. v. Pinette*, 515 U.S. 753, 761-62 (1995). Permitting the Club’s meetings would have placed the school’s imprimatur on religious instruction of young children immediately after the school day, creating at least the appearance of government endorsement. *See Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992). Milford’s policy therefore satisfied strict scrutiny.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
