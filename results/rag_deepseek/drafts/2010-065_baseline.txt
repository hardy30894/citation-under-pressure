## II. Argument

### A. Bond Lacks Article III Standing to Raise a Tenth Amendment Challenge

The question of standing is jurisdictional and must be resolved before the merits. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998). To establish Article III standing, a plaintiff must demonstrate a personal injury fairly traceable to the defendant’s conduct and likely redressable by a favorable decision. Bond cannot satisfy this requirement because the Tenth Amendment does not confer individual rights; it is a structural protection for the States.

The Supreme Court squarely held in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. TVA*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), that private parties “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” That holding binds this Court. Bond is a private individual, not a State or state officer. She therefore cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge Congress’s authority to enact 18 U.S.C. § 229.

Every court of appeals to address the issue after *Tennessee Electric* has reached the same conclusion. *United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004). These decisions uniformly hold that the Tenth Amendment “is not a source of individual rights,” *Medeiros*, 431 F.3d at 35, and that only States may invoke it.

Bond’s reliance on *Campbell v. Louisiana*, 523 U.S. 392 (1998), is misplaced. *Campbell* addressed a criminal defendant’s standing to raise an equal protection challenge to discriminatory grand jury selection, not a federalism challenge under the Tenth Amendment. The equal protection guarantee is an individual right; the Tenth Amendment is not. Bond does not claim any infringement of her personal rights. Instead, she asserts that § 229 improperly intrudes on state sovereignty. That is precisely the kind of structural claim that only a State may press.

### B. Bond’s Status as a Criminal Defendant Does Not Confer Standing

Bond argues that her conviction gives her standing because she is the object of the challenged statute. But the “injury” she alleges—being prosecuted under an allegedly ultra vires federal law—is insufficient to establish standing to assert a Tenth Amendment claim. The Tenth Amendment addresses the allocation of power between the federal government and the States, not the rights of individuals. Bond’s interest in avoiding federal prosecution is an interest shared by every person; it is a “generalized grievance” that does not satisfy Article III’s injury-in-fact requirement. *See* *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 107 (declining to adjudicate generalized grievances).

Moreover, Bond does not deny that the conduct for which she was convicted—possessing and using toxic chemicals to poison another person—is prohibited by state law. Her real complaint is that federal law should not apply to her. Yet “the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of its delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States.” *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). The proper party to challenge a federalism violation is the State whose prerogatives are allegedly invaded, not the individual who happens to be prosecuted.

Bond’s claim that § 229 exceeds Congress’s treaty-implementing power is a merits argument, not a jurisdictional one. She cannot bypass the threshold standing requirement by dressing a Tenth Amendment claim in the language of individual harm.

### Conclusion

Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on the ground that § 229 violates the Tenth Amendment. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
