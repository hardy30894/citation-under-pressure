**Argument**

The District Court erred in holding, as a matter of law, that Erdman failed to satisfy the FMLA’s 1,250-hour eligibility requirement. Summary judgment is proper only when there is no genuine issue of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317 (1986). But where the record permits competing inferences, judgment must be denied. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986). Here, the undisputed record shows that Nationwide had long permitted Erdman to work extra hours and use them as comp time, and a reasonable factfinder could conclude those hours were compensable working time that should have been counted toward FMLA eligibility.

The FMLA is remedial legislation and must be construed to effectuate its protective purpose. Long before Congress enacted the FMLA, the Supreme Court recognized that wage-and-hour statutes are to be applied broadly to cover work actually required or suffered to be performed. In *Tennessee Coal, Iron & Railroad Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123*, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944), the Court held that “work” includes physical or mental exertion controlled or required by the employer and pursued primarily for the employer’s benefit. Likewise, in *Armour & Co. v. Wantock*, 323 U.S. 126, 132-33 (1944), the Court explained that an employee may be “working” even when waiting or on call if the time is spent predominantly for the employer’s benefit. And in *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 136-37 (1944), the Court rejected rigid formalism and held that the question turns on the practical realities of the employment relationship.

Those principles favor Erdman. Nationwide’s own supervisor, Sarno, repeatedly authorized Erdman’s extra hours or allowed her to bank them as comp time. Getgen later acknowledged the existence of that practice when Erdman explained her accrued vacation calculations, and she did not object until February 10, 2003. A factfinder could conclude that the extra time Erdman worked before that date was actual working time, undertaken with Nationwide’s knowledge and for its benefit. Under *Tennessee Coal*, *Armour*, and *Skidmore*, such hours are not erased simply because Nationwide later changed its policy.

Nationwide’s belated effort to disavow comp time cannot defeat eligibility on summary judgment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that employer labels do not control where the substance of the relationship shows compensable work. See *Walling v. Portland Terminal Co.*, 330 U.S. 148, 152 (1947) (statutory coverage depends on whether the worker is an “employee” in substance, not in name). Here, Erdman did not volunteer idle time; she worked additional hours at Nationwide’s direction or with its approval, and Nationwide had a practice of paying or crediting those hours. Under settled principles, the trier of fact—not the court on summary judgment—must decide whether those hours count.

The District Court also erred to the extent it treated Erdman’s FMLA request as untimely or irrelevant because Nationwide later fired her. Retaliation principles have long prohibited adverse action for asserting statutory rights. In *Mitchell v. Robert DeMario Jewelry, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 288, 292-93 (1960), the Court held that courts must not permit employers to use discharge to chill the assertion of workplace rights. Erdman submitted her FMLA paperwork on April 21, 2003, after seeking clarification that Nationwide would honor her August vacation, and a human resources employee responded that she did not “see any problems” with the FMLA request. A reasonable jury could find that Nationwide’s May 9 discharge was motivated, at least in part, by Erdman’s leave request and by her insistence on caring for her disabled daughter.

Nationwide’s asserted behavioral justification also presents classic pretext questions that should not have been resolved as a matter of law. The Supreme Court has long recognized that where motive and credibility are disputed, the issue belongs to the factfinder. See *Bailey v. Alabama*, 219 U.S. 219, 245 (1911) (courts must look to “the actual operation of the law” rather than mere form); *NLRB v. Walton Mfg. Co.*, 369 U.S. 404, 408 (1962) (credibility determinations are for the trier of fact). Erdman offered evidence that she was a reliable employee, that her overtime had long been accepted, and that the company changed course only after she announced an intent to seek FMLA leave. That evidence creates a genuine dispute as to whether the stated reason for termination was genuine or pretextual.

Finally, if there is any ambiguity in whether the hours Erdman worked before February 10, 2003 count toward FMLA eligibility, that ambiguity should have been resolved in her favor on summary judgment. The Supreme Court has instructed that remedial statutes are not to be cut down by narrow construction. *United States v. Stewart*, 311 U.S. 60, 64 (1940). Because the record supports a finding that Erdman had reached eligibility when she requested leave, the District Court’s contrary ruling was error.

The judgment should be reversed.
