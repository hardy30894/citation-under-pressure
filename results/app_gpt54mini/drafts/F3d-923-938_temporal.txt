## ARGUMENT

The Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo’s claimed loss deduction. Section 1366 limits a shareholder’s deduction to the sum of his stock basis and his basis in the corporation’s indebtedness to him. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). The question here is whether the repeated transfers among Merco and the entities through which Meruelo held interests, later booked by the accountant as shareholder debt, created a bona fide indebtedness running to Meruelo. Under settled pre-1970 principles, the answer is yes.

A debt exists when the parties intended a real obligation to repay and the transaction, in substance as well as form, creates a debtor-creditor relationship. Gilman v. Commissioner, 53 F.2d 47 (8th Cir. 1931); Commissioner v. Meridian & Thirteenth Realty Co., 132 F.2d 182 (7th Cir. 1942). Tax law looks to substance, not merely bookkeeping labels. Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465 (1935); Higgins v. Smith, 308 U.S. 473 (1940). Here, the record showed far more than casual intercompany clearing: Merco affiliates advanced over $15 million to or for Merco, Merco repaid less than $6 million, and year-end books reflected substantial net payables. Those obligations were not erased by the fact that the advances moved through entities partly owned by Meruelo. Where a taxpayer arranges business dealings through controlled entities, courts respect the true economic relationship if the transactions create actual liability. See Griffiths v. Helvering, 308 U.S. 355 (1939); Moline Properties, Inc. v. Commissioner, 319 U.S. 436 (1943).

The Commissioner and the Tax Court focused too narrowly on the path of the funds and ignored the way the parties treated the advances. Carreras, the CPA who prepared the returns, testified that he netted the affiliates’ intercompany balances and annually charged Merco’s line of credit for Meruelo’s allocable share of net indebtedness. That is powerful evidence that the parties themselves understood the advances as producing a real, continuing obligation attributable to Meruelo. Courts have long held that contemporaneous treatment by the parties is probative of indebtedness. See Deputy v. du Pont, 308 U.S. 488 (1940); John Kelley Co. v. Commissioner, 326 U.S. 521 (1946). Although the Tax Court discounted the promissory note because it was drafted in March 2004 and the indebtedness later reflected in the books was reclassified annually, formal documentation is not indispensable where the surrounding facts establish an enforceable debt. Gilman, 53 F.2d at 50; Meridian, 132 F.2d at 185.

The Tax Court also erred in insisting on an “actual economic outlay” in the rigid sense that Meruelo himself had to write checks directly to Merco. No pre-1970 authority imposes so wooden a requirement. Instead, the relevant inquiry is whether the shareholder was, in substance, poorer because he incurred a genuine liability to the corporation. See Putnam v. Commissioner, 352 U.S. 82 (1956) (recognizing that a guarantor who pays a corporation’s debt acquires a loss from a real economic obligation); Williams v. Commissioner, 58 F.2d 563 (5th Cir. 1932) (looking to the reality of the transaction rather than form). Meruelo’s controlled entities advanced funds to Merco, Merco’s books reflected repayable balances, and the CPA treated those balances as loans chargeable to Meruelo. That is enough to show a bona fide debt running to him for basis purposes.

Nor does the interposition of affiliated entities defeat basis. Taxpayers may conduct business through multiple corporations and partnerships, and the inquiry remains whether the particular advances created a creditor interest in the shareholder. Moline Properties, 319 U.S. at 438-39. A taxpayer who uses multiple entities to channel financing does not lose basis merely because the lender and borrower are not the same legal person at each step. Where the advances are made for the shareholder’s benefit and are consistently carried as debt, the shareholder’s basis increases. See Commissioner v. Smiley, 86 F.2d 658 (2d Cir. 1936); Helvering v. Clifford, 309 U.S. 331 (1940).

The IRS’s concession of basis for the $4,985,035 transfer confirms the principle. That transfer moved through Akoya, an entity partly owned by Meruelo, yet the Commissioner accepted that Meruelo obtained shareholder basis because the funds were his and were used for Merco’s deposit. The same economic reality governs the remaining net advances. The only difference is that the later transfers were made repeatedly through affiliate accounts rather than in a single lump sum. But repeated advances recorded as payable, repaid in part, and netted annually are no less real because they were operationally efficient.

In short, the Tax Court’s formalistic approach conflicts with the governing pre-1970 authorities. The proper focus is on whether Meruelo bore a bona fide, economically real obligation to Merco. The record compels that conclusion. The judgment disallowing the loss deduction should be reversed.
