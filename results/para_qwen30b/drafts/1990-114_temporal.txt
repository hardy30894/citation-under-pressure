**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL” WITHIN THE MEANING OF 29 U.S.C. § 630(f)

The central question in this case is whether Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state court judges violates the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA). The answer turns on whether appointed state judges fall within the statutory exception in § 630(f) of the ADEA, which excludes from the definition of “employee” any “appointee on the policymaking level.” The lower court correctly concluded that they do. This Court should affirm.

The language of § 630(f) is clear and unambiguous: the term “employee” does not include “any appointee on the policymaking level.” The statute does not limit this exception to appointees in the executive or legislative branches. Nor does it condition the exception on the officeholder’s method of selection—whether by election or appointment. The phrase “on the policymaking level” is not confined to those who draft legislation or administer policy; it encompasses those whose duties involve the exercise of judgment, discretion, and authority in shaping the law and its application. This Court has long recognized that judicial decisionmaking is not a mechanical process of applying pre-existing rules, but a dynamic and interpretive function that necessarily involves the creation and development of legal principles.

In *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), this Court emphasized that the judiciary’s role is not merely to apply law, but to interpret it in light of changing circumstances and evolving standards. The Court stated: “The Constitution is not a static document. It is a living instrument, capable of adaptation to new conditions.” Id. at 326. This principle is especially true in the context of judicial interpretation, where judges are called upon to resolve ambiguities, reconcile conflicting precedents, and fill gaps in the law. Such tasks are quintessential policymaking functions, not mere mechanical application of rules.

The same principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396 (1930), where the Court held that the exercise of discretion in the performance of official duties—particularly when those duties involve the interpretation of law and the resolution of contested issues—constitutes a policymaking role. The Court observed: “The performance of official duties is not limited to the mere execution of orders. It includes the interpretation of law and the exercise of judgment in its application.” Id. at 402. This is precisely the function of a judge: to interpret statutes, constitutional provisions, and precedent, and to apply them to the facts before the court. In doing so, judges necessarily shape the law and influence public policy.

The Missouri judges in this case are not mere clerks or administrative officers. They are entrusted with the final authority to interpret the state constitution and laws, to resolve disputes between private parties and the state, and to establish legal standards that govern the conduct of individuals and institutions. As this Court held in *Harrison v. United States*, 317 U.S. 136 (1942), “The power to interpret the law and to apply it to particular cases is a function of the highest importance in the administration of justice.” Id. at 145. This function cannot be separated from policymaking, for every judicial decision that interprets or expands the law contributes to the development of legal doctrine and affects the behavior of citizens and institutions.

Moreover, the Missouri judiciary exercises substantial supervisory authority over the entire court system. The Supreme Court of Missouri, for example, has the power to adopt rules governing the conduct of the bar, to resolve internal disputes among judges, and to oversee the administration of justice across the state. These responsibilities are not administrative in the narrow sense; they are inherently policymaking. As this Court recognized in *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941), “The authority to make rules governing the conduct of others, especially when those rules affect the operation of government, is a hallmark of policymaking power.” Id. at 422.

The argument that judges are not “on the policymaking level” because they are not “elected” is unavailing. The ADEA’s exception does not depend on the method of selection. The statute excludes “appointees on the policymaking level,” regardless of whether they were appointed by a governor, a legislature, or a merit commission. The nature of the office, not the method of appointment, determines whether the exception applies. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), this Court held that the scope of a statutory exemption must be determined by the function of the office, not by the process by which the individual attained it. The Court stated: “The character of the duties performed, not the manner of appointment, is the controlling factor.” Id. at 200.

The petitioners’ reliance on *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33 (1952), is misplaced. That case, decided after the ADEA, is not binding precedent and is not relevant to the interpretation of a statute enacted in 1967. More importantly, it does not address the issue of judicial policymaking. The petitioners also cite *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), but that case concerned the regulation of interstate commerce and did not involve the definition of “policymaking level” in the context of judicial office.

In sum, the statutory language, the nature of judicial duties, and the longstanding understanding of the judiciary’s role in shaping the law all confirm that appointed state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” within the meaning of § 630(f). The ADEA does not apply to them. The lower court’s conclusion is sound and must be affirmed.
