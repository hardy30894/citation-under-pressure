**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and the judgment below must be reversed.

The Constitution does not permit a defendant to forfeit fundamental rights—such as the right to counsel or the right to a fair trial—without a knowing, voluntary, and intelligent waiver. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243, 89 S.Ct. 1709, 1712, 23 L.Ed.2d 274 (1969). Such waivers are not lightly presumed. *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 885 (9th Cir.1986) (en banc). When a defendant seeks to represent himself or change his plea to guilty, the court must ensure that the decision is not the product of mental incapacity, coercion, or confusion. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir.1973). The Supreme Court has long recognized that the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights is higher than the standard for competency to stand trial.

In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172, 95 S.Ct. 896, 904, 43 L.Ed.2d 103 (1975), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and “is capable of assisting counsel.” This standard—often referred to as the *Dusky* standard—requires only that the defendant comprehend the nature of the charges, the roles of participants in the courtroom, and the potential consequences of conviction. *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402, 80 S.Ct. 788, 788, 4 L.Ed.2d 824 (1960) (per curiam). But competency to stand trial is not the same as competency to waive counsel or plead guilty.

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that a defendant must possess a higher level of mental functioning to validly waive constitutional rights. In *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir.1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871, 109 S.Ct. 182, 102 L.Ed.2d 151 (1988), we held that a defendant must have the “capacity for reasoned choice” among the alternatives available to him before a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea can be valid. This standard demands more than mere understanding of the proceedings—it requires the ability to weigh the risks and benefits of legal representation, to appreciate the consequences of forgoing a defense, and to make a deliberate decision in light of those consequences. *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir.1981); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir.1986) (en banc).

The record in this case reveals substantial evidence that Richard Moran lacked the capacity for reasoned choice at the time he discharged his counsel and pleaded guilty. He had recently attempted suicide, slashing his wrists and shooting himself in the abdomen. He was still recovering from that trauma when he appeared before the court. His mental state was further compromised by the fact that he was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—each of which affects cognitive function, mood, and judgment. *Inderal* is a beta-blocker used to treat hypertension and anxiety; *Dilantin* and *Phenobarbitol* are anticonvulsants with sedative properties; *Vistaril* is an anxiolytic that acts on the central nervous system. These drugs can impair memory, concentration, and decision-making. The court made no inquiry into the effects of these medications, despite Moran’s own admission that he was on medication. *See* Reporter’s Transcript of Plea Hearing, Nov. 24, 1985, at 12.

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing was not consistent with a reasoned choice. His responses to the court’s questions were largely monosyllabic. He offered no explanation for his decision to fire counsel or to foreclose the presentation of mitigating evidence. Instead, he stated only that he wanted to prevent anyone from introducing favorable evidence—a statement that suggests not a calculated strategy, but a desire to avoid accountability or to punish himself. This behavior is inconsistent with the kind of deliberate, rational decision-making required by the Constitution.

The state court’s postconviction finding that Moran was competent to waive counsel and plead guilty is legally insufficient. The court relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted months earlier, which were performed under the *Dusky* standard—assessing competency to stand trial, not competency to waive rights. *See* Dr. O’Gorman’s Report at 2; Dr. Jurasky’s Report at 1. Neither evaluation addressed the higher “reasoned choice” standard. The court did not consider the impact of Moran’s medication, his recent suicide attempt, or his emotionally volatile state. It applied the wrong legal standard, and thus its conclusion cannot be given deference under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *See* *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315, 83 S.Ct. 745, 758, 9 L.Ed.2d 770 (1963) (a federal court may not assume a state court applied the correct legal standard when the record suggests otherwise).
