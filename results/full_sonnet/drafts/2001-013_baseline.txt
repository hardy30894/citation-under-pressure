# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 4(b)(1) Withdraws OSHA's Jurisdiction Wherever Another Federal Agency Possesses Statutory Authority Over the Working Conditions at Issue

Congress wrote § 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act to prevent duplicative and conflicting federal safety regimes. The provision states that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard possesses exactly such authority over vessels like Rig 52. Under 14 U.S.C. § 2, the Coast Guard "shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States." That charge is not limited to "inspected" vessels; Congress separately empowered the Coast Guard to regulate uninspected vessels for fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and emergency equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. The Coast Guard has exercised that authority, promulgating an extensive body of regulations governing uninspected vessels. 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.*, 26.01 *et seq.* Because the statutory grant of authority—not the happenstance of whether a particular vessel has been inspected—defines the scope of § 4(b)(1), the exemption applies with equal force here.

## II. This Court's Maritime Precedent Confirms That the Coast Guard's Jurisdiction Over Seamen's Working Conditions Is Exclusive and Industry-Wide

The Fifth Circuit's holding rests on settled circuit law that "OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation." *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826-27 (5th Cir. 1983); *see also Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120, 1121-22 (5th Cir. 1980). In *Clary*, this rule was applied to a drilling barge materially indistinguishable from Rig 52—a vessel of the same type, engaged in the same work, staffed by the same category of maritime worker. 609 F.2d at 1121. The court there held that because "the Coast Guard was the federal agency with statutory authority over the working conditions of seamen," OSHA regulations could not be introduced even to establish a standard of care. *Id.* at 1122. Nothing in *Clary*'s reasoning turned on whether the barge had undergone Coast Guard inspection; the decision spoke categorically of "vessels operating on the high seas." *Id.*

*Donovan* reaffirmed and explained this rule three years later, describing "the Coast Guard's comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen's working conditions" as creating "an industry-wide exemption [from OSHA] for seamen serving on vessels operating on navigable waters." 720 F.2d at 826. The court explained why Congress structured § 4(b)(1) this way: "Section 4(b)(1) evidences a general Congressional intent to forbid overlapping regulation of working conditions in any given industry by multiple federal agencies. Such redundant control programs offer little except confusion, governmental proliferation, and stultification of enterprise." *Id.* at 827. Divided authority "might well produce [the] . . . anomaly . . . of steaming in and out of OSHA coverage" as a vessel crosses from state waters to the high seas and back. *Id.* at 829. The court's conclusion followed ineluctably: "a single, uniform set of rules should govern the maritime workplace," and "[b]ecause of OSHA's geographic limitations . . . this cannot be those of OSHA[;] . . . it must be those of the Coast Guard." *Id.*

That reasoning reflects a principle of far older vintage in the maritime context. This Court has long recognized the necessity of "uniformity" in the law governing maritime affairs, precisely because vessels move across jurisdictional lines that a patchwork of state and agency regulation cannot sensibly track. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 216 (1917). The same logic that has long confined the reach of state regulation of maritime activity applies to competing *federal* regulatory regimes: a rig drilling in Louisiana's territorial waters one day may be towed to blue water the next, and its crew's safety should not depend on which agency's inspectors last visited.

## III. The Uninspected Status of Rig 52 Does Not Remove It From the Coast Guard's Exclusive Jurisdiction

The Secretary's attempt to distinguish *Clary* on the ground that it did not "specifically consider" the inspected/uninspected distinction fails on the record. The vessel in *Clary* was itself a drilling barge with no indication of inspected status, and the Fifth Circuit's holding did not "turn on any such distinction." The Coast Guard's regulatory authority, as codified in 46 U.S.C. § 4102 and implemented at 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.* and 26.01 *et seq.*, expressly reaches uninspected vessels, covering life preservers, fire extinguishing equipment, ventilation, emergency signaling, and safety orientation. The Coast Guard is, in short, "no stranger to uninspected vessels." That the barge before this Court happened not to fall within the categories subject to full inspection reflects only a legislative judgment about the intensity of regulation certain vessel types require—not an abdication of the Coast Guard's statutory authority, nor an invitation for OSHA to fill a perceived gap. Section 4(b)(1) asks whether another agency has authority to regulate, not whether it has regulated with maximal stringency.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
