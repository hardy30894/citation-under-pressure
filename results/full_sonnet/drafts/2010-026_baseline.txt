# ARGUMENT

## THE NINTH CIRCUIT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT EXEMPTION 2 SHIELDS DOCUMENTS THAT ARE NOT "RELATED SOLELY TO THE INTERNAL PERSONNEL RULES AND PRACTICES OF AN AGENCY"

Exemption 2 of the Freedom of Information Act permits an agency to withhold matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The Ninth Circuit's adoption of a judicially-created "High 2" category—exempting documents that are merely "predominantly internal" and whose disclosure "may risk circumvention of agency regulation"—cannot be reconciled with the statutory text, this Court's precedent, or FOIA's foundational purpose.

### A. The Statutory Text Forecloses the "High 2" Exemption

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that FOIA exemptions "must be narrowly construed" because the statute reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language." *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976). The nine statutory exemptions are "explicitly exclusive." *U.S. Department of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting *Administrator, FAA v. Robertson*, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)).

The plain text of Exemption 2 exempts only matters "related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency." The word "solely" is not surplusage—it is a deliberate limitation demonstrating that Congress intended a narrow carve-out for mundane administrative matters, not a broad shield for any information an agency deems sensitive. The ESQD data at issue here—technical calculations regarding the blast radius of explosives stored on Indian Island—has nothing to do with "personnel rules and practices" in any ordinary sense of that phrase. It is not a rule governing employees' conduct; it is scientific and technical information about the physical properties of explosives and their effects on surrounding areas.

The Ninth Circuit's "predominantly internal" test, borrowed from *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), impermissibly rewrites the statute by substituting "predominantly" for "solely." No canon of construction permits a court to relax an unambiguous statutory qualifier because the resulting exemption is more convenient for the agency. This Court has never sanctioned such a substitution, and the panel's willingness to do so contravenes the settled instruction that FOIA exemptions "are to be interpreted narrowly." *Lahr v. NTSB*, 569 F.3d 964, 973 (9th Cir. 2009).

### B. This Court's Decision in *Rose* Confirms That Exemption 2 Does Not Encompass Technical, Non-Personnel Information

In *Rose*, this Court addressed Exemption 2 in the context of Air Force Academy honor and ethics case summaries. The Court explained that the exemption's core concern is materials such as those governing "parking facilities, lunch hours, and sick leave"—rules of "no genuine and significant public interest." *Rose*, 425 U.S. at 369 (citing S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 8 (1965)). While the Court left open whether disclosure risking "circumvention of agency regulation" might also fall within the exemption's ambit, it did so in the context of internal disciplinary rules directly analogous to personnel practices—not technical data about explosive yields having no connection whatsoever to employment matters.

The ESQD arc data at issue here bears no resemblance to the personnel-related materials that animated *Rose*. As Commander Whitbred's own affidavit confirms, the Navy uses this information "to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety." This is engineering and safety data, not a rule governing the conduct of agency personnel. Stretching Exemption 2 to cover such material—simply because the agency finds it useful for internal planning—eviscerates the "solely" limitation Congress imposed and converts Exemption 2 into a generalized "internal use" exemption unmoored from the statutory text.

### C. The "Risk of Circumvention" Rationale Cannot Justify Withholding Non-Personnel, Non-Law-Enforcement Materials

The panel's "circumvention" rationale is borrowed from cases like *Crooker* and this Circuit's own *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir. 1980), both of which concerned law enforcement manuals describing techniques for executing search warrants. The concern in those cases was that disclosure would allow regulated parties to evade detection of illegal conduct—a concern rooted in the law enforcement context. That rationale has no application to ESQD data, which does not describe any "regulation" that private parties might seek to evade. There is no "regulation" susceptible to circumvention when the information concerns the physical effects of an explosion; the data is not a rule of conduct but a physical description of an explosive's blast effects.

By extending the "circumvention" concept beyond the law-enforcement context into a freestanding basis for withholding any information whose disclosure the agency deems risky, the Ninth Circuit converted Exemption 2 into precisely the sort of catch-all provision that this Court has consistently rejected. As Judge Fletcher recognized in dissent below, national security concerns are addressed through Exemption 1, which requires formal classification, and Exemption 7(F), which requires a showing that disclosure "could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual." Allowing Exemption 2 to serve as a backdoor for withholding sensitive-but-unclassified information "distorts Congress's careful balance" between disclosure and legitimate government secrecy. *See John Doe Agency v. John Doe Corp.*, 493 U.S. 146, 157 (1989).

### D. The Navy's Own Conduct Undermines Its Exemption 2 Claim

The record demonstrates that the Navy voluntarily disclosed comparable ESQD arc maps for its Bangor facility—located near a sparsely populated area—while refusing to disclose analogous maps for Indian Island, located near the more populous and politically engaged communities of Port Townsend and Port Hadlock. This inconsistency suggests that the Navy's withholding decision was driven not by genuine risk-of-circumvention concerns but by a desire to avoid public scrutiny and political controversy—precisely the sort of agency self-interest that FOIA was designed to check. *See Rose*, 425 U.S. at 361 (FOIA reflects "a general philosophy of full agency disclosure").

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that Exemption 2 does not extend to technical, non-personnel materials such as the ESQD arc data at issue here.
