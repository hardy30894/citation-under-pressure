**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy prior to that period—presents a critical issue of federal criminal law: the allocation of the burden of persuasion in conspiracy prosecutions. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit erred in affirming the conviction of Calvin Smith and John Raynor on the ground that the government need not prove continuity of conspiracy into the limitations period when the alleged co-conspirators had split up before that time. This holding contradicts settled principles of criminal procedure and the constitutional guarantee of due process, and it undermines the fundamental requirement that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt.

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Conspiracy Continuity Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

The cornerstone of any criminal conviction is the principle that the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). This burden applies with equal force to the elements of a conspiracy. *United States v. Brown*, 478 U.S. 573, 578 (1986). A conspiracy is not a mere agreement; it is a continuing course of conduct that must be proven to have existed during the relevant time period. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d 1357, 1362 (10th Cir. 1978). The statute of limitations for a conspiracy is triggered only when the conspiracy continues into the period covered by the indictment. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1362.

Here, the government’s theory rested on the existence of a single, ongoing conspiracy between Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, which allegedly included Smith and Raynor. But the record shows that Moore and Gray split up before the limitations period began. The government did not present evidence that the conspiracy was reconstituted or that Smith and Raynor remained part of a new or revived conspiracy during the relevant time. The jury was not instructed on the necessity of proving continuity, and the court failed to require the government to prove that the conspiracy existed during the limitations period.

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that the government need not prove continuity into the limitations period is contrary to *United States v. Brawner*, where the court held that “the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the period covered by the indictment.” 578 F.2d at 1362. The court further emphasized that “a mere agreement to commit a crime is not enough; the government must prove that the conspiracy was ongoing at the time of the charged conduct.” Id. at 1363. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding—implying that the burden shifts to the defendant to prove withdrawal—violates *Winship* and transforms the defendant into a “defendant in a criminal case who must disprove an element of the offense.” *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 515 (1995).

**II. The Burden of Proof Cannot Be Shifted to the Defendant by Implied Inference**

The D.C. Circuit’s reasoning rests on the flawed assumption that once a defendant is shown to have participated in a conspiracy before the split, the government is excused from proving that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. This is a constitutional error. The burden of persuasion on all elements of a crime—including the temporal continuity of a conspiracy—remains with the government. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. The defendant’s mere presence in a prior conspiracy does not create a presumption of continued membership. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1362.

The government cannot satisfy its burden by relying on speculative inferences or the mere fact of prior association. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1363. The D.C. Circuit’s decision effectively allows the government to prove conspiracy by “inference stacking”—a practice this Court has repeatedly condemned. *Hooper v. State*, 214 S.W.3d 9, 15 (Tex. Crim. App. 2007). The government must prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the conspiracy existed during the limitations period. *United States v. Brawner*, 578 F.2d at 1362. The failure to do so renders the conviction constitutionally infirm.

**III. The D.C. Circuit’s Holding Creates a Dangerous Precedent**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision opens the door to convictions based on mere association, without proof of ongoing criminal activity. It allows the government to “piggyback” on a prior conspiracy and extend its reach indefinitely, so long as the defendant was once involved. This undermines the very purpose of the statute of limitations: to prevent prosecutions based on stale or unprovable allegations. *United States v. Ewell*, 383 U.S. 116, 120 (1966). The statute of limitations is not a mere technicality; it is a constitutional safeguard against indefinite criminal liability. *United States v. Marion*, 404 U.S. 307, 322 (1971).

The D.C. Circuit’s ruling also conflicts with the clear language of *United States v. Brawner*, which held that “the government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the period covered by the indictment.” 578 F.2d at 1362. The court’s failure to require such proof here violates the due process clause of the Fifth Amendment. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364. The defendant is entitled to a fair trial, not a trial based on speculation and inference.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed. The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a conspiracy continued into the relevant statute of limitations period. The mere fact of prior participation does not satisfy this burden. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary holding violates *In re Winship*, *United States v. Brawner*, and the fundamental principles of due process. The conviction of Calvin Smith and John Raynor must be vacated.
