ARGUMENT

I. This Court's Precedent Establishes That a Credible Claim of Actual Innocence, Without More, Equitably Tolls AEDPA's Statute of Limitations.

This Court has already resolved the question presented. In Souter v. Jones, 395 F.3d 577 (6th Cir. 2005), this Court held that AEDPA's one-year limitations period must yield when a petitioner presents a credible claim of actual innocence, recognizing that the "fundamental miscarriage of justice exception" applies with equal force to a time-barred petition as it does to a procedurally defaulted one. Id. at 588-90. That holding rests on the bedrock principle, announced in Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298 (1995), that a habeas petitioner may pass through a procedural gateway by showing "that it is more likely than not that no reasonable juror would have convicted him in the light of the new evidence." Id. at 327. The Supreme Court reaffirmed that standard in House v. Bell, 547 U.S. 518 (2006), explaining that the Schlup inquiry is "demanding and permits review only in the extraordinary case," but nowhere conditioned that review on the petitioner's diligence in uncovering or presenting the exculpatory evidence. Id. at 538.

The rationale for treating actual innocence as sufficient, standing alone, follows directly from the purpose of the gateway itself: it exists to prevent the "conviction of one who is actually innocent," Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 496 (1986), regardless of the procedural vehicle—default or limitations—that would otherwise foreclose review. Perkins's affidavits, which implicate the prosecution's sole eyewitness in the very murder for which Perkins was convicted, present precisely the sort of new, reliable evidence Schlup contemplates. The district court erred in imposing a categorical diligence requirement on top of this showing.

II. Pace v. DiGuglielmo Does Not Impose a Diligence Precondition on the Actual-Innocence Gateway.

The Warden's reliance on Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), is misplaced. Pace held that "a petitioner is entitled to equitable tolling only if he shows (1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way and prevented timely filing." Id. at 418. But Pace was not an actual-innocence case; the petitioner there sought tolling based on confusion over which state filings tolled the federal clock, not on a claim that he was factually innocent of the crime. Id. at 410-12. The Supreme Court's later decision in Holland v. Florida, 560 U.S. 631 (2010), likewise applied Pace's two-part test in the ordinary tolling context of attorney misconduct, again without any actual-innocence component. Id. at 649.

Pace's diligence requirement governs the generic equitable-tolling analysis applicable to garden-variety excuses for late filing. It does not displace the distinct, substantive miscarriage-of-justice exception recognized in Schlup and House, which serves a different function: ensuring that no procedural rule, including a limitations period, operates to imprison the innocent. Nothing in Pace purports to overrule or even discuss Schlup, and the Supreme Court does not silently abrogate a doctrine of that significance. Cf. Shalala v. Illinois Council on Long Term Care, Inc., 529 U.S. 1, 18 (2000) (courts do not lightly presume that intervening decisions overrule settled precedent by implication). Reading Pace to impose a diligence gatekeeping function onto Schlup's gateway would require precisely that kind of unwarranted inference.

III. Diligence, at Most, Bears on the Credibility of the Innocence Claim—It Is Not an Independent Threshold Requirement.

Even where timing matters, the Supreme Court has treated delay as a factor relevant to the reliability of the new evidence under Schlup's demanding standard, not as a freestanding jurisdictional bar. See House, 547 U.S. at 537 (noting that the timing of new evidence may bear on its credibility). That is a case-by-case evidentiary inquiry conducted within the Schlup analysis itself, not a categorical precondition that forecloses review before the merits of the innocence claim are even considered. The district court here inverted that framework: rather than weighing the timing of Perkins's affidavits as part of assessing their reliability, it treated diligence as an independent, dispositive bar—precisely the approach Schlup and House do not require.

IV. Perkins's Petition Should Be Evaluated Under the Actual-Innocence Standard Alone.

Applying the correct standard, Perkins's claim should proceed to the merits. His affidavits—from his sister, from Demond Louis describing Jones's bloodstained clothing and admission of guilt, and from the dry-cleaning clerk corroborating that account—together present new evidence bearing directly on Jones's culpability, the very witness whose testimony convicted Perkins. Whatever weight the delay in presenting this evidence deserves, it goes to credibility under Schlup, not to an antecedent diligence gate imported from Pace.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that a credible claim of actual innocence, without more, warrants equitable tolling of AEDPA's statute of limitations, vacate the district court's judgment, and remand for consideration of Perkins's petition on the merits.
