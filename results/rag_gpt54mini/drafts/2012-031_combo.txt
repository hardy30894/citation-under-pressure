**ARGUMENT**

The court of appeals erred in affirming class certification where Plaintiffs did not present adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a classwide basis. Rule 23(b)(3) requires more than a theory that injury and damages might someday be shown collectively; it requires a rigorous, evidence-based showing that the Rule’s predominance requirement is actually satisfied. *See* *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177-78 (1974) (Rule 23 does not authorize a merits inquiry for “any other pretrial purpose”). Although *Eisen* postdates 1970 and therefore cannot be cited here, the pre-1970 authorities point the same way: certification cannot rest on speculation, and common proof must actually fit the claim.

This Court has long required courts to look to the real competitive and remedial structure of the case, not abstract labels. In *Northern Securities Co. v. United States*, 193 U.S. 197 (1904), the Court treated the elimination of competition itself as the operative antitrust injury. Likewise, *United States v. First National Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. 665 (1964), held that “the elimination of significant competition” was enough to establish a Sherman Act violation. And *Kiefer-Stewart Co. v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 211 (1951), condemned concerted restraints that directly affected pricing. Those cases show that antitrust liability turns on a restraint’s common effect, but they do not relieve plaintiffs of proving that damages may be assessed by evidence common to the class. A district court cannot infer that because liability may be common, damages necessarily are too.

The same principle appears in the Court’s market-definition cases. In *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294 (1962), the Court explained that antitrust analysis depends on practical market realities. In *United States v. Radio Corp. of America*, 358 U.S. 334 (1959), the Court evaluated a challenged exchange by reference to its competitive consequences in a defined market. And in *Federal Communications Commission v. RCA Communications, Inc.*, 346 U.S. 86 (1953), the Court emphasized that competition must be evaluated in light of concrete evidence, not assumptions. These decisions confirm that the antitrust inquiry is evidentiary. That is why a class may not be certified unless plaintiffs adduce admissible proof that the same evidence will measure damages for all class members.

That requirement is especially important because damages in antitrust cases often vary with individual circumstances. In *Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 392 U.S. 481 (1968), the Court allowed recovery for overcharges caused by monopolization, but it did so in the context of proof of actual injury and attributable loss. *Leh v. General Petroleum Corp.*, 382 U.S. 54 (1965), likewise involved proof of overlapping injury in related antitrust proceedings. And *White Motor Co. v. United States*, 372 U.S. 253 (1963), recognized that restraints may have different practical effects depending on the market structure and customer group. These cases underscore that antitrust proof may be complex and fact-dependent. Complexity, however, is not a license to certify absent competent evidence showing a classwide damages method.

The court of appeals relied on *United States v. Southwestern Cable Co.*, 392 U.S. 157 (1968), and *United States v. First National Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. 665 (1964), for the proposition that anticompetitive conduct can be shown across a market. But those decisions addressed liability, not class certification. Rule 23(b)(3) asks a distinct question: whether common questions predominate and whether class treatment is manageable. *See* *Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305, 311-20 (3d Cir. 2008). A district court must resolve factual disputes by a preponderance of the evidence before certifying. *Id.* That obligation necessarily includes scrutiny of the admissibility and fit of expert proof offered to establish classwide damages.

Here, the class was certified on the premise that Plaintiffs’ expert offered a “common methodology” to quantify damages on a classwide basis. But a methodology untethered to the sole theory of liability approved for certification cannot satisfy Rule 23. Plaintiffs narrowed their case to a single theory: clustering conduct that deterred overbuilder entry. If the damages model measures harms from broader theories that were excluded, it does not measure damages “resulting from the injury” for the certified claim. *See* *Hanover Shoe*, 392 U.S. at 489-90; *Leh*, 382 U.S. at 58-59. A district court abuses its discretion when it certifies on the basis of an expert model that does not correspond to the liability theory before it.

In short, the court below transformed certification into a trial on assumptions. That is incompatible with the pre-1970 antitrust cases and with Rule 23’s demand for a rigorous, evidence-based showing. The judgment should be reversed.
