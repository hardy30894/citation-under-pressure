ARGUMENT

The district court erred in denying the motion to suppress. The Fourth Amendment prohibits the warrantless seizure of a person absent probable cause or, in the limited circumstances of a Terry stop, reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). A pat-frisk requires “specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant” the belief that the suspect is armed. Id. at 21. Nervous or agitated behavior, without more, does not satisfy this standard. Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266, 271 (2000); see also Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968) (mere association or presence insufficient).

The stop itself was justified by the observed traffic violations. Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106, 109 (1977) (per curiam). Officers may order passengers from a vehicle during a traffic stop. Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997). But the authority to frisk is narrower and demands individualized suspicion that the passenger poses a danger. Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323, 327 (2009); Ybarra v. Illinois, 444 U.S. 85, 94 (1979). Here, the record shows only that Soares moved his hands, rocked in his seat, and spoke profanely after being ordered to remain still. These actions occurred after the officers had already secured the scene and obtained the driver’s documents. United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411, 417-18 (1981) (totality must be assessed from the perspective of a reasonable officer).

The government’s reliance on the high-crime area and late hour is misplaced. Illinois v. Wardlow, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000) (location is relevant but insufficient alone). No bulge, furtive reaching toward a weapon, or reliable tip indicated that Soares was armed. Compare United States v. McKoy, 428 F.3d 38, 41 (1st Cir. 2005) (hand movements near console, without more, do not justify frisk). The officers’ subjective unease, unmoored from objective facts, cannot validate the search. United States v. Brown, 510 F.3d 57, 66 (1st Cir. 2007); United States v. Kimball, 25 F.3d 1, 8 (1st Cir. 1994).

Because the pat-frisk exceeded constitutional bounds, the firearm must be suppressed. The conditional guilty plea should be vacated and the case remanded for further proceedings.
