**ARGUMENT**

**THE RULE ANNOUNCED IN JOHNSON V. UNITED STATES IS A SUBSTANTIVE RULE THAT APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW**

**A. Substantive Rules of Constitutional Law Apply Retroactively as a Matter of Constitutional Necessity**

This Court has long distinguished between two categories of new constitutional rules for retroactivity purposes: substantive rules, which "generally apply retroactively," and procedural rules, which generally do not. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). A substantive rule is one that "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Id.* at 353. Such rules "necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant"—or here, a sentenced offender—"faces a punishment that the law cannot impose upon him," *id.*, and therefore must apply retroactively regardless of when a conviction or sentence became final, because "[t]here is no grandfather clause that permits States to enforce punishments the Constitution forbids." *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 208 (2016). As this Court explained just months before this case was argued, "[a] penalty imposed pursuant to an unconstitutional law is no less void because the prisoner's sentence became final before the law was held unconstitutional," and "the Constitution requires substantive rules to have retroactive effect regardless of when a conviction became final." *Id.* at 205, 206. This retroactivity principle is not merely a matter of judicial grace under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989); it is compelled by the Constitution itself, because "[a] court has no authority to leave in place a conviction or sentence that violates a substantive rule, regardless of whether the conviction or sentence became final before the rule was announced." *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 203.

By contrast, procedural rules "regulate only the manner of determining the defendant's culpability," *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353, and apply retroactively only in the narrow circumstances identified in *Teague*.

**B. Johnson Announced a Substantive Rule Because It Narrowed the Class of Predicate Offenses and the Class of Persons Subject to ACCA's Fifteen-Year Mandatory Minimum**

In *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), this Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act—covering offenses that "otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another," 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii)—"denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges" and therefore "is unconstitutionally vague." *Id.* at 597, 606. That holding did not merely change the *procedure* by which courts determine whether a predicate offense qualifies; it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses from ACCA's reach altogether. After *Johnson*, a conviction can no longer support the fifteen-year mandatory minimum on the theory that it "presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another." *Id.* at 606. This is precisely the kind of rule that "narrow[s] the scope of a criminal statute by interpreting its terms," which this Court has repeatedly treated as substantive. *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620–21 (1998) (holding that a decision narrowing the conduct criminalized by a statute applies retroactively on collateral review because it may result in the "conviction and punishment for an act that the law does not make criminal").

The residual clause's invalidation is functionally indistinguishable from a decision narrowing a substantive criminal statute. Before *Johnson*, individuals such as Mr. Welch were subject to a fifteen-year mandatory minimum under 18 U.S.C. § 924(e) if their prior convictions were deemed to present a "serious potential risk of physical injury," even though no one—not the courts, not the parties, not later reviewing panels—could predict with any consistency which offenses met that standard. Compare *Sykes v. United States*, 564 U.S. 1 (2011) (finding vehicle flight to be a violent felony under the residual clause), *with Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 601 (overruling *Sykes* because the residual clause's standard "produces more unpredictability and arbitrariness than the Due Process Clause tolerates"). After *Johnson*, that entire category of predicate offenses is gone: the "class of persons that the law punishes" under ACCA's fifteen-year mandatory minimum has been narrowed by the elimination of an entire clause defining "violent felony." *Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 353. That is not a change in the *procedures* used to adjudicate ACCA eligibility—it is a change in the *substantive* scope of the statute's punishment provision, altering "the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes." *Id.*

**C. The Consequences for Petitioner Confirm the Substantive Character of the Johnson Rule**

Petitioner Gregory Welch received a mandatory fifteen-year sentence premised in part on the residual clause's vague "serious potential risk" standard as applied to his Florida strong-arm robbery conviction. Pet. App. Because *Johnson* forecloses reliance on that clause altogether, Mr. Welch's sentence rests on a constitutional provision that no longer exists. As in *Montgomery*, where this Court held that a substantive Eighth Amendment rule "rendered [a] mandatory life-without-parole sentence . . . unconstitutional," 577 U.S. at 209, so too here, *Johnson*'s invalidation of the residual clause renders unconstitutional any ACCA sentence that depends on it, regardless of when that sentence became final. Denying retroactive application would allow Mr. Welch's punishment to stand under a legal standard that this Court has held violates due process—precisely the outcome the Constitution forbids. *Id.* at 206.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* is substantive and must be given retroactive effect on collateral review.
