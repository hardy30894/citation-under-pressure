**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment permits a state court to exercise personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant only when the defendant has purposefully availed itself of the privileges of conducting activities within the forum state, such that the litigation arises out of or relates to those activities. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). This principle ensures that a defendant is not subjected to the jurisdiction of a court in a state with which it has no meaningful connection. Where the plaintiff’s claims do not arise from the defendant’s contacts with the forum, the exercise of jurisdiction violates due process.

Here, Ford Motor Company has no contacts with Montana that gave rise to the claims in this case. Ford did not manufacture, sell, or advertise the vehicle in Montana. The vehicle was assembled in Kentucky, first sold in Washington, and later transferred to an Oregon resident before being brought into Montana. Ford’s only connection to Montana is the sale of replacement parts through a distributorship. But the sale of parts—unrelated to the specific vehicle involved in the accident—cannot form the basis for specific personal jurisdiction over a claim arising from a tire failure in a vehicle that never entered Ford’s distribution chain in Montana.

The Montana Supreme Court’s conclusion that Ford “availed itself” of the privilege of doing business in Montana by selling parts is legally unsound. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. To “avail” oneself of a forum state’s benefits, a defendant must engage in conduct that is purposefully directed toward that state. The mere sale of parts through a distributor does not constitute such purposeful availment. In *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court held that a defendant’s mere presence in a state, even for the purpose of conducting business, does not justify jurisdiction unless the cause of action arises from that presence. Here, the cause of action—tire tread separation in a vehicle never sold or distributed in Montana—does not arise from Ford’s sale of parts.

Moreover, the Due Process Clause requires that the defendant have “minimum contacts” with the forum such that the exercise of jurisdiction does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. The Montana Supreme Court’s decision creates a regime in which a corporation may be haled into court in any state where it sells parts, regardless of whether those parts are related to the product at issue or whether the product ever entered the state. This would allow plaintiffs to forum-shop based on the availability of parts sales, not on any meaningful connection between the defendant and the injury. Such a rule would be fundamentally unfair and contrary to the principles of due process.

The Court has long recognized that jurisdiction must be tied to the cause of action. In *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714 (1877), the Court held that a state cannot exercise jurisdiction over a defendant unless the cause of action arises from the defendant’s presence or activities within the state. The same principle applies today. Where the injury occurred in Montana and the vehicle was not sold or distributed there, the claim does not arise from Ford’s contacts with Montana. The sale of parts, even if it occurs in Montana, is not sufficient to establish jurisdiction over a claim unrelated to those parts.

The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on *Conway v. Benefis Health System* and *Newbury v. State Farm* is misplaced. Those cases involved insurance contracts and the application of state law to payments made by insurers. They do not address personal jurisdiction. The Court has never held that the sale of goods or services in a state, unrelated to the injury, can justify jurisdiction over a claim arising from an entirely different transaction. To the contrary, in *Hess*, 274 U.S. at 356, the Court emphasized that “the mere fact that a person may be found in a state does not subject him to the jurisdiction of its courts unless the cause of action arises from his presence or activities there.”

The Due Process Clause does not permit jurisdiction based on fortuitous or attenuated contacts. The Montana Supreme Court’s decision allows a plaintiff to assert jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant based on conduct that is entirely unrelated to the injury. This violates the fundamental requirement that jurisdiction be grounded in the defendant’s purposeful connection to the forum. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court erred in concluding that Ford was subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana. The claim does not arise from Ford’s contacts with the state, and the exercise of jurisdiction over Ford would be fundamentally unfair under the Due Process Clause. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court must be reversed.
