## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Bryant's Claim to Enforce the Arbitration Award

The district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim to enforce the arbitration award because Bryant has standing to bring the claim. As an employee covered by a collective bargaining agreement, Bryant has the right to enforce an arbitration award in his favor. See Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171 (1967). In Vaca, the Supreme Court held that an employee may bring a claim to enforce an arbitration award, even if the union is the primary party to the collective bargaining agreement. Id. at 195-196. Here, Bryant won an arbitration award against Bell Atlantic, and he has a legitimate interest in enforcing that award. See also United Steelworkers of America v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593, 596 (1960) (holding that an arbitration award is final and binding on the parties).

Furthermore, the district court's dismissal of Bryant's claim to enforce the arbitration award was improper because it failed to consider the strong federal policy in favor of enforcing arbitration awards. See Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp., 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983) (noting that "federal law favors arbitration"). The district court should have given deference to the arbitration award and enforced it accordingly. See United Paperworkers International Union v. Misco, Inc., 484 U.S. 29, 37-38 (1987) (holding that courts should give deference to arbitration awards and only vacate them in limited circumstances).

### II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Bryant's Title VII and Section 1981 Claims

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Bryant's Title VII and Section 1981 claims because it failed to apply the correct burden-shifting standard. Under the burden-shifting standard, a plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case of discrimination. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Once a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case, the burden shifts to the defendant to articulate a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for the adverse employment action. Id. If the defendant meets this burden, the plaintiff must then show that the defendant's proffered reason is pretextual. Id. at 804.

In this case, Bryant established a prima facie case of discrimination by showing that he was disciplined and ultimately terminated for failing to work his Red Letter Days, while white employees were not held to the same standard. See Texas Department of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981) (noting that a prima facie case of discrimination requires a showing that the plaintiff is a member of a protected class, was qualified for the job, and suffered an adverse employment action under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination). Bell Atlantic's proffered reason for terminating Bryant - his repeated failure to work his Red Letter Days - is pretextual because it failed to accommodate Bryant's child care responsibilities, while accommodating other employees. See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133, 147 (2000) (holding that a plaintiff may show pretext by demonstrating that the defendant's proffered reason is false or that the defendant has treated similarly situated employees more favorably).

Moreover, the district court erred in granting summary judgment because there are genuine issues of material fact still in dispute. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (noting that summary judgment is only appropriate if there is no genuine issue of material fact). Here, there is a dispute over whether Bell Atlantic's refusal to accommodate Bryant's child care responsibilities was discriminatory. See also Desert Palace, Inc. v. Costa, 539 U.S. __, No. 02-679, slip op. at 8 (June 9, 2003) (holding that a plaintiff may prove discrimination through direct or circumstantial evidence).

### III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Bryant's Retaliation Claim

The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Bryant's retaliation claim because Bryant established a prima facie case of retaliation. To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show that he engaged in a protected activity, suffered an adverse employment action, and that there is a causal connection between the two. See Ross v. Communications Satellite Corp., 759 F.2d 355, 365 (4th Cir. 1985). Here, Bryant engaged in protected activity by filing a grievance and complaining about the Red Letter Day policy, and he suffered an adverse employment action when he was terminated. See also Williams v. Cerberonics, Inc., 871 F.2d 452, 457 (4th Cir. 1989) (noting that a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse employment action may be inferred if the events are closely related in time).

In conclusion, the district court erred in dismissing Bryant's claim to enforce the arbitration award and in granting summary judgment on his Title VII, Section 1981, and retaliation claims. This Court should reverse the district court's decision and remand the case for further proceedings.
