**ARGUMENT**

**I. Because Title VII liability rests on a minimal showing of intent, § 1981a(b)(1) must be construed to require conduct that is independently egregious before punitive damages may issue.**

Title VII liability does not require proof of animus in any conventional sense. A plaintiff need only establish a prima facie case and discredit the employer's proffered explanation; at that point "no additional proof of discrimination is required." *St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks*, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (quoting *Hicks v. St. Mary's Honor Ctr.*, 970 F.2d 487, 493 (8th Cir. 1992)). Under the familiar burden-shifting framework of *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973), a jury may infer "intentional discrimination" from little more than disbelief of an employer's stated reasons. *Id.* at 804-05. If the terms "malice" and "reckless indifference" in 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) are read merely as synonyms for the intent already required to establish liability, then every Title VII plaintiff who wins on the merits will presumptively qualify for punitive damages. That result collapses Congress's carefully separate liability and punitive-damages provisions into one, and it cannot be what Congress intended when it created a distinct, capped remedy for punitive damages in the Civil Rights Act of 1991. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3); *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994) (describing the 1991 Act's punitive-damages provision as a significant, deliberately bounded expansion of relief). An independent showing of egregiousness—conduct markedly beyond the discriminatory intent necessary for liability—is therefore required to give § 1981a(b)(1) any operative content distinct from § 1981a(a).

**II. This Court's punitive-damages jurisprudence confirms that punitive liability is reserved for conduct of heightened reprehensibility.**

This Court has repeatedly instructed that punitive damages are an extraordinary remedy, reserved for the most blameworthy conduct, not a routine adjunct to compensatory liability. In *BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore*, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), the Court identified "the degree of reprehensibility of the defendant's conduct" as the most important guidepost in assessing the propriety of a punitive award, explaining that "some wrongs are more blameworthy than others." *Id.* at 575-76. Similarly, in *Pacific Mutual Life Insurance Co. v. Haslip*, 499 U.S. 1 (1991), the Court upheld a punitive award only after confirming that the underlying conduct involved a pattern of deliberate deception, and cautioned that punitive damages must bear a "reasonable relationship" to both the harm and the reprehensibility of the defendant's actions. *Id.* at 22. These decisions reflect the settled common-law understanding, applicable equally to statutory punitive-damages schemes, that punishment beyond compensation is warranted only where the defendant's conduct is qualitatively worse than the conduct sufficient to establish liability in the first instance.

This Court's treatment of punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 in *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), does not compel a contrary conclusion. Although the Court there held that punitive damages could be awarded upon a showing of "reckless or callous indifference," *id.* at 56, it did so against a statutory backdrop, and record of egregious prison-guard misconduct, wholly different from the routine promotion dispute at issue here. Nothing in *Smith* suggests that Congress, in fashioning a wholly new, capped remedy for Title VII in 1991, intended to import that standard without the egregiousness that characterized the conduct actually before the Court in that case.

**III. Ordinary business decisions of the kind challenged here—an employer's good-faith, if ultimately erroneous, selection among qualified internal candidates—do not approach the level of reprehensibility necessary to justify punishment.**

The conduct found by the jury here consisted of ADA's preference for one qualified internal candidate over another, following interviews and a comparison of relevant experience. *See* discussion, *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass'n*, 108 F.3d 1431, 1435-36 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (en banc), *vacated in part*. This is precisely the kind of case in which vicarious liability principles counsel restraint. Employers act through agents, and this Court has recognized, in a closely related Title VII context, that agency-law principles limit an employer's exposure for the discriminatory acts of its managers where the employer has made good-faith efforts to comply with the law. *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 806 (1998); *Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 764-65 (1998). Those same principles counsel that an employer's good-faith, if mistaken, business judgment—rendered without any showing of the kind of deliberate, deceptive, or malicious conduct condemned in *Gore* and *Haslip*—cannot sustain an award meant to punish egregious wrongdoing. Nor does the fact that ADA's asserted reasons were ultimately disbelieved by the jury, *see McDonnell Douglas*, 411 U.S. at 804-05; *Hicks*, 509 U.S. at 511, transform an ordinary personnel decision into the sort of outrageous conduct Congress meant to punish.

For these reasons, the court of appeals correctly required a showing of egregiousness independent of the state-of-mind terms in § 1981a(b)(1), and correctly concluded that ADA's conduct did not meet that standard. The judgment below should be affirmed.
