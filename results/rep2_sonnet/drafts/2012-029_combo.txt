# ARGUMENT

## I. Federal "arising under" jurisdiction is confined by the well-pleaded complaint rule, and a state cause of action does not become federal merely because it requires the application of patent-law principles.

This Court has long held that "the question whether a case is one arising under the Constitution or a law or treaty of the United States . . . must be determined from what necessarily appears in the plaintiff's statement of his own claim . . . unaided by anything alleged in anticipation or avoidance of defenses which it is thought the defendant may interpose." *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Minton's complaint against Gunn pleads a single cause of action: legal malpractice under Texas common law. That claim did not spring from the Patent Act; it sprang from the duties Texas imposes on attorneys toward their clients. As Justice Holmes explained, "[a] suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action." *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Texas law, not federal patent law, created Minton's right to sue his former lawyers.

That a patent-law question must be answered along the way does not transform the character of the claim. In *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912), this Court held that "a suit does not so arise [under federal law] unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." The "result" of Minton's malpractice suit does not depend on the validity, construction, or effect of the '643 patent in any way that binds the Patent Office, NASD, or any future litigant; it depends only on whether *these* attorneys committed malpractice under Texas negligence standards, using a hypothetical, backward-looking application of the experimental-use doctrine to facts frozen in the historical record.

## II. The mere presence of a patent issue as one link in the causal chain of a state claim does not satisfy the substantiality requirement.

This Court's decisions have repeatedly refused to allow patent issues embedded in state-created rights to swallow state jurisdiction. In *Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co.*, 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897), the Court held that a case is not one "arising under" the patent laws simply because a patent question is presented as an element of the controversy; something more is required. Similarly, in *New Marshall Engine Co. v. Marshall Engine Co.*, 223 U.S. 473, 478 (1912), this Court held that a suit for an accounting under a contract touching patented machinery does not arise under the patent laws merely because the underlying subject matter is patented technology. And in *Excelsior Wooden Pipe Co. v. Pacific Bridge Co.*, 185 U.S. 282, 285–86 (1902), the Court likewise declined to treat a contract dispute as a patent case simply because resolving it required reference to patent rights.

*Hopkins v. Walker*, 244 U.S. 486, 490–91 (1917), confirms the proper inquiry: a case is a "patent case" for jurisdictional purposes only when the plaintiff's right to relief itself depends upon construing or applying the patent laws—not when a patent issue arises collaterally, as a hypothetical defense to a hypothetical defense in an underlying, already-concluded federal suit. Here, the on-sale bar and experimental-use exception are relevant only to reconstruct what a federal court *would have done*, a purely retrospective and case-specific inquiry that resolves nothing about the scope, validity, or enforceability of any patent right going forward.

This Court's teaching in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936), governs: federal courts must apply "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Minton's malpractice claim is exactly the kind of case that must be laid aside. The federal issue is not substantial in the sense this Court requires—it will not control the outcome of other patent litigation, guide the Patent Office, or affect the uniform administration of the patent system. It is substantial only to the two parties before the court, which is never enough.

## III. The well-pleaded complaint rule bars consideration of hypothetical patent defenses that might have been raised in the prior federal suit.

Under *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 672–73 (1950), federal jurisdiction cannot be manufactured by reference to defenses—real or hypothetical—that a defendant might have raised. Minton's malpractice theory requires reconstructing a defense (experimental use) that was never adjudicated on the merits in the underlying patent suit and was raised, in the state proceeding, only to explain what *should* have happened. That is precisely the kind of anticipatory, defense-driven federal question *Skelly Oil* and *Mottley* forbid from serving as the jurisdictional hook.

## IV. Even outside the patent context, this Court's "arising under" framework confirms that federally created elements embedded in state claims remain state claims absent a substantial, forward-looking federal interest.

Judge Friendly's oft-cited formulation in *T.B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu*, 339 F.2d 823, 828 (2d Cir. 1964), captures the correct rule by analogy: a case "arises under" federal intellectual-property law when federal law "creates the cause of action" or when a right created by federal law is "an essential" element requiring construction—not whenever federal law merely lurks in the background of proof. Minton's malpractice suit fails that test. It is, and always was, a state action sounding in tort.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the state courts of Texas possessed jurisdiction to adjudicate Minton's malpractice claim, and the judgment below should be reversed.
