**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of MSPB decisions to the Federal Circuit, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), except for “mixed cases” alleging both an appealable action and prohibited discrimination, which are reviewed in district court, § 7703(b)(2); § 7702(a)(1). Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41, 133 S. Ct. 596 (2012), holds that this district-court route applies whenever the Board dismisses a mixed case without reaching the merits, whether on procedural or jurisdictional grounds.

Kloeckner construed the statutory phrase “affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the” MSPB, § 7702(a)(1)(A), to turn on the nature of the underlying personnel action, not the Board’s ultimate disposition. 568 U.S. at 604. The Court therefore rejected any distinction between merits and pre-merits dismissals. Id. at 602–03. The same reasoning governs jurisdictional dismissals: the action (here, a thirty-day suspension and retirement) remains one the employee could have appealed had the Board found involuntariness. See Garcia v. Dep’t of Homeland Sec., 437 F.3d 1322, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2006).

Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), reached the contrary conclusion for jurisdictional dismissals. But Powell is no longer binding. A circuit precedent is “eviscerated” when a subsequent Supreme Court decision “effectively overrules” it. Dellums v. U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Comm’n, 863 F.2d 968, 978 n.11 (D.C. Cir. 1988); United States v. Williams, 194 F.3d 100, 105 (D.C. Cir. 1999). Kloeckner supplies that overruling. It expressly resolved the circuit split that included Harms v. IRS, 321 F.3d 1001, 1007–08 (10th Cir. 2003), and Downey v. Runyon, 160 F.3d 139, 145–46 (2d Cir. 1998), both of which had distinguished procedural from jurisdictional dismissals—the precise distinction Powell adopted. 568 U.S. at 603 & n.3.

Conforto v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 713 F.3d 1111, 1118 n.1 (Fed. Cir. 2013), attempted to preserve the distinction by noting the Board’s inability to waive jurisdictional defects. That formalistic line is incompatible with Kloeckner’s focus on the statutory text and its insistence that “the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction is not at all a matter of what the employee and the agency choose to characterize as a ‘mixed case.’” 568 U.S. at 605. Uniform district-court review of all mixed-case dismissals also eliminates the forum uncertainty that would otherwise arise from the Act’s strict filing deadlines. See § 7702(e).

Accordingly, the petition should be transferred to the district court.
