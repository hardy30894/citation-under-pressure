**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana because Gullett’s claims arise out of Ford’s in-state activities.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984) (quoting *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945)). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the “arise out of or relate to” requirement is met when the defendant’s forum conduct forms a “substantial connection” to the plaintiff’s claims. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of Cal., San Francisco Cty.*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017) (citing *Kulko v. Superior Court of Cal., City and County of San Francisco*, 436 U.S. 84, 92 (1978)).

Here, Gullett’s claims directly arise from Ford’s Montana-based activities. Ford designed, manufactured, and sold the Explorer in question, and it marketed and serviced the vehicle in Montana. *See Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 2020 WL 5997042, at *3 (Mont. Oct. 9, 2020). The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that these in-state activities provided a sufficient basis for jurisdiction because Gullett’s injuries were proximately caused by Ford’s Montana-connected conduct. *Id.* at *4 (citing *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 779-80 (1984)).

Ford’s reliance on *Bristol-Myers* is misplaced. In that case, the plaintiffs’ claims did not arise from the defendant’s California activities, as the drug at issue was not designed, manufactured, or marketed in the forum state. *Bristol-Myers*, 137 S. Ct. at 1781. Here, by contrast, Gullett’s claims are inextricably tied to Ford’s Montana-connected conduct. *See Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014) (jurisdiction exists where the defendant’s forum conduct forms the “basis for the suit”).

**II. Ford’s assertion that jurisdiction is improper because the vehicle was not first sold in Montana is unavailing.**

Ford contends that jurisdiction is improper because the Explorer was not first sold in Montana. This argument misreads the jurisdictional inquiry. The relevant question is whether the plaintiff’s claims arise from the defendant’s forum activities—not where the product was initially sold. *See Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 788-89 (1984) (jurisdiction upheld where defendant’s forum-directed activities formed the basis of the suit). Here, Ford’s Montana-based design, marketing, and servicing activities directly contributed to Gullett’s injuries. *Ford Motor Co.*, 2020 WL 5997042, at *4.

Moreover, the stream-of-commerce theory remains viable for specific jurisdiction. *See J. McIntyre Mach., Ltd. v. Nicastro*, 564 U.S. 873, 882 (2011) (plurality opinion). Ford’s nationwide distribution of the Explorer, including in Montana, subjected it to jurisdiction where its in-state activities caused the plaintiff’s harm. *Id.* at 884 (Breyer, J., concurring in the judgment).

**III. Ford’s due process rights are not violated by Montana’s exercise of jurisdiction.**

Ford argues that jurisdiction violates due process because it did not “purposefully avail” itself of Montana’s market. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). This contention ignores Ford’s extensive Montana-based activities. Ford advertises, sells parts, and services vehicles in Montana, and it maintains dealerships and service centers in the state. *Ford Motor Co.*, 2020 WL 5997042, at *5. These activities demonstrate purposeful availment. *See id.* (citing *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475-76).

Ford also contends that jurisdiction is unreasonable because it would impose an undue burden. *See Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court of Cal., Solano Cty.*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987). However, Montana has a strong interest in adjudicating claims involving injuries occurring within its borders. *See id.* at 113-14. Ford’s burden of defending in Montana is outweighed by these interests. *See id.*

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly upheld jurisdiction over Ford. Gullett’s claims arise from Ford’s Montana-based activities, and Ford purposefully availed itself of the state’s market. The judgment below should be affirmed.
