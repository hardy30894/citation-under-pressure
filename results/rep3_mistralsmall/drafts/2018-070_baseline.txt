**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A GAP IN FEDERAL COVERAGE BEFORE STATE LAW MAY BE ADOPTED AS SURROGATE FEDERAL LAW.

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) provides that “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations of the Secretary now in effect or hereafter adopted, the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State … are declared to be the law of the United States” for fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). This Court has long recognized that the OCSLA “does not operate as a blank check for importing state law wholesale.” Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 357 (1969). Rather, state law is adopted as surrogate federal law only where federal law is “deficient in the provisions necessary to furnish suitable remedies.” Id. at 357 (citing 42 U.S.C. § 1988(a)); see also Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson, 404 U.S. 97, 103 (1971) (state law applies under OCSLA only where federal law “is silent or inadequate”).

The Fifth Circuit’s “gap-filling” test is consistent with this Court’s precedents. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law applies under § 1333(a)(2)(A) only where “there is a significant void or gap in federal law.” This test was reaffirmed in *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990), where the court explained that state law is adopted only when federal law “is silent or inadequate to govern the subject matter at issue.” The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—allowing state law to apply whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law—ignores this foundational principle.

II. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME THAT LEAVES NO GAP FOR STATE WAGE AND HOUR LAWS.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) is a comprehensive federal statute governing minimum wages, overtime, and related employment practices. See 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq. The FLSA includes an express savings clause permitting state laws that are “more protective” of workers, but this clause does not displace the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement. See id. § 218 (“Nothing in this chapter shall excuse noncompliance with any Federal or State law or municipal ordinance establishing a minimum wage higher than the minimum wage established under this chapter.”). As this Court has recognized, the FLSA “was designed to be a floor beneath which no worker could be forced to go, not a ceiling above which employers could not go.” *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 737 (1981).

The Department of Labor’s regulations confirm that the FLSA provides a complete framework for wage and hour disputes. See 29 C.F.R. §§ 778.107, 785.14 (meal periods). Where federal law addresses a subject matter in detail, state law may not be imported under the OCSLA. See *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 357. The Ninth Circuit’s holding that state wage and hour laws apply whenever they are “not inconsistent” with the FLSA would render the OCSLA’s gap-filling requirement meaningless.

III. CALIFORNIA’S WAGE AND HOUR LAWS ARE PRECLUDED BY THE FLSA’S COMPREHENSIVE SCHEME.

Newton’s claims under California law—including meal period violations and overtime calculations—are governed by the FLSA. The FLSA’s savings clause does not authorize state laws that conflict with or duplicate federal standards. See *Sullivan v. Compass Envtl., Inc.*, 181 F.3d 1256, 1260 (11th Cir. 1999) (“The FLSA’s savings clause does not permit states to impose additional requirements that are inconsistent with the FLSA.”). Because the FLSA provides a complete remedy for wage and hour disputes, no gap exists for California law to fill.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that state wage and hour laws apply under the OCSLA whenever they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. State law may be adopted as surrogate federal law only where federal law is deficient. The FLSA is a comprehensive statute that leaves no gap for state regulation. This Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s judgment and remand with instructions to dismiss Newton’s state law claims.
