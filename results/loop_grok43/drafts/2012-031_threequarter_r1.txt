The district court abused its discretion by certifying the class absent adequate admissible evidence that antitrust damages are susceptible to class-wide measurement and quantification. Class actions demand that common questions predominate, a requirement rooted in the equitable origins of representative suits. Hansberry v. Lee, 311 U.S. 32, 41 (1940); Supreme Tribe of Ben Hur v. Cauble, 255 U.S. 356, 366 (1921); Smith v. Swormstedt, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 302 (1853).

In antitrust actions, damages must be established by evidence that is neither speculative nor individualized. Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946); Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931); Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co., 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927).

Where, as here, the proposed class spans thousands of subscribers across multiple counties, common proof of injury and damages is indispensable. Thomsen v. Cayser, 243 U.S. 66, 88 (1917); Continental Wall Paper Co. v. Louis Voight & Sons Co., 212 U.S. 227, 238 (1909). Standard Oil Co. v. United States, 221 U.S. 1, 75 (1911), confirms that antitrust remedies turn on concrete proof of harm, not abstract theories.

The record contains no such common, admissible evidence. Petitioners’ expert models rest on assumptions that vary by subscriber location, contract terms, and viewing preferences—precisely the individualized inquiries that preclude predominance. Because the district court accepted these models without requiring proof that damages can be measured uniformly for every class member, its certification order rests on an erroneous legal standard and must be reversed.
