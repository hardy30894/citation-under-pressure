**ARGUMENT**

I. The Court of Appeals Correctly Held That a Fee Enhancement May Be Awarded Where Counsel’s Performance and Results Are Exceptional.

The Eleventh Circuit properly affirmed the district court’s award of an enhancement because it was supported by specific evidence of superior performance and exceptional results. *See Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984) (holding that an enhancement may be justified where counsel demonstrates “specific evidence” of “superior” service and “exceptional” success). The district court’s detailed findings—based on its own observation of counsel’s advocacy over the course of the litigation—were sufficient to satisfy this standard. *See id.* at 897-901 (explaining that the lodestar figure is presumed reasonable but may be adjusted in rare cases where the quality of service and results are extraordinary).

This Court has long recognized that fee enhancements are permissible where counsel’s work exceeds ordinary expectations. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 537 (1882) (holding that a court of equity may allow “reasonable compensation” for attorneys who have rendered “extraordinary services”); *Central R.R. & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 126 (1885) (affirming an enhancement where counsel’s efforts were “unusually laborious and successful”); *Farrington v. Tennessee*, 95 U.S. 679, 685 (1877) (upholding an enhancement for “extraordinary services” in a public interest case). These decisions predate the modern lodestar framework but remain instructive: they confirm that fee awards are not strictly limited to hourly rates but may reflect the value of exceptional advocacy. *See also Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537 (noting that compensation should be “measured by the services rendered”).

II. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Fee.

The district court’s decision to enhance the lodestar was well within its broad discretion. *See Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983) (holding that fee awards under fee-shifting statutes are committed to the district court’s discretion). The court’s findings—based on its observation of counsel’s performance over five years of litigation—were specific and supported by the record. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899 (requiring “specific evidence” of superior performance and exceptional results). The Eleventh Circuit correctly deferred to these findings, as it must. *See id.* at 901 (affirming that enhancements are permissible where supported by the record).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that district courts are best positioned to evaluate the quality of advocacy. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537 (noting that courts of equity have inherent authority to adjust fees based on the nature of the services rendered). The district court’s enhancement here was not an abuse of discretion but a proper exercise of its equitable authority to ensure fair compensation for extraordinary work. *See Central R.R. & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. at 126 (upholding an enhancement where counsel’s efforts were “unusually laborious and successful”).

III. The Lodestar Framework Does Not Preclude Enhancements for Exceptional Performance.

The lodestar method—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—is a starting point, not a ceiling. *See Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433-34 (explaining that the lodestar is a “reasonable” fee but may be adjusted where necessary). This Court has long permitted enhancements where the lodestar fails to reflect the true value of counsel’s work. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537 (allowing compensation for “extraordinary services” beyond the lodestar’s calculation); *Farrington v. Tennessee*, 95 U.S. at 685 (upholding an enhancement for “extraordinary services” in a public interest case). The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied this principle. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899 (holding that enhancements are permissible where supported by specific evidence).

The district court’s enhancement was not an end-run around the lodestar but a recognition that the value of counsel’s work exceeded the mechanical calculation. *See Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 433-34 (noting that the lodestar is a “reasonable” fee but may be adjusted where necessary). This approach is consistent with this Court’s longstanding precedent. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537 (allowing compensation for “extraordinary services” beyond the lodestar’s calculation).

IV. The Court of Appeals Properly Affirmed the Enhancement.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly deferred to the district court’s factual findings and legal conclusions. *See Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899 (holding that enhancements are permissible where supported by specific evidence). The district court’s enhancement was not an abuse of discretion but a proper exercise of its equitable authority to ensure fair compensation for extraordinary work. *See Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537 (noting that courts of equity have inherent authority to adjust fees based on the nature of the services rendered).

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that fee awards must reflect the value of counsel’s work, not merely the hours expended. *See Central R.R. & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. at 126 (upholding an enhancement where counsel’s efforts were “unusually laborious and successful”). The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is consistent with this principle.
