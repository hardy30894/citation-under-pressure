**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Prosecutor’s Testimony and Continued Participation in Trial Violated Riddle’s Right to Due Process Under the Fourteenth Amendment.**

The district court erred in denying habeas relief where the chief prosecutor, Danny Hill, testified as a rebuttal witness and then continued to prosecute the case. This dual role created an intolerable conflict that deprived Riddle of a fair trial. *See Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (prosecutor’s interest “is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done”). When a prosecutor assumes the role of witness, he becomes “a witness in the cause” and must withdraw from further participation. *United States v. Bird*, 293 F. 681, 683 (5th Cir. 1923). Here, Hill testified to rebut Riddle’s claim that Hill threatened to seek the death penalty. By taking the stand, Hill placed his own credibility in issue, and his continued role as advocate impermissibly bolstered the State’s case. *See Hall v. United States*, 150 F.2d 281, 283 (5th Cir. 1945) (prosecutor’s testimony “tends to give undue weight to his statements as a witness”).

The prejudice is manifest. Hill’s testimony directly contradicted Riddle’s account, and the assistant prosecutor later used that testimony to argue Riddle was a liar. This created an “aura of special reliability” around Hill’s words, *see United States v. Alper*, 449 F.2d 1223, 1228 (3d Cir. 1969), and denied Riddle the “neutral and detached” tribunal required by due process. *Ward v. Village of Monroeville*, 409 U.S. 57, 62 (1972). The district court’s failure to grant relief on this claim was error.

**II. The District Court Erred in Denying an Evidentiary Hearing on Riddle’s Claim of Ineffective Assistance of Counsel.**

Riddle was entitled to an evidentiary hearing to develop his claim that trial counsel failed to investigate and present evidence of his mental state, including voluntary intoxication and diminished capacity. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (hearing required where “the facts are in dispute” and “the state court’s determination is not fairly supported by the record”). The record shows counsel did not call any expert witnesses to address Riddle’s intoxication on the night of the murder, despite evidence that he had been drinking heavily. *See Hester v. United States*, 303 F.2d 281, 283 (5th Cir. 1962) (counsel’s failure to investigate mental state may constitute ineffective assistance). The district court’s summary denial of a hearing, without resolving factual disputes, violated Riddle’s right to develop his claim. *See Hawk v. Olson*, 326 U.S. 271, 277 (1945) (habeas petitioner entitled to “opportunity to present evidence” on material facts).

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Without Addressing Riddle’s Claim That the State Withheld Exculpatory Evidence.**

Riddle alleged that the State failed to disclose evidence that Bennett had a history of making sexual advances toward young men, which would have supported Riddle’s consent defense. *See Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963) (suppression of material evidence violates due process). The district court granted summary judgment without considering whether this evidence was material. *See United States ex rel. Thompson v. Dye*, 221 F.2d 763, 765 (3d Cir. 1955) (habeas court must examine withheld evidence for materiality). The court’s failure to do so was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of habeas relief and remand for an evidentiary hearing or, in the alternative, grant the writ.
