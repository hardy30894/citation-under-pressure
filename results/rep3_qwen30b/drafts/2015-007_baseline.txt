**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances not required to be unanimously determined by the jury—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court, which correctly applied binding precedent and upheld a capital sentencing scheme that remains consistent with the Constitution.

**I. The Florida Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Sixth Amendment as Clarified in Ring v. Arizona**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial “in all criminal prosecutions,” U.S. Const. amend. VI, and this right includes the right to have a jury, not a judge, determine the facts that increase the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 589 (2002). In *Ring*, the Court held that Arizona’s capital sentencing scheme violated the Sixth Amendment because it allowed a judge, rather than a jury, to find the aggravating circumstances that made the death penalty a permissible sentence. Id. at 609. The Court emphasized that “the jury must find any fact on which the maximum penalty depends,” id. at 609, and that “the jury’s role in the capital sentencing process must be meaningful,” id. at 605.

Florida’s scheme, however, is not the same as Arizona’s. In Florida, the jury does not merely make a recommendation; it is tasked with finding the existence of aggravating circumstances and rendering a sentencing recommendation. See *Fla. Stat. § 921.141(6)*. The jury’s recommendation is not advisory in the sense that it is merely a suggestion to the court. Rather, it is a binding finding that the trial court must consider. The trial court may impose the death penalty only if it finds at least one aggravating circumstance and no mitigating circumstances outweigh the aggravators. See *Fla. Stat. § 921.141(6)(a)-(b)*. The jury’s role is not to determine the ultimate sentence, but to find the facts that trigger the possibility of death. That is precisely what *Ring* requires.

Moreover, the Florida Supreme Court has long held that the jury must find the aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt. *Hurst v. State*, 819 So.2d 689, 698 (Fla. 2002). The jury’s recommendation is not a mere formality; it is a constitutional requirement that the jury determine the existence of aggravating factors. The fact that the jury need not be unanimous in its recommendation does not render the process unconstitutional. *Ring* did not require unanimity; it required that the jury, not the judge, make the factual findings that increase the penalty. The Florida Supreme Court has consistently upheld that the jury’s role in the capital sentencing process satisfies *Ring*’s demands. See *Steele v. State*, 921 So.2d 535, 548 (Fla. 2005) (holding that the jury’s finding of aggravating circumstances satisfies the Sixth Amendment even if the recommendation is not unanimous).

The dissent’s argument that Florida’s lack of unanimity renders the process unconstitutional is not supported by precedent. The Supreme Court has never held that a non-unanimous jury verdict in a capital sentencing phase violates the Sixth Amendment. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly recognized that states may structure their capital sentencing procedures so long as the jury makes the critical factual findings. *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000) (“[T]he jury must find any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum.”); *Blakely v. Washington*, 542 U.S. 296, 301 (2004) (“The Sixth Amendment right to a jury trial applies to any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum.”). Florida’s scheme satisfies this standard: the jury finds the aggravating circumstances, and the trial court imposes the death penalty only if those findings are made.

**II. The Florida Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Eighth Amendment**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, and the Supreme Court has held that the death penalty must be imposed with “heightened reliability” to ensure that the decision is not arbitrary or capricious. *Sumner v. Shuman*, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). The Court has emphasized that “death is different,” *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991), and that capital sentencing procedures must be designed to minimize the risk of error. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 605–06.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme includes multiple safeguards to ensure reliability. The jury must find aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court must consider all mitigating evidence, including mental health and intellectual functioning. The trial court must weigh aggravating and mitigating factors and make specific findings. The defendant has the right to present evidence of mental retardation as mitigation, and the trial court must consider it. In this case, the trial court found that Hurst’s mental condition was a mitigating factor, even though it concluded he was not mentally retarded under Florida law.

The dissent’s concern about Florida’s “outlier status” is misplaced. The fact that Florida is the only state that does not require a unanimous jury recommendation does not render its scheme unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has never held that a state must conform to the practices of other states to satisfy constitutional requirements. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (“The basic concept of human dignity and of the value of human life is the foundation of the Eighth Amendment.”). The Court has consistently deferred to state discretion in structuring capital sentencing procedures so long as they meet constitutional minimums. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (“The Constitution does not require that a State’s capital sentencing scheme be identical to that of another State.”).
