The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its decisions in Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), provide guidance.

In Bullington, the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to capital sentencing proceedings because they share "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" (Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439). The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding resembled a trial, with a jury making a determination based on proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Similarly, in Rumsey, the Court applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a capital sentencing proceeding in Arizona, where a judge made a factual determination regarding the existence of aggravating circumstances.

The same principles that led the Court to apply the Double Jeopardy Clause in capital sentencing proceedings should apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. California's statutory scheme for determining prior conviction allegations under Penal Code section 1025 provides a trial-like proceeding, with the prosecution required to prove the allegations beyond a reasonable doubt (People v. Tenner, 6 Cal.4th 559, 566 (1993)). The accused enjoys the privilege against self-incrimination (In re Yurko, 10 Cal.3d 857, 863, fn. 5 (1974)), and the rules of evidence apply (People v. Reed, 13 Cal.4th 217, 224 (1996)).

The Supreme Court's decision in Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), does not undermine the application of Bullington to noncapital sentencing proceedings. In Caspari, the Court noted that Bullington and Rumsey were capital cases, but it did not hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause is limited to capital cases. Instead, the Court addressed whether the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to a noncapital sentencing proceeding was a new rule of law for purposes of habeas corpus review.

The California Supreme Court's attempt to distinguish Bullington is unpersuasive. The court argued that the procedural protections in a section 1025 trial rest on statutory grounds, not federal constitutional grounds. However, this distinction is irrelevant, as the Supreme Court in Bullington applied the Double Jeopardy Clause to a sentencing proceeding that was governed by state statute, not federal constitutional law.

The Double Jeopardy Clause should apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings like the one in this case. The trial court's determination of the prior conviction allegation was based on insufficient evidence, and the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial on that allegation. The judgment of the California Court of Appeal should be affirmed.
