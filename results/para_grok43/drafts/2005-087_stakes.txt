ARGUMENT

Arizona law permissibly confines expert mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense. The Due Process Clause affords no independent right to introduce the same evidence to negate mens rea.

States possess “broad latitude” to define the elements of crimes and the scope of affirmative defenses. Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 43 (1996). That latitude includes the authority to “regulate the admissibility of evidence” without violating fundamental fairness. Id. at 42. Arizona’s rule is such a regulation. It channels mental-disease testimony to the defense on which the defendant bears the burden of proof, rather than allowing the same testimony to relieve the State of its burden to prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt. See Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 207 (1977) (“the Due Process Clause does not require a State to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact” that might negate an element).

This Court has repeatedly sustained analogous limitations. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799 (1952), the Court upheld a requirement that the defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the claim that due process demanded admission of the same evidence to defeat the prosecution’s case-in-chief. Likewise, in Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), the Court held that Congress could preclude psychiatric testimony offered solely to show diminished capacity. Montana v. Egelhoff confirmed the principle in the context of voluntary intoxication, holding that a state may exclude evidence that would otherwise be relevant to mens rea. 518 U.S. at 56.

Clark’s proposed right would upend this framework. It would convert every insanity claim into a simultaneous mens-rea defense, shifting to the prosecution the practical burden of disproving mental disease in every case. Nothing in the Due Process Clause compels that result. See Martin v. Ohio, 480 U.S. 228, 236 (1987) (state may require defendant to prove self-defense even though the evidence may also bear on intent).

The Arizona Court of Appeals correctly applied these precedents. Its judgment should be affirmed.
