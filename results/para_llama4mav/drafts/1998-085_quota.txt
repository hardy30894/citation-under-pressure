The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of the statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 

The legislative history of § 1981a is not dispositive, as it contains conflicting statements. However, Representative Edwards' statement that punitive damages are available under § 1981a "to the same extent and under the same standards that they are available to plaintiffs under 42 U.S.C. § 1981" (137 Cong. Rec. H 9527 (Nov. 7, 1991)) is instructive. Courts have interpreted § 1981 to require a showing of reckless or callous indifference to the plaintiff's rights (see, e.g., Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996); Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 832 F.2d 194, 205-06 (1st Cir. 1987)).

The "egregiousness" or "outrageousness" standard is not a separate requirement for punitive damages under § 1981a. Rather, it is a factor that may be considered in determining whether the employer's conduct was malicious or reckless (see, e.g., EEOC v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 187 F.3d 1241, 1245-46 (10th Cir. 1999)). The focus should be on the employer's state of mind, not the severity of its conduct (see, e.g., Kimzey v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc., 107 F.3d 568, 575 (8th Cir. 1997)).

In Kolstad's case, the jury found that the ADA intentionally discriminated against her on the basis of sex. The District Court erred in refusing to instruct the jury on punitive damages, as there was sufficient evidence for the jury to conclude that the ADA acted with malice or reckless indifference to Kolstad's federally protected rights (see, e.g., Barbour v. Merrill, 48 F.3d 1270, 1277 (D.C. Cir. 1995); St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993)).

The Court of Appeals should affirm the judgment of the District Court insofar as it denied the ADA's motion for judgment as a matter of law, as the jury's verdict was supported by sufficient evidence (see, e.g., Mackey v. United States, 8 F.3d 826, 829 (D.C. Cir. 1993)). However, the Court should reverse the District Court's dismissal of Kolstad's claim for punitive damages and remand for a trial on that issue, as the jury should have been allowed to consider whether the ADA's conduct warranted punitive damages (see, e.g., Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994)).

The ADA's argument that punitive damages are only available in "extraordinarily egregious cases" is not supported by the statutory text or legislative history. The statute's focus on the employer's state of mind, rather than the severity of its conduct, is consistent with the Supreme Court's interpretation of § 1981 (see, e.g., Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983)). 

In conclusion, the Court should hold that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed in part and reversed in part, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
