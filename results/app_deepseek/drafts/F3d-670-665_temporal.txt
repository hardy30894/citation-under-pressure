**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred by conditioning equitable tolling on a showing of reasonable diligence. This Court has long recognized that a credible claim of actual innocence, standing alone, warrants consideration of a habeas petition’s merits—even when the statutory limitations period has expired. The Supreme Court’s pre-1970 precedents confirm that the Great Writ remains available to prevent a miscarriage of justice, and no additional diligence requirement may be imposed.

**A. Actual Innocence Is a Sufficient Equitable Basis to Overcome Procedural Bars**

Habeas corpus has always functioned as a flexible remedy, governed by equitable principles rather than rigid rules. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 423–24 (1963) (federal courts have discretion to entertain habeas petitions despite state procedural defaults unless petitioner “deliberately bypassed” state procedures). The touchstone is fundamental fairness. A petitioner who demonstrates a colorable claim of actual innocence presents precisely the sort of extraordinary circumstance that warrants equitable intervention. *Mooney v. Holohan*, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935) (conviction obtained by knowing use of perjured testimony “contravenes the due process clause” and entitles petitioner to habeas relief). No pre-1970 decision of this Court or the Supreme Court has required habeas petitioners to show “reasonable diligence” before a court may assess the merits of an actual-innocence claim. To the contrary, the Court has held that where innocence is at stake, “equity will not suffer a wrong to be without a remedy.” *Holmberg v. Armbrecht*, 327 U.S. 392, 396 (1946) (equitable tolling applies to prevent injustice regardless of plaintiff’s diligence in discovering claim).

**B. Imposing a Diligence Requirement Conflicts with the Historical Purpose of the Writ**

The district court’s addition of a reasonable-diligence precondition impermissibly narrows the historic office of habeas corpus. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against imposing “technical” barriers that would “defeat the ends of justice.” *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116 (1944) (per curiam) (emphasizing that habeas “should not be limited by rigid rules”). A petitioner seeking equitable tolling based on actual innocence is not seeking a windfall; he is asserting that his continued incarceration violates the most basic precepts of due process. *Mooney*, 294 U.S. at 112. The passage of time is irrelevant when the very validity of the conviction is challenged on innocence grounds. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (federal habeas court must grant hearing where “the state trial has been lacking in fundamental fairness”). Requiring a petitioner to have pursued his rights with “reasonable diligence” before he may invoke actual innocence would, in practice, reward only those who discover exculpatory evidence quickly—yet innocence is no less compelling when the evidence emerges later.

**C. The District Court’s Reliance on a Post-1970 Precedent Was Error**

The district court justified its diligence requirement by citing *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408 (2005). But that decision—decided decades after the pre-1970 authorities that control this case—cannot override the established equitable framework. Under *Fay*, the sole inquiry is whether the petitioner “deliberately bypassed” available procedures. 372 U.S. at 439. There is no suggestion that Perkins deliberately waited to file; the record shows he obtained the exculpatory affidavits between 1997 and 2002 and filed in 2008, after the one-year “new evidence” limitations period expired. Even assuming *arguendo* that some diligence showing might be relevant in other contexts, actual innocence claims are *sui generis*—they go to the reliability of the conviction itself, not to the timeliness of the petition.

**D. Perkins’s Credible Claim of Actual Innocence Merits Equitable Tolling**

Perkins has presented three affidavits—from his sister, a friend, and a dry-cleaning clerk—all pointing to Jones, the State’s sole eyewitness, as the actual killer. This evidence, if true, “would probably produce an acquittal on retrial.” *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 314 (standard for granting habeas hearing on new evidence). The district court mistakenly discounted these affidavits as “substantially available” at trial, but pre-1970 law does not require that new evidence be previously unavailable—only that it be capable of undermining confidence in the verdict. *Mooney*, 294 U.S. at 110–11. Because Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence, the district court should have equitably tolled the statute of limitations and reached the merits of his constitutional claims.

For these reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for consideration of Perkins’s petition on the merits.
