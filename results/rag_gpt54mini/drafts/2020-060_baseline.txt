**Argument**

Guam should bear responsibility for the Ordot Dump cleanup costs because Guam’s 2004 consent decree with EPA triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), which is the exclusive CERCLA remedy for those costs and carries a three-year limitations period. The Supreme Court has made clear that CERCLA “expressly permits the recovery of any ‘necessary costs of response,’” and also that Congress created “two express avenues for contribution” in § 113. *Meghrig v. KFC W. Inc.*, 516 U.S. 479, 483–84 (1996); *Cooper Indus., Inc. v. Aviall Servs., Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 166–67 (2004). Once a party has resolved liability in a judicially approved settlement, § 113—not § 107—controls. *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 166–67.

That rule fits this case. EPA’s 2002 Clean Water Act complaint sought injunctive relief requiring Guam to “submit plans and a compliance schedule for a cover system” and to “complet[e] construction of the cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate.” Guam and EPA then entered a consent decree requiring Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the dump, and design and install the cover system. A consent decree is the “compromise of a disputed claim,” and federal law gives such decrees their operative force according to their terms. *United States v. ITT Cont’l Baking Co.*, 420 U.S. 223, 236–37 (1975). Here, the decree was judicially approved and imposed concrete obligations tied to the very response action at issue. That is enough to resolve liability within § 113(f)(3)(B). See *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167.

Guam’s contrary position would nullify § 113’s structure. CERCLA’s contribution provision was enacted to address the gap identified in *Cooper* and to give PRPs an express route for allocation after settlement or a civil action. 543 U.S. at 161–67. If Guam could invoke § 107 years after entering a consent decree that required it to close and cover the dump, the three-year limitations period in § 113(g)(3) would be meaningless. Courts do not construe statutes to defeat the limits Congress chose. *Hallstrom v. Tillamook Cnty.*, 493 U.S. 20, 31 (1990). And where Congress has spoken plainly, courts must apply the statute as written. *Meghrig*, 516 U.S. at 484.

Guam cannot avoid that result by emphasizing that the decree recites no “finding or admission of liability.” A settlement need not include an admission of fault to resolve liability. Parties often settle “without any finding or admission of liability,” yet still undertake obligations that settle the matter. *ITT Cont’l Baking*, 420 U.S. at 236–37. The operative question is not whether Guam confessed liability, but whether the decree resolved its liability for some of the response action. The decree’s requirement that Guam close the dump and install a cover system did exactly that. See *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 166–67.

Nor does Guam’s territorial status alter the CERCLA analysis. Guam remains an unincorporated territory, but Congress has long treated Guam’s government as a juridical entity capable of being sued and of suing in federal court. *Ngiraingas v. Sanchez*, 495 U.S. 182, 187–88 (1990); *Chase Manhattan Bank v. S. Acres Dev. Co.*, 434 U.S. 236, 236–37 (1978). Nothing in CERCLA exempts Guam from the ordinary consequences of a judicially approved environmental settlement. To the contrary, CERCLA’s text applies to “any person” liable for response costs and authorizes contribution against “any other person” liable under § 107(a). *Meghrig*, 516 U.S. at 483–84. Guam falls within that statutory framework like any other PRP. See *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 161, 167.

Finally, Guam’s suggestion that the Clean Water Act suit could not trigger CERCLA contribution because it was not a CERCLA case misunderstands § 113(f)(3)(B). The provision turns on whether the party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” CERCLA does not require the settlement to arise in a CERCLA enforcement action. The decree here required cleanup measures that CERCLA itself identifies as remedial action, including collection of leachate and runoff. See *Meghrig*, 516 U.S. at 483–84. Guam therefore resolved liability for part of the response action and had to proceed, if at all, under § 113. Because it waited more than three years, its claim is time-barred. *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167; *Hallstrom*, 493 U.S. at 31.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
