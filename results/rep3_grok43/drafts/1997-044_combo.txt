The discharge exception in 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) reaches only those debts “obtained by” actual fraud. This language confines the bar to the sum actually procured through the fraudulent conduct. Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (exception limited to “positive fraud”); Friend v. Talcott, 228 U.S. 27, 38 (1913) (distinguishing the debt “obtained” from separate liabilities arising later).

Statutory treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act were not obtained by the debtor; they were imposed by operation of law after the rents had already been collected. The Court has long held that such additional recoveries fall outside the fraud exception. Crawford v. Burke, 195 U.S. 176, 193 (1904); McIntyre v. Kavanaugh, 242 U.S. 138, 141 (1916).

The fresh-start policy of the Bankruptcy Act reinforces this limit. A debtor who has surrendered his property is entitled to discharge of all remaining obligations except those Congress expressly excepted. Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Treble damages are not among the enumerated exceptions. Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915).

Nor may courts enlarge the statutory list by treating punitive awards as part of the original debt. Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473, 488 (1904) (courts may not expand exceptions beyond their terms). The same principle precludes reading the treble-damage provision into § 523(a)(2)(A). Strang v. Bradner, 114 U.S. 555, 559 (1885).

Equity likewise forbids expanding nondischargeable claims beyond the statute. Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295, 304 (1939) (bankruptcy courts apply equitable principles only within statutory bounds). Because the treble damages were not obtained by fraud, they are dischargeable.

The judgment below should be reversed.
