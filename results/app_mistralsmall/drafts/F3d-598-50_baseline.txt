**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING OPINION TESTIMONY THAT IMPROPERLY VOUCHED FOR THE APPELLEES’ CREDIBILITY AND USURPED THE JURY’S ROLE IN DETERMINING PROBABLE CAUSE.**

The admission of opinion testimony that vouchs for a witness’s credibility or resolves legal questions violates the Federal Rules of Evidence and this Court’s precedents. *See United States v. Dukagjini*, 326 F.3d 45, 54 (2d Cir. 2003) (holding that a witness may not “express an opinion on the credibility of another witness”); *United States v. Mejia*, 552 F.3d 104, 112 (2d Cir. 2009) (stating that witnesses may not “testify in the form of legal conclusions”). Here, the District Court permitted two Assistant District Attorneys and a police lieutenant to opine on the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, as well as on whether probable cause existed for the arrests. This testimony was plainly erroneous.

The Second Circuit has repeatedly condemned such vouching. In *Dukagjini*, the court reversed a conviction where a government witness testified that another witness was “credible” and “truthful.” 326 F.3d at 54. Similarly, in *Mejia*, the court found reversible error where a detective opined that the defendant’s version of events was “not believable.” 552 F.3d at 112. The same principles apply here. The ADAs’ and lieutenant’s statements that Ramos and Rivera were credible and acted with probable cause were inadmissible vouching that improperly bolstered the appellees’ case. *See also United States v. Garcia*, 29 F.3d 101, 105 (2d Cir. 1994) (holding that opinion testimony on credibility is impermissible even if the witness purports to base the opinion on demeanor).

The appellees’ reliance on *United States v. Locascio*, 6 F.3d 924 (2d Cir. 1993), is misplaced. In *Locascio*, the court permitted a witness to testify that a defendant’s story was “not believable,” but only because the witness’s opinion was based on his own observations and the defendant’s demeanor. 6 F.3d at 931. Here, by contrast, the ADAs and lieutenant did not base their opinions on personal observations of the incident; they merely endorsed the appellees’ version of events. This is precisely the type of improper vouching condemned in *Dukagjini* and *Mejia*.

Moreover, the testimony invaded the jury’s province by resolving the ultimate legal question of probable cause. *See Mejia*, 552 F.3d at 112 (noting that witnesses may not “testify in the form of legal conclusions”). The ADAs and lieutenant opined that the appellees had probable cause to arrest the appellants, a determination that the jury was solely responsible for making. This error was not harmless, as the testimony directly addressed the two most contested issues in the case: the appellees’ credibility and the existence of probable cause. *See Hynes v. Coughlin*, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996) (requiring reversal where erroneously admitted evidence “may have influenced the jury’s verdict”).

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY DENIED APPELLANTS’ MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW, AS THE SECURITY CAMERA PHOTOGRAPHS DO NOT DEFINITIVELY CONTRADICT THE APPELLEES’ ACCOUNT.**

Appellants argue that the security camera photographs “blatantly contradict” the appellees’ version of events and entitle them to judgment as a matter of law. Appellants’ Br. 31. This argument fails. The photographs do not definitively resolve the dispute, as they are ambiguous and susceptible to multiple interpretations. *See Scott v. Harris*, 550 U.S. 372, 381 (2007) (holding that a court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party when ruling on a motion for summary judgment). The District Court therefore did not err in denying the motion.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY THAT, UNDER NEW YORK LAW, OBSTRUCTING GOVERNMENTAL ADMINISTRATION REQUIRES A LAWFUL ARREST.**

Under New York law, obstructing governmental administration in the second degree, N.Y. Penal Law § 195.05, requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *See People v. Case*, 42 N.Y.2d 98, 101 (1977) (“The offense of obstructing governmental administration ... requires that the officer be engaged in the performance of a lawful duty.”). The District Court failed to instruct the jury accordingly, depriving the appellants of a critical element of their defense.

The Second Circuit has held that a district court must instruct the jury on all elements of a state law claim. *See, e.g., United States v. Pabon-Cruz*, 391 F.3d 86, 94 (2d Cir. 2004). Here, the appellees’ theory of the case hinged on the allegation that Higgenbottom interfered with a lawful arrest. The failure to instruct the jury that the arrest must have been lawful was reversible error.

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FAILING TO INSTRUCT THE JURY ON PUNITIVE DAMAGES, AS APPALLANTS’ VERSION OF EVENTS WOULD HAVE SUPPORTED A FINDING OF MALICE.**

Under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is motivated by malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights. *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983). The District Court refused to instruct the jury on punitive damages, despite evidence that Ramos and Rivera fabricated charges against Cameron out of malice.

This Court has held that punitive damages are appropriate where a defendant’s conduct is “motivated by evil motive or intent, or ... involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” *Cruz v. City of New York*, 761 F.2d 154, 156 (2d Cir. 1985) (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, the appellants presented evidence that Ramos and Rivera knowingly fabricated a story to justify the arrests, which would support a finding of malice. The District Court’s refusal to instruct the jury on punitive damages was therefore erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the District Court should be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with this opinion.
