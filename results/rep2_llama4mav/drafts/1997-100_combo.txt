The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment (Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969)), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has established that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal (North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)). Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its precedents suggest that the Clause's protections extend beyond traditional trial settings.

In Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163 (1873), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits not only a second trial for the same offense but also the imposition of multiple punishments for the same offense. This principle is relevant to sentencing proceedings, where the determination of a prior conviction allegation can significantly impact the defendant's sentence. The Court has also recognized that certain sentencing proceedings share characteristics with trials on guilt or innocence, making the Double Jeopardy Clause applicable (United States v. Ball, 163 U.S. 662 (1896)).

The respondent argues that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings, relying on Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949). However, Williams is distinguishable because it involved a sentencing proceeding where the judge considered information outside the record, and the Court held that the Due Process Clause did not require a trial-like procedure. In contrast, California's statutory scheme provides a trial-like procedure for determining prior conviction allegations (Penal Code § 1025).

The Supreme Court's decision in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184 (1957), is instructive. In Green, the Court held that a defendant who was convicted of second-degree murder and implicitly acquitted of first-degree murder could not be retried for first-degree murder after successfully appealing the conviction. Similarly, in the present case, the Court of Appeal's determination that the evidence was insufficient to support the prior conviction allegation is akin to an acquittal, and retrial would violate the Double Jeopardy Clause.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause is not limited to traditional trial settings. In Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to appellate reversals of convictions based on insufficient evidence. The same principle should apply to sentencing proceedings where the determination of a prior conviction allegation is at issue.

In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15 (1919), the Court held that a defendant who was convicted of first-degree murder and sentenced to life imprisonment could be retried and sentenced to death after successfully appealing the conviction. However, Stroud is distinguishable because it involved a retrial on the underlying offense, not a sentencing proceeding.

The Cramer v. United States, 325 U.S. 1 (1945), decision also supports the petitioner's argument. In Cramer, the Court emphasized the importance of protecting the defendant's rights against double jeopardy, stating that "the safeguard provided by the Constitution is against a second trial" (id. at 36).
