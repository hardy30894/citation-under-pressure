**Argument**

A district court’s omission of the Rule 32 appeal advisement does not entitle a prisoner to habeas relief where the defendant already knew of the right to appeal and deliberately chose not to pursue one. Habeas relief is reserved for errors that produce a cognizable injury, not for every deviation from a procedural rule. See Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52, 59 (1985) (habeas relief requires a showing of prejudice from the challenged error); Blackledge v. Allison, 431 U.S. 63, 75-76 (1977) (allegations entitling a defendant to relief must raise a factual issue showing a real basis for setting aside the judgment). That principle controls here.

Rule 32’s advisement requirement is designed to ensure that a defendant has notice of appellate rights. But when the record shows the defendant already possessed that notice, the rule’s purpose has been satisfied and the omission is harmless. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error analysis. This Court has repeatedly held that collateral relief does not follow automatically from a procedural violation where the movant cannot show a substantial effect on his ability to obtain review. In Hill, the Court rejected habeas relief absent prejudice from counsel’s alleged failure to advise the defendant about parole eligibility, explaining that the petitioner had not alleged the kind of prejudice that would justify relief. 474 U.S. at 59. The same logic applies here: if the defendant knew of his right to appeal and elected not to appeal, the omission did not cause the loss of appellate review.

Peguero’s contrary position—that any Rule 32 violation requires vacatur—would convert a procedural omission into a per se ground for collateral attack, regardless of whether the defendant was actually misled or harmed. That approach conflicts with the Court’s treatment of postconviction claims generally. In Blackledge, the Court emphasized that collateral relief depends on allegations that, if proved, would entitle the prisoner to relief, and that summary disposition is appropriate where the record reveals no material basis for setting aside the conviction. 431 U.S. at 75-76. Likewise, in Lane v. Williams, 455 U.S. 624, 631-33 (1982), the Court declined to grant habeas relief based on completed sentencing consequences absent a live injury traceable to the alleged defect. A failure to recite appellate rights, when the defendant already knew them, is no more than a technical error without a cognizable injury.

The federal rules themselves confirm that relief is not automatic for every omission. Rule-based claims are governed by ordinary harmless-error principles unless the violation infects the proceeding in a way that prejudices substantial rights. The Court has long distinguished between errors that justify collateral relief and errors that do not. See Canizio v. New York, 327 U.S. 82, 85-86 (1946) (rejecting collateral relief where the alleged procedural defect did not warrant upsetting the conviction); Woods v. Nierstheimer, 328 U.S. 211, 214-15 (1946) (recognizing that collateral review turns on whether the alleged federal error actually supports relief). Here, the district court found as a fact that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at sentencing. That finding defeats any claim that the omission affected his decisionmaking.

Nor does the availability of habeas relief turn on the mere existence of a rule violation divorced from prejudice. In the plea context, the Court has required a showing that the error mattered to the defendant’s choice. Hill, 474 U.S. at 59. The same requirement should govern a Rule 32 omission: if the defendant was already aware of the right to appeal, he cannot show that the court’s silence caused him to forgo review. That is especially true here, where the district court credited evidence that Peguero elected not to appeal despite knowing he could.

Peguero’s proposed rule would also undermine finality for no corresponding benefit. Habeas is an extraordinary remedy; it is not a vehicle for windfall relief when the asserted defect produced no actual harm. See Caspari v. Bohlen, 510 U.S. 383, 389-90 (1994) (reaffirming the Court’s reluctance to expand collateral remedies absent a compelling basis in existing law); Dugger v. Adams, 489 U.S. 401, 409-10 (1989) (collateral review is constrained by the usual limits applicable to postconviction litigation). The Third Circuit properly refused to order vacatur on a record showing that the purpose of Rule 32 had already been fulfilled.

Because Peguero knew of his right to appeal and knowingly chose not to appeal, the district court’s failure to reiterate that right was harmless and affords no basis for habeas relief. The judgment below should be affirmed.
