**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee as interpreted by this Court. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002), held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find the facts that authorize a death sentence. Florida’s scheme satisfies this requirement because it entrusts the jury with the critical factfinding function. *See id.* at 602 (explaining that the Sixth Amendment “requires a jury determination of any fact on which the legislature conditions an increase in the maximum punishment”).

Florida law directs the jury to weigh statutory aggravating factors and consider mitigating evidence before rendering an advisory sentence. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2) (2015). The trial court then independently weighs the aggravating and mitigating factors to impose the sentence. *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam) (upholding Florida’s scheme because the jury’s role is advisory). This Court has repeatedly affirmed that Florida’s system does not violate the Sixth Amendment. *See, e.g., Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647-48 (1990) (rejecting Sixth Amendment challenge to Florida’s scheme).

Petitioner’s reliance on *Ring* is misplaced. *Ring* invalidated Arizona’s scheme because it allowed a judge to find an aggravating factor and impose death. 536 U.S. at 609. Florida’s scheme, by contrast, requires the jury to find aggravating factors and render an advisory sentence. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.141(2). The trial court’s independent weighing does not offend the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s factfinding is the “constitutionally indispensable” component of the sentencing process. *Ring*, 536 U.S. at 609.

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

Florida’s scheme also withstands Eighth Amendment scrutiny. The Eighth Amendment permits a state to entrust the jury with an advisory sentencing role, so long as the ultimate sentencing decision is guided by statutory factors and subject to meaningful judicial review. *See, e.g., Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 190-95 (1976) (upholding Georgia’s guided discretion statute).

Petitioner’s argument that Florida’s scheme is unconstitutional because it does not require a unanimous jury recommendation is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. In *Hildwin*, the Court rejected a similar challenge, holding that the Eighth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. 490 U.S. at 640-41. The Court reasoned that Florida’s scheme provides sufficient safeguards to ensure reliability, including the jury’s advisory role and the trial court’s independent weighing of aggravating and mitigating factors. *Id.*

Moreover, Florida’s scheme does not deviate from national norms in a way that offends the Eighth Amendment. *See, e.g., Kansas v. Marsh*, 548 U.S. 163, 177-78 (2006) (upholding Kansas’ scheme despite its lack of unanimity requirement). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that states enjoy “wide discretion” in designing capital sentencing procedures. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 199. Florida’s scheme falls within this discretion.

**III. FLORIDA’S TREATMENT OF MENTAL RETARDATION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT.**

Petitioner’s Eighth Amendment challenge to Florida’s mental retardation procedures also fails. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids the execution of mentally retarded individuals. However, the Court left to the states the task of defining and enforcing this restriction. *Id.*

Florida has done so through a statutory framework that requires a defendant to prove mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence. Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4) (2015). This scheme comports with *Atkins* because it provides a meaningful mechanism for identifying mentally retarded individuals. *See, e.g., Florida v. Allen*, 858 So. 2d 948, 952 (Fla. 2003) (upholding Florida’s mental retardation procedures under *Atkins*).

Petitioner’s argument that Florida’s scheme violates the Eighth Amendment because it does not permit the jury to determine mental retardation as an absolute bar is unavailing. The trial court, not the jury, is tasked with making the ultimate determination of mental retardation under Florida law. *See* Fla. Stat. § 921.137(4). This allocation of responsibility does not offend the Eighth Amendment, as it ensures that the decision is made by a judge applying a clear legal standard. *See, e.g., Walton*, 497 U.S. at 647-48 (upholding Florida’s scheme despite judge’s role in sentencing).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
