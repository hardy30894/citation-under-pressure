## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot's RLA Conspiracy Claim Because Genuine Disputes of Material Fact Existed

Summary judgment is proper only when “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 247-48 (1986). All inferences must be drawn in favor of the nonmoving party. *Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp.*, 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). Smoot presented direct evidence that UTU Vice-Chairman Earley—appointed to represent him—argued *against* Smoot’s interests during the executive session of the Public Law Board hearing, and that Earley later colluded with CSX to suppress the recording. This evidence created triable issues regarding whether UTU and CSX conspired to deny Smoot’s stock claim in violation of the Railway Labor Act. The district court improperly resolved these factual disputes on summary judgment, contrary to *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986). A plaintiff need only “set forth specific facts showing that there is a genuine issue for trial.” *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 256. Smoot met that burden.

### II. The District Court Erred in Finding That Smoot Violated the Federal Wiretap Act

**A. The Executive Session Was Not a “Private” Conversation Under the Act**

The Wiretap Act protects only communications “uttered by a person exhibiting an expectation that such communication is not subject to interception under circumstances justifying such expectation.” 18 U.S.C. § 2510(2). The Supreme Court in *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 351 (1967), requires both a subjective expectation of privacy and that society recognizes that expectation as reasonable. Here, the PLB executive session occurred in a hearing room where Smoot had placed two unobtrusive but **visible** tape recorders. The PLB members knew Smoot had brought recorders; they simply “requested that everyone else leave the room” but did *not* search for or remove the devices. No reasonable person would expect privacy in such circumstances. See *United States v. White*, 401 U.S. 745, 750 (1971) (plurality) (expectation of privacy diminished when one knows others may be listening). Because the members’ objective conduct—leaving recorders in the room—negated any justifiable expectation of confidentiality, the recording did not violate the Act. *Nardone v. United States*, 302 U.S. 379, 381-82 (1937), is inapposite; it involved surreptitious wiretapping of private telephone lines.

**B. Smoot’s Recording Was Authorized Under the Act’s “Party” Exception**

The Act permits interception by a “person acting under color of law” or where “one of the parties to the communication has given prior consent,” 18 U.S.C. § 2511(2)(d), unless the interception is “for the purpose of committing any criminal or tortious act.” Smoot was a party to the PLB proceeding as the grievant; his recording was for the lawful purpose of preserving evidence for his grievance and later union disciplinary charges—not for any illegal or tortious objective. The district court’s contrary finding conflates mere intentional recording with wrongful purpose, a standard rejected by the Sixth Circuit. *Dorris v. Absher*, 179 F.3d 420, 425-26 (6th Cir. 1999) (recording by a participant not per se unlawful).

### III. The District Court’s Damages Award Was Excessive and Legally Erroneous

**A. Punitive Damages Were Improper Because Smoot Lacked Actual Malice**

Punitive damages under the Wiretap Act require evidence that the defendant acted “with actual knowledge or reckless disregard of the illegality.” *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323, 349-50 (1974). Smoot reasonably believed his recording was lawful—he had placed recorders openly, the executive session was part of a quasi-judicial arbitration, and he sought only to protect his rights. No evidence suggests he acted with “evil motive or intent” to harm defendants. *Moore v. City of Paducah*, 790 F.2d 557, 562 (6th Cir. 1986). The $100,000 punitive award thus violates due process and Eighth Amendment proportionality standards.

**B. Attorneys’ Fees Were Unreasonably Awarded**

The district court awarded $86,308.74 in fees without requiring defendants to demonstrate reasonableness. The Act authorizes fees only for “prevailing parties” whose suit “was not frivolous.” *Bibbo v. Dean Witter Reynolds, Inc.*, 151 F.3d 559, 562 (6th Cir. 1998). Given the legitimate, nonfrivolous nature of Smoot’s defense and his good-faith belief in the legality of his recording, the fee award was an abuse of discretion. *Thompson v. Johnson*, 219 F.3d 450, 454 (6th Cir. 2000).

**C. The $250,000 Statutory Award Was Unsupported and Excessive**

The Act sets statutory damages at $100 per day for each day of violation, 18 U.S.C. § 2520(c)(2)(B), but Smoot’s single recording—never disseminated—cannot support an award far exceeding the statutory maximum. The district court failed to calculate actual days or justify the punitive-like amount.

For these reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s summary judgment on the RLA conspiracy claim, vacate the damages and fee awards, and remand for trial.
