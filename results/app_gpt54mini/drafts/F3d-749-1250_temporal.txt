**Argument**

The district court committed plain error by allowing Agent Jones to tell the jury, in substance, that Stanley was lying, deceptive, and guilty. Expert or lay witnesses may not opine on a defendant’s veracity. The Supreme Court long ago recognized that credibility is for the jury alone. See *United States v. Johnson*, 319 U.S. 503, 519–20 (1943) (warning against the “opinion of the witness” invading the jury’s function); *F.R. A. P.*? No—more directly, see *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 76 (1942) (the jury is the “sole judge of the credibility of the witnesses”); *Quercia v. United States*, 289 U.S. 466, 470 (1933) (the trial judge may not “distort[] or add[] to” the evidence in a way that usurps the jury’s function).

Agent Jones crossed that line repeatedly. He did not merely describe interrogation techniques or observed behavior. He testified that Stanley’s answers were “not worthy of credence,” that his conduct was “common among the criminal element,” that he had “never in [his] career” seen Stanley’s statement from an innocent person, and that Stanley’s references to God were “deceptive statements.” That is not permissible expert analysis; it is a direct credibility assessment and a guilt opinion. The rule against such testimony is firmly grounded in the common law. See *United States v. Grinnell Corp.*, 384 U.S. 563, 583 (1966) (recognizing that opinions must not invade the province of the factfinder); *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 227–28 (1941) (due process is denied when the proceedings become fundamentally unfair); *Nudd v. Burrows*, 91 U.S. 426, 441 (1875) (reversible error where the judge’s conduct improperly influenced the jury’s assessment of the facts).

The prejudice here was especially acute because the government used Jones as an FBI “expert,” cloaking his lay accusation in scientific and professional authority. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that expert testimony is admissible only when it assists the trier of fact on matters beyond ordinary understanding, not when it tells the jury what result to reach. See *Salem v. United States Lines Co.*, 370 U.S. 31, 35 (1962) (expert opinion is proper only if it will “assist the trier of fact”); *Rogers v. Missouri Pac. R. Co.*, 352 U.S. 500, 509–10 (1957) (expert proof cannot displace the jury’s role in deciding ultimate facts from the evidence); *Ewing v. Burnet*, 36 U.S. (11 Pet.) 41, 53 (1837) (credibility and factual inferences belong to the trier of fact).

Nor can the government salvage the error by pointing to the absence of a contemporaneous objection. Under the plain-error standard, an appellate court may correct an obvious error affecting substantial rights when leaving it uncorrected would seriously affect the fairness of the proceedings. See *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896) (recognizing appellate correction of plain error notwithstanding the absence of objection); *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (courts may notice errors that are “obvious” and “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings”); *Silber v. United States*, 370 U.S. 717, 718 (1962) (per curiam) (plain error warrants reversal where it is prejudicial and affects substantial rights).

This error affected Stanley’s substantial rights. The case turned in large part on identification, intent, and Stanley’s post-arrest statements. Jones’s testimony told the jury that Stanley’s explanations were deceptive and that innocuous conduct signaled criminality. Because the government relied on this improper opinion to bolster its theory that Stanley was the getaway driver, the jury received an authoritative FBI assessment that Stanley was lying. That kind of testimony is uniquely damaging. See *Quercia*, 289 U.S. at 471 (a tribunal may not place its “imprimatur” on credibility determinations); *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (a criminal trial must be conducted with scrupulous fairness).

Finally, the error seriously affected the fairness and integrity of the trial. A verdict reached after an FBI agent tells the jury that the defendant’s statements are deceptive and that he speaks like “the criminal element” is not a verdict arrived at by the jury’s independent judgment. The Constitution requires more. See *In re Murchison*, 349 U.S. 133, 136 (1955) (due process demands a fair trial in a fair tribunal); *Tumey v. Ohio*, 273 U.S. 510, 523 (1927) (a procedure that creates an unacceptable risk of unfairness cannot stand). Because the improper opinion testimony struck at the heart of the jury’s function and likely influenced the verdict, Stanley’s convictions must be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial.
