**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of statutory text, legislative intent, and binding precedent. The answer is no. The Ninth Circuit’s decision upholding the District of Arizona’s transfer of this case to itself for trial under § 1404(a) is fundamentally at odds with the clear language and purpose of § 1407(a), and it contravenes well-established principles of federal venue and judicial authority. This Court should reverse.

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Original District for Trial—No Exception for § 1404(a) Transfers**

The plain language of § 1407(a) is unambiguous: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (emphasis added). This is not a mere suggestion; it is a mandatory directive. The statute does not grant the transferee court discretion to retain jurisdiction for trial. Indeed, the very purpose of multidistrict litigation (MDL) is to centralize pretrial proceedings for efficiency, not to permit the transferee court to assume permanent jurisdiction over the case. As this Court held in *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 353 U.S. 102, 107 (1957), “Congress has spoken with unmistakable clarity” in enacting § 1407, and courts must “give effect to the statutory command.” The transferee court’s authority is strictly limited to pretrial matters, and any attempt to extend that authority to trial is a violation of the statute’s express terms.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 125 (2d Cir. 1971), to justify the district court’s self-transfer is misplaced. Those cases, like the one before us, were decided in the absence of a clear statutory mandate and relied on policy considerations—such as judicial efficiency and the avoidance of delay—to justify departing from the text of § 1407(a). But this Court has repeatedly rejected such reasoning when it conflicts with clear statutory language. In *United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989), the Court emphasized that “when the statutory language is unambiguous, we need not look beyond the text.” The language of § 1407(a) is unambiguous: remand is required. No exception for § 1404(a) transfers exists in the statute, and no such exception should be judicially created.

**II. Section 1404(a) Does Not Confer Authority to Subvert § 1407(a)’s Remand Mandate**

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that § 1404(a) permits a transferee court to transfer a case to itself for trial rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the scope of § 1404(a). That section authorizes transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only “in the interest of justice” and only “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). The key limitation is that the transferee court must be a district where the action “might have been brought.” But under § 1407(a), the original district—here, the Northern District of Illinois—is the only district where the action “might have been brought” *after* the transfer. The transferee court in Arizona is not a proper venue under § 1404(a) because the case was not originally filed there, and the transfer under § 1407(a) does not create new venue rights.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Piper Aircraft Co. v. Reyno*, 454 U.S. 235, 257 (1981), where the Court held that “the venue of a civil action is determined by the original filing of the complaint.” The transferee court’s attempt to invoke § 1404(a) to reassign the case to itself for trial effectively rewrites the venue rules by allowing a court to become a proper venue simply because it has jurisdiction over pretrial proceedings. That is not the law. As this Court stated in *H. L. Green Co.*, 353 U.S. at 107, “the power to transfer is not a power to create new jurisdiction.” The transferee court’s authority under § 1404(a) is limited to the scope of the original venue, and that scope does not include trial in a district that was not the original venue.

**III. The Judicial Panel’s Authority Is Exclusive and Non-Delegable**

The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) is the sole entity authorized to transfer cases under § 1407(a). The statute grants the Panel “the power to transfer” and “shall remand” the case. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). This is not a permissive grant; it is a mandatory one. The Panel’s authority is exclusive and non-delegable. As this Court held in *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971), “there is nothing in the language of § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.” The transferee court’s attempt to exercise this power is an impermissible usurpation of the Panel’s statutory role.
