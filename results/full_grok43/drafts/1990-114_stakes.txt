I. The ADEA Does Not Cover Appointed State Judges.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan and retained by election perform precisely that function. As the First Circuit explained, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (quoting 680 F. Supp. 455, 462 (D. Mass. 1988)). Missouri Supreme Court decisions establish statewide common law, Gustafson v. Benda, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), and resolve questions carrying “far-reaching ethical, moral, and public policy implications,” Cruzan v. Harmon, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), cert. granted, 492 U.S. 917 (1989). Administrative supervision of the judicial system likewise constitutes policymaking. Mo. Const. art. V, § 4; In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc).

Congress placed the policymaking exception among exemptions for elected officials and their immediate advisors, confirming that the exception reaches all three branches. EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d at 56. A contrary reading would leave appointed judges entirely unprotected by any exemption while elected judges are expressly excluded—an implausible result. Id. at 55-56. The legislative history’s reference to “cabinet officers” and “comparable responsibilities” does not narrow the statutory text. Id.

Because appointed Missouri judges fall outside the ADEA’s definition of employee, the Act does not preempt the state constitutional retirement requirement.

II. The Retirement Provision Satisfies Equal Protection.

Age classifications receive rational-basis review. Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313-14 (1976) (per curiam). Missouri’s rule need only be “rationally related to a legitimate state interest.” Id. at 314. The State may conclude that “the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode,” Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir.), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 906 (1979), and that mandatory retirement “eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges,” Malmed v. Thornburgh, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir.), cert. denied, 449 U.S. 955 (1980). The provision also facilitates judicial renewal and increases opportunities for women and minorities. Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 401 Mass. 427, 436, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988). These considerations supply a rational basis. Vance v. Bradley, 440 U.S. 93, 97 (1979). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
