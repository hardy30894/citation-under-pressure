**ARGUMENT**

**THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING A PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION BECAUSE SHAPIRO FAILED TO DEMONSTRATE A REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION WAS REQUIRED UNDER THE FAIR HOUSING AMENDMENTS ACT.**

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988, 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f), prohibits discrimination in housing against individuals with disabilities. Discrimination includes a refusal “to make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford such person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling.” 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). To prevail on a reasonable accommodation claim, a plaintiff must show that the requested accommodation is both reasonable and necessary to afford an equal opportunity to use and enjoy housing. *See Bryant Woods Inn, Inc. v. Howard County*, 124 F.3d 597, 603 (4th Cir. 1997); *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Mgmt. Co.*, 29 F.3d 1413, 1416 (9th Cir. 1994). The district court erred in concluding that Shapiro satisfied this standard.

**A. The Requested Accommodation Was Not “Necessary” Because Shapiro Had Access to Alternative Transportation.**

An accommodation is “necessary” only if, without it, the disabled individual is denied the “equal opportunity” to use and enjoy housing that nondisabled individuals enjoy. *See Shapiro v. Cadman Towers, Inc.*, 51 F.3d 328, 335 (2d Cir. 1995). Here, Shapiro already had access to public transportation, private car services, and street parking with a handicapped sticker. These alternatives allowed her to commute to work and engage in social activities. The district court’s finding that these options caused “difficulty” or “inconvenience” does not establish that a parking space was necessary for her to enjoy her apartment. *See id.* at 334 (noting that the Act does not require accommodations that merely “improve” a disabled person’s quality of life). Indeed, Shapiro lived in Cadman Towers for two years without a parking space, demonstrating that the accommodation was not essential to her tenancy. *See id.* at 335.

**B. The Requested Accommodation Was Not “Reasonable” Because It Would Fundamentally Alter Cadman Towers’ Parking Allocation Policy.**

A proposed accommodation is unreasonable if it imposes an undue hardship or fundamentally alters the nature of the housing provider’s operations. *See Oxford House, Inc. v. Town of Babylon*, 819 F. Supp. 1179, 1186 (E.D.N.Y. 1993). Cadman Towers’ first-come, first-served parking policy is a neutral, uniformly applied rule designed to fairly allocate a limited resource—66 parking spaces for 302 apartments. Granting Shapiro an immediate space would bypass the waiting list, displacing other residents who have waited years for a spot. This would fundamentally alter the cooperative’s allocation system and unfairly burden nondisabled residents. *See Shapiro*, 51 F.3d at 335 (recognizing that accommodations that “impose undue financial or administrative burdens” are unreasonable). Moreover, the Act does not require housing providers to give disabled individuals priority over others in the allocation of scarce amenities. *See id.* at 334.

**C. The District Court Improperly Shifted the Burden to Cadman Towers to Prove Unreasonableness.**

The district court erroneously required Cadman Towers to demonstrate that the accommodation was unreasonable. To the contrary, the burden rests on the plaintiff to prove that the requested accommodation is both reasonable and necessary. *See Bryant Woods Inn*, 124 F.3d at 603. Shapiro failed to meet this burden. She presented no evidence that a parking space was essential to her ability to live in her apartment, nor did she show that the accommodation could be granted without disrupting the cooperative’s longstanding policies.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court’s order granting a preliminary injunction should be reversed.
