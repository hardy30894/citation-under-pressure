**ARGUMENT**

The conviction of Anthony Elonis must be reversed. The text of 18 U.S.C. §875(c), the constitutional bedrock of the First Amendment, and the fundamental presumption of mens rea all require that the government prove a defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat. The district court's objective "reasonable speaker" standard—asking only whether a reasonable person would foresee the statement as threatening—dispensed with this essential element and cannot stand.

**I. The Presumption of Mens Rea Compels a Subjective Intent Requirement**

Congress enacted §875(c) against a "background of Anglo-American criminal law" that has "persistently" required a culpable mental state. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The presumption that a criminal statute requires mens rea is "as old as the common law itself." *Id.* at 252. This Court has never permitted that presumption to be overcome by silence, and §875(c) contains no language displacing the usual requirement of intent. The statute is not a public welfare offense; it targets core speech and carries severe penalties. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922), recognized only a narrow class of strict-liability regulatory offenses, and this is not among them. The word "threat" itself denotes a communication made with the purpose of intimidating or coercing. *See United States v. Winkl*, 79 F.2d 346, 347 (6th Cir. 1935) (a threat is "a declaration of intention to do injury to another"). Without proof of the defendant's subjective intent to communicate that declaration, the statute fails to distinguish between genuine threats and protected expression.

**II. The First Amendment Demands Subjective Intent to Threaten**

The "true threat" exception to First Amendment protection covers only statements the speaker intentionally communicates as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). There, this Court reversed a conviction under a similar threat statute because the defendant's statement was "political hyperbole" and not a "true threat." *Id.* at 707–08. The Court examined the context and the speaker's apparent intent—not merely how a reasonable listener would react. An objective standard would sweep in protected speech—jests, artistic expression, emotional outbursts—that the speaker never intended to be threatening.

This Court's incitement cases reinforce the same principle: speech cannot be punished without proof of the speaker's intent to cause harm. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969) (requiring intent to incite "imminent lawless action"); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (reversing because "no evidence that [the defendant] intended to incite" a breach of the peace); *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (speech may not be punished unless "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action"). The same logic governs threats: the government must prove the defendant intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (considering "the intent with which the act was done").

Moreover, the First Amendment prohibits strict liability in speech-related offenses. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 150 (1959) (striking down ordinance punishing bookseller without proof of scienter). An objective standard for threats is a form of strict liability that chills protected expression, because speakers cannot know whether their words will be deemed threatening until a jury decides how a reasonable person would interpret them. *Garrison v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 64, 74 (1964) (requiring intent to inflict harm for libel of public officials). The objective standard adopted below—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as threatening—punishes speech based on the reaction of a hypothetical listener, not the speaker's purpose. That approach invites juries to convict based on the offensiveness of the words rather than the speaker's culpable intent.

**III. The Objective Standard Is Void for Vagueness and Violates the Rule of Lenity**

A statute that criminalizes speech without requiring subjective intent fails to give fair warning of what conduct is prohibited. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (striking down statute that "forbids no specific or definite act" and leaves "to the courts and juries the duty of fixing its meaning"). Under the objective standard, a speaker cannot know whether his words will be deemed threatening until a jury decides how a reasonable person would interpret them. That uncertainty is intolerable when speech is at stake. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1876) ("It would certainly be dangerous if the legislature could set a net large enough to catch all possible offenders, and leave it to the courts to step inside and say who could be rightfully detained."). The rule of lenity therefore requires that §875(c) be construed to require subjective intent. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221; *see also United States v. Weintraub*, 273 F.2d 808, 811 (2d Cir. 1959) (threat statute requires proof that defendant intended to communicate a threat).

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury must find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
