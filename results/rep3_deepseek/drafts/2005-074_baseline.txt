**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before Its Effective Date.**

The Immigration and Nationality Act’s reinstatement provision, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), became effective on April 1, 1997. *See* Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, Pub. L. No. 104-208, § 309(a), 110 Stat. 3009-546, 3009-625. Mr. Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States in 1982—fifteen years before the statute’s enactment. The plain text of § 1231(a)(5) states that it applies to “an alien who has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed.” It does not specify whether it reaches reentries that occurred before its enactment.

This Court has long held that statutes are presumed to apply prospectively only. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). “Congress’s enactment of a general effective date provision does not demonstrate that it clearly intended a statute to apply retroactively.” *Id.* at 257. Here, Congress included no express statement that § 1231(a)(5) would apply to pre-enactment reentries. The Tenth Circuit erred by inferring such intent from the statute’s broad language. *See Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 466 F.3d 1125, 1132 (10th Cir. 2006) (en banc). This Court should reverse because “the presumption against retroactivity is a rule of statutory interpretation that applies unless Congress has clearly manifested its intent to the contrary.” *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001).

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Pre-Enactment Reentries Is Impermissibly Retroactive.**

Even if Congress intended § 1231(a)(5) to apply to pre-enactment reentries, such application would violate the presumption against retroactivity because it attaches new legal consequences to past conduct. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. A statute operates retroactively if it “would impair rights a party possessed when he acted, increase a party’s liability for past conduct, or impose new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Id.* at 280.

Mr. Fernandez-Vargas reentered the United States in 1982. At that time, the law provided that an alien who reentered after deportation could be removed, but remained eligible for discretionary relief such as adjustment of status. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f) (1994) (repealed 1996). Section 241(a)(5) eliminated that eligibility entirely, barring any form of relief for aliens subject to reinstatement. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (“[T]he alien is not eligible and may not apply for any relief under this chapter.”). This Court has recognized that “the elimination of any possibility of discretionary relief” constitutes a “new disability” that is impermissibly retroactive when applied to pre-enactment conduct. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321.

The Tenth Circuit held that Mr. Fernandez-Vargas had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status because he remained in the country illegally. *Fernandez-Vargas*, 466 F.3d at 1138. But this Court has rejected the notion that an alien’s unlawful status precludes a retroactivity claim. In *St. Cyr*, the petitioner had entered the United States illegally and pleaded guilty to a deportable offense before the enactment of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 293. This Court held that applying the new law to bar discretionary relief was retroactive, even though the petitioner had no vested right to remain. *Id.* at 321. The same reasoning applies here: Mr. Fernandez-Vargas’s reentry in 1982 gave rise to a legal regime under which he could seek adjustment of status. Section 241(a)(5) “attaches an important new legal consequence” to that past reentry—the permanent loss of any opportunity for relief. *Id.* at 321.

Moreover, this Court has distinguished between statutes that regulate ongoing conduct and those that penalize completed acts. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 275. Section 241(a)(5) does not regulate Mr. Fernandez-Vargas’s continued presence; it punishes his 1982 reentry by foreclosing all avenues of relief. That is a quintessentially retroactive effect. *See Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997) (holding that a statute that “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment” is retroactive).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before its effective date, or, in the alternative, that such application is impermissibly retroactive. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
