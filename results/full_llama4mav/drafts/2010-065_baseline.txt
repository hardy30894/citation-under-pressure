The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and infringes upon the Tenth Amendment.

As the Third Circuit Court of Appeals correctly held, Bond's challenge to § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds is barred due to her lack of standing. Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-144 (1939), establishes that private parties, like Bond, do not have standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims. The Supreme Court's holding in Tennessee Electric Power Co. is controlling, and the circuit split on this issue does not undermine its authority. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (characterizing the Tennessee Electric Power Co. language as an alternative holding, rather than dicta).

The Tenth Amendment is designed to protect the states' sovereignty, and it is the states, not private parties, that have standing to challenge federal statutes on Tenth Amendment grounds. See Tennessee Electric Power Co., 306 U.S. at 144. Bond's argument that the language in Tennessee Electric Power Co. is dicta is unpersuasive, as the Supreme Court's resolution of the standing question predominates its discussion of the merits. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env't, 523 U.S. 83, 94-95 (1998).

Even if Bond had standing, her challenge to § 229 would fail on the merits. Congress enacted § 229 pursuant to its Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18; art. II, § 2, cl. 2. The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government under the treaty. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). The Treaty Power grants Congress the authority to enact legislation implementing treaty obligations, even if such legislation might otherwise be beyond Congress's enumerated powers. See United States v. Lue, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998).

Bond's vagueness challenge to § 229 also fails. As applied to her conduct, § 229 is not vague, as it clearly prohibits the possession and use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes. See 18 U.S.C. § 229F(1)(A); United States v. Powell, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975). Bond's actions, involving the theft and use of toxic chemicals to harm Haynes, fall squarely within the statute's prohibitions.

For the foregoing reasons, the respondent respectfully submits that the judgment of the Third Circuit Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
