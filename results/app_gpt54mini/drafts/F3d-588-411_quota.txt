## Argument

### I. The district court’s definition of “shielding” misstated the offense and broadened § 1324 beyond Congress’s chosen limits.

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) criminalizes “conceal[ing], harbor[ing], or shield[ing] from detection” an alien unlawfully present in the United States. 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). Because “shielding” appears as one term in a criminal statute, the jury should have been instructed in a way that preserved the statute’s text and avoided collapsing “shielding” into mere assistance to undocumented workers. See United States v. Costello, 666 F.3d 1040, 1044-45 (7th Cir. 2012) (holding that harboring requires conduct that substantially facilitates an alien’s remaining in the United States and helps evade detection); United States v. McClellan, 868 F.2d 210, 216 (7th Cir. 1989) (same). A supplemental definition that permits conviction based on ordinary employment or housing assistance risks converting § 1324 into a general unlawful-employment statute, something Congress addressed separately in § 1324a. See United States v. Evans, 333 U.S. 483, 495 (1948) (courts must avoid constructions that render statutory language surplusage); Moskal v. United States, 498 U.S. 103, 109-10 (1990) (statutes should be read to give effect to every word).

Other circuits have required more than routine aid. The Fifth Circuit has held that harboring means conduct “tending substantially to facilitate” an alien’s remaining in the country unlawfully and to prevent detection. United States v. Shum, 496 F.3d 390, 391-92 (5th Cir. 2007). The Eleventh Circuit likewise requires an affirmative act that both substantially facilitates remaining in the United States and helps evade detection. United States v. Zelaya, 293 F.3d 1294, 1297-98 (11th Cir. 2002). The Ninth Circuit has cautioned that the statute does not reach merely “associated” or incidental conduct. United States v. Aguilar, 883 F.2d 662, 690-91 (9th Cir. 1989). The district court’s definition here should have tracked that limiting principle rather than expanding “shielding” to ordinary employment practices.

That limitation is especially important because criminal statutes must provide fair notice and not invite arbitrary enforcement. Kolender v. Lawson, 461 U.S. 352, 357-58 (1983); United States v. Williams, 553 U.S. 285, 304 (2008). If “shielding” is defined too broadly, employers will have no clear notice where lawful hiring ends and felony “shielding” begins. The rule of lenity therefore requires the narrower reading when Congress has not clearly made routine employment-related conduct a felony. United States v. Santos, 553 U.S. 507, 514 (2008); United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 347-49 (1971).

The government may argue that Ye’s conduct went beyond passive employment and included housing and transportation. But those facts do not answer the legal question whether the instruction told the jury that “shielding” required conduct intended to hide workers from detection, rather than merely facilitating their employment. A conviction cannot rest on a jury charge that omits an essential limiting element. Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510, 520-24 (1979); Francis v. Franklin, 471 U.S. 307, 313-18 (1985). The supplemental instruction here allowed precisely that error. The conviction on Count One should therefore be reversed.

### II. Even under a proper instruction, the evidence was insufficient to prove Ye intended to prevent government detection.

The government was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Ye acted with the purpose of shielding illegal aliens from detection. See United States v. Costello, 666 F.3d at 1044-45; United States v. McClellan, 868 F.2d at 216. On appellate review, the evidence must be sufficient for any rational juror to find that element beyond a reasonable doubt. Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 318-19 (1979). Here, the proof showed that Ye hired undocumented workers, failed to keep paperwork, paid cash, and provided housing and transportation. Those facts may support the § 1324a hiring count, but they do not necessarily prove the specific intent to prevent detection required for § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). See United States v. Barajas-Chavez, 358 F.3d 1263, 1267-68 (9th Cir. 2004) (en banc) (harboring requires conduct that substantially facilitates remaining and tends to prevent detection); United States v. Ozcelik, 527 F.3d 88, 100-01 (3d Cir. 2008) (reversing where evidence showed assistance but not intent to conceal from authorities).

Ye openly met with immigration officials, acknowledged undocumented workers, and even discussed documentation with them. The record thus shows not concealment, but blunt awareness and poor compliance. A jury may not infer the specific intent to shield from detection merely from proof of illegal hiring. See United States v. Rubio-Gonzalez, 674 F.2d 1067, 1073 (5th Cir. 1982) (requiring proof of purposeful concealment, not merely employment); United States v. Uresti-Hernandez, 968 F.2d 1042, 1046-47 (10th Cir. 1992) (same). Because the government’s evidence did not establish the requisite intent, the conviction on Count One cannot stand.

### III. At minimum, the instructional error was not harmless.

An erroneous jury instruction is harmless only if it is clear beyond a reasonable doubt that the verdict would have been the same absent the error. Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 15-19 (1999). Here, the jury specifically asked for definitions of “concealing” and “shielding,” showing that the meaning of the term was central to deliberations. Under those circumstances, the broadened instruction cannot be deemed harmless. See Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 279-82 (1993). Ye respectfully requests reversal of the conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and remand for a new trial, or at minimum entry of judgment of acquittal on that count.
