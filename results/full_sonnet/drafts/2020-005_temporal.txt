# ARGUMENT

## I. Ford’s Continuous and Extensive Activities in Montana Satisfy the Minimum-Contacts Standard That This Court Established in *International Shoe*.

The touchstone of specific personal jurisdiction has never been a mechanical causation test; it is whether the defendant’s conduct and connection with the forum State are such that it should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there. This Court held in *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), that due process is satisfied when a corporation’s activities within a State are “not only continuous and systematic, but also give rise to the liabilities sued on,” *id.* at 317, and that jurisdiction turns on “the quality and nature” of the defendant’s in-state activity, not on a rigid causal nexus, *id.* at 319. The Court further explained that a defendant who “exercises the privilege of conducting activities within a state” thereby “enjoys the benefits and protection of the laws of that state,” obligations that “may be deemed sufficient to make it reasonable and just” to subject the corporation to suit there, even for “acts done within the state.” *Id.* at 319–20 (emphasis added). Ford’s Montana activities—advertising, selling, and servicing Ford Explorers of the very same model line involved here—are precisely the kind of purposeful, continuous exploitation of the Montana market that *International Shoe* held sufficient.

## II. Ford’s Contacts With Montana “Relate To” Gullett’s Claims, Which Is All That Due Process Requires.

Ford errs in reading *International Shoe* to require that the specific transaction sued upon be the mechanical cause of the plaintiff’s injury. This Court’s subsequent decisions confirm that the relevant inquiry is broader. In *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), the Court sustained jurisdiction over an insurer whose only contact with California was the solicitation and issuance of a single insurance contract, reasoning that “it is sufficient for purposes of due process that the suit was based on a contract which had substantial connection with that State.” *Id.* at 223 (emphasis added). The Court did not ask whether California’s regulatory or economic environment *caused* the insurer to breach the policy; it asked whether the contact and the claim were *connected*. The same principle controls here: Ford’s decades of marketing, selling, and servicing the Explorer model in Montana bear a substantial connection to a product-liability suit arising from a tire failure on that same model, driven by a Montana resident on a Montana highway.

*Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235 (1958), does not aid Ford. Although the Court there rejected jurisdiction because the defendant “trust company has no office in Florida” and “transacts no business there,” *id.* at 251, it reaffirmed that jurisdiction is proper wherever a defendant has “purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws.” *Id.* at 253. Ford did exactly that in Montana—for decades it sold, marketed, and warranted Explorers to Montana consumers, invoking the benefits of Montana’s roads, dealer network, and consumer market. Unlike the trust company in *Hanson*, Ford deliberately cultivated a Montana market for the very product line at issue.

*Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), further illustrates that this Court has long tied the jurisdictional inquiry to the extent and nature of a corporation’s forum activities rather than to strict causation. There, the Court upheld jurisdiction over claims that did not arise from the corporation’s Ohio activities at all, because the company’s president had conducted “continuous and systematic” business affairs from Ohio. *Id.* at 438, 445. If jurisdiction may rest on general business activity untethered to the specific claim in *Perkins*, it follows a fortiori that jurisdiction is proper where, as here, the defendant’s forum activity involves the precise product line and category of harm at issue.

## III. Exercising Jurisdiction in Montana Comports With Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice.

*International Shoe*’s ultimate touchstone is whether maintenance of the suit offends “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” 326 U.S. at 316 (quoting *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940)). Nothing about defending a products-liability action in Montana offends those notions when Ford has systematically sold and serviced that very product line to Montana residents for decades. Ford is a multistate manufacturer that annually places thousands of vehicles into the Montana market through its dealer network, invoking Montana’s laws and courts as fully as any locally chartered dealer. Montana, moreover, has an obvious and legitimate interest—recognized since *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714, 722 (1877), established that States retain sovereign authority to adjudicate injuries occurring within their own territory to their own residents—in providing a forum for a resident killed on a Montana highway.

## CONCLUSION

Ford’s systematic exploitation of the Montana market for the Explorer, combined with a death occurring on a Montana road, satisfies the standard this Court articulated in *International Shoe*, *McGee*, *Hanson*, and *Perkins*. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
