**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is governed by the clear text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a TPS grant satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That interpretation distorts the statutory framework, conflates distinct legal concepts, and undermines Congress’s deliberate design. We respectfully submit that a grant of TPS does not constitute an “admission” under § 1255, and the judgment below must be reversed.

### I. THE TEXT OF THE INA CLEARLY DISTINGUISHES “ADMISSION” FROM “LAWFUL STATUS”

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive and exclusive. The term “admission” refers to the *act* of entering the country lawfully, not the *status* one holds once inside. As this Court has held, “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country,” a “procedural event” that occurs at the border. *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013). The grant of TPS, by contrast, occurs *after* entry and does not involve any inspection or authorization at the border. It is a form of *status*, not an *admission*.

Appellees’ argument—that being “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” under § 1254a(f)(4) equates to admission—misreads the statute. The provision does not say TPS recipients are “admitted”; it says they are “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The use of “considered” is deliberate and significant. It signals that TPS confers a *status*, not an *admission*. The distinction is not semantic—it is foundational. As the Fifth Circuit has recognized, “admission and status are fundamentally distinct concepts.” *Gomez v. Lynch*, 831 F.3d 652, 658 (5th Cir. 2016). Admission is an event; status is a condition. One may be in lawful status without ever having been admitted—e.g., an alien granted asylum or parole. *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (AAO 2019); *In re Fajardo Espinoza*, 26 I. & N. Dec. 603, 605 (BIA 2015). The same is true of TPS.

Appellees’ reliance on *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653 (BIA 2011), is misplaced. That case held that an adjustment of status “generally constitutes an admission,” but only because the adjustment process *follows* a lawful admission. It does not stand for the proposition that any grant of status—especially one that occurs *after* unlawful entry—constitutes an admission. The BIA has consistently distinguished between the two. *In re V-X-*, 26 I. & N. Dec. 147, 150 (BIA 2013) (grant of asylum does not constitute admission); *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. at 635 (same). The logic applies with equal force to TPS.

### II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE INA REQUIRES A DISTINCTION BETWEEN “ADMISSION” AND “STATUS”

Congress did not treat TPS recipients as “admitted” for purposes of adjustment of status. Instead, it created a separate, more limited pathway. Section 1255(a) requires an alien to have been “inspected and admitted or paroled” to adjust status. Section 1255(k) provides an exception for certain aliens who are “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission” but have failed to maintain lawful status. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(1). The fact that Congress included this exception—while *not* including one for TPS recipients—demonstrates its intent to preserve the distinction between admission and status.

Moreover, Congress explicitly barred TPS recipients from adjusting status without a supermajority vote in the Senate. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h)(2). This provision exists precisely because TPS is *not* a pathway to permanent residence. If a grant of TPS were equivalent to admission, Congress would have no need for § 1254a(h)(2)—the door to adjustment would already be open. To read § 1254a(f)(4) as satisfying § 1255(a)’s admission requirement would render § 1254a(h)(2) superfluous. As this Court has held, “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). Appellees’ interpretation violates that principle.
