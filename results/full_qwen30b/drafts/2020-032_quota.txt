**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were moot upon Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) abandonment of its challenged “Freedom of Expression Policy” and “Student Code of Conduct,” and that the claim for nominal damages could not revive a case otherwise rendered moot. This result is compelled by settled precedent, which recognizes that a live controversy is required to maintain standing, and that nominal damages alone cannot sustain jurisdiction when the underlying injury has been remedied.

**I. The Claims for Declaratory and Injunctive Relief Are Moot Because GGC’s Policy Changes Were Unambiguous and Permanent.**

A case becomes moot when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). This principle applies with particular force in constitutional challenges to governmental policies. When a challenged policy is abandoned or materially altered such that the plaintiff can no longer suffer the alleged injury, the case is moot. *See* *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992) (“[A] case is moot when the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.”).

Here, GGC revised its “Freedom of Expression Policy” to permit students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances, and removed the challenged provisions of its student code of conduct. These changes were not merely procedural or temporary; they were permanent and unambiguous. The district court correctly concluded that there was “no reasonable basis to expect that [GGC] will return to [the Prior Policies].” *See* *Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc) (holding that a policy change that “unambiguously terminates” the challenged policy renders the claim moot). The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, applying the three-part test from *Flanigan’s*—(1) whether the change was permanent, (2) whether it was unambiguous, and (3) whether there was a reasonable expectation of future enforcement—and found all three satisfied. *See* *Uzuegbunam v. Preczewski*, 18-12676, 2019 WL 2787595, at *6 (11th Cir. July 1, 2019).

The Supreme Court has long held that “a voluntary cessation of allegedly illegal conduct does not deprive the court of jurisdiction if there is a reasonable expectation that the wrong will recur.” *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Envtl. Servs. (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 190 (2000). But where, as here, the policy change is permanent and the plaintiff has no intention of returning to the prior conduct, the expectation of recurrence is absent. *See* *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982) (holding that a case is moot when the challenged conduct has ceased and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence). Uzuegbunam and Bradford have not attempted to speak on campus since the incidents in 2016, and neither has expressed any intent to do so under the new policy. Their failure to return to the campus to exercise their rights under the revised policy confirms the absence of a live controversy.

**II. Nominal Damages Cannot Save a Moot Claim.**

Even if the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were not moot, the claim for nominal damages alone cannot sustain jurisdiction. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that nominal damages do not suffice to preserve standing when the underlying injury has been remedied. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255 (“A claim for nominal damages generally will not save an otherwise moot challenge to an allegedly unconstitutional policy or law.”). This principle is rooted in the constitutional requirement that federal courts only hear actual cases and controversies. *See* *U.S. Const. art. III, § 2*.

In *Flanigan’s*, the court held that a plaintiff seeking only nominal damages could not maintain a claim after the challenged policy was changed, even though the plaintiff had alleged a violation of his First Amendment rights. *Id.* at 1256. The court emphasized that “nominal damages are not a substitute for a live controversy.” *Id.* at 1257. The same logic applies here. Uzuegbunam and Bradford did not allege any actual injury—such as financial loss, emotional distress, or physical harm—beyond the abstract violation of their constitutional rights. *See* *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000) (holding that compensatory damages in a § 1983 suit must be based on actual injury, not “the abstract value” of a constitutional right). Their complaint contained no factual allegations of harm beyond the mere fact of being stopped.

The district court correctly rejected the argument that the prayer for relief—“nominal damages”—could be construed as a request for compensatory damages. *See* *Uzuegbunam*, 2019 WL 2787595, at *8*. At the motion to dismiss stage, courts must accept allegations as true, but they need not accept conclusory or ambiguous statements as dispositive. *See* *Chaparro v. Carnival Corp.*, 693 F.3d 1333, 1335 (11th Cir. 2012). The prayer for relief explicitly requested only nominal damages, and the factual allegations did not support a claim for compensatory damages. Thus, the claim for damages was not “sufficient to support standing and save this case.” *Id.* at *7*.
