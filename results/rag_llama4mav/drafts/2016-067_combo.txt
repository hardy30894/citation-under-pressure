The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The petitioners argue that Santander is a "debt collector" because it acquired their defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto. However, the plain language of the FDCPA and relevant case law demonstrate that Santander is not a "debt collector" under the Act.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The key distinction between a debt collector and a creditor is whether the person collects debt on behalf of others or for its own account. See Heintz v. Jenkins, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (quoting § 1692a(6)). A creditor, on the other hand, is a person to whom the debt is owed, and when a creditor collects its debt for its own account, it is not generally acting as a debt collector. See Selden v. Equitable Trust Co., 94 U.S. 419 (1877) (distinguishing between a creditor's collection of its own debt and a debt collector's collection of debt on behalf of another).

The petitioners' argument relies heavily on the fact that Santander acquired their defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto. However, this fact alone does not make Santander a debt collector. As the Fourth Circuit correctly held, the default status of a debt has no bearing on whether a person qualifies as a debt collector under the threshold definition set forth in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). See also Mason v. Gamble, 62 U.S. 390 (1859) (holding that the status of a debt at the time of assignment is not determinative of the assignee's status as a creditor or debt collector).

The FDCPA's definitions of debt collector and creditor are mutually exclusive, but the petitioners' interpretation would create a loophole where an entity acting as a debt collector while servicing defaulted debts becomes a creditor simply by purchasing the defaulted debt. This interpretation is not supported by the statutory text or relevant case law. See Ivanhoe Building & Loan Assn. v. Orr, 295 U.S. 243 (1935) (holding that a creditor's rights are determined by the terms of the debt instrument, not by the creditor's status as a debt collector).

Furthermore, the petitioners' argument ignores the fact that Santander purchased the loans outright, becoming the new creditor. See Libby v. Hopkins, 104 U.S. 303 (1881) (holding that an assignee of a debt becomes the new creditor). As the new creditor, Santander is collecting its own debts, not those of another. See Western Tie & Timber Co. v. Brown, 196 U.S. 502 (1905) (holding that a creditor's collection of its own debt is not subject to the same regulations as a debt collector's collection of debt on behalf of another).

The Supreme Court has long recognized the distinction between a creditor and a debt collector. See Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick, 43 U.S. 202 (1844) (holding that a factor who sells goods on behalf of another is not necessarily a debt collector). In Nixdorff v. Smith, 41 U.S. 132 (1842), the Court held that an assignee of a debt becomes the new creditor, with the right to collect the debt.

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it is collecting its own debts, not those of another. The petitioners' argument would create a loophole in the FDCPA, allowing entities to avoid regulation by purchasing defaulted debts. The plain language of the FDCPA and relevant case law, including Wilson v. Edmonds, 130 U.S. 472 (1889), East St. Louis v. Amy, 120 U.S. 600 (1887), and United States v. Erie Railway Co., 107 U.S. 1 (1883), support the conclusion that Santander is a creditor, not a debt collector. Therefore, the judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
