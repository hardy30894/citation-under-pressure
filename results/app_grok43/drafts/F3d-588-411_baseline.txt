A. The Supplemental Jury Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous

Whether jury instructions accurately state the law is reviewed de novo. United States v. DiSantis, 565 F.3d 354, 359 (7th Cir. 2009). A district court abuses its discretion by giving an instruction that “misstates the law.” United States v. Ervin, 540 F.3d 623, 631 (7th Cir. 2008). Because Ye preserved his objection, the government must show the error was harmless. Neder v. United States, 527 U.S. 1, 15 (1999).

Section 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) makes it a crime to “conceal[], harbor[], or shield[] from detection” an alien known to be present unlawfully. The statute supplies no definition of “shielding.” When Congress uses a term without defining it, courts give the term its ordinary meaning. Asgrow Seed Co. v. Winterboer, 513 U.S. 179, 187 (1995). Other circuits have therefore required that “shielding” consist of “affirmative acts” that “make it more difficult for the government to detect the alien’s presence.” United States v. Kim, 193 F.3d 567, 574 (2d Cir. 1999); accord United States v. Cantu, 557 F.2d 1173, 1180 (5th Cir. 1977). This standard prevents the word from swallowing the distinct statutory terms “conceal” and “harbor.”

The supplemental instruction departed from that standard. By defining “shielding” to encompass any conduct that “lessens the possibility of detection,” the court eliminated the requirement of affirmative, targeted action and permitted conviction on the basis of passive or incidental assistance. That formulation is both overbroad and vague; it fails to distinguish criminal shielding from ordinary employment or housing arrangements that do not involve deliberate efforts to frustrate detection. See United States v. Lopez, 521 F.2d 437, 441 (2d Cir. 1975) (mere employment insufficient).

Because the instruction misstated the elements of the offense, the jury was permitted to convict without finding the specific conduct the statute demands. The error therefore requires reversal of the conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii).
