**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS Cannot Collect a Partnership’s Tax Deficiency from Individual Partners Without First Making Individualized Assessments Against Those Partners.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires that an assessment be made against a specific “taxpayer” to extend the statute of limitations for collection. 26 U.S.C. § 6203 provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” A “taxpayer” is defined as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and a “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Because an “individual” is distinct from a “partnership” under the statute, each is a separate taxpayer requiring a separate assessment.

The IRS’s timely assessment against the Partnership extended the statute of limitations to ten years only for the Partnership. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). It had no effect on the limitations period applicable to the individual partners. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that a lien cannot exist against an individual’s property unless the underlying tax obligation has been assessed against that individual under § 6203). The IRS’s failure to assess the Gallettis and Briguglios within the three-year period under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) bars collection from them directly.

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the partner voluntarily paid the tax and sought a refund. The court held only that a partner cannot recover a refund of taxes he paid merely because the assessment did not name him. *Id.* at 910. The district court in *Young* expressly acknowledged that to collect from a partner who refused to pay, the IRS would need to “resort to court action” or individually assess the partner. *Young v. Riddell*, 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,050 (S.D. Cal. 1959). Here, the partners have not paid and object to collection, making *Young* inapposite.

**II. State Law Does Not Permit Collection Without a Judgment Against the Partners.**

The IRS argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, allowing collection without individual assessments. But California law explicitly requires a judgment against the partner before a partnership debt can be satisfied from the partner’s personal assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) provides: “A judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner. A judgment against a partnership may not be satisfied from a partner’s assets unless there is also a judgment against the partner.”

The IRS has obtained no judgment against the Gallettis or Briguglios. The time to do so has expired under 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The assessment against the Partnership extended the limitations period only for the Partnership, not for the individual partners. *See United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562-63 (7th Cir. 1995) (distinguishable because the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners). Without a judgment, the IRS’s state-law claim is unenforceable against the partners, and the bankruptcy court properly disallowed the claims under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1).

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
