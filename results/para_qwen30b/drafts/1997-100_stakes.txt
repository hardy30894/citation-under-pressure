**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution bars retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding in *Monge v. California*—that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing enhancements—misreads and misapplies Supreme Court precedent, undermines the fundamental protections of the Clause, and creates a dangerous exception to a constitutional guarantee that must be uniformly enforced. This Court should reverse.

### I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Applies to Sentencing Proceedings Where the Prior Conviction Allegation Is a “Same Offense”

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall “be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Clause is not limited to trials on guilt or innocence; it protects against successive prosecutions for the same offense, even when those prosecutions are part of a sentencing process. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that a defendant who had been acquitted of a capital offense could not be retried on the same offense after the state appealed the acquittal and the appellate court reversed. The Court emphasized that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal,” and that “the State’s interest in obtaining a conviction is not sufficient to justify a second trial after an acquittal.” Id. at 434–435. The Court further noted that “the State’s interest in obtaining a conviction is not sufficient to justify a second trial after an acquittal,” and that “the protections of the Double Jeopardy Clause are not limited to the trial on guilt or innocence.” Id. at 439.

The Court in *Bullington* did not limit its analysis to capital cases. Rather, it applied the Clause to a sentencing proceeding that involved the same offense—first-degree murder—because the state’s interest in obtaining a death sentence was not sufficient to override the defendant’s right not to be twice put in jeopardy. The Court’s reasoning was grounded in the nature of the proceeding: the state sought to impose a more severe punishment based on the same underlying conduct that had already been adjudicated. The same logic applies here.

In *Rumsey v. United States*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), the Court reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies when a sentencing proceeding “involves characteristics that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” Id. at 209. The Court held that a defendant could not be retried on a prior conviction allegation after a federal court had reversed a sentencing enhancement based on insufficient evidence. The Court emphasized that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense,” and that “the fact that the proceeding is part of a sentencing process does not remove it from the Clause’s protection.” Id. at 210. The Court concluded that “the risk of multiple prosecutions for the same offense is sufficient to trigger the Clause’s protection.” Id.

These cases establish a clear principle: when a prior conviction allegation is essential to the imposition of a more severe sentence for the same underlying conduct, and when the state seeks to retry that allegation after a reversal based on insufficient evidence, the Double Jeopardy Clause applies. The state’s interest in enhancing a sentence does not outweigh the defendant’s constitutional right not to be twice prosecuted for the same offense.

### II. The California Supreme Court’s Distinction Between Capital and Noncapital Cases Is Unsupported by Precedent

The California Supreme Court in *Monge* attempted to distinguish *Bullington* and *Rumsey* on the ground that they involved capital cases. But the Supreme Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies only in capital cases. In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), the Court acknowledged that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, but did not limit their reasoning to that context. The Court stated only that “our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding,” and that “the State’s interest in obtaining a death sentence is not sufficient to justify a second trial after an acquittal.” Id. at 392. This observation was made in the context of a Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), analysis regarding the retroactive application of new rules, not a holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply in noncapital cases.

The Court in *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389 (1995), reaffirmed that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to sentencing proceedings. The Court held that “the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense,” and that “the Clause applies to sentencing enhancements that are based on prior convictions.” Id. at 398. The Court emphasized that “the Clause is not limited to the trial on guilt or innocence,” and that “the risk of multiple prosecutions for the same offense is sufficient to trigger the Clause’s protection.” Id. at 399. The Court did not distinguish between capital and noncapital cases.

### III. The State’s Interest in Sentencing Enhancements Does Not Override the Defendant’s Constitutional Rights

The California Supreme Court held that the state’s interest in enforcing the Three Strikes law justifies allowing retrial of prior conviction allegations after a reversal. But the state’s interest in punishment does not override the defendant’s constitutional right not to be twice prosecuted for the same offense. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a death sentence based on information not presented at trial, noting that “the due process clause should not be treated as a device for freezing the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” Id. at 251. But *Williams* did not address the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court in *Williams* recognized that sentencing is not a trial on guilt or innocence, but it did not hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to sentencing proceedings.
