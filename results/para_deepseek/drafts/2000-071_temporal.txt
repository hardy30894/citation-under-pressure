**ARGUMENT**

**I. The School’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Did Not Violate the First Amendment Because the School Created a Limited Public Forum and Reasonably Excluded Religious Worship and Instruction.**

Milford Central School’s Community Use Policy created a limited public forum, not an open forum. Under *Adler v. Board of Education*, 342 U.S. 485 (1952), a school may reasonably regulate the use of its facilities to further its educational mission. The Policy expressly permits use for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but it “foreclose[s] use for religious purposes.” This distinction is permissible because the State, “no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated.” *Adler*, 342 U.S. at 492.

The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, Bible lessons, scripture memorization, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as a personal savior—constitute religious worship and instruction, not the secular moral and character development for which the forum was opened. In *West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624 (1943), this Court recognized that compelled participation in religious exercises implicates core First Amendment concerns. Here, the school did not compel any speech; it simply declined to open its forum to religious exercises. The distinction between secular moral instruction and religious worship is well-settled. *See Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940) (distinguishing “religious beliefs” from “ritual” and “worship”).

**II. The Exclusion Was Viewpoint Neutral Because the School Excluded the Entire Subject Matter of Religious Worship and Instruction, Not a Particular Religious Viewpoint.**

The Club argues that its activities represent a “religious viewpoint” on the secular subject of morals and character. But the school’s policy excludes all religious uses, regardless of denomination or creed. This is subject-matter discrimination, not viewpoint discrimination. In *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), this Court held that a municipality may regulate the use of its public property for speech activities so long as the regulation is reasonable and not based on the content of the speaker’s message. Here, the school’s policy is reasonable because it prevents the school from being used as a venue for religious indoctrination, which would conflict with its educational mission.

The Club’s activities are not merely a “viewpoint” on morals; they are religious exercises. The lesson plans explicitly distinguish between “saved” and “unsaved” children and invite children to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” for salvation. This is not a discussion of moral values from a religious perspective; it is an invitation to religious conversion. In *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1 (1947), this Court held that the Establishment Clause prohibits the State from “sponsor[ing]” or “participat[ing] in” religious activities. Permitting the Club to use school facilities for such purposes would place the school in the position of sponsoring religious instruction, which the First Amendment forbids.

**III. Even If the Exclusion Implicated the Club’s Free Speech Rights, It Was Justified by Milford’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.**

The school’s concern that permitting the Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause is well-founded. In *McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203 (1948), this Court struck down a program that allowed religious instruction on public school premises during school hours, holding that the “use of tax-supported property for religious instruction” violates the Establishment Clause. *Id.* at 210. Although the Club’s meetings occur after school hours, the school’s provision of facilities, combined with its prior approval of the Club’s use, would create an appearance of endorsement. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 312-13 (1952) (distinguishing *McCollum* on the ground that religious instruction occurred off school premises).

Moreover, the school’s policy is consistent with the principle that “the State may not, by statute or otherwise, aid, foster, or promote one religion or religious theory against another.” *Everson*, 330 U.S. at 15. By excluding all religious uses, the school avoids the risk of favoring one religion over another or religion over non-religion. This is a permissible exercise of the school’s authority to “maintain strict neutrality” in matters of religion. *Id.* at 18.

**IV. The Club’s Activities Are Not Analogous to the Secular Groups Permitted to Use the School.**

The Club compares itself to the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club, which focus on moral and character development. But those organizations do not engage in prayer, scripture memorization, or invitations to religious conversion. The record shows that the Club’s meetings are “quintessentially religious,” as the court below found. In *Minersville School District v. Gobitis*, 310 U.S. 586 (1940), this Court recognized that schools may distinguish between secular and religious activities when regulating the use of their facilities. The school’s decision to permit secular groups while excluding religious groups is a reasonable exercise of its discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
