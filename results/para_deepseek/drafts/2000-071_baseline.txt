## ARGUMENT

Milford Central School did not violate the First Amendment because it permissibly excluded the Good News Club’s proposed use—religious worship and instruction—from a limited public forum that was never opened to such activities. Alternatively, even if the exclusion constituted viewpoint discrimination, it was justified by Milford’s compelling interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation.

### I. Milford’s Exclusion Was Permissible Subject-Matter Discrimination, Not Unconstitutional Viewpoint Discrimination.

When the government creates a limited public forum, it may restrict speech to the specific purposes for which the forum was opened, so long as the restrictions are reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s Community Use Policy opened school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but expressly excluded “religious purposes.” This policy defined the forum’s boundaries by subject matter, not by viewpoint.

The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and an invitation to accept Jesus Christ as personal Savior—were not a religious *viewpoint* on a secular subject like morals or character development. Rather, they were quintessentially religious *activities* that constituted religious instruction and worship. The Second Circuit correctly held that “the Club’s activities are properly characterized as religious instruction and prayer and not the teaching of values or morals from a religious viewpoint.” *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). The Club’s lesson plans explicitly distinguished between “saved” and “unsaved” children and urged the unsaved to “believe on the Lord Jesus Christ” for salvation. *Id.* at 507. Such activities fall squarely within the excluded subject of “religious purposes.”

This case is controlled by *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384 (1993), where the Court held that a school could not exclude a religious group’s film series on family values when the forum was open to secular presentations on the same subject. *Id.* at 393–94. But *Lamb’s Chapel* involved a religious *viewpoint* on a secular topic—child rearing—not religious worship. Here, the Club’s meetings were not a discussion of morals from a religious perspective; they were religious exercises. The Club’s own description of its meetings—“singing songs, hearing a Bible lesson and memorizing scripture”—confirms that the subject matter was religion itself, not a secular topic. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 508.

Unlike the groups Milford permitted—Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H—the Club’s activities “remotely approach the type of religious instruction and prayer” that the policy excludes. *Id.* at 510. Milford did not discriminate against a viewpoint; it simply declined to open its forum to an entirely different subject matter. That is permissible under *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806.

### II. Even If the Exclusion Were Viewpoint-Based, It Was Justified by Milford’s Compelling Interest in Avoiding an Establishment Clause Violation.

The government has a compelling interest in not violating the Establishment Clause. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981). Permitting the Club’s meetings—which included prayer, proselytizing, and invitations to accept Jesus—on elementary school premises after hours could reasonably be perceived as school endorsement of religion, particularly given the young age of the children involved. *See Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (noting the heightened sensitivity to coercion in the public school context). Milford’s concern that allowing the Club’s activities would violate the Establishment Clause was therefore a legitimate justification for the exclusion.

The Club’s meetings targeted children as young as six, and the school provided bus transportation. Under these circumstances, a reasonable observer could conclude that the school was endorsing the Club’s religious message. *See Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308 (2000). Milford’s decision to exclude the Club was a reasonable step to avoid such an appearance, and it was narrowly tailored to serve that compelling interest.

### Conclusion

Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was a lawful restriction on the use of a limited public forum, and even if it were not, it was justified by Establishment Clause concerns. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
