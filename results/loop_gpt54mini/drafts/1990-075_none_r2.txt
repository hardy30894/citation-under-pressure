The Controlled Substances Act is constitutional. Congress supplied an intelligible principle to govern temporary scheduling, and it expressly authorized the Attorney General to delegate departmental functions to the DEA. Under settled Supreme Court precedent predating 1970, both propositions are correct.

I. Congress Validly Delegated Temporary Scheduling Authority

The nondelegation doctrine permits Congress to confer discretion on executive officers so long as it provides a “sufficiently definite” standard or an “intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated power. J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress fixed the policy, identified the relevant factors, or limited the circumstances in which the delegate may act. Field v. Clark, 143 U.S. 649, 692-94 (1892); Cargo of the Brig Aurora v. United States, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 382, 388 (1813); Buttfield v. Stranahan, 192 U.S. 470, 496 (1904); Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 424-27 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 249-50 (1947); American Power & Light Co. v. Securities and Exchange Commission, 329 U.S. 90, 104-05 (1946); Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage and Hour Division of Department of Labor, 312 U.S. 126, 145-46 (1941).

The CSA satisfies that standard. Congress determined the policy: dangerous drugs should be controlled to protect the public. It specified the factors the Attorney General must consider before scheduling a substance, including abuse potential, scientific evidence, current scientific knowledge, history and pattern of abuse, public-health risk, dependence liability, and precursor status. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). It required scientific evaluation by the Secretary of Health and Human Services, which is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). And for temporary scheduling, Congress limited action to an “imminent hazard to the public safety,” required consideration of specified abuse-related factors, mandated notice, and imposed a short duration. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h).

That is far more guidance than the statutes upheld in Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-27, and Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250. In Yakus, the Court sustained delegation of broad wartime price-setting authority because Congress had articulated a policy and provided standards. 321 U.S. at 426. In Fahey, the Court upheld delegation to the Comptroller of the Currency because the statute contained workable standards in a specialized field. 332 U.S. at 249-50. The CSA likewise regulates a technical, fast-moving subject in which Congress sensibly relied on executive expertise. American Power & Light Co., 329 U.S. at 105; Opp Cotton Mills, 312 U.S. at 145-46.

Petitioners’ reliance on Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those decisions invalidated statutes that left the executive with essentially standardless policymaking authority. Panama Refining, 293 U.S. at 430; Schechter Poultry, 295 U.S. at 541-42. Here, by contrast, Congress did not hand off a blank check. It set the policy, identified the emergency trigger, prescribed the criteria, and imposed temporal limits. The statute thus resembles the delegations sustained in Field, 143 U.S. at 693-94, Buttfield, 192 U.S. at 496, and Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 517-19 (1911), where the Court recognized that Congress may authorize executive officials to carry out the details of a legislative scheme.

The fact that temporary scheduling may support criminal prosecution does not change the constitutional analysis. Congress may attach criminal consequences to violations of valid administrative regulations. Grimaud, 220 U.S. at 518-19; Eaton, 144 U.S. 677, 688 (1892). The question is whether Congress supplied a lawful standard, not whether the regulation can be enforced through criminal law. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 424-27. It did.

II. Congress Also Authorized the Attorney General to Delegate to the DEA

Petitioners’ subdelegation argument fails for the separate reason that Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) provides that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions under” the drug laws. That is a direct congressional authorization for internal departmental delegation. This Court has long recognized that executive duties may be performed through subordinates when Congress has vested authority in a department head rather than requiring personal action. Shurtleff v. United States, 189 U.S. 311, 318-19 (1903); United States v. George, 228 U.S. 14, 24-25 (1913).

The Attorney General’s regulation assigning temporary scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator therefore falls squarely within the authority Congress granted. The CSA did not require the Attorney General to personally conduct every scheduling decision, and Congress affirmatively permitted delegation of departmental functions. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a); Shurtleff, 189 U.S. at 318-19; George, 228 U.S. at 24-25.

III. The CSA Is Especially Appropriate in This Technical, Rapidly Changing Area

This Court has repeatedly recognized that delegation is proper where Congress must regulate a complex and rapidly changing subject. Yakus, 321 U.S. at 425-27; Fahey, 332 U.S. at 250; American Power & Light Co., 329 U.S. at 105. The temporary scheduling provision addresses exactly that problem: designer drugs can emerge faster than ordinary legislative or rulemaking processes can respond. Congress therefore enacted a narrow emergency mechanism to preserve public safety while retaining legislative control over the standards and limits of action. That is a constitutionally permissible choice. J.W. Hampton, 276 U.S. at 409; Field, 143 U.S. at 692-94.

For these reasons, the Court should affirm.
