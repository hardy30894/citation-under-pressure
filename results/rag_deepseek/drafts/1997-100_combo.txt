ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), prohibits retrying a defendant for the same offense after an acquittal. *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 188 (1957). The California Court of Appeal’s determination that the evidence was insufficient to prove Monge’s prior conviction allegation constituted an acquittal of that allegation. *Sapir v. United States*, 348 U.S. 373, 374 (1955) (judgment of acquittal for insufficient evidence bars retrial). Retrial would therefore violate the Double Jeopardy Clause.

The Clause protects against “jeopardy of life or limb,” which encompasses any criminal punishment. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. 163, 173 (1873) (double jeopardy protects against multiple punishments for the same offense). A prior conviction allegation that doubles a defendant’s prison term exposes him to additional punishment, placing him in jeopardy. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969) (double jeopardy protects against multiple prosecutions for the same offense). The State’s attempt to retry that allegation after an acquittal is precisely the evil the Clause forbids. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896) (acquittal bars second trial).

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that double jeopardy does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause’s text draws no distinction between capital and noncapital cases. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 795. This Court has applied double jeopardy protections to sentencing determinations that are “part of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. California’s three-strikes proceeding, which requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of a prior conviction, is functionally identical to a trial on the merits. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904) (jeopardy attaches when a defendant is put to trial before a court of competent jurisdiction). The State cannot avoid the Clause by labeling the proceeding “sentencing.” *United States v. Ewell*, 383 U.S. 116, 121 (1966) (double jeopardy policies protect societal interest in finality).

Moreover, the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to state proceedings that determine facts increasing punishment. *Murphy v. Massachusetts*, 177 U.S. 155, 160 (1900) (habitual criminal statute does not create new offense but punishment enhancement still subject to constitutional limits). The Clause’s protection against multiple prosecutions for the same offense extends to allegations that, if proved, increase the maximum sentence. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 190 (implied acquittal of greater offense bars retrial). Here, the prior conviction allegation was a separate “offense” for double jeopardy purposes because it required proof of distinct facts and carried its own penalty enhancement. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 670.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on the absence of a federal constitutional right to a jury trial at sentencing is misplaced. The Double Jeopardy Clause is not a procedural right but a substantive protection against repeated prosecutions. *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717. Once the State afforded Monge a trial-like proceeding with a beyond-reasonable-doubt standard, jeopardy attached. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 133. The State cannot now retry him after an acquittal simply because it could have chosen a less formal procedure. *Sapir*, 348 U.S. at 374.

Finally, the State’s interest in punishing recidivists does not override the Clause’s command. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187. The Double Jeopardy Clause “protects the individual from being subjected to the hazards of trial and possible conviction more than once for an alleged offense.” *Ewell*, 383 U.S. at 121. That protection applies with full force to noncapital sentencing allegations. *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 796.

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
