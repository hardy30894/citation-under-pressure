**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE TEXT, STRUCTURE, AND PURPOSE OF THE IMMIGRATION AND NATIONALITY ACT (“INA”) DEMONSTRATE THAT A GRANT OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS (“TPS”) CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255.**

A. **The Plain Text of 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4) Provides That TPS Recipients Are “Considered as Being in, and Maintaining, Lawful Status as a Nonimmigrant,” Which Satisfies § 1255’s Admission Requirement.**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that § 1254a(f)(4) does not satisfy § 1255’s admission requirement. *See Sanchez v. Mayorkas*, 992 F.3d 409, 418 (3d Cir. 2021). Section 1254a(f)(4) explicitly states that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). The INA defines “lawful status” as a nonimmigrant as a statutorily recognized presence in the United States. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15) (defining nonimmigrant categories). The Third Circuit’s conclusion that this provision does not equate to an “admission” under § 1255(a) ignores the plain text of the statute. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“We begin with the language of the statute.”).

The Government argues that “admission” and “lawful status” are distinct concepts, but this distinction is not supported by the statutory text. *See Sanchez*, 992 F.3d at 418. The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). However, the statute does not require that an alien’s *initial* entry be lawful to qualify for adjustment of status under § 1255. *See id.* § 1255(a) (permitting adjustment for aliens who were “inspected and admitted or paroled”). The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479 (3d Cir. 2012), is misplaced because *Hanif* did not address whether TPS constitutes an admission. *See Sanchez*, 992 F.3d at 418.

B. **The Statutory Structure of the INA Supports the Conclusion That TPS Satisfies § 1255’s Admission Requirement.**

The Third Circuit’s interpretation renders § 1254a(f)(4) superfluous. *See id.* at 420. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that TPS recipients are “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). If TPS does not satisfy § 1255’s admission requirement, then § 1254a(f)(4) would have no practical effect for TPS recipients seeking adjustment of status. This would contravene the canon of statutory construction that courts must give effect to all provisions of a statute. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.”).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on § 1254a(h) is similarly misplaced. *See Sanchez*, 992 F.3d at 420. Section 1254a(h) permits Congress to adjust the status of TPS recipients by a supermajority vote, but this provision does not limit the Executive Branch’s authority to adjust the status of TPS recipients under § 1255. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(h). The Third Circuit’s interpretation would render § 1254a(h) the *only* mechanism for TPS recipients to adjust their status, which is not supported by the statutory text. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432.

C. **The Purpose of the TPS Program Supports the Conclusion That TPS Satisfies § 1255’s Admission Requirement.**

The Third Circuit’s interpretation undermines the humanitarian purpose of the TPS program. *See Sanchez*, 992 F.3d at 421. Congress enacted the TPS program to provide temporary relief to nationals of countries facing extraordinary conditions, such as natural disasters or armed conflict. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s holding would force TPS recipients who entered the United States illegally to leave and reenter lawfully before seeking adjustment of status, which would contravene the humanitarian purpose of the TPS program. *See id.*

The Government argues that TPS recipients may remain in the United States without seeking adjustment of status, but this ignores the fact that many TPS recipients have lived in the United States for decades and have established deep ties to their communities. *See, e.g.,* *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 964 (9th Cir. 2017) (recognizing that TPS recipients “have built lives in the United States, often with family members who are U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents”). The Third Circuit’s interpretation would force these individuals to choose between remaining in the United States without lawful permanent resident status or risking removal by departing the country. This is not a reasonable interpretation of the statute. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432.

II. **THE GOVERNMENT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD LEAD TO AN ANOMALOUS AND UNJUST RESULT.**

The Government’s interpretation would create an anomalous result by permitting TPS recipients who entered the United States legally to adjust their status under § 1255 while denying the same benefit to TPS recipients who entered illegally. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 962. This would contravene the principle that similarly situated individuals should be treated alike. *See City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Ctr.*, 473 U.S. 432, 439 (1985) (“The Equal Protection Clause...prohibits discrimination that is...arbitrary and irrational.”).

The Government’s interpretation would also force TPS recipients who entered illegally to depart the United States and reenter lawfully before seeking adjustment of status. This would contravene the principle that the Executive Branch should not adopt interpretations of statutes that would force individuals to choose between remaining in the United States without lawful status or risking removal. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully request that this Court reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
