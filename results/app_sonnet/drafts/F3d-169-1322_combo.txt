**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Correctly Held That the Burtons Were Not "Joint Employers" of Luxama's Crew Under Common-Law Agency Principles.**

The AWPA borrows the common-law test of employment that this Court's predecessors developed under analogous labor statutes. That test does not turn on any single fact but on "the circumstances of the whole activity," *Rutherford Food Corp. v. McComb*, 331 U.S. 722, 730 (1947), including who controls the manner and means of the work, who bears the risk of profit or loss, and who supplies the tools of the trade. *United States v. Silk*, 331 U.S. 704, 716 (1947). The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that "employees are those who as a matter of economic reality are dependent upon the business to which they render service." *Bartels v. Birmingham*, 332 U.S. 126, 130 (1947).

Applying those factors here confirms the district court's conclusion. Luxama, not the Burtons, recruited, hired, transported, supervised, and paid the crew. Luxama alone determined each worker's wage per box and alone directed the day-to-day mechanics of the harvest. The Burtons' role was limited to pointing Luxama's crew toward a particular field and observing the harvest's progress "two to three times a day" — a degree of oversight far short of the pervasive control that transforms a farmer into an employer. As the Supreme Court explained in *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 129 (1944), the touchstone of employee status is whether the putative employer exercises the kind of control that makes the worker "part of [its] business," not whether it retains an incidental interest in the outcome of another's labor. The Burtons' interest in the snap bean harvest was that of a principal purchasing a finished product from an independent contractor, not that of a master directing a servant.

This distinction is confirmed by *Walling v. Portland Terminal Co.*, 330 U.S. 148, 152 (1947), which recognized that the statutory concept of "employ" — to "suffer or permit to work" — must still be tethered to some exercise of authority over the work performed; it does not sweep in every party who benefits from another's labor. Nor does *Goldberg v. Whitaker House Cooperative, Inc.*, 366 U.S. 28, 32 (1961), assist the appellants, for there the putative employer retained authority over price, output, and working conditions of the homeworkers — authority the Burtons never possessed over Luxama's crew. Because Luxama, and Luxama alone, controlled the recruitment, supervision, and payment of the workers, the district court correctly found that the Burtons were not their "employer" for purposes of the AWPA's insurance and vehicle-safety provisions, and summary judgment on that issue should be affirmed.

**II. Even Assuming a Registration Violation, the Farm Workers Cannot Recover Actual Damages Because They Failed to Prove Any Loss Caused by the Burtons' Failure to Verify Luxama's Registration.**

The Burtons do not dispute that they failed to verify Luxama's registration under 29 U.S.C. § 1842. But a technical violation of a registration-verification duty does not, by itself, establish the kind of causally connected loss necessary to support an award of actual damages rather than the statutory damages Congress provided as the ordinary remedy. It has long been the rule that a plaintiff seeking actual damages must prove them "with reasonable certainty," and that recovery is barred only where the damages "are not the certain result of the wrong," not where the fact of injury is clear but its precise measure is difficult to calculate. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 562 (1931). Here, appellants proved neither the fact nor the amount of any loss attributable specifically to the Burtons' failure to check Luxama's registration, as distinct from Luxama's own failure to maintain insurance — an omission for which the Burtons were found not to be liable under Part I above.

Damages "must be shown by evidence with sufficient certainty," *United States v. Behan*, 110 U.S. 338, 345 (1884), and where the connection between the defendant's omission and the plaintiff's injury rests on speculation, the law properly channels the plaintiff to the liquidated remedy the statute itself supplies. Congress's provision of statutory damages under 29 U.S.C. § 1854(c)(1) exists precisely for cases such as this one, in which a technical recordkeeping-type violation occurred but no discrete actual loss traceable to that violation, as opposed to the accident itself, was proven. To permit appellants to recover the full measure of tort-like actual damages for the accident merely because the Burtons failed to double-check a registration card — when the accident's proximate cause was Luxama's own failure to insure his vehicles, for which the Burtons bear no employer liability — would sever the causal nexus that *Story Parchment* and *Behan* require.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be affirmed in all respects.
