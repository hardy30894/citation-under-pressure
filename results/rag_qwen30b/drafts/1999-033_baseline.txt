**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects individuals from arbitrary governmental action, even when they are not members of a traditionally recognized class. This Court has long recognized that the Clause is not limited to cases involving discrimination against discrete groups; it also safeguards the individual against governmental actions that are “so arbitrary as to be irrational.” *City of Joplin v. Southwest Missouri Light Co.*, 191 U.S. 150, 157 (1903). In this case, Grace Olech was subjected to a condition—requiring a 33-foot easement for water connection—that was not imposed on any other property owner seeking access to the municipal water supply. The Village of Willowbrook demanded an additional 18 feet beyond the 15-foot easement routinely granted to others. This disparate treatment, unmoored from any legitimate state interest and driven solely by animus, constitutes a violation of the Equal Protection Clause.

The Court has previously held that a plaintiff may state a claim under the Equal Protection Clause even when not part of a protected class. In *Rogers Park Water Co. v. Fergus*, 180 U.S. 624, 630 (1901, the Court recognized that a private corporation could challenge a municipal grant of exclusive rights to another entity, even though the corporation was not a member of a class defined by race, gender, or economic status. The Court emphasized that the Constitution protects against “arbitrary” and “unreasonable” governmental action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a class. The same principle applies here: Olech’s claim is not about membership in a group, but about the absence of any rational basis for treating her differently from others in identical circumstances.

The Village’s insistence on a 33-foot easement, while granting only 15 feet to others, cannot be justified by any legitimate governmental interest. The Village offered no evidence that a larger easement was necessary for safety, engineering, or public health reasons. Indeed, the record shows that the Village had previously approved 15-foot easements for similar properties. The only plausible explanation for the disparity is personal animus—what the Seventh Circuit described as a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective.” *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 199 F.3d 942, 946 (7th Cir. 2000). Such motive is precisely the kind of arbitrary conduct the Equal Protection Clause was designed to prevent.

This Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that equal protection requires proof of a class. In *Skaneatales Water Works Co. v. Skaneatales*, 184 U.S. 354, 363 (1902), the Court stated that “the property of the plaintiff remains wholly untouched,” yet the plaintiff’s claim was not dismissed merely because it did not involve a class-based injury. The Court held that the village’s exercise of its sovereign power to build its own waterworks did not constitute a taking, but it did not deny the plaintiff’s right to challenge the fairness of the action. The Court emphasized that “what the village ought to do in the moral aspect of the case is, of course, not a question for us to determine.” Id. at 363. But the question of whether the village acted in a manner that is arbitrary, irrational, or discriminatory is precisely within the Court’s purview.

The Village’s argument that Olech must belong to a class to state a claim is inconsistent with the fundamental purpose of the Equal Protection Clause: to ensure that no individual is treated differently by the government without a rational basis. As this Court has recognized, “the Constitution does not permit the government to act in a manner that is so arbitrary as to be irrational.” *City of Joplin*, 191 U.S. at 157. The demand for a 33-foot easement, while others received only 15 feet, is not rationally related to any legitimate governmental objective. It is, instead, a classic example of “class of one” discrimination—where a person is singled out for disparate treatment without any justification.

This Court has long held that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from such arbitrary treatment. In *Consumers’ Co. v. Hatch*, 224 U.S. 148, 155 (1912), the Court struck down a regulation that required a utility to bear the cost of a service connection, finding that such a requirement would amount to a “confiscation of its property in violation of the due process clause.” The Court did not require the plaintiff to belong to a class; it focused on the fairness and reasonableness of the government’s action. Similarly, here, the Village’s demand is not rationally related to any public purpose and cannot be justified by any legitimate state interest.

The judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed. The Equal Protection Clause does not require a plaintiff to belong to a class. It protects individuals from arbitrary governmental action, including the kind of discriminatory treatment Olech suffered. The Village’s demand for an excessive easement, while granting lesser ones to others, was not based on any rational or legitimate state interest. It was, instead, an act of spite. Such conduct violates the Constitution.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
