The Controlled Substances Act's delegation of authority to the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances does not violate the non-delegation doctrine. The Constitution grants Congress the power to make laws, but it is well established that Congress may delegate authority to executive branch officials when it provides an intelligible principle to guide their actions (J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). 

In this case, the Controlled Substances Act provides specific factors for the Attorney General to consider when temporarily scheduling a substance, including the history, current pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse, as well as the risk to public health (21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6)). These factors provide a clear guideline for the Attorney General's exercise of discretion. The Supreme Court has upheld delegations with similarly broad standards (Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947)).

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the DEA is also proper. The statute grants the Attorney General the power to delegate his functions to other officials (21 U.S.C. § 871(a)). The Attorney General has done so through a regulation, which is a valid exercise of his authority (United States v. Chemical Foundation, 272 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1926)).

The Toubys argue that the delegation is improper because it allows the Attorney General to create crimes. However, the Supreme Court has upheld delegations that involve the creation of crimes, as long as the statute provides a clear standard for the delegatee to follow (United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-17 (1911)). 

The Controlled Substances Act meets this standard, and the delegation of authority to the Attorney General is therefore constitutional. The sub-delegation to the DEA is also proper, as it is authorized by statute and carried out through a valid regulation. The convictions should be affirmed.
