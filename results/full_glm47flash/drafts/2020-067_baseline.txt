**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Federal Appellate Court May Review Matters Outside the Trial Record to Determine Whether Plain Error Affected a Defendant’s Substantial Rights**

The United States respectfully submits that a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record to determine whether a plain error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or impacted the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. Under the plain error standard, the court must consider the "totality of the circumstances" to assess prejudice, which includes the sentencing record and prior convictions.

The standard for plain error requires a determination of whether the error affected the defendant's substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993). This inquiry is not limited to the evidence presented to the jury but encompasses the entire record, including sentencing proceedings and prior convictions, to determine if the error was harmless. *Id.* at 736-37. The court may look to the record to see if the error was harmless, even if the error concerned a factual element not proven to the jury. *Id.* at 737. Here, the Eleventh Circuit properly looked to the record of Greer’s prior convictions to determine if the error in the jury instruction regarding knowledge of his status affected his substantial rights.

**II. The Error Was Harmless Beyond a Reasonable Doubt**

Even if the Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to give a proper instruction on knowledge of status, that error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967). The government proved Greer’s status as a felon through the prior convictions admitted at sentencing. *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 17 (1999). The record demonstrates that Greer possessed the requisite knowledge of his status, rendering the error harmless. *Id.* at 18. Because the record established that Greer was a felon, the error did not affect the substantial rights of the defendant or the fairness of the trial. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. The public reputation of the judicial system was not tarnished by the error, and the conviction stands.
