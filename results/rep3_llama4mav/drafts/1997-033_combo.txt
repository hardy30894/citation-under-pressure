The plain language of 28 USC section 1407(a) mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's clear directive is reinforced by a long line of cases emphasizing the importance of adhering to its terms.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case, however, disregards the statutory mandate, relying instead on a flawed interpretation of section 1407(a) that has been perpetuated by some courts. The Second Circuit's decision in Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is often cited as the origin of this misinterpretation. However, even the Pfizer court acknowledged that its holding was not supported by the plain language of the statute. Id. at 124-25.

In reality, the Pfizer decision and its progeny are at odds with the statutory text and the principles enunciated in cases such as United States v. National City Lines, 334 U.S. 573 (1948), and Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949). These cases emphasize the importance of respecting the statutory framework governing the transfer of cases.

Moreover, the practice of allowing transferee courts to invoke section 1404(a) to assign transferred cases to themselves for trial undermines the policy choices reflected in section 1407(a). As the Supreme Court noted in Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501 (1947), the plaintiff's choice of forum is entitled to significant deference. See also Baltimore & Ohio R. Co. v. Kepner, 314 U.S. 44 (1941) (emphasizing the importance of respecting the plaintiff's choice of forum).

The decision in Foster-Milburn Co. v. Knight, 181 F.2d 949 (2d Cir. 1950), highlights the need for courts to carefully consider the statutory framework governing the transfer of cases. Similarly, in All States Freight, Inc. v. Modarelli, 196 F.2d 1010 (3d Cir. 1952), the Third Circuit emphasized the importance of adhering to the statutory requirements governing transfer.

Furthermore, cases such as Paramount Pictures v. Rodney, 186 F.2d 111 (3d Cir. 1950), and Wiren v. Laws, 194 F.2d 873 (D.C. Cir. 1951), demonstrate that courts have long recognized the need to respect the statutory limits on their authority to transfer cases.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the District of Arizona erred in invoking section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial. The plain language of section 1407(a) and the relevant case law require that the case be remanded to the Northern District of Illinois for trial.

The Ninth Circuit's decision affirming the District Court's order should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to the original district as mandated by section 1407(a). This outcome is consistent with the statutory text and the principles enunciated in the relevant case law, including United States v. National City Lines, 334 U.S. 573 (1948), and Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55 (1949).
