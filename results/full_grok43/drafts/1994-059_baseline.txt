**ARGUMENT**

The Qualifications Clauses fix the exclusive criteria for service in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2 (“No Person shall be a Representative who shall not have attained to the Age of twenty five Years, and been seven Years a Citizen of the United States, and who shall not, when elected, be an Inhabitant of that State in which he shall be chosen.”); art. I, § 3, cl. 3. These provisions “fix the qualifications of members” and “preclude the states from imposing additional qualifications.” *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (quoting 2 Records of the Federal Convention of 1787, at 251 (M. Farrand ed. 1911)). The Convention explicitly rejected proposals to permit property or other state-imposed restrictions, confirming that the listed criteria were meant to be complete. *Id.* at 532-33.

States therefore lack authority to engraft further eligibility requirements. *See also Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651, 663 (1884) (qualifications for federal office “are defined in the Constitution” and “cannot be added to” by state law). Amendment 73’s prohibition on ballot access for candidates who have served three House terms or two Senate terms imposes precisely such an additional qualification. It “ha[s] the same effect as if the Constitution had expressly provided that no person should be eligible” after the stated number of terms. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 537.

The Framers’ design of a uniform national legislature reinforces this conclusion. Allowing each state to vary eligibility would produce “a patchwork of qualifications” inconsistent with the national character of Congress. *See Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1964). The power to judge qualifications rests exclusively with each House, not with the states. U.S. Const. art. I, § 5, cl. 1; *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 550.

Because the term-limit provisions of Amendment 73 add qualifications the Constitution withholds from state authority, they are invalid.
