**ARGUMENT**

**I. A FAILURE TO GIVE THE RULE 32(a)(2) ADVICE IS SUBJECT TO HARMLESS-ERROR ANALYSIS**

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) commands that "[a]ny error, defect, irregularity, or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded." This Court has applied that principle to violations of the very Rule at issue here. In *United States v. Hill*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the Court held that a sentencing court's failure to comply with Rule 32's requirement that a defendant be given an opportunity to speak did not entitle the defendant to collateral relief, explaining that "[t]he formal requirements of Rule 32(a) are not jurisdictional," and that not "every violation of a procedural rule ... can be the predicate for collateral attack." *Id.* at 428. Rule 32(a)(2)'s advisement requirement stands on identical footing: it is a procedural safeguard, not a substantive right, and its breach must be measured against whether it caused actual harm.

This Court's general harmless-error jurisprudence confirms that result. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court instructed that an error should be disregarded unless it "had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining" the outcome. *Id.* at 776. Where, as here, the very purpose the Rule serves—ensuring the defendant knows of his appellate rights—has already been accomplished through the defendant's independent knowledge, the omission cannot be said to have had any "injurious effect" whatsoever.

**II. COLLATERAL RELIEF UNDER 28 U.S.C. § 2255 REQUIRES A SHOWING OF ACTUAL PREJUDICE**

The demand for a showing of prejudice is especially acute on collateral review, where finality interests are at their zenith. In *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979), this Court held that a technical violation of Rule 11—the guilty-plea analogue to Rule 32(a)(2)—did not warrant relief under § 2255 absent a showing that the defendant was prejudiced, because "the concern with finality served by the limitation on collateral attack has special force with respect to convictions based on guilty pleas." *Id.* at 784. The Court reasoned that "the technical violation of the Rule" was not "the kind of fundamental defect which inherently results in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Id.* at 783 (internal quotation marks omitted). The same logic controls here: a district court's failure to recite an advisement that the defendant already possessed is a technical defect, not a fundamental one.

*United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), reinforces this framework, holding that a collateral challenge to an unraised trial error requires the movant to show "actual prejudice," not merely a "possibility of prejudice." *Id.* at 170. Peguero cannot make that showing, for the district court found as fact—after an evidentiary hearing—that he knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That factual finding forecloses any claim that the omission affected the outcome.

**III. PEGUERO'S ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE OF HIS RIGHT TO APPEAL SATISFIES THE PURPOSE OF RULE 32(a)(2) AND IS ANALOGOUS TO A KNOWING WAIVER**

This Court has long held that what matters for the protection of a right is whether the defendant possessed actual knowledge of it, not whether a court's formal recitation supplied that knowledge. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court defined waiver as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, focusing the inquiry on the defendant's actual awareness rather than on any ritual incantation by the court. Likewise, in *Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742 (1970), the Court upheld a guilty plea because the defendant's choice was made "voluntarily," with "sufficient awareness of the relevant circumstances," *id.* at 748, without requiring that every fact bearing on that awareness be supplied by the court itself. Peguero's admitted, pre-existing knowledge of his right to appeal is functionally indistinguishable from the "known right" contemplated in *Zerbst* and the "awareness" required in *Brady*. The advisement requirement of Rule 32(a)(2) exists to ensure that knowledge; where the defendant already has it, the rule's office has been performed regardless of the district court's oversight.

Finally, this Court's decision in *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), though addressing constitutional trial error, illustrates the general principle applicable here: even serious errors do not require automatic reversal where the government can show, and the record demonstrates, that the error was "harmless beyond a reasonable doubt." *Id.* at 24. If even certain constitutional violations may be excused upon a showing of harmlessness, then surely a purely procedural, non-constitutional omission—one that left the defendant's actual knowledge and choices unaffected—cannot mandate automatic vacatur.

**CONCLUSION**

Because Peguero indisputably knew of his right to appeal, the district court's failure to repeat that which he already knew did not affect his substantial rights, caused no prejudice, and does not entitle him to relief under § 2255. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
