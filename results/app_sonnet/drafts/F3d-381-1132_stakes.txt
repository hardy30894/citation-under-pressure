# ARGUMENT

## I. THE GOVERNMENT'S USE OF THE EASEMENT TO INSTALL AND LEASE FIBER OPTIC CAPACITY FAR IN EXCESS OF ITS OPERATIONAL NEEDS EXCEEDED THE SCOPE OF THE 1953 EASEMENT AND EFFECTED A PHYSICAL TAKING OF TUTHILL'S PROPERTY

### A. The Court of Federal Claims Erred in Holding That the Scope of an Easement Is Irrelevant to a Physical Takings Analysis

The Court of Federal Claims fundamentally misapprehended the nature of a physical taking when it declared that "[t]he scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. This holding cannot be reconciled with settled takings jurisprudence, which recognizes that the government's authority to occupy private property is always bounded by the terms of the grant under which it claims that authority. Where the government exceeds the scope of an easement, its continued physical presence on the land is no longer an authorized use—it is an unauthorized physical occupation compensable under the Fifth Amendment.

This Court has long held that a permanent physical occupation of property authorized by the government constitutes a per se taking requiring just compensation. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982) ("[A] permanent physical occupation authorized by government is a taking without regard to the public interests that it may serve."). Critically, *Loretto* did not hold that any physical presence authorized by *some* grant is immune from scrutiny; rather, it held that the government must compensate a landowner when it authorizes an occupation *beyond* what the landowner has consented to allow. Here, Tuthill's predecessor consented only to signal lines "necessary in connection" with the transmission of electrical power—not to the creation of a wholesale telecommunications infrastructure for lease to unrelated third parties. The Court of Federal Claims' refusal to examine whether the BPA's fiber optic installation and leasing arrangement fell within this grant improperly collapsed the distinct inquiry of *scope* into the separate question of *occupation*.

This Court's own precedent confirms that the government's occupation of an easement must be assessed against the boundaries of the property right at issue. In *Hendler v. United States*, 952 F.2d 1364 (Fed. Cir. 1991), this Court held that "[a] taking can occur even where the invasion is temporary" and emphasized that the touchstone of a physical takings inquiry is whether the government's occupation exceeds the property interest it was granted. *Id.* at 1376–77. If the government's placement of monitoring wells on private land beyond the scope of any granted authority constitutes a physical taking, then a fortiori, the BPA's installation of thirty-six-fiber cables—when its own operational needs require only a handful of those fibers, now and for years into the future—falls outside the "necessary" signal lines contemplated by the 1953 easement.

### B. The BPA's Installation and Leasing of Excess Fiber Optic Capacity Constitutes an Unauthorized Physical Occupation

The undisputed facts establish that the BPA installed cables containing far more fiber capacity than its power transmission operations require, and that it leases this excess capacity to a telecommunications company for profit. The easement authorizes only "signal lines... necessary in connection" with the operation of electrical transmission lines. The plain terms of this grant do not authorize the BPA to construct a commercial telecommunications network and lease that network to third parties having nothing to do with electrical power transmission.

The Court of Federal Claims excused this conduct on the theory that "[w]hat is excess capacity today may or may not be excess in the future." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. But speculative future necessity cannot retroactively authorize a present physical occupation that exceeds the scope of the grant. The relevant inquiry is not what the BPA might need by 2025, but what is "necessary" now, and whether the excess capacity—concededly leased to a private communications company—constitutes a use within the four corners of the 1953 easement. It does not. The government cannot bootstrap an unauthorized commercial use into the "necessary" signal lines the easement was designed to accommodate merely by asserting a projection window extending decades into the future while presently profiting from the excess through third-party leases.

This is precisely the type of unauthorized government-sanctioned occupation condemned in *Loretto*. There, the Supreme Court held that even a minimal, physically minor installation—cable television equipment occupying only a small portion of a building's exterior—constituted a per se taking because it was a permanent physical occupation not authorized by the property owner's consent. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 438. If the installation of small cable boxes without the owner's consent to that specific use effects a taking, so too does the BPA's installation of vastly oversized fiber cables and its leasing of that capacity to unrelated commercial telecommunications companies, notwithstanding that the BPA initially possessed *some* easement rights over the property.

### C. Tuthill Possesses a Cognizable Property Interest That Has Been Taken

The Court of Federal Claims further erred in concluding that Tuthill "has not identified a property interest that could have been taken." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. Tuthill's property interest is the servient estate itself—the fee interest in land burdened only to the extent of the easement's terms. Any use of that land beyond the easement's scope necessarily invades Tuthill's retained property rights in the servient estate. This Court has recognized that owners of land burdened by an easement retain a compensable property interest in uses that exceed the easement's scope. *See Hendler*, 952 F.2d at 1377. The government's leasing of excess fiber capacity to a private telecommunications company is precisely such an excess use, invading Tuthill's retained rights in the servient estate.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims and remand with instructions to enter summary judgment in favor of Tuthill, or, in the alternative, to permit further proceedings on the scope of the easement and the resulting physical taking.
