# ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, provides that no person shall be subject for the same offense to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb. U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has long recognized that the Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, against a second prosecution for the same offense after conviction, and against multiple punishments for the same offense. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That protection is not a technical nicety; it is, as this Court held in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), a guaranty "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," binding on California with the same force it binds the federal government. The California Supreme Court's holding that this fundamental guaranty simply does not apply to noncapital sentence-enhancement proceedings cannot be reconciled with this Court's double jeopardy jurisprudence, which has never confined the Clause's protection to the adjudication of guilt on the underlying substantive offense.

## I. A Finding That the Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove the Prior-Conviction Allegation Is the Functional Equivalent of an Acquittal and Forecloses Retrial.

The Court of Appeal held that the evidence introduced against Monge was insufficient to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that he had personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury in his prior offense. That determination is indistinguishable in substance from a judgment of acquittal, and this Court has consistently held that such judgments are final regardless of the correctness of the reasoning underlying them. In Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100 (1904), this Court held that once a defendant has been acquitted, the government may not obtain appellate correction of that acquittal, because doing so would subject the defendant to a second trial for the same offense in violation of the Double Jeopardy Clause. The Court reaffirmed that principle in Fong Foo v. United States, 369 U.S. 141 (1962), holding that a judgment of acquittal is final and unreviewable—even where the trial court erred as a matter of law—because permitting further proceedings would place the defendant twice in jeopardy for the same offense.

These cases rest on a simple but profound principle: once a tribunal charged with determining a factual predicate for punishment has found the proof wanting, the government does not get a second chance to marshal better evidence. See Sealfon v. United States, 332 U.S. 575 (1948) (double jeopardy and related principles preclude the government from relitigating facts necessarily resolved against it in a prior proceeding). There is no principled reason why this rule should evaporate merely because the factual predicate at issue is a prior conviction used to enhance punishment rather than an element of the charged offense. The consequence to the defendant—years added to a term of imprisonment—is no less severe, and the government's incentive to relitigate a losing factual issue is no less real.

## II. The Multiple-Punishment Component of the Double Jeopardy Clause Independently Bars Retrial of the Enhancement Allegation.

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not merely bar successive prosecutions after acquittal; it also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. Pearce, 395 U.S. at 717. This Court has recognized that principle since the nineteenth century. In Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163 (1874), the Court held that once a defendant has suffered one lawful punishment for an offense, the Constitution forbids that he be subjected to a second, additional punishment for that same offense. That protection has no less force where the enhancement allegation itself is the vehicle for the additional punishment—an allegation that, under California's own procedures, must be tried, proven beyond a reasonable doubt, and defended against with meaningful procedural safeguards. See Specht v. Patterson, 386 U.S. 605, 608-10 (1967) (due process requires that a defendant subject to a factfinding proceeding materially affecting the length of confinement be afforded a hearing with the assistance of counsel and an opportunity to challenge the evidence against him). Once the State has attempted to prove the enhancement and failed, permitting a second attempt exposes Monge to the very evil the multiple-punishment guarantee was designed to prevent: repeated governmental attempts to secure an enhanced sentence for the same alleged prior conduct.

## III. The Proceeding Below Bore the Hallmarks of a Trial and Therefore Falls Within the Core Protection of the Clause.

California itself has treated prior-conviction proceedings as trial-like adjudications, affording the reasonable-doubt standard, the rules of evidence, and confrontation rights. Having voluntarily structured its enhancement proceeding to mirror a trial on guilt or innocence in every material respect, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend such proceedings. As this Court explained in Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187–88 (1957), the constitutional policy against double jeopardy exists precisely because the State should not be permitted to make repeated attempts to convict or punish an individual for an alleged offense, "thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal and compelling him to live in a continuing state of anxiety and insecurity." That concern applies with undiminished force to a defendant facing a second attempt to prove a sentence-enhancing allegation after the State's first attempt has failed for want of proof.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Court of Appeal's insufficiency ruling functions as an acquittal, and because the Double Jeopardy Clause's guarantees against successive prosecution and multiple punishment for the same offense are not confined to the adjudication of the substantive charge, this Court should hold that the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation and reverse the judgment of the California Supreme Court.
