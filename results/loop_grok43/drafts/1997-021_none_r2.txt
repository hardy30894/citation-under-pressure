ARGUMENT

The National Labor Relations Act safeguards employees' freedom to select or reject union representation and obligates employers to bargain only with a union holding majority support. NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 33 (1937). An incumbent union's majority status remains rebuttable, permitting an employer to test that status upon objective indications of lost support. Republic Aviation Corp. v. NLRB, 324 U.S. 793, 805 (1945). The Board's "objective reasonable doubt" standard for polling must therefore align with the statutory emphasis on employee free choice rather than administrative restriction. NLRB v. Virginia Electric & Power Co., 314 U.S. 469, 477 (1941).

The Board's rule equates the evidentiary threshold for polling with that for withdrawal of recognition and RM elections. This equivalence contravenes the Act's differentiation among unilateral employer action, Board-supervised elections, and internal polls. Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944). Imposing identical proof requirements renders polling superfluous, contrary to the principle that administrative standards must advance rather than undermine congressional policy. NLRB v. Pennsylvania Greyhound Lines, Inc., 303 U.S. 261, 265 (1938). The Board may not nullify a statutory option by demanding proof sufficient to justify withdrawal before any poll may occur. NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc., 322 U.S. 111, 123 (1944).

The Board's factual determination that Allentown lacked reasonable doubt likewise fails substantial-evidence review. That standard requires the record as a whole to furnish adequate support for the agency's conclusion. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The record contains multiple unrefuted employee statements, made before the poll was announced, expressing opposition to the union. Such contemporaneous expressions supply objective evidence that majority support had eroded. NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939). The Board improperly disregarded these statements through an evidentiary filter lacking statutory foundation. Local 357, International Brotherhood of Teamsters v. NLRB, 365 U.S. 667, 677 (1961). Additional pre-poll expressions from unit members, including those relayed by coworkers and supervisors, further corroborated the loss of support. NLRB v. Crompton-Highland Mills, Inc., 337 U.S. 217, 224 (1949).

Because the Board's standard is irrational and its factual finding rests on less than substantial evidence, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
