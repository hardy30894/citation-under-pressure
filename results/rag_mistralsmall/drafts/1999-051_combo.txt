**ARGUMENT**

Respondent submits that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. The Sixth Circuit erred in holding otherwise. This Court’s decisions establish that a prisoner may not use a defaulted claim—whether styled as ineffective assistance or otherwise—to excuse another default. *Smith v. Bennett*, 365 U.S. 708, 710 (1961) (“[T]he State’s refusal to accept the petition without prepayment of fees denied petitioner access to the federal habeas remedy.”); *Nowakowski v. Maroney*, 386 U.S. 542, 543 (1967) (per curiam) (denial of federal habeas relief affirmed where state court had considered and rejected ineffective-assistance claim on the merits).

The Court has repeatedly refused to allow a prisoner to bootstrap one default into cause for another. In *Walker v. Wainwright*, 390 U.S. 335 (1968), the Court summarily reversed a federal habeas denial where the district court had refused to consider the merits of a constitutional claim because the petitioner’s life sentence had not yet been fully served. The Court held that the petitioner was entitled to have his constitutional claims adjudicated, notwithstanding the state’s procedural default. *Id.* at 336. The principle is the same here: a prisoner may not use a defaulted claim to excuse another default. *See also Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950) (exhaustion doctrine bars federal habeas where state remedies remain open, even if those remedies are defaulted).

The Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with the settled rule that a prisoner must raise all constitutional claims at the first available opportunity. *Holiday v. Johnston*, 313 U.S. 342, 349 (1941) (petitioner may not “split” his claims and raise them piecemeal in successive habeas petitions). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would permit precisely that sort of piecemeal litigation. *See United States ex rel. McCann v. Adams*, 320 U.S. 220, 223 (1943) (federal habeas corpus is not a “mere substitute for appeal” and will not be used to relitigate claims that could have been raised earlier).

Moreover, the Court has long held that a prisoner may not use a defaulted claim to excuse another default, even if the defaulted claim is meritorious. *Price v. Johnston*, 334 U.S. 266, 285 (1948) (“The rule is well settled that a prisoner may not use a writ of habeas corpus to relitigate questions already adjudicated in a prior habeas proceeding.”). The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule would permit prisoners to circumvent the exhaustion and procedural-default doctrines by raising ineffective-assistance claims in a second habeas petition, even if those claims were defaulted in the first. *See Long v. District Court of Iowa, Lee Cty.*, 385 U.S. 192, 194 (1966) (per curiam) (federal habeas corpus is not a “continuation of the original criminal case” and will not be used to relitigate claims that could have been raised earlier).

Finally, the Court has recognized that the exhaustion and procedural-default doctrines serve important comity and federalism interests. *Peyton v. Rowe*, 391 U.S. 54, 65 (1968) (federal habeas corpus is “not a substitute for appeal” and will not be used to undermine state procedural rules). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would undermine those interests by encouraging prisoners to default claims in state court and then raise them in federal habeas. *See Newsom v. Smyth*, 365 U.S. 604, 606 (1961) (per curiam) (federal habeas corpus will not be used to relitigate claims that could have been raised in state court).

In sum, the Sixth Circuit’s holding conflicts with this Court’s decisions and should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.
