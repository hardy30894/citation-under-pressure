ARGUMENT

I. The Transfers Among Merco and Its Affiliates Constituted Bona Fide Indebtedness Running Directly to Meruelo.

The Tax Court erred by treating the corporate form of the intercompany transfers as dispositive of their substance. It is axiomatic that "the legal right of a taxpayer to decrease the amount of what otherwise would be his taxes, or altogether avoid them, by means which the law permits, cannot be doubted." Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465, 469 (1935). But the converse is equally true: where a taxpayer's chosen form reflects genuine economic substance, the Commissioner and the courts must respect it. Id. The proper inquiry into whether an obligation is bona fide debt does not turn on the label the parties place on a transaction, but on "whether, considering all the facts . . . the parties in good faith and acting with a business purpose" created a genuine obligation. Commissioner v. Culbertson, 337 U.S. 733, 742 (1949).

Applying that substance-over-form principle to debt-versus-equity questions, the Third Circuit held that the "various factors" courts examine "are only aids in answering the ultimate question whether the investment, analyzed in terms of its economic reality," reflects "a strict debtor-creditor relationship." Fin Hay Realty Co. v. United States, 398 F.2d 694, 696-97 (3d Cir. 1968). Here, every objective indicium of bona fide debt was present: a promissory note fixing a $10 million line of credit, a stated six percent interest rate, and consistent contemporaneous accounting treatment on both Merco's and the affiliates' books as loans, not equity contributions. The Tax Court's contrary conclusion rested on the mistaken premise that funds could not "run directly" to Meruelo merely because they passed through entities he partly owned and controlled.

That premise cannot be reconciled with the settled principle that the discharge of an obligation by a third party at a taxpayer's direction is treated as equivalent to a direct transaction by the taxpayer himself. See Old Colony Trust Co. v. Commissioner, 279 U.S. 716, 729 (1929) (the "discharge by a third person of an obligation to him is equivalent to receipt by the person taxed"). Meruelo's personal loan proceeds funded Akoya, which in turn funded Merco's deposit and continuing operations; the intervening bookkeeping steps did not sever the debt from its true source—Meruelo. The transaction is thus readily distinguishable from Deputy v. du Pont, 308 U.S. 488, 497 (1940), where the Supreme Court found no "debtor-creditor relationship" because the taxpayer had done nothing more than guarantee another's obligation, risking loss only contingently. Meruelo did not merely stand behind Merco's debts; his own borrowed capital was actually advanced, tracked, and repaid with interest according to the parties' consistent course of dealing. That is precisely the kind of "true creditor-debtor relationship" that du Pont contemplated as sufficient. Id.

II. Meruelo Made an Actual Economic Outlay Sufficient to Support the Claimed Basis.

The Tax Court also erred in holding that Meruelo failed to make the requisite "actual economic outlay." A shareholder's basis in indebtedness under Subchapter S must reflect a genuine cost borne by the shareholder, not a contingent or illusory promise. See Raynor v. Commissioner, 50 T.C. 762, 770-71 (1968) (shareholder does not obtain basis merely by guaranteeing corporate debt; basis requires that the shareholder actually part with something of economic value). Raynor's rule was designed to prevent a shareholder from manufacturing basis through a paper promise while risking nothing. That concern is entirely absent here.

The Commissioner concedes that Meruelo's $4,985,035 transfer—borrowed under his own personal obligation and routed through Akoya to fund Merco's deposit—constituted a genuine economic outlay sufficient to establish basis. The remaining $9 million in net advances arose from the same economic reality: funds generated by, or funneled through, affiliated entities in which Meruelo held a direct ownership stake and for which he bore proportional economic risk. Just as the taxpayer in Old Colony Trust was properly charged with income realized through a third party's payment made on his behalf, 279 U.S. at 729, Meruelo should be credited with the outlay reflected in advances made by his affiliated companies for Merco's benefit. To hold otherwise elevates bookkeeping mechanics over the "economic reality" that Fin Hay Realty instructs courts to examine. 398 F.2d at 697.

Nor can the Tax Court's decision be justified by any suggestion that the transactions were contrived to avoid tax without economic substance. Unlike arrangements found devoid of substance because no genuine risk or cost existed, see Deputy, 308 U.S. at 497, the Merco affiliates' advances were real, were repaid in part, and were consistently reflected as debt—not equity—for years before the losses at issue arose. Under Gregory's teaching that a taxpayer's chosen, substantively real form governs the tax consequences, 293 U.S. at 469, Meruelo's economic outlay through his affiliated entities must be recognized as his own for purposes of computing his basis in Merco's indebtedness.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the decision of the Tax Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for entry of judgment recognizing Meruelo's full $13 million basis in Merco's indebtedness.
