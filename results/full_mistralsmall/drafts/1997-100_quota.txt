**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits the government from “subject[ing]” a defendant “for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V; Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This protection extends beyond the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution to sentencing proceedings where the State seeks to enhance punishment based on prior convictions. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that sentencing proceedings that determine facts necessary to impose additional punishment are subject to double jeopardy constraints. *See Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438–40 (1981); *Rumsey v. Maryland*, 467 U.S. 203, 209–10 (1984); *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 154–55 (1997). The California Supreme Court erred in holding that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings such as the one at issue here.

The Supreme Court first addressed the application of double jeopardy principles to sentencing in *Bullington*, a capital case in which the jury found the defendant guilty of first-degree murder but declined to impose the death penalty. 451 U.S. at 433–34. On retrial, the State sought the death penalty, and the jury imposed it. Id. at 434. The Court held that the second sentencing proceeding violated the Double Jeopardy Clause because the first sentencing proceeding had the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence,” including a jury determination, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and a final judgment. Id. at 438–40. The Court emphasized that the sentencing proceeding was “like the trial on the question of guilt or innocence” in that it resolved a factual dispute critical to punishment. Id. at 441.

In *Rumsey*, the Supreme Court extended *Bullington*’s reasoning to a noncapital sentencing proceeding under Maryland’s “exceptional circumstances” statute, which required a jury to find that a defendant’s conduct was “exceptionally heinous, cruel, or depraved” before imposing a life sentence. 467 U.S. at 205–06. The Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause barred retrial of the sentencing issue after reversal because the proceeding had “the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence.” Id. at 209–10. The Court rejected the State’s argument that *Bullington* was limited to capital cases, noting that the critical factor was the “nature of the proceeding” rather than the severity of the potential punishment. Id. at 210.

The Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle in *Watts*, where it held that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of sentencing factors that were not determined by a jury or judge at the first sentencing proceeding. 519 U.S. at 154–55. The Court distinguished *Bullington* and *Rumsey* on the ground that those cases involved “a jury’s consideration of sentencing factors after a finding of guilt,” whereas in *Watts* the sentencing factors were not submitted to the jury. Id. at 155. The Court’s reasoning confirms that when a sentencing proceeding involves a jury determination of facts necessary to enhance punishment, double jeopardy protections apply.

Here, the California trial court conducted a bench trial on the prior conviction allegation, resolving a factual dispute critical to Monge’s enhanced sentence. The court’s finding that Monge had suffered a prior serious felony conviction was a final adjudication of a sentencing factor that increased his punishment. Under *Bullington* and *Rumsey*, this proceeding had the “hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence,” and the Double Jeopardy Clause therefore barred retrial of the prior conviction allegation after reversal. The California Supreme Court’s contrary holding misapplied controlling Supreme Court precedent.

The California Supreme Court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. In *Williams*, the Supreme Court upheld a sentencing judge’s consideration of unadjudicated conduct in imposing a death sentence, but the Court emphasized that the sentencing judge was not resolving a disputed factual issue necessary to enhance punishment. Id. at 245–46. By contrast, here the trial court resolved a factual dispute—whether Monge had a prior serious felony conviction—after a full evidentiary hearing. *Williams* does not support the proposition that the Double Jeopardy Clause is inapplicable to such proceedings.

The California Supreme Court also erred in suggesting that the Double Jeopardy Clause does not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has never limited *Bullington* and *Rumsey* to capital cases, and its reasoning applies with equal force to noncapital sentencing proceedings where a jury or judge resolves a factual dispute critical to punishment. *See Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994) (noting that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* involved capital cases but not holding that their reasoning was limited to such cases).

Finally, the California Supreme Court’s reliance on statutory protections is irrelevant to the constitutional question. The Double Jeopardy Clause applies regardless of whether a State chooses to provide procedural protections for sentencing determinations. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438–40. The fact that California law grants defendants a jury trial on prior conviction allegations does not diminish the constitutional protection against retrial after reversal.

For these reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
