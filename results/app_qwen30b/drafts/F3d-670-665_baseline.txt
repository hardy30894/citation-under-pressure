**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit must reverse the district court’s denial of equitable tolling because Floyd Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence, and the Supreme Court’s decision in *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 125 S. Ct. 1807, 161 L. Ed. 2d 669 (2005), does not require a petitioner to demonstrate reasonable diligence *before* invoking actual innocence as a basis for equitable tolling. The district court erred in treating diligence as a precondition to the application of the actual innocence exception, thereby misapplying this Court’s precedent and misreading *Pace*. The correct legal standard, as established by this Circuit, is that a credible claim of actual innocence may independently justify equitable tolling, regardless of a petitioner’s prior diligence—especially where the new evidence was not reasonably discoverable at the time of trial.

This Court has long held that “a credible claim of actual innocence may serve as a basis for equitable tolling of the AEDPA statute of limitations.” *Hill v. United States*, 545 F.3d 407, 412 (6th Cir. 2008). In *Hill*, we recognized that “actual innocence, if proven, would render the conviction fundamentally unjust,” and that “equitable tolling may be warranted when a petitioner presents a credible claim of actual innocence.” Id. at 413. This principle is grounded in the equitable nature of tolling: when a petitioner’s claim of innocence is so compelling that it would “undermine the integrity of the judicial process,” the statute of limitations may be suspended to prevent a manifest injustice. *See also* *Buck v. Davis*, 137 S. Ct. 759, 772 (2017) (“[T]he Court has long recognized that the doctrine of equitable tolling is designed to prevent the harsh consequences of a rigid application of time limits when justice demands otherwise.”).

The district court’s reliance on *Pace v. DiGuglielmo* to impose a diligence requirement *before* considering actual innocence is misplaced. In *Pace*, the Supreme Court held that a petitioner must demonstrate “reasonable diligence” in pursuing his claims in order to qualify for equitable tolling. 544 U.S. at 418, 125 S. Ct. at 1815. But *Pace* did not address the actual innocence exception. Rather, it applied the general equitable tolling standard to a petitioner who had not made a claim of innocence at all. The Court emphasized that “equitable tolling is not available to a petitioner who has been negligent in pursuing his rights.” Id. at 418, 125 S. Ct. at 1815. That holding is consistent with the general rule that equitable tolling is not a license to ignore procedural deadlines. But it does not alter the well-established principle that a credible claim of actual innocence may itself justify tolling, even in the absence of prior diligence.

This Circuit has made clear that the actual innocence exception operates as a separate and independent basis for equitable tolling. In *Hill*, we rejected the notion that a petitioner must first prove diligence before invoking actual innocence. Instead, we held that “a credible claim of actual innocence may justify equitable tolling even if the petitioner has not been diligent.” 545 F.3d at 413. The Court reasoned that “the actual innocence exception is not a mere procedural device but a substantive safeguard against wrongful incarceration.” Id. at 414. To require a petitioner to demonstrate diligence *before* presenting a claim of innocence would effectively render the exception meaningless, as most prisoners who are actually innocent would not have known of their innocence at the time of trial and thus could not have pursued their claims with diligence.

Here, Perkins has presented a credible claim of actual innocence supported by three newly discovered affidavits. The affidavits from Ronda Hudson, Demond Louis, and Linda Fleming collectively establish that Jones—*the prosecution’s sole eyewitness*—admitted to killing Henderson and that Jones’s clothing, matching the description provided by Louis, was taken to a dry cleaner shortly after the murder. These statements, particularly Louis’s firsthand account of Jones’s admission and the physical evidence of blood-stained clothing, are not merely cumulative of prior arguments. They directly contradict the prosecution’s narrative and point to a different perpetrator. The district court erred in dismissing them as “substantially available” at trial. The evidence was not known to Perkins at the time of trial, nor could it have been discovered through the exercise of due diligence. The affidavits were signed years after the trial, and the dry cleaner’s statement was only made after the passage of time and the opportunity for Jones to be questioned.

Moreover, the district court’s conclusion that Perkins failed to act with reasonable diligence is unsupported by the record. Perkins filed his petition in 2008, nearly five years after the *new evidence* statute of limitations expired. But the *new evidence* statute of limitations—under § 2244(d)(1)(D)—does not begin to run until the factual predicate of the claim could have been discovered through due diligence. 28 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(1)(D). The affidavits were not signed until 1997, 1999, and 2002. The last affidavit was signed in 2002, and the statute of limitations under § 2244(d)(1)(D) expired on July 16, 2003. Perkins filed his petition in 2008—five years after the deadline. But the *actual innocence* exception, as recognized by this Court, allows for equitable tolling *despite* the passage of time, precisely because the petitioner’s innocence is not discoverable until new evidence emerges.

The district court’s approach would create a perverse incentive: a prisoner who discovers exculpatory evidence years after trial must file immediately, or risk losing the right to invoke actual innocence. That is not the law. As this Court has held, “equitable tolling is not a reward for diligence, but a remedy for injustice.” *Hill*, 545 F.3d at 414. Perkins’s claim of innocence is not only credible but compelling. The evidence points to Jones as the killer, and the prosecution’s case rested entirely on Jones’s testimony.
