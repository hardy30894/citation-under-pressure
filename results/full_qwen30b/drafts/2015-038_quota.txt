**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service’s regulation prohibiting the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve is fully consistent with the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”), and the district court correctly granted summary judgment in favor of the federal appellees. Sturgeon’s claim—that ANILCA bars the NPS from regulating activities on state-owned lands and waters within National Park System units—misreads the plain text of § 103(c) and ignores the fundamental principle that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters is not ceded by state ownership of submerged lands. The judgment must be affirmed.

### I. ANILCA’s Plain Text Does Not Prohibit the NPS from Regulating Activities on State-Owned Lands and Waters Within National Park Units

The central issue is whether § 103(c) of ANILCA, which provides that “the Secretary of the Interior shall not exercise regulatory authority over any lands or waters within a unit of the National Park System in Alaska that are not federally owned,” bars the NPS from enforcing its hovercraft ban on the Nation River. The answer is no. The regulation at issue does not regulate *land* or *water* ownership; it regulates *conduct*—specifically, the operation of a motorized vessel on navigable waters. The statute’s text, read in context, permits such regulation.

Section 103(c) limits the Secretary’s *regulatory authority* over nonfederal lands and waters, but it does not define the scope of that authority in a way that excludes all federal regulation of activities on such lands and waters. The statute’s language is not a blanket prohibition on federal regulation but a restriction on *regulatory authority* over *nonfederal* property. See *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 102, 120 S.Ct. 1135, 146 L.Ed.2d 69 (2000) (“Congress may regulate conduct on private property even if it does not regulate the property itself.”). The NPS regulation at issue is not a land-use regulation; it is a safety and resource protection rule applicable to all users of the preserve, regardless of land ownership.

Moreover, the NPS’s authority to regulate navigational activities on federal lands and waters is rooted in the federal government’s *navigational servitude*, a doctrine that vests the federal government with exclusive authority over the use of navigable waters for purposes of commerce, navigation, and public safety. *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 393, 61 S.Ct. 291, 85 L.Ed. 243 (1940) (“The power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters.”). This authority is not diminished by state ownership of the submerged lands beneath those waters. *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 470, 35 S.Ct. 309, 59 L.Ed. 673 (1915) (“The ownership of the soil under navigable waters does not affect the sovereignty of the United States over the waters themselves.”).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the NPS’s hovercraft ban is not a regulation “applied solely to public lands” within conservation system units (CSUs), as Sturgeon contends. The regulation applies to *all* users of the preserve, including private citizens, state agencies, and Native corporations. It is not a land-use permit regime but a safety rule designed to protect fragile ecosystems and ensure public safety. *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28, 34, 34 S.Ct. 101, 58 L.Ed. 107 (1913) (“The power to regulate navigation is a power of the United States, and not of the states.”).

### II. The NPS’s Authority to Regulate Conduct on Navigable Waters Is Not Superseded by State Ownership

Sturgeon’s argument rests on the flawed premise that state ownership of submerged lands extinguishes federal regulatory authority. That is incorrect. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that state ownership of submerged lands does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate navigational uses of those waters. *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 127, 88 S.Ct. 263, 19 L.Ed.2d 324 (1967) (“The fact that the State owns the submerged lands does not affect the federal government’s power to regulate navigation.”). The federal government’s authority over navigable waters is “independent of state ownership” and “derives from the Constitution’s Commerce Clause and the navigational servitude.” *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 393, 61 S.Ct. 291.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. City of New York*, 327 U.S. 833, 835, 66 S.Ct. 724, 90 L.Ed. 1047 (1946), where the Court upheld federal regulation of a harbor despite state ownership of the submerged lands. The Court emphasized that “the federal government’s power to regulate navigation is paramount and not subject to state control.” Id. at 835, 66 S.Ct. 724. Similarly, in *United States v. State of Washington*, 354 U.S. 47, 55, 77 S.Ct. 1109, 1 L.Ed.2d 1176 (1957), the Court held that federal authority over navigable waters “is not limited by state ownership of the bed or banks.”
