The District of Arizona's decision to invoke 28 USC section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial directly contravenes the clear and unambiguous language of 28 USC section 1407(a). The statute explicitly mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." This statutory directive is not merely a suggestion, but a binding requirement that must be followed.

The plain meaning of section 1407(a) is controlling, as the Supreme Court has consistently held that statutory provisions should be interpreted in a manner that gives effect to their plain meaning. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. 76, 95-96 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.). The language of section 1407(a) is plain, and its meaning is clear: cases transferred for pretrial proceedings must be remanded to the original district at or before the conclusion of those proceedings.

The Ninth Circuit's decision to affirm the District of Arizona's invocation of section 1404(a) undermines the statutory scheme established by section 1407(a) and contravenes the policy choice reflected in the statute. Section 1407(a) is designed to facilitate the efficient management of pretrial proceedings in multidistrict litigation, while preserving the plaintiff's traditional right to select the forum for trial. See Baltimore & Ohio R. Co. v. Kepner, 314 U.S. 44, 52-53 (1941). By permitting transferee courts to retain cases for trial, the Ninth Circuit's decision would effectively eviscerate this policy choice and grant transferee courts a power not contemplated by the statute.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that specific statutory provisions take precedence over more general provisions. See Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932); Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58 (1949). In this case, the specific mandate of section 1407(a) governs the transfer of cases for pretrial proceedings, and its provisions should be given effect over the more general transfer authority granted by section 1404(a).

Furthermore, the practice of allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial under section 1404(a) is inconsistent with the principles of statutory construction. See FTC v. Bunte Bros., 312 U.S. 349, 352-53 (1941); United States v. Dickerson, 310 U.S. 554, 562 (1940). The Ninth Circuit's decision fails to give effect to the plain meaning of section 1407(a) and undermines the statutory scheme established by Congress.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the District of Arizona erred in invoking section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial. The plain language of section 1407(a) and the principles of statutory construction require that the case be remanded to the original district for trial. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the District of Arizona's decision should be reversed, and the case should be remanded to the Northern District of Illinois.
