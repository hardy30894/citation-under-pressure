**ARGUMENT**

**THE SCHOOL DISTRICT VIOLATED THE FIRST AMENDMENT BY DENYING LAMB’S CHAPEL ACCESS TO SCHOOL PREMISES BASED SOLELY ON THE RELIGIOUS CONTENT OF ITS PROPOSED FILM SERIES.**

**I. THE DISTRICT CREATED A PUBLIC FORUM BY OPENING ITS FACILITIES TO A WIDE RANGE OF COMMUNITY GROUPS, THEREBY REQUIRING CONTENT-NEUTRAL REGULATION.**

A public forum exists where the government intentionally opens property for expressive activity by the public. *See Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939); *Jamison v. Texas*, 318 U.S. 413, 416 (1943). Once a forum is created, content-based exclusions are permissible only to serve a compelling state interest. *See Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 307 (1940).

Here, the School District adopted a policy permitting “community use of school facilities” for numerous secular purposes. The record shows the District allowed groups such as the Southern Harmonizers, the Center Moriches Free Public Library (for a lecture series), the Boy Scouts, and other civic organizations to use school premises. By opening its doors to such a broad array of speakers and topics, the District created a forum akin to the streets and parks long recognized as traditional public forums. *See Hague*, 307 U.S. at 515. The District cannot now exclude Lamb’s Chapel merely because its speech is religious.

**II. THE DISTRICT’S EXCLUSION OF RELIGIOUS SPEECH IS A CONTENT-BASED RESTRICTION THAT VIOLATES THE FREE SPEECH CLAUSE.**

The District’s Rule No. 7, which bars any group from using school premises for “religious purposes,” is a plain content-based restriction. This Court has consistently held that the government may not suppress speech because of its religious viewpoint. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 307 (religious speech is “within the protection of the First Amendment”); *Murdock v. Pennsylvania*, 319 U.S. 105, 108-09 (1943) (invalidating license tax on religious colporteurs); *Sala v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (government may not condition religious speech on discretionary approval).

In *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268 (1951), this Court reversed a conviction of Jehovah’s Witnesses for using a public park without a permit, where the permit system allowed the city to deny access based on the religious content of the speech. *Id.* at 272-73. Similarly, in *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67 (1953), the Court held that a city could not exclude a religious group from a public park while allowing other groups to use it for meetings. *Id.* at 69-70. “To call the services a ‘religious’ ceremony,” the Court explained, “is merely to identify the nature of the communication. It does not remove the communication from the protection of the First Amendment.” *Id.* at 69.

The District’s exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel is indistinguishable. The District permitted a wide range of secular groups to use its facilities but singled out religious speech for exclusion. This is the very “discrimination based on the content of the speech” that the First Amendment forbids. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 307; *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 537 (1945).

**III. THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE OR PERMIT THE EXCLUSION OF RELIGIOUS SPEECH FROM A PUBLIC FORUM.**

The District argues that exclusion is necessary to avoid an establishment of religion. But this Court has repeatedly held that the Establishment Clause does not require hostility to religion. *See Zorach v. Clauson*, 343 U.S. 306, 313-14 (1952) (“We are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being.”); *Everson v. Board of Education*, 330 U.S. 1, 18 (1947) (the First Amendment requires neutrality, not “hostility” toward religion). Permitting religious groups equal access to a public forum on the same terms as secular groups does not establish religion. *See McCollum v. Board of Education*, 333 U.S. 203, 232 (1948) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) (noting that allowing religious use of public facilities “does not amount to an establishment of religion”).

Indeed, the Court has recognized that “religion” is not a suspect classification; it is a form of speech entitled to full constitutional protection. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 307. The District’s fear of an Establishment Clause violation does not justify suppressing Lamb’s Chapel’s free speech rights. *See West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette*, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943) (“The very purpose of a Bill of Rights was to withdraw certain subjects from the vicissitudes of political controversy.”).

**IV. THE DISTRICT’S VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION IS NOT JUSTIFIED BY ANY COMPELLING INTEREST.**

Even if the District had a compelling interest in avoiding an establishment of religion, its exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel is not narrowly tailored to serve that interest. *See Thomas*, 323 U.S. at 530. The District already permits a wide range of secular speech on its premises, including speech that might touch on moral or family values. The District’s only justification for excluding Lamb’s Chapel is the religious character of its films. But the First Amendment does not permit the government to “distinguish between the religious and secular when the religious viewpoint is expressed in an otherwise public forum.” *Niemotko*, 340 U.S. at 272-73.

Moreover, the District’s policy is vastly overbroad. It bars all religious speech, even speech that is non-proselytizing and available to the public free of charge. This Court has never permitted such a sweeping exclusion. *See Cantwell*, 310 U.S. at 307; *Fowler*, 345 U.S. at 69-70.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
