**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I Fix Exclusive, Uniform Standards That No State May Enlarge.**

Article I, §2, cl. 2 and §3, cl. 3 prescribe the age, citizenship, and residency qualifications for Representatives and Senators. This Court has already held that such enumerated qualifications are fixed and may not be supplemented even by Congress itself. *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), holds that the House lacked authority to exclude a duly elected member who satisfied the constitutional standards, because the Framers rejected a system of discretionary qualifications in favor of the "fixed standards" set out in the constitutional text. If Congress—the body most directly interested in the composition of its own membership—cannot add to those standards, a single State surely cannot do so unilaterally on behalf of the whole Union.

That conclusion follows from the structural nature of the federal government created by the Constitution. As Chief Justice Marshall explained in *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819), the States retain no power to obstruct the operation of federal instrumentalities, because "an unlimited power to tax involves, necessarily, a power to destroy." *Id.* at 431. The same logic controls here: if Arkansas may bar three-term incumbents from the ballot, every other State may impose its own idiosyncratic restriction, and the "power to burden" congressional candidacy becomes, in practical effect, a power to destroy the uniformity of the federal legislature that the Framers so carefully constructed.

Congressional office is not a creature of state law. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), this Court recognized that the right to elect members of Congress "has its foundation in the Constitution of the United States," not merely in state constitutions or statutes. *Id.* at 663. Likewise, in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), the Court held that "the right of qualified voters within a state to cast their votes and have them counted for candidates for Representative in Congress is . . . expressly and definitely secured by the Constitution." *Id.* at 314. Because the office and the electoral process that fills it are federal in character, the qualifications for holding that office must likewise be governed exclusively by federal law, not by the shifting will of individual state electorates.

**II. Amendment 73's Ballot-Access Bar Is Functionally an Additional Qualification.**

Petitioners' attempt to characterize Section 3 as a mere "ballot access" provision cannot rescue it. A rule that permanently forecloses an otherwise-qualified citizen from appearing on the ballot for federal office, based solely on prior service, operates precisely as an additional qualification for holding that office. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that "the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp"—and it is too fine a point for constitutional analysis as well. Under *Powell*, the qualifications enumerated in Article I are exclusive; a State cannot evade that exclusivity by dressing an eligibility bar in electoral-administration clothing.

**III. Permitting States to Add Congressional Qualifications Would Undermine the Uniform, National Character of Congress and the Article V Amendment Process.**

The Constitution supplies its own mechanism for altering the qualifications of federal officeholders: Article V. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that functions relating to the structure of the federal government reserved to Article V are federal in nature and cannot be controlled by state constitutional processes. In *Hawke v. Smith*, 253 U.S. 221 (1920), the Court held that ratification of a federal constitutional amendment "is a federal function" that "transcends any limitations sought to be imposed by the people of a State." *Id.* at 230. Similarly, in *Dillon v. Gloss*, 256 U.S. 368 (1921), the Court confirmed that the terms governing constitutional amendment are matters of national, not local, concern. If Arkansas voters wish to change the qualifications for United States Senators and Representatives, their remedy lies in the amendment process contemplated by Article V—not in a state initiative that purports to rewrite Article I's Qualifications Clauses through the back door of ballot exclusion.

**IV. Amendment 73 Also Burdens Fundamental Voting and Associational Rights.**

Even apart from the Qualifications Clauses, Section 3 offends the principle of political equality underlying this Court's voting-rights jurisprudence. In *Gray v. Sanders*, 372 U.S. 368 (1963), the Court held that the concept of political equality "can mean only one thing—one person, one vote." *Id.* at 381. In *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), the Court reaffirmed that "no right is more precious in a free country than that of having a voice in the election of those who make the laws." *Id.* at 17. And in *Williams v. Rhodes*, 393 U.S. 23 (1968), the Court struck down ballot restrictions that denied candidates "an equal opportunity to win votes," recognizing the derivative burden such restrictions place on voters' associational rights. *Id.* at 31. Amendment 73's bar imposes precisely this kind of burden on Arkansas voters who wish to support experienced candidates of their choosing for national office.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court holding Section 3 of Amendment 73 unconstitutional should be affirmed.
