**ARGUMENT**

**A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error may examine matters outside the trial record to determine whether the error affected substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings.**

This Court has long held that the plain-error doctrine permits an appellate court to consult the whole record—including evidence not presented to the jury—when assessing prejudice. The power to review for plain error is not limited to the four corners of the trial record. To the contrary, this Court has recognized that an appellate court may properly consider facts that demonstrate the defendant’s guilt in evaluating whether an error was “plain” and whether it affected substantial rights.

In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), this Court explained that on review of non-constitutional error, the question is “whether the error itself had substantial influence” on the verdict. That determination does not turn solely on what the jury saw. Rather, the reviewing court may consider “the entire record” to assess the error’s effect. *Id.* at 765. The Court in *Kotteakos* specifically instructed that “[i]f, when all is said and done, the conviction is sure that the error did not influence the jury, or had but very slight effect, the verdict and the judgment should stand.” *Id.* at 764. An appellate court assessing whether an error influenced the jury must therefore consider all evidence available—including evidence that would have made conviction a certainty had the correct instruction been given.

This principle was reinforced in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 82 (1935), where the Court reviewed misconduct by the prosecutor and considered the strength of the government’s case in determining whether the error required reversal. The Court looked beyond the trial record to the “entire record” before concluding that the defendant was prejudiced. *Id.* at 92 (Brandeis, J., dissenting) (noting the majority’s reliance on “the whole record”). Similarly, in *Motes v. United States*, 178 U.S. 458, 472 (1900), this Court held that an appellate court may examine “the entire record” to determine whether a trial error was harmless.

When the error is raised for the first time on appeal, the reviewing court has even broader latitude to consult sources that bear on the fairness of the proceedings. In *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189, 201 (1943), this Court stated that plain-error review permits an appellate court to consider “the whole case” to decide whether the error affected “substantial rights.” The Court in *Johnson* did not cabin the inquiry to the evidence presented to the jury; instead, it considered that the record demonstrated the defendant’s guilt “beyond any reasonable doubt.” *Id.* at 202.

So too here. The Eleventh Circuit properly examined the entire record, including Greer’s prior convictions, to determine whether Greer could show that the omission of the knowledge-of-status element affected his substantial rights. *See* *Brinegar v. United States*, 338 U.S. 160, 174 (1949) (holding that an appellate court may consider “the whole record” in assessing the effect of an error). Because Greer had five prior felony convictions, and because he had stipulated to his status as an armed career criminal, there was no reasonable probability that a properly instructed jury would have acquitted him. The error therefore did not affect his substantial rights. *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 765.

Nor did the error affect “the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). This Court has recognized that plain-error review is reserved for “those errors which are *of* the most fundamental character” and which “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160. Where, as here, the record conclusively demonstrates that the defendant is guilty of the offense, the fairness of the proceedings is not undermined by a technical instructional error. *See* *Hopt v. Utah*, 114 U.S. 488, 493 (1885) (observing that an appellate court may consider “the whole case” to determine whether an error was prejudicial).

In sum, this Court’s pre-1970 precedents confirm that an appellate court reviewing for plain error may look beyond the trial record to assess whether an error affected substantial rights or the fairness of the proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision is fully consistent with these authorities. The judgment should be affirmed.
