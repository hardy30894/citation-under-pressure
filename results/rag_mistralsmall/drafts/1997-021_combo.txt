**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The Board’s rule that an employer may poll employees only when it has “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” is arbitrary and capricious. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 490 (1951) (agency action must be “reasoned and reasonable”); *FCC v. Allentown Broadcasting Corp.*, 349 U.S. 358, 364 (1955) (agency must “articulate a satisfactory explanation” for its policy). The Board’s own rationale—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—does not justify imposing a higher evidentiary threshold for polling than for withdrawal of recognition. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989), aff’d in part, rev’d in part sub nom. *NLRB v. Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 923 F.2d 398 (5th Cir. 1991). This asymmetry defies logic and undermines the Act’s goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships. *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990) (Act seeks to “encourage the practice and procedure of collective bargaining”).

The Board’s standard also conflicts with this Court’s recognition that employers must have a meaningful mechanism to test union support without immediately withdrawing recognition or risking an unfair labor practice. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987) (employer may withdraw recognition only if it has “objective evidence” of loss of majority). The Board’s refusal to permit polling absent such evidence renders the polling option illusory, rendering the Board’s policy irrational. *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978) (agency action must be “rational and consistent with the Act”).

II. The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

The Board erred in discounting employee statements and the poll results as insufficient to raise a reasonable doubt. The ALJ and Board improperly excluded statements by employees who were no longer in the bargaining unit as of January 25, 1991—the date Allentown Mack decided to poll. *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 37-41 (presumption of majority status applies to successor employers, but may be rebutted by objective evidence). The Board also improperly discounted statements made during job interviews where employees were told the company would be non-union. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc. v. NLRB*, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (1st Cir. 1978) (employee statements during hiring process are unreliable where employer signals anti-union stance).

Moreover, the Board ignored the overwhelming evidence of employee disaffection, including the poll itself, which showed 19 employees opposed to the union and only 13 in favor. *NLRB v. Arkansas Oak Flooring Co.*, 351 U.S. 62, 72 (1956) (Board must consider “all relevant factors” in assessing majority status). The Board’s refusal to credit this evidence is arbitrary and capricious. *Universal Camera*, 340 U.S. at 490 (Board’s findings must be supported by “substantial evidence on the record as a whole”).

III. The Board’s standard conflicts with the Act’s goal of avoiding bargaining with minority unions.

The Board’s policy permits employers to withdraw recognition only when they have objective evidence of a loss of majority, but denies them a reliable mechanism to obtain such evidence through polling. This creates a perverse incentive: employers who poll risk unfair labor practice liability, while those who withdraw recognition without polling may violate § 8(a)(5) if the union still enjoys majority support. *NLRB v. Lion Dry Goods, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 17, 27 (1962) (Act prohibits bargaining with a minority union). The Board’s standard thus undermines the Act’s core purpose. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42 (1937) (Act seeks to “mitigate and eliminate” industrial strife).

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked such doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand for further proceedings consistent with the Act.
