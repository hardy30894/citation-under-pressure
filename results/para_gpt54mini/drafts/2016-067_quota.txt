**Argument**

The FDCPA does not turn every purchaser of defaulted receivables into a “debt collector.” Congress drew a line between those who collect for another and those who collect debts for their own account. *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 137 S. Ct. 1718, 1721-23 (2017). Under the statute, a “debt collector” is one who “regularly collects or attempts to collect . . . debts owed or due another,” or whose “principal purpose” is debt collection. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6); *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). A “creditor,” by contrast, is a person “to whom a debt is owed.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Santander fits the latter description when it collects debts it owns. *Henson*, 137 S. Ct. at 1721-22.

The text is decisive. In *Henson*, this Court rejected the very theory petitioners press here: that a company becomes a debt collector merely because it purchased defaulted debts. 137 S. Ct. at 1723-24. The Court explained that the second prong of § 1692a(6) reaches only those who regularly seek to collect debts “owed or due another,” not those collecting debts owed to themselves. *Id.* at 1721-22. That reading accords with the ordinary meaning of “creditor” and the Act’s structure. *Id.* at 1722-24. Petitioners’ contrary reading would collapse the statutory distinction and treat a creditor collecting its own receivables as a regulated debt collector simply because it once acquired them after default. *Id.* at 1723-24.

The lower courts have repeatedly recognized the same point. In *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1313-18 (11th Cir. 2015), the Eleventh Circuit held that an entity collecting debts for itself does not become a debt collector merely because the debts were purchased after default. The court reasoned that the FDCPA’s phrase “owed or due another” refers to debt owed to another at the time of collection, not originally owed to another at some earlier point. *Id.* at 1318. The Ninth Circuit reached the same conclusion in *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1208-10 (9th Cir. 2013), holding that “owed or due another” is not equivalent to “originally owed or due another.” And the Sixth Circuit likewise explained in *Bridge v. Ocwen Federal Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355, 359-60 (6th Cir. 2012), that the Act distinguishes creditors from debt collectors and does not transform a debt owner into a debt collector merely because the debt was acquired in default.

Petitioners’ reliance on the default status of the account misreads the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). That provision exempts persons collecting debts “owed or due another” where the debt was not in default when obtained. But an exclusion cannot do the work of the statute’s main definition. As the Eleventh Circuit explained, a defendant must first fall within the statute’s principal definition of debt collector before any exclusion matters. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314-15. The same structure was recognized in *Henson*, which held that the default-status theory cannot override the statutory text limiting the second prong to debts owed “another.” 137 S. Ct. at 1721-23.

Nor does petitioners’ broader-purpose argument help them. The FDCPA targets abusive practices by debt collectors, not all collection activity by entities that also service or acquire loans. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 294-95; 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). The statute’s purpose, however, cannot displace its language. *Henson*, 137 S. Ct. at 1721-24. When Santander collected debts it owned, it was acting as a creditor collecting for itself, not as a debt collector collecting “owed or due another.” *Id.* at 1722-23.

Petitioners cite authority suggesting that defaulted-debt purchasers are debt collectors, but those cases do not control after *Henson*. See *FTC v. Check Investors, Inc.*, 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007); *Ruth v. Triumph Partnerships*, 577 F.3d 790, 796-97 (7th Cir. 2009). To the extent those decisions rested on default alone, they are incompatible with the statutory text as construed by this Court. *Henson*, 137 S. Ct. at 1721-24.

Finally, petitioners cannot salvage their claims by pointing to Santander’s prior role as a servicer or debt collector. FDCPA liability is conduct-specific; the statute requires that the defendant be acting as a debt collector “in connection with” the challenged collection activity. 15 U.S.C. § 1692e; *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293-95. Once Santander purchased the loans and collected them for itself, it was no longer collecting “owed or due another.” *Henson*, 137 S. Ct. at 1722-24; *Schlegel*, 720 F.3d at 1209-10.

The judgment should be affirmed.
