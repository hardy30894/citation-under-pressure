**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams under the equal footing doctrine only because the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time Montana achieved statehood in 1889. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“The title thus vested in the State is subject to the paramount power of Congress to control the navigation of the waters of the United States.”); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 50 (1894) (“The title of the State to the beds of navigable waters within its borders passed to it, upon its admission to the Union, by virtue of the Constitution, and is subject to the paramount right of Congress to control their navigation in all appropriate ways.”). This Court has long recognized that the equal footing doctrine turns on whether a river was navigable *at the time of statehood*. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212, 223 (1845) (“When the Revolution took place, the people of each state became themselves sovereign, and in that character hold the absolute right to all their navigable waters and the soils under them for their own common use.”); *Hardin v. Jordan*, 140 U.S. 371, 382 (1891) (“The title of the state to lands under navigable waters within its borders passed to it upon its admission to the Union by virtue of the Constitution, and is subject to the paramount right of Congress to control their navigation.”).

The trial court did not err in applying this settled rule. It considered historical evidence—including territorial surveys, territorial governor reports, and contemporaneous legislative enactments—to determine whether the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable in 1889. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931) (“The question whether a river is navigable for title purposes is a federal question, but it is to be determined as of the time of the state’s admission to the Union.”); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (same). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that navigability for title purposes is a federal question, to be determined by federal law and as of the time of statehood. *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 50; *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 223.

**II. The Trial Court Properly Relied on Historical Evidence to Determine Navigability at Statehood.**

PPL errs in arguing that the trial court improperly relied on historical evidence to determine navigability. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that courts may consider historical evidence—including hearsay-based historical reports and periodicals—to determine whether a river was navigable at statehood. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (upholding the use of historical evidence to determine navigability); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 76 (same). This Court has long permitted the use of historical evidence in navigability determinations, including territorial surveys, legislative reports, and contemporaneous accounts. *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 50; *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 223.

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in admitting the State’s historical evidence. The Montana Rules of Evidence permit the admission of historical documents under the ancient documents exception (Rule 901(8)) and the public records exception (Rule 803(8)). *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (recognizing that historical evidence may be admitted to determine navigability). The trial court properly relied on this evidence to determine that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at statehood.

**III. The Federal Power Act Does Not Preempt the State’s Claims for Compensation.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the Federal Power Act (FPA) does not preempt the State’s claims for compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act (HRA). *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152, 167-68 (1946) (“The Federal Power Act establishes a dual system of control between the states and the federal government.”); *Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239, 249-51 (1954) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state law claims for compensation). The FPA expressly preserves state authority over “the beds and banks and to the appropriation, diversion, and use of water for power purposes.” 16 U.S.C. § 802 (1946); *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 161 n.6.

The trial court correctly concluded that the FPA does not preempt the State’s claims. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76 (holding that the FPA permits state law to govern proprietary rights affected by FERC-licensed facilities). The State’s claims for compensation under the HRA are not preempted by the FPA. *Niagara Mohawk*, 347 U.S. at 249-51 (same).

**IV. The Federal Navigational Servitude Does Not Bar the State’s Claims.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the federal navigational servitude does not bar the State’s claims for compensation. *United States v. Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pac. R.R. Co.*, 312 U.S. 592, 596-97 (1941) (“The federal navigational servitude extends to all state-owned lands below the high-water mark.”); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. at 551 (same). However, the servitude does not extinguish the State’s title to riverbeds underlying navigable rivers. *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 50; *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 223. The State’s claims for compensation are not barred by the navigational servitude. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14 (same).

**Conclusion**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams under the equal footing doctrine because the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of statehood. The trial court properly relied on historical evidence to determine navigability and correctly concluded that the FPA and the federal navigational servitude do not preempt the State’s claims for compensation. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
