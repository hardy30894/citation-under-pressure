**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” apart from its state of mind, to support an award of punitive damages under Title VII. It need not. Punitive damages are available whenever the employer acts with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to the employee’s federally protected rights, regardless of whether the conduct is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) (1994); *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (holding that punitive damages are awarded based on the defendant’s mental state, not the isolated egregiousness of the act). This Court should reject the notion that punitive damages under Title VII require a showing of conduct that is independently “egregious” or “outrageous,” as such a requirement finds no support in the text or history of Title VII or in this Court’s precedents.

I. **Punitive Damages Under Title VII Are Predicated on the Employer’s State of Mind, Not the Outrageousness of Its Conduct**

The plain text of Title VII, as amended by the Civil Rights Act of 1991, provides that punitive damages may be awarded if the plaintiff demonstrates that the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) (emphasis added). The statute thus focuses on the employer’s mental state—“malice” or “reckless indifference”—not on whether the conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” *See also* *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 958, 965 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (Tatel, J.) (noting that the statute’s text “makes no mention of egregious or outrageous conduct”).

This Court has long held that punitive damages are awarded based on the defendant’s mental state, not the isolated egregiousness of the act. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363 (1851), this Court explained that punitive damages are “given ... for the fraud or malice of the party, and not for the particular character of the injury.” *Id.* at 371. Similarly, in *Hanna v. Fletcher*, 97 U.S. 484 (1878), the Court reiterated that punitive damages are awarded “not because of the character of the injury, but because of the aggravated misconduct of the wrongdoer.” *Id.* at 487. These principles apply with equal force in the Title VII context. *See also* *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56 (holding that punitive damages are awarded based on the defendant’s mental state, not the isolated egregiousness of the act).

II. **The Legislative History of Title VII Confirms That Punitive Damages Are Tied to the Employer’s State of Mind**

The legislative history of Title VII’s punitive damages provision confirms that Congress intended to tie punitive damages to the employer’s state of mind, not to whether the conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” The Conference Report on the Civil Rights Act of 1991 states that punitive damages are available where the employer acts “with malice or reckless indifference,” without any reference to the egregiousness of the conduct. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 46 (1991), *reprinted in* 1991 U.S.C.C.A.N. 549, 584. This Court has repeatedly deferred to such legislative history in interpreting Title VII. *See, e.g., Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 433-34 (1971) (relying on legislative history to interpret Title VII’s prohibition on employment practices that “tend to deprive” individuals of employment opportunities).

III. **This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedents Confirm That Punitive Damages Are Awarded Based on the Defendant’s Mental State**

This Court’s pre-1970 precedents confirm that punitive damages are awarded based on the defendant’s mental state, not the isolated egregiousness of the act. In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371, the Court held that punitive damages are awarded “for the fraud or malice of the party, and not for the particular character of the injury.” Similarly, in *Hanna v. Fletcher*, 97 U.S. at 487, the Court reiterated that punitive damages are awarded “not because of the character of the injury, but because of the aggravated misconduct of the wrongdoer.” These principles apply with equal force in the Title VII context. *See also* *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56 (holding that punitive damages are awarded based on the defendant’s mental state, not the isolated egregiousness of the act).

IV. **The Majority Below Erred in Suggesting That Punitive Damages Require a Showing of “Egregious” or “Outrageous” Conduct**

The majority below erred in suggesting that punitive damages require a showing of “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct. *Kolstad*, 139 F.3d at 965. The statute’s text makes no mention of such a requirement, and this Court’s precedents confirm that punitive damages are awarded based on the defendant’s mental state, not the isolated egregiousness of the act. *See Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. at 371; *Hanna v. Fletcher*, 97 U.S. at 487. The majority’s suggestion to the contrary is inconsistent with the text and history of Title VII and this Court’s precedents.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment below and hold that punitive damages under Title VII are available whenever the employer acts with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to the employee’s federally protected rights, regardless of whether the conduct is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.”
