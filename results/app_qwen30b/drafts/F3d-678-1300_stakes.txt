**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting judgment on the jury’s verdict of invalidity under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) because the jury’s finding was not supported by substantial evidence and was contrary to controlling precedent. The evidence at trial established that the Leader2Leader® product, while publicly used and offered for sale prior to December 10, 2002, did not embody the full scope of the asserted claims of the '761 patent—particularly the requirement of *dynamic* metadata association and *automatic* data and application migration across user environments. The jury’s conclusion that the prior art rendered the claims invalid fails under the standard of review set forth in *In re Moore*, 435 F.3d 1332, 1335 (Fed. Cir. 2006), which requires that a finding of invalidity be supported by “substantial evidence” and not merely a plausible inference.

The crux of the § 102(b) inquiry is whether the prior art “discloses” the claimed invention in a manner that would have enabled a person of ordinary skill in the art to make and use the invention without undue experimentation. *See* *Pfizer, Inc. v. Apotex, Inc.*, 424 F.3d 1324, 1330 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Here, the prior art—Leader2Leader®—did not disclose the *dynamic* and *automated* metadata updating required by claim 9. The jury was instructed that “dynamic” meant “changing in response to user actions,” but the evidence showed that Leader2Leader® required manual intervention to reassign data to new contexts. As McKibben testified, “the system didn’t automatically follow the user” unless the user manually re-uploaded or re-tagged content. J.A. 25587. This is not “dynamic updating” as required by the claim. *See* *In re Oetiker*, 977 F.2d 1443, 1445 (Fed. Cir. 1992) (holding that a claim term must be given its ordinary meaning in light of the specification, and that “dynamic” implies automatic, real-time change).

Moreover, the district court erred in allowing the jury to infer that the Leader2Leader® product was “on sale” more than one year before the critical date under § 102(b). The record shows that the January 2002 white paper was a *proposal* for licensing, not a sale. The paper offered 20,000 licenses at a fixed price, but no actual transfer of title or payment occurred. *See* *In re Brouwer*, 871 F.2d 1036, 1039 (Fed. Cir. 1989) (holding that a mere offer to sell does not trigger the on-sale bar unless there is a binding agreement or transfer of rights). The white paper itself stated that the product was “operational now with low user volumes,” J.A. 27207, suggesting limited deployment, not commercial sale. The absence of any evidence of actual sales, contracts, or delivery of software to customers undermines the conclusion that the product was “on sale” within the meaning of § 102(b).

The district court further erred in denying Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(a). *See* *In re Moore*, 435 F.3d at 1335. When the evidence is “so one-sided that no reasonable jury could have found for the non-moving party,” a judgment as a matter of law is appropriate. *See* *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 251 (1986). Here, the evidence overwhelmingly showed that the Leader2Leader® product lacked the *automatic* and *dynamic* metadata updating required by the claims. The system did not “follow” users across environments; it required manual reconfiguration. The jury’s finding that this system anticipated the claims thus defied the plain language of the claims and the specification.

Finally, the jury’s verdict was undermined by the district court’s erroneous claim construction. The court construed “dynamically updating” to mean “changing in response to user actions,” but the specification defines “dynamic” as “automatically changing in response to user movement.” '761 patent, col. 7, ll. 46–49. The specification further states that “as users create and change their contexts, the data (e.g., files) and applications automatically follow.” Id. The jury was not instructed on this critical distinction, and the court failed to correct the misstatement. *See* *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 52 F.3d 967, 979 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (holding that claim construction is a matter of law for the court, not the jury). The failure to properly instruct the jury on the meaning of “dynamic” rendered the verdict legally unsound.

For these reasons, the judgment of invalidity must be reversed. The jury’s finding was not supported by substantial evidence, the prior art did not disclose the claimed invention, and the claim construction was erroneous. The district court’s denial of Leader’s motion for judgment as a matter of law was therefore an abuse of discretion.
