**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Seek Clarification of the Jury’s Ambiguous Verdict and in Interpreting the Verdict Forms to Award Unit No Damages.

A general verdict is ambiguous when the jury’s intent cannot be ascertained with certainty from the face of the returned forms. *See* *United States v. Walker*, 575 F.2d 209, 213 (10th Cir. 1978) (verdict must be “sufficiently definite” to permit entry of judgment). Here the jury returned Form 1 awarding Unit $70,768.02 on its contract claim and Form 3 awarding Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The district court subtracted the latter from the former and entered judgment for neither party. That arithmetic, however, is irreconcilable with the instructions, which directed the jury to reduce Unit’s recovery only if it found substantial performance and then to award the net amount on Form 1 alone. The two forms therefore cannot be reconciled without speculation.

When a verdict is ambiguous, the trial court “has a duty to attempt to clarify it before discharging the jury.” *See* *Nissho-Iwai Co. v. Occidental Crude Sales, Inc.*, 729 F.2d 1530, 1540 (5th Cir. 1984); *accord* *Taco Cabana, Inc. v. Two Pesos, Inc.*, 932 F.2d 1113, 1121 (5th Cir. 1991). Unit timely requested such clarification before the jury was discharged. The court’s refusal left the record devoid of any reliable indication of the jury’s actual intent and forced the court to choose between two equally plausible but contradictory constructions of the same verdict.

The court’s chosen construction—that the jury intended a net-zero recovery—contradicts the plain language of the instructions. Those instructions permitted the jury to reduce Unit’s damages for defective performance only on Form 1; they did not authorize a separate set-off on Form 3 for the same defect. Because the jury used both forms, the only consistent reading is that the $70,768.02 figure already reflected any reduction the jury deemed appropriate. The district court’s contrary interpretation therefore invaded the province of the jury. *See* *Walker*, 575 F.2d at 213.

The error was not harmless. Had the court resubmitted the verdict forms with a clarifying instruction, the jury could have confirmed or corrected the award in minutes. Instead, Unit was deprived of any opportunity to obtain the judgment the jury intended. The judgment must be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial or, at minimum, for clarification proceedings consistent with the jury’s original intent.
