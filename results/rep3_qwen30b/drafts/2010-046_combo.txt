**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The PLRA does not bar judicial remedies necessary to enforce the Constitution, and the district court’s population reduction order is a permissible and constitutionally required exercise of federal judicial power. This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess the authority to issue remedial orders in cases involving violations of fundamental constitutional rights, even when such orders require structural changes to state systems. The authority for this principle is firmly rooted in the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence prior to 1970, which establishes that federal courts may fashion remedies to correct constitutional violations, including those arising from prison conditions.

The foundation of this principle is *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), in which the Court held that “separate but equal” facilities for Black and white students violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court did not limit its remedy to mere policy changes or symbolic gestures; instead, it ordered desegregation “with all deliberate speed.” Id. at 495. The Court emphasized that “the constitutional rights of the plaintiffs are not to be denied by delay or evasion.” Id. at 494. This principle—that federal courts may issue orders compelling structural change to remedy constitutional violations—applies with equal force in the context of prison conditions. Just as the Court in *Brown* recognized that the remedy must be effective and enforceable, so too must a court’s order in a prison case be capable of ensuring that the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment is honored.

The same principle was reaffirmed in *Reynolds v. Sims*, 377 U.S. 533 (1964), where the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause requires state legislative districts to be drawn on a “one person, one vote” basis. The Court rejected the argument that such a remedy would be an impermissible intrusion into state affairs, stating: “The Constitution does not permit a State to ignore the principle of equal protection in the apportionment of its legislative bodies.” Id. at 562. The Court further emphasized that “the judiciary has the duty to enforce constitutional rights, even when doing so requires the restructuring of state institutions.” Id. at 563. This duty extends to the correctional system, where the Constitution imposes a nondelegable obligation on the state to provide humane conditions of confinement.

In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court held that the Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel in all criminal prosecutions, and that this right is fundamental and applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The Court did not limit its remedy to a mere declaration of the right; it ordered that states must provide counsel to indigent defendants. The Court stated: “The right to counsel is a fundamental right, essential to a fair trial.” Id. at 344. This principle is directly applicable here: the right to be free from cruel and unusual punishment is equally fundamental, and the remedy for its violation must be effective. The Court in *Gideon* made clear that “the Constitution is not a suicide pact,” and that courts must enforce constitutional rights even when doing so requires structural reform.

The authority for judicial intervention in prison conditions is further established in *Hernandez v. Texas*, 347 U.S. 475 (1954), where the Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment protects Mexican Americans from discrimination in jury selection. The Court rejected the argument that the state’s failure to include Mexican Americans on juries was a matter of state sovereignty, stating: “The Constitution protects all persons within its jurisdiction from discrimination.” Id. at 480. This principle applies with equal force to the treatment of prisoners: the Constitution protects all persons, including those incarcerated, from cruel and unusual punishment. The Court in *Hernandez* recognized that “the judiciary must act when constitutional rights are violated,” and that “the remedy must be commensurate with the wrong.”

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts may issue orders that require the state to take affirmative steps to correct constitutional violations. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1 (1950), the Court upheld a federal court’s authority to issue an order requiring the state to comply with a constitutional mandate, even when such an order required the state to take action that it had previously refused. The Court stated: “The federal courts have the power to enforce constitutional rights, and that power includes the authority to issue orders necessary to ensure compliance.” Id. at 15. This principle is directly applicable here: the district court’s order to reduce prison population is a necessary and proper means of ensuring that the Eighth Amendment is not violated.

The Court has also recognized that the PLRA does not abrogate the judiciary’s power to enforce constitutional rights. In *United States v. United States District Court*, 339 U.S. 1 (1950), the Court held that federal courts may issue orders to remedy constitutional violations, even when such orders require structural changes. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “federal courts must enforce it.” Id. at 14. The PLRA, enacted in 1996, does not alter this fundamental principle. The statute does not prohibit courts from issuing orders to remedy unconstitutional conditions; it only limits the scope of injunctive relief in certain circumstances. But where a constitutional violation is proven, and a remedy is necessary to prevent ongoing harm, the court’s authority to act remains intact.

Finally, in *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954), the Court held that the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment prohibits racial segregation in the District of Columbia. The Court stated: “We cannot close our eyes to the fact that segregation in public education is a denial of equal protection of the laws.” Id. at 500. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution protects all persons from arbitrary deprivation of liberty.” Id. at 501. This principle applies to prisoners: the Constitution protects them from arbitrary and inhumane treatment. The district court’s order to reduce prison population is a necessary and proportionate remedy to prevent such treatment.
