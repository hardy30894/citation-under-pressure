**ARGUMENT**

I. THE BANKRUPTCY COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT COHEN’S FAILURE TO COMPLY WITH A RENT CONTROL ORDINANCE CONSTITUTES “ACTUAL FRAUD” UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A)

The Bankruptcy Court’s determination that Cohen’s conduct amounted to “actual fraud” under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code is fundamentally flawed. That section excepts from discharge only debts “to the extent obtained by false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The Supreme Court has made clear that “actual fraud” requires more than mere negligence or failure to comply with a regulatory scheme—it demands a deliberate misrepresentation made with the intent to deceive. *In re Grogan*, 498 U.S. 279, 286 (1991). Here, Cohen made no affirmative misrepresentation to the tenants. He did not tell them that his rent charges were lawful; he did not claim compliance with the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance; and he did not affirmatively misstate any fact. The Court of Appeals erred in concluding that silence, in the absence of a duty to disclose, can constitute a “false representation” under § 523(a)(2)(A).

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991 (Bankr.D.N.J.1992), is misplaced. *Poskanzer* involved a debtor who misrepresented his financial condition in a loan application—conduct that is explicitly covered by the exception in § 523(a)(2)(A). See 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) (excluding debts obtained by “a false representation, or actual fraud, other than a statement representing the debtor’s or other insider’s financial condition”). The present case, by contrast, involves a failure to comply with a municipal rent control law, not a misrepresentation about one’s financial status. The distinction is critical. As the Supreme Court held in *Grogan*, “the purpose of § 523(a)(2)(A) is to protect creditors from intentional fraud, not to enforce regulatory compliance.” 498 U.S. at 286. The Court of Appeals’ extension of the “false representation” doctrine to include implied representations based on silence and inaction exceeds the scope of the statute and contravenes settled precedent.

Moreover, the Court of Appeals’ conclusion that Cohen’s conduct amounted to “reckless indifference” to the truth is unsupported by the record. The Court of Appeals held that “reckless conduct refers to unreasonable conduct in disregard of a known or obvious risk from which it is highly probable that harm would follow.” *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830, 834 (Bankr.E.D.Va.1991). But the record shows that Cohen believed he was entitled to charge market-rate rents and that he had been informed he could pass on increases in taxes and water charges. He had no reason to believe that the rent control ordinance limited the rent he could charge for vacant units. He consulted no attorney, but neither did he act with conscious disregard for the law. The record contains no evidence of a pattern of conduct, nor any indication that Cohen was aware of the ordinance’s existence or that he deliberately ignored it. The Court of Appeals’ finding of “reckless indifference” is therefore clearly erroneous under *Bankruptcy Rule 8013*, which mandates that findings of fact be upheld unless clearly erroneous.

The Court of Appeals further erred in treating the Bankruptcy Court’s determination of “actual fraud” as a mixed question of law and fact subject to de novo review. *Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes & Co.*, 669 F.2d 98, 102 (3d Cir.1981). The Court of Appeals correctly identified that the determination of whether a debtor made a false representation or acted with intent to deceive is an “ultimate fact” requiring de novo review. But the Court of Appeals failed to apply the correct standard: the finding of fraud must be supported by a preponderance of the evidence. *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286. Here, the evidence does not meet that threshold. Cohen’s failure to read the ordinance or consult an attorney does not equate to fraud. As the Supreme Court has emphasized, “a debtor’s ignorance of the law is not a basis for finding fraud.” *In re Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286. The Court of Appeals’ conclusion that Cohen’s conduct constituted “actual fraud” is therefore legally and factually unsustainable.

II. THE TREBLE DAMAGES AWARDED UNDER THE NEW JERSEY CONSUMER FRAUD ACT ARE DISCHARGEABLE UNDER § 523(a)(2)(A)

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are non-dischargeable. The statute provides for treble damages “to the extent obtained by” fraud. N.J.S.A. 56:8-19. But the Bankruptcy Code’s exception in § 523(a)(2)(A) applies only to debts “obtained by” fraud, false pretenses, or false representations. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The Supreme Court has held that “the term ‘obtained by’ in § 523(a)(2)(A) refers to the manner in which the debt was incurred, not the manner in which the damages were calculated.” *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 195, 198 (9th Cir.1991). In *Levy*, the Ninth Circuit held that punitive damages—awarded at the discretion of a jury—were dischargeable because they were not “obtained by” fraud. Id. The Court of Appeals here incorrectly distinguished *Levy* on the ground that the damages were statutory. But the source of the damages—statutory or discretionary—does not alter the fundamental principle that the debt must have been “obtained by” fraud to be excepted from discharge.
