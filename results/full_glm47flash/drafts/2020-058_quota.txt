**Argument**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) does not constitute an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The statutory text, legislative history, and the purpose of the TPS program support the conclusion that TPS confers the necessary admission for adjustment of status. The court’s rigid distinction between “admission” and “lawful status” ignores the functional reality of the immigration system and renders a key provision of the statute superfluous.

**I.  A Grant of TPS Constitutes an “Admission” for Purposes of Adjustment of Status**

The Third Circuit correctly defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012). However, the court failed to recognize that a grant of TPS constitutes the statutory equivalent of such inspection and authorization. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4), an alien with TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This status is the functional equivalent of admission for the purpose of adjustment of status under § 1255(a). The Ninth Circuit reached the correct conclusion in this regard, holding that “a grant of TPS constitutes an admission” for adjustment purposes. *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 961 (9th Cir. 2017). The court reasoned that the statutory scheme allows for specific grants of status to satisfy the admission requirement, and TPS is precisely such a grant.

**II.  The Distinction Between Status and Admission is Not Absolute**

The Third Circuit relied on the distinction between “admission” and “status” to deny TPS holders admission. *See* *Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (holding that TPS grants status but not admission). However, this distinction is not absolute. The Board of Immigration Appeals has held that “an adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.” *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). This principle supports the view that a grant of TPS, which facilitates adjustment, constitutes an admission. Furthermore, the Board has noted that while a grant of asylum places an individual in valid immigration status, it is not an “admission.” *In re H-G-G-*, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (AAO 2019). This demonstrates that the distinction is nuanced; the specific statutory language granting TPS was intended to bridge the gap between status and admission for adjustment purposes.

**III.  The Statute Should Not Be Read to Render a Provision Superfluous**

The Third Circuit’s interpretation risks rendering a key provision of the TPS statute superfluous. *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). If a grant of TPS does not satisfy the admission requirement of § 1255(a), then the clause in § 1254a(f)(4) stating that TPS holders are “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” becomes meaningless for adjustment purposes. Congress would have no reason to include this specific language if it did not serve to satisfy the admission requirement. *Id.*; *Cardoza-Fonseca v. INS*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (stating that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion”).

**IV.  The Purpose of the TPS Statute Supports This Interpretation**

The purpose of the TPS program is to provide temporary relief to aliens already in the United States due to extraordinary conditions. *Mejia Rodriguez v. U.S. Dep’t of Homeland Sec.*, 562 F.3d 1137, 1140 (11th Cir. 2009). Denying TPS holders the ability to adjust their status creates a “catch-22” that undermines this humanitarian purpose. By reading § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy the admission requirement, Congress ensured that TPS holders could eventually seek permanent residence, thereby fulfilling the dual purpose of providing temporary safety and a pathway to stability. The Third Circuit’s interpretation, which forces TPS holders to leave the country and reenter lawfully to adjust status, ignores the reality that such reentry is often impossible due to unsafe conditions in their home countries. *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 962.
