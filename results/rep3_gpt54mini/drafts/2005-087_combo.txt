Arizona’s rule is constitutional because due process requires only that a defendant be permitted to contest the State’s proof, not that he be allowed to introduce every category of mental-state evidence in whatever doctrinal form he prefers. The Fourteenth Amendment does not freeze one particular common-law method for litigating mens rea, and it certainly does not prohibit a State from channeling psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense while leaving the State’s proof of intent to be tested by other admissible evidence. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 799-803 (1952); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 202-07 (1977) (recognizing that States retain broad authority to define and allocate defenses and mental-state issues). Arizona’s evidentiary limitation therefore does not violate due process.

This Court has long recognized that States may define the elements of crimes and the incidents of defenses. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1895), the Court distinguished the Government’s burden of proving the charged offense from affirmative defenses and upheld the allocation of the insanity burden to the defendant in federal prosecutions. In Leland, 343 U.S. at 798-99, the Court reaffirmed that due process is not offended when a State requires the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. If a State may constitutionally place the full burden of proving insanity on the accused, it follows that the State may also limit the evidentiary use of psychiatric proof to that defense. See also Foster v. State, 36 Ark. 258, 264-65 (1880) (upholding a rule excluding partial responsibility evidence outside insanity); People v. Troche, 206 P. 329, 331-32 (Cal. 1922) (same).

Clark’s theory would constitutionalize diminished-capacity evidence as a separate due process right, but this Court has never held that due process requires such a doctrine. To the contrary, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that states are not compelled to adopt a particular insanity formulation or mental-state excuse. In Clark v. Arizona, 548 U.S. 735, 767-77 (2006), the Court confirmed that Arizona may confine expert psychiatric evidence to insanity because such evidence poses special risks of confusion and invites jurors to misuse diagnoses as a proxy for legal incapacity. Although decided after the events here, Clark relied on settled due process principles long recognized in earlier cases. See Davis, 160 U.S. at 484-85; Leland, 343 U.S. at 798-99.

The Court’s due process decisions concerning proof of guilt likewise show that Arizona’s rule does not impermissibly relieve the State of its burden to prove intent. In In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court held that due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged. But Winship does not dictate the admissibility of every item of defense evidence; it concerns the standard of proof, not the admissibility of evidence or the scope of expert testimony. See also Speiser v. Randall, 357 U.S. 513, 523-26 (1958) (due process concerns the allocation of burdens, not a right to any particular evidentiary theory). Arizona still must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt; it simply permits that proof to be contested by ordinary evidence rather than by a psychiatric mini-trial on partial responsibility.

Nor does the Constitution require a defendant to be allowed to present any and all evidence he labels “rebuttal” to mens rea. The Court has upheld evidentiary rules that exclude otherwise relevant proof when the State has a legitimate basis for doing so. See Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 22-23 (1967) (recognizing a right to present witnesses, but not abolishing ordinary evidentiary limits); Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683, 689-91 (1986) (same principle, but subject to traditional evidence rules). Arizona’s rule is especially justified because psychiatric testimony about capacity to form intent is notoriously ambiguous and can collapse legal insanity into an amorphous moral defense. See Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 316-17 (1957) (the law may insist on clear legal categories rather than invite confusion over mental state). A State may reasonably conclude that jurors should not hear expert testimony that risks turning the insanity defense into a partial exculpation doctrine under another name.

Finally, historical practice supports Arizona. At common law, insanity was the recognized route for mental disease evidence to excuse criminal liability; diminished responsibility was not a constitutional entitlement. See M’Naghten’s Case, 8 Eng. Rep. 718, 722-23 (H.L. 1843); Davis, 160 U.S. at 483-85. Because due process protects against fundamentally unfair procedures, not against every restriction on a preferred defense theory, Arizona’s restriction is valid. See Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-08 (1934). The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
