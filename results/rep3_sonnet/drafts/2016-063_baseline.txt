# ARGUMENT

The Civil Service Reform Act channels judicial review of "mixed cases"—cases in which a federal employee challenges an action appealable to the Merit Systems Protection Board and alleges that the action was discriminatory—to federal district court rather than the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); id. § 7703(b)(2). The Board dismissed Mr. Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction, finding his retirement voluntary. Under this Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), that dismissal belongs in district court.

## I. Kloeckner Establishes That All Board Dismissals of Mixed Cases—Regardless of the Ground—Are Reviewable in District Court

In Kloeckner, this Court confronted the identical statutory text at issue here and held that when the Board dismisses a mixed case "without deciding whether discrimination occurred," judicial review lies in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 568 U.S. at 44. The Court's holding rested on the plain text of § 7702(a)(1), which defines a mixed case by two elements: the employee "has been affected by an action which the employee . . . may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board," and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." Id. at 44 (quoting 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)). The Court emphasized that "[t]he statute does not say" that the forum depends on how the Board disposes of the case. Id. at 50. Rather, "Title 5 of the U.S. Code establishes a uniform review scheme," and "the appropriate forum for judicial review depends not on how the MSPB decided the case, but on the nature of the case the employee brought before the MSPB." Id. at 50, 56.

The Court's reasoning was categorical, not limited to procedural dismissals. This Court explained that "Congress crafted a scheme in which the forum for judicial review is fixed by the nature of the claims an employee raises, not by the way the MSPB decides those claims." Id. at 56. It further reasoned that bifurcating review based on the Board's disposition would produce "administrative unmanageability," because "the way the Board decides a case could change over time" as facts developed, requiring shifts in forum "in midstream." Id. at 53. That practical concern applies with equal—if not greater—force to jurisdictional dismissals, which frequently turn on contested factual questions (like voluntariness) that overlap substantially with the merits of a discrimination claim.

## II. The D.C. Circuit's Procedural/Jurisdictional Distinction Cannot Survive Kloeckner

The D.C. Circuit below acknowledged that its precedent in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), routed jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit, but concluded that Kloeckner did not "eviscerate" Powell because Kloeckner addressed only "procedural" dismissals. This distinction is untenable for three reasons.

**First**, it cannot be squared with Kloeckner's statutory analysis. Section 7702(a)(1)(A) asks whether the employee "has been affected by an action which the employee . . . may appeal" to the Board. The D.C. Circuit reasoned that a jurisdictionally barred appeal was never "appealable" at all, whereas a procedurally barred one remains "appealable" because the Board could have excused the procedural defect. But this reads far too much into the word "may." The relevant inquiry is whether the type of action—removal, suspension, retirement treated as involuntary—falls within the categories Congress made appealable under 5 U.S.C. §§ 7512–7513, not whether the Board ultimately agreed that the employee's particular circumstances satisfied jurisdictional prerequisites like involuntariness. Mr. Perry's retirement and suspension were unquestionably actions of the type "which the employee may appeal to the Board"; the Board simply disagreed with his factual contention that the action was involuntary. That is indistinguishable from Kloeckner's own case, where the Board could have found her appeal untimely and thus barred, yet this Court held the timeliness question irrelevant to forum selection.

**Second**, Kloeckner's rationale for a uniform forum applies precisely to jurisdictional dismissals premised on voluntariness. This Court warned against making "judicial review . . . fluctuate" depending on how the Board rules, because such rules would be "bizarre" and productive of only "chaos." 568 U.S. at 55. A voluntariness determination—like the one here—often requires the same factual inquiry into the employer's conduct that underlies the discrimination claim itself. Split-forum review of intertwined merits and jurisdictional questions creates exactly the inefficiency and confusion Kloeckner sought to eliminate.

**Third**, this Court's broader admonition in Kloeckner that mixed-case review is governed by "the nature of the case," not "the way the MSPB disposed of it," id. at 56, forecloses any dismissal-based line-drawing. The Court did not qualify its holding by reference to the type of threshold ground for dismissal; it announced a rule keyed to the nature of the claims presented to the Board, full stop.

## III. Practical Administration of the Civil Service Scheme Confirms District Court Review

This Court in Kloeckner noted the "singular purpose" of the CSRA's review provisions is to move federal employees "expeditiously" through a "unified" scheme. Id. at 44 (quoting Elgin v. Department of Treasury, 567 U.S. 1, 5 (2012)). Requiring employees to guess, at the time of filing, which forum will ultimately have jurisdiction—depending on how the Board eventually rules on a fact-intensive voluntariness question—undermines that purpose. Deadlines for seeking judicial review, see 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)–(2), function coherently only if the forum is fixed at the outset by the nature of the claims, exactly as Kloeckner held.

## CONCLUSION

Because Mr. Perry's case was a mixed case under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1), and because Kloeckner forecloses any distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals for purposes of forum selection, review of the Board's decision belongs in federal district court. This Court should reverse.
