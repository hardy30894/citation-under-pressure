The School District's refusal to permit Lamb's Chapel to exhibit films addressing family values from a religious perspective abridges the First Amendment right to freedom of speech. When a state opens its property for expressive activity by the public, it may not exclude speech on the basis of its religious content. Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public"); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939) (municipalities may not "abridge the right of free speech" by selective exclusion).

The District's policy and practice of granting access to civic, cultural, and educational groups created a forum open to the community. In such a forum, "the right of free speech" cannot be denied "because of its supposed injurious consequences." Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 310 (1940). Content-based restrictions are invalid unless they serve a compelling interest and are narrowly drawn. Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (licensing schemes must be "narrowly drawn" to avoid "arbitrary" suppression).

The District's exclusion of religious speech while permitting secular programs on identical subjects is viewpoint discrimination. Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (denial of permits "based on the religious content" of speech violates the First Amendment); Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 293 (1951) (ordinance permitting officials to suppress speech "because of its religious character" is unconstitutional). Fowler v. Rhode Island, 345 U.S. 67, 70 (1953) (state may not "prefer some religious groups over others").

Even in a limited forum, the government may not discriminate among speakers on the basis of the ideas they express. Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (amplification permit system struck down where it allowed officials to favor certain messages). The District's rule barring "religious purposes" while authorizing other community uses fails this test. Edwards v. South Carolina, 372 U.S. 229, 237 (1963) (peaceful expression on public property may not be suppressed "because the ideas expressed may be unpopular").

The same principle applies to after-hours use of school facilities opened for community expression. Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 536, 557 (1965) (content-based exclusion from public property "must be narrowly drawn"). Allowing secular films on family life while barring religious perspectives on the same topic is not a permissible time, place, or manner regulation. Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503, 513 (1969) (state may not "suppress expression" merely because it disapproves the viewpoint).

The District's denial therefore violates the First Amendment.
