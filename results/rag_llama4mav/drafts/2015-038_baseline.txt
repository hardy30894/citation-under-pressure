ARGUMENT

I. The Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) permits the National Park Service (NPS) to regulate activities on state and private land within the boundaries of Conservation System Units (CSUs) in Alaska.

The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) governs this case. The statute provides that "only those public lands within the boundaries of any conservation system unit which are designated as part of such unit pursuant to this Act shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that this language does not preclude NPS from regulating activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. Sturgeon v. Masica, 768 F.3d 1066, 1075-76 (9th Cir. 2014).

Sturgeon's interpretation of § 103(c) as limiting NPS's regulatory authority to federally owned lands within CSUs is unfounded. The statute's language does not explicitly restrict NPS's authority to public lands. See 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). In the absence of such a limitation, it is reasonable to infer that Congress intended to grant NPS authority to regulate activities within CSUs, regardless of land ownership.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that federal agencies have authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands when necessary to protect federal interests. See Colorado v. Toll, 268 U.S. 228, 231-32 (1925) (upholding federal regulation of private lands within a national park). In this case, NPS's regulation of hovercraft use within the Yukon-Charley River National Preserve is necessary to protect the preserve's natural resources and fulfill the purposes of ANILCA.

II. The National Park Service's regulation of hovercraft use within the Yukon-Charley River National Preserve is a valid exercise of its authority under ANILCA.

NPS's hovercraft ban, codified at 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), is a reasonable exercise of its authority to regulate activities within CSUs. The regulation is designed to protect the preserve's natural resources and maintain the integrity of the National Park System. See 54 Fed. Reg. 2046, 2047 (Jan. 19, 1989) (explaining the rationale behind the hovercraft ban).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that NPS's regulation is not limited to public lands within CSUs. Sturgeon, 768 F.3d at 1076. The regulation applies to all lands and waters within the preserve, including state-owned lands and waters. This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court's holding in United States v. Pacific & Arctic Railway & Navigation Co., 228 U.S. 87, 107-08 (1913), which established that federal laws can regulate activities on non-federal lands when necessary to protect federal interests.

III. Sturgeon lacks a valid claim that the NPS regulation violates his rights under the Privileges or Immunities Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Sturgeon's claim that the NPS regulation infringes upon his right to navigate the Nation River is unfounded. The Supreme Court has held that the Privileges or Immunities Clause protects the right to navigate navigable waters, but this right is subject to reasonable federal regulation. See The Slaughter-House Cases, 83 U.S. 36, 79 (1872); see also Courtney v. Goltz, 736 F.3d 1152, 1160 (9th Cir. 2013). NPS's hovercraft ban is a reasonable regulation designed to protect the preserve's natural resources, and it does not impermissibly infringe upon Sturgeon's right to navigate the Nation River.

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
