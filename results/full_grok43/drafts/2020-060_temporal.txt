The United States Navy bears financial responsibility for the environmental hazards at Ordot Dump. The Navy constructed and operated the landfill throughout its period of exclusive military governance over Guam. See De Lima v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 1, 2 (1901) (noting that upon acquisition “the island became territory of the United States”). That governance continued until the Guam Organic Act of 1950 transferred only civil administration; antecedent liabilities incurred under federal military authority remained with the United States. See Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901) (“The power to acquire territory . . . is derived from the treaty-making power”).

When the United States relinquished sovereignty, it did not thereby extinguish its own prior obligations. See Cross v. Harrison, 57 U.S. 164, 193 (1853) (holding that rights and liabilities arising under military occupation survive the transition to civil rule). The Navy’s continued use of the dump for military waste after 1950 did not alter this allocation; the United States remained the actor that introduced the contaminants. See United States v. Lee, 106 U.S. 196, 220 (1882) (recognizing that the federal government may be held to account for acts performed by its officers within territory under its control).

Guam, an unincorporated territory lacking plenary sovereignty, cannot be charged with costs originating in federal military operations. See Hawaii v. Mankichi, 190 U.S. 197, 211 (1903) (distinguishing the status of unincorporated territories from that of the several States). Principles of equitable recoupment long recognized at common law therefore require the United States to answer for the consequences of its own conduct. See The Ship Virgin v. Vyfhius, 33 U.S. 538, 550 (1834) (affirming that one who creates a nuisance must bear the expense of its abatement).

Accordingly, Guam may pursue full recovery from the Navy.
