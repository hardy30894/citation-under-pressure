The plain language of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) commands that actions transferred for pretrial proceedings "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." This directive is mandatory. Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947). Section 1404(a) supplies no authority for a transferee court to circumvent that command by assigning the case to itself for trial. A specific statutory mandate controls over a general venue provision. Radzanower v. Touche Ross & Co., 426 U.S. 148, 153 (1976).

Section 1407(a) reflects Congress's deliberate policy choice that multidistrict transfers are limited to pretrial matters, preserving plaintiffs' traditional right to select the trial forum. Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947). Allowing self-assignment would nullify the statutory obligation to remand and expand the Judicial Panel's authority beyond the text. See Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 342-43 (1960) (venue statutes must be applied as written).

Decisions permitting self-assignment rest on policy considerations rather than statutory text. Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971); In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litig., 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982). Those rationales cannot override the unambiguous remand requirement. Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 616-17 (1964) (transfers must respect statutory limits). The Judicial Panel's Rule 14(b) likewise lacks power to amend the statute.

Petitioners possess no other adequate remedy once trial proceeds in the wrong district, and the error is not correctable on appeal after judgment. Bauman v. United States Dist. Court, 557 F.2d 650, 653 (9th Cir. 1977). The district court's order therefore exceeded its authority under §§ 1404(a) and 1407(a).
