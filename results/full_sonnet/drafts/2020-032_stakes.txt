# ARGUMENT

## I. A CLAIM FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES ALONE CANNOT SATISFY THE REDRESSABILITY REQUIREMENT OF ARTICLE III ONCE THE UNDERLYING CONDUCT HAS CEASED

Article III restricts the "judicial Power" to actual "Cases" and "Controversies." U.S. Const. art. III, § 2. This Court has repeatedly held that "a federal court has neither the power to render advisory opinions nor to decide questions that cannot affect the rights of litigants in the case before them." *Preiser v. Newkirk*, 422 U.S. 395, 401 (1975) (internal quotation marks omitted). Mootness is "the doctrine of standing set in a time frame," and a case becomes moot "when the issues presented are no longer 'live' or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome." *Los Angeles County v. Davis*, 440 U.S. 625, 631 (1979) (internal quotation marks omitted).

Standing itself requires that a favorable decision be likely to redress the plaintiff's injury. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 561 (1992). This Court has cautioned that redressability cannot be manufactured through requests for relief that are themselves symbolic rather than substantive. In *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83 (1998), the Court held that a plaintiff's "generalized interest" in retribution for a past violation, unaccompanied by any continuing or future injury, could not sustain federal jurisdiction, because a favorable decision would do nothing more than "gratify" the plaintiff's "psychic satisfaction." *Id.* at 107. The Court explained that "relief that does not remedy the injury suffered cannot bootstrap a plaintiff into federal court." *Id.* at 107.

Nominal damages occupy precisely this constitutionally insufficient space. They are, by definition, not compensation for any injury; they are a token award "in name only." *See* Restatement (Second) of Torts § 907 (Am. L. Inst. 1979). Once a defendant has voluntarily ceased the challenged conduct and there is no reasonable expectation that it will recur, a request for a nominal award does not remedy any actual, present, or future harm—it simply asks a federal court to declare, for its own sake, that the past conduct was unlawful. That is the paradigmatic advisory opinion Article III forbids.

## II. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT VOLUNTARY CESSATION OF THE CHALLENGED POLICY MOOTED PETITIONERS' CLAIMS FOR DECLARATORY AND INJUNCTIVE RELIEF, AND A BARE DEMAND FOR NOMINAL DAMAGES CANNOT REVIVE THEM

This Court has long recognized that governmental defendants are entitled to a presumption of good faith when they voluntarily discontinue challenged policies. In *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin's Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283 (1982), the Court observed that voluntary cessation by a government actor carries a "presumption" that the "actions were taken in good faith," distinguishing such cases from the "capable of repetition, yet evading review" exception applicable to private parties. *Id.* at 289 n.11. Where, as here, a public university amends its policy and there is no evidence—let alone the "reasonable expectation" required by *Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.*, 528 U.S. 167, 189 (2000)—that it will revert to the prior policy, the claims for prospective relief are moot.

Petitioners did not challenge that conclusion below. Pet. App. 7 n.1. Their sole argument is that a request for nominal damages, standing alone, preserves a live case or controversy notwithstanding the mootness of all prospective relief. That argument fails for two independent reasons.

**A. Nominal damages are not compensatory and therefore cannot supply the redressability that Article III demands.**

This Court's compensatory-damages jurisprudence under § 1983 confirms that damages must be tied to actual injury. In *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), the Court held that damages for violation of a constitutional right may not be presumed from "the abstract value of a constitutional right," and that absent proof of actual injury, a plaintiff is entitled to no more than nominal damages "not to exceed one dollar." *Id.* at 266–67. *Carey* thus treats nominal damages not as a species of compensation at all, but as a bookkeeping device to mark a technical violation where no provable injury exists. A request for that token award, once the offending conduct has stopped and no continuing injury is alleged, seeks nothing that a court's judgment can meaningfully redress—precisely the deficiency identified in *Steel Co.*, 523 U.S. at 107.

**B. Allowing nominal damages alone to save otherwise moot claims would eliminate mootness doctrine as applied to constitutional challenges to policies.**

Every plaintiff who pleads a completed constitutional violation could, under petitioners' theory, tack on a request for a token dollar and thereby guarantee a live controversy forever, regardless of subsequent policy change, transfer, or graduation. That result cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated instruction that mootness exists to ensure federal courts resolve only "definite and concrete" disputes touching the legal relations of adverse parties "having adverse legal interests." *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937). It would also override the carefully bounded exceptions this Court has crafted—voluntary cessation with reasonable expectation of recurrence, *Friends of the Earth*, 528 U.S. at 189, and capable-of-repetition-yet-evading-review, *Weinstein v. Bradford*, 423 U.S. 147, 149 (1975)—by creating a third, unlimited exception triggered by the plaintiff's own pleading choice. Nothing in this Court's precedent supports such an end-run around Article III's case-or-controversy requirement.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
