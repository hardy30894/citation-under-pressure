ARGUMENT

The government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Nieves-Castaño knew the firearm she possessed was a machinegun. Every criminal statute is to be construed to require proof of scienter as to each element that makes the conduct criminal. Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952) (“mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent should not be read as elimination of that element”). Where the prohibited item is defined by a specific characteristic, the prosecution must establish the defendant’s knowledge of that characteristic. United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922).

This requirement of personal knowledge cannot be satisfied by proof that the defendant was aware of some other attribute of the object. Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101 (1945) (“A criminal intent to do an act ‘generally’ is not enough; the intent must be to do the act prohibited by the statute.”). Nor may the element be inferred from mere possession or from the defendant’s handling of the item. Lambert v. California, 355 U.S. 225, 229 (1957) (“There is no sufficient notice of the prohibited act.”).

The evidence here showed only that Nieves-Castaño recognized the weapon as an AK-47 rifle. That fact does not establish knowledge that the rifle was capable of automatic fire. See United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277, 281 (1943) (liability attaches only upon proof of the defendant’s awareness of the facts constituting the violation). Circumstantial evidence must be of such quality that it excludes every reasonable hypothesis except that of guilt. Holland v. United States, 348 U.S. 121, 139 (1954). A verdict resting on speculation or conjecture cannot stand. Mortensen v. United States, 322 U.S. 369, 374 (1944).

The same principle governs regulatory offenses involving firearms. The defendant must be shown to have known the particular feature that the statute forbids. Boyce Motor Lines, Inc. v. United States, 342 U.S. 337, 342 (1952). Proof that the defendant knew the object was a firearm, without more, is insufficient. Smith v. California, 361 U.S. 147, 153 (1959) (“The constitutional requirement of scienter cannot be dispensed with.”). Because the record contains no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle was a machinegun, the conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) must be reversed. Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 516 (1951) (due process demands proof of every element of the offense).
