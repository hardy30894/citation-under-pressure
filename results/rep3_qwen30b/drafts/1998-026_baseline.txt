**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically warrant habeas relief—particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of that right at the time of sentencing and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to appeal. The violation of Rule 32 is subject to harmless-error review, and in this case, any error was plainly harmless.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires that “the court must inform the defendant of the right to appeal.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2). This requirement exists to ensure that defendants are fully informed of their appellate rights, but it is not a constitutional mandate and does not create a per se basis for vacating a sentence. *See United States v. Berríos*, 147 F.3d 105, 108 (1st Cir. 1998) (“[T]he rule is procedural, not substantive, and its violation does not automatically invalidate a conviction or sentence.”); *United States v. Soto*, 160 F.3d 124, 127 (2d Cir. 1998) (“Failure to comply with Rule 32(a)(2) is not itself grounds for reversal absent prejudice.”).

This Court has long recognized that procedural rules, even those designed to protect fundamental rights, are subject to harmless-error analysis when their violation does not affect substantial rights. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 511, 540 (1946) (“Error which does not affect substantial rights is not ground for setting aside a conviction.”). The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—to ensure that defendants understand their right to appeal—is fulfilled when the defendant is actually aware of that right, regardless of whether the court explicitly stated it. *See United States v. Mendoza*, 152 F.3d 1200, 1203 (9th Cir. 1998) (holding that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his right to appeal negates any prejudice from a failure to be advised”).

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero “knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing.” That finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous. The record reflects that Peguero had been represented by counsel throughout the proceedings, including during sentencing, and that he did not file a notice of appeal within the required timeframe. His failure to appeal, combined with his awareness of the appellate process, demonstrates that he understood his right and chose not to exercise it. *See United States v. D’Amato*, 115 F.3d 104, 107 (2d Cir. 1997) (finding no prejudice where defendant “was represented by counsel and failed to appeal despite having full knowledge of his rights”).

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that a defendant’s failure to appeal, when he is aware of his right to do so, constitutes a waiver of that right. *See United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 542 (1982) (“A defendant who fails to appeal after being properly advised of his right to do so is deemed to have waived that right.”). In this case, there is no indication that Peguero was misled or coerced into silence. He was not deprived of information necessary to make a rational decision about appeal. Thus, the absence of a formal advisement did not deprive him of a meaningful opportunity to appeal.

The Third Circuit correctly applied harmless-error review and concluded that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) had been satisfied. *See United States v. Cervantes*, 122 F.3d 1242, 1245 (9th Cir. 1997) (“When a defendant knows of his right to appeal, the failure to advise him is harmless.”). The Court should follow precedent and hold that a Rule 32 violation is not reversible per se, especially where the defendant was aware of his right and elected not to appeal.

To hold otherwise would transform a procedural safeguard into a mechanical bar to finality, undermining judicial efficiency and encouraging post-hoc challenges based on technicalities. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993) (“Harmless-error review preserves the integrity of the trial process while avoiding the wastefulness of reversing convictions for nonprejudicial errors.”). Such a rule would invite abuse: defendants could delay appeals, then claim they were never advised of their right—despite knowing it—simply to reopen judgments years later.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. The failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless error, given his actual knowledge and deliberate choice not to appeal. No constitutional or statutory right was violated, and no miscarriage of justice occurred. The judgment below stands.
