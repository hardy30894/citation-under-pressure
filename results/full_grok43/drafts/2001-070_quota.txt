The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments that are both cruel and unusual. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 171 (1976). A punishment is unusual if it is contrary to “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). When this Court last addressed the issue, it concluded that “the Eighth Amendment does not mandate a categorical exemption from the death penalty for mentally retarded persons.” Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989).

That conclusion remains sound. At the time of the founding, the common law permitted execution of persons with mental disabilities short of insanity. See Ford v. Wainwright, 477 U.S. 399, 406-07 (1986). Contemporary practice confirms the absence of a national consensus against the practice: when Penry was decided, only two States barred execution of the mentally retarded, 492 U.S. at 334, and today a substantial majority of death-penalty jurisdictions continue to authorize it. See Stanford v. Kentucky, 492 U.S. 361, 370-72 (1989) (plurality opinion) (requiring “objective indicia” of consensus). Legislative trends therefore do not demonstrate the “clear” and “consistent” direction required to find an Eighth Amendment violation. Coker v. Georgia, 433 U.S. 584, 594-96 (1977) (plurality opinion).

Petitioner’s reliance on clinical definitions of mental retardation does not alter the constitutional calculus. The Eighth Amendment inquiry focuses on whether a punishment is excessive in light of the offender’s culpability and the goals of retribution and deterrence, not on medical classifications. See Enmund v. Florida, 458 U.S. 782, 798-801 (1982). Mental retardation varies widely in degree; many such offenders possess sufficient understanding to distinguish right from wrong and to control their conduct. Penry, 492 U.S. at 338-39. Individualized consideration of these factors through sentencing procedures satisfies the Constitution. See McCleskey v. Kemp, 481 U.S. 279, 303-04 (1987); Gregg, 428 U.S. at 188-89.

Because no national consensus has emerged against executing mentally retarded capital offenders, and because existing procedures permit fair assessment of culpability, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
