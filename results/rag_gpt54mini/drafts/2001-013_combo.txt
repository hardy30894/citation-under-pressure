OSHA has jurisdiction here because the Act withdraws OSHA only when another federal agency actually “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health” over the working conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s general authority over marine safety does not itself displace OSHA. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated Coast Guard regulation as compatible with other federal regulation unless Congress has made the Coast Guard’s regime exclusive. See Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit, 362 U.S. 440, 443-44 (1960); Cook v. United States, 288 U.S. 102, 120-21 (1933); Maul v. United States, 274 U.S. 501, 521-22 (1927).

Mallard’s vessel was an uninspected drilling barge. Congress has expressly authorized Coast Guard regulation of uninspected vessels, but only in limited subject areas such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and alerting equipment. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. That limited scheme falls far short of the “comprehensive code” that would preempt OSHA. Huron Portland Cement, 362 U.S. at 443-44. In Huron, the Court recognized that federal approval of ship equipment did not oust local regulation where the local rule was complementary rather than conflicting. Id. at 443. So too here: OSHA’s standards supplement, rather than contradict, the Coast Guard’s sparse rules for uninspected vessels.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary approach in Clary and Donovan misreads § 4(b)(1). Those decisions involved “seamen” on “vessels in navigation,” and the court there reasoned that “OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.” Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825, 826-27 (5th Cir. 1983); Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120, 1121-22 (5th Cir. 1980). But those cases did not confront the statutory distinction between comprehensive regulation and minimal regulation of uninspected vessels. Here, the Coast Guard has only partial authority. Congress did not say that any Coast Guard power, however limited, extinguishes OSHA jurisdiction. Section 4(b)(1) requires more: actual exercise of authority over the occupational safety or health standard in question. See Secretary of Labor v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., 25 O.S.H. Cas. (BNA) 1188 (O.S.H.R.C. 2001) (recognizing the statutory inquiry is whether another agency has exercised authority over the cited conditions).

Supreme Court precedent also counsels against reading general maritime safety statutes to displace otherwise applicable federal remedies absent a clear statement. In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-57 (1969), the Court rejected an overbroad displacement theory and looked carefully to the specific statutory scheme governing the location and activity at issue. Likewise, in Roper v. United States, 368 U.S. 20, 22-23 (1961), the Court focused on whether the vessel was “in navigation” for the asserted maritime doctrine, demonstrating that vessel status matters and that categorical rules are disfavored when Congress has drawn distinctions.

Here, Congress drew one. It allowed OSHA to operate unless another agency’s standards actually cover the working conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That reading fits with the Court’s repeated insistence that federal maritime regulation depends on the precise scope of statutory authorization. See Street v. Shipowners’ Ass’n of the Pacific Coast, 263 U.S. 334, 344-45 (1923); Blamberg Bros. v. United States, 260 U.S. 452, 458-60 (1923); Northern Belle v. Robson, 154 U.S. 571, 577-78 (1870). It also accords with the Court’s recognition that commerce and navigation are not left to piecemeal, overlapping regulation unless Congress says so. Sherlock v. Alling, 93 U.S. 99, 103-04 (1876).

Mallard’s vessel was not under a comprehensive Coast Guard safety code. The Coast Guard’s limited uninspected-vessel rules do not “exercise statutory authority” over the broad occupational hazards OSHA cited. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Because Congress did not preempt OSHA in that circumstance, the citations were valid.
