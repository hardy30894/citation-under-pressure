**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. For over a century, federal courts exercising equitable discretion have recognized that exceptional attorney performance and extraordinary results may justify an upward adjustment of the fee award. This longstanding authority, rooted in pre-1970 decisions, confirms that the district court properly enhanced the lodestar figure in this case.

**I. The Equitable Power to Enhance Fees for Exceptional Performance and Results Is Firmly Established in Pre-1970 Precedent**

Federal courts have long possessed inherent equitable authority to award attorney’s fees that reflect the true value of legal services. That authority includes the power to enhance fees when the quality of representation and the results obtained are extraordinary. *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 537 (1882) (holding that a court may allow “such compensation as is reasonable” and may consider “the benefit conferred” in setting the fee). In *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 127 (1885), the Court affirmed that a fee award may be “liberal” where the attorney’s efforts “produced results of great value.” Similarly, *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939) recognized that a district court “has the power to award counsel fees” in equity and may exercise “a discretion that is not controlled by rigid rules.”

Lower federal courts consistently applied these principles. In *McFarland v. Hurley*, 286 F. 775, 781 (8th Cir. 1923), the court stated that “where the services are rendered under extraordinary circumstances, and the results are of great value, the court may allow a larger compensation than the ordinary rate.” *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d 222, 224 (2d Cir. 1941) listed “the skill and ability of the attorney and the results accomplished” as proper factors in setting fees. *Guardian Trust Co. v. Kansas City Southern Ry. Co.*, 28 F.2d 877, 881 (8th Cir. 1928) affirmed that a court may consider “the character and importance of the litigation, the amount involved, the benefit conferred, and the skill and labor required.” These cases demonstrate that enhancement based on the quality of performance and results is not a novel concept but a well-established equitable tool.

**II. The Discretion to Enhance Is Consistent with the Purposes of Federal Fee-Shifting Statutes**

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, it incorporated the common-law equitable tradition. The Supreme Court in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) emphasized that a fee award under a civil rights statute should be “reasonable” and that “a plaintiff who obtains an injunction against a defendant should ordinarily recover attorney’s fees.” The reasonableness standard, as the Court explained, ensures that successful plaintiffs can obtain “effective legal representation.” *Id.* The discretion to set a reasonable fee necessarily includes the power to enhance fees when the lodestar fails to compensate fully for exceptional performance and extraordinary results. *Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 167 (recognizing that a court may allow a fee that is “liberal” in light of the results obtained).

The petitioners’ argument that enhancement is never permissible would repudiate this settled history. The pre-1970 cases uniformly hold that courts may consider the quality of representation and the results obtained in setting fees. *In re Gilbert*, 276 U.S. 294, 296 (1928) (holding that a district court “has a large discretion in the allowance of fees” and may consider “the results obtained”). The district court here acted within the bounds of that discretion.

**III. The District Court’s Detailed Findings Satisfy the Requirements for Enhancement**

The district court did not rely on “solely” the quality of performance and results; it made detailed findings based on its own substantial experience and familiarity with prevailing rates in Atlanta, combined with its observation of counsel’s stellar performance throughout the long and difficult case. The court specifically found that the representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee. Such findings are precisely the “specific evidence” required by the equitable tradition. *In re Gilbert*, 276 U.S. at 296 (discretion “must be exercised with due regard to all the circumstances”). The trial judge’s personal observation of counsel’s performance is a proper factor in setting fees. *McFarland*, 286 F. at 781 (noting that the court may use its own knowledge of the case).

The circuit court correctly affirmed. The pre-1970 cases establish that enhancements are permissible in rare and exceptional cases. The district court’s enhancement was based on specific evidence and detailed findings, not on a “solely” quality-based rationale. The judgment should be affirmed.
