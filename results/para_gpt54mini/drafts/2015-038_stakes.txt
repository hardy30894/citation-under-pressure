ANILCA Does Not Strip the National Park Service of Authority To Enforce Park-Wide Regulations Within Alaska Park Units

ANILCA § 103(c) preserves state, Native, corporate, and private inholdings from being deemed “public lands” merely because they lie inside a park boundary; it does not create a jurisdictional enclave exempting those lands and waters from otherwise valid Park Service regulations. The statutory text confirms that “the term ‘public lands’ means land situated in Alaska which, after December 2, 1980, are Federal lands,” while “lands, waters, and interests therein” conveyed to the State, Native corporations, or other nonfederal owners “within the exterior boundaries of any conservation system unit” are “not deemed to be public lands.” Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act, Pub. L. No. 96-487, § 103(c), 94 Stat. 2371, 2374 (1980). The provision limits only the category of land that counts as “public lands”; it says nothing about eliminating federal regulatory authority over conduct occurring in or affecting a park unit. See Sturgeon v. Frost, 577 U.S. 424, 441-42 (2016) (ANILCA “does not bar the application of all NPS regulations to non-public lands within conservation system units”).

The Supreme Court’s prior decisions in this case make that point explicit. In Sturgeon v. Frost, 136 S. Ct. 1061 (2016), the Court rejected the broad rule that § 103(c) categorically disables NPS regulation on nonfederal lands and waters within Alaska park units, explaining that the statute must be read in light of ANILCA’s structure and the distinct treatment of “public lands” and nonpublic inholdings. Sturgeon v. Frost, 577 U.S. 424, 441-45 (2016). The Court observed that § 103(c) prevents federal lands from being “deemed to be public lands” simply by virtue of adjacency, but it does not follow that Congress intended to exempt nonpublic lands from every park regulation. Id. at 444-45. That is especially true where, as here, the regulation is a generally applicable NPS rule governing the use of a motorized vehicle in a park unit. Id. at 446-47.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied that understanding on remand in holding that the hovercraft ban is not the kind of regulation § 103(c) displaces. Sturgeon v. Frost, 872 F.3d 927, 934-38 (9th Cir. 2017). The court reasoned that NPS regulations applicable to the park unit as a whole remain valid even when they reach state-owned waters within the unit, because the statute does not withdraw all federal authority over conduct occurring there. Id. at 936-37. That conclusion accords with Supreme Court precedent recognizing that Congress may authorize broad federal regulation in park areas notwithstanding competing state ownership interests. See Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525-29 (1897); United States v. Alford, 274 U.S. 264, 267-68 (1927).

Petitioner’s contrary reading would convert § 103(c) into a sweeping exception that the text does not support. If Congress had intended to prohibit the Park Service from applying any regulation to any state, Native, corporate, or private land physically inside a park boundary, it could have said so expressly. Instead, Congress used narrower language targeted to federal land status. Courts do not “rewrite statutes” to expand exceptions beyond their terms. Lamie v. U.S. Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 538 (2004). Nor may a court infer a jurisdiction-stripping rule from silence where Congress knows how to speak clearly when it wishes to displace federal authority. See Arlington Cent. Sch. Dist. Bd. of Educ. v. Murphy, 548 U.S. 291, 296 (2006).

Petitioner also relies on Alaska’s sovereign ownership of the submerged lands beneath the Nation River. But state title to riverbeds does not eliminate federal regulatory power over uses that occur within a federally designated park unit. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that navigable waters and submerged lands may be subject to overlapping federal and state authority. See United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935); PPL Mont., LLC v. Montana, 565 U.S. 576, 591-92 (2012). ANILCA did not disturb that baseline by turning nonfederal inholdings into federal enclaves immune from park regulations. Sturgeon v. Frost, 577 U.S. at 443-45.

Finally, petitioner’s as-applied challenge fails for the independent reason that the NPS rule at issue is a park-wide hovercraft restriction, not a regulation aimed uniquely at nonpublic lands. The Court has already held that such a regulation is enforceable within Alaska park units notwithstanding § 103(c). Sturgeon v. Frost, 577 U.S. at 447-48. Because ANILCA does not withdraw that authority, the judgment below should be affirmed.
