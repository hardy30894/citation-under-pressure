**ARGUMENT**

I. The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period.

The Fifth Circuit erred in concluding that § 1231(a)(6) permits indefinite detention so long as the Government continues “good faith efforts” to effectuate removal. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699 (2001). This construction ignores the constitutional imperative to avoid “serious constitutional concerns” arising from prolonged detention without termination. *Id.* at 690. The Court recognized that indefinite detention of aliens who have entered the United States would raise grave due process concerns under the Fifth Amendment. *Id.* at 690-91. To avoid these concerns, the Court construed § 1231(a)(6) as containing an implicit “reasonable time” limitation. *Id.* at 699.

This Court has long held that statutes must be construed to avoid constitutional infirmity where possible. *United States v. Thirty-seven Photographs*, 402 U.S. 363, 376 (1971) (citing *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937)). The principle is rooted in the earliest days of this Republic. In *United States v. The Peggy*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 103, 110 (1801), Chief Justice Marshall instructed that “[w]here a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter.” This canon of constitutional avoidance is particularly apt where, as here, the statute’s text is ambiguous and the constitutional concerns are substantial. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 690-91.

The Fifth Circuit’s construction of § 1231(a)(6) as permitting indefinite detention conflicts with this Court’s precedents on the permissible duration of civil commitment. In *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972), the Court held that “a person charged by a State with a criminal offense who is committed solely on account of his incapacity to proceed to trial cannot be held more than the reasonable period of time necessary to determine whether there is a substantial probability that he will attain that capacity in the foreseeable future.” Though *Jackson* involved a criminal defendant, its reasoning applies with equal force to civil detention under § 1231(a)(6). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Due Process Clause prohibits indefinite civil commitment absent a constitutionally adequate purpose. *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 629 (1935) (citing *Myers v. United States*, 272 U.S. 52, 175 (1926)).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. *Mezei* involved an excludable alien held at the border, not a resident alien subject to a final removal order. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700. The Court distinguished *Mezei* on precisely this ground, noting that the alien in *Mezei* had “never entered the United States.” *Id.* at 700. The Court has long recognized that resident aliens are entitled to greater constitutional protections than excludable aliens. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 370 (1886) (“The Fourteenth Amendment to the Constitution is prohibitory upon the States, and the first section of the Civil Rights Act of 1866, 14 Stat. 27, c. 31, in effect, enforces the prohibitions of the amendment.”).

II. The habeas court must determine whether continued detention exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.

The Fifth Circuit erred in placing the burden on petitioner to show that deportation is impossible. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 704. The Court held that the habeas court must ask “whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” *Id.* at 704. The Court further held that “[i]f removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.” *Id.* The Court rejected the Government’s argument that the alien must show that deportation is impossible, explaining that such a standard “would seem to require an alien seeking release to show the absence of any prospect of removal—no matter how unlikely or unforeseeable—which demands more than our reading of the statute can bear.” *Id.*

This Court has long held that the Due Process Clause prohibits detention that is not reasonably necessary to effectuate a legitimate government purpose. In *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896), the Court held that “[a]ll persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection named in the [Fifth] Amendment, and the legislation of Congress can neither enlarge nor abridge those rights.” The Court has repeatedly emphasized that detention must be limited to the least restrictive means necessary to achieve a compelling government interest. *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 129 (1958) (citing *Aptheker v. Secretary of State*, 378 U.S. 500, 509 (1964)).

III. The presumptively reasonable period of detention is six months.

The Court held that “it is practically necessary to recognize some presumptively reasonable period of detention.” *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 705. The Court declined to hold that such period ends with the 90-day removal period, but instead adopted a six-month period beginning with the removal period. *Id.* The Court explained that “[a]fter this 6-month period, once the alien provides good reason to believe that there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must respond with evidence sufficient to rebut that showing.” *Id.*

This six-month presumption is consistent with this Court’s precedents on the permissible duration of civil commitment. In *Jackson v. Indiana*, 406 U.S. at 738, the Court held that a person committed solely on account of incapacity to proceed to trial cannot be held more than a reasonable period necessary to determine whether there is a substantial probability of attaining that capacity. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that detention must be limited in duration and subject to periodic review. *Humphrey’s Executor*, 295 U.S. at 629.

IV. The habeas court must consider the risk of the alien’s committing further crimes as a factor potentially justifying confinement within the reasonable removal period.

The Court held that the habeas court should consider the risk of the alien’s committing further crimes as a factor potentially justifying confinement within the reasonable removal period. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 704. The Court emphasized, however, that this factor is relevant only within the reasonable removal period. *Id.* The Court rejected the Government’s argument that detention is justified solely on the basis of danger to the community, explaining that “[t]he choice, however, is not between imprisonment and the alien ‘living at large.’ It is between imprisonment and supervision under release conditions that may not be violated.” *Id.* at 702.

This Court has long held that detention must be narrowly tailored to the government’s legitimate interests. In *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 748 (1987), the Court held that “[i]n our society liberty is the norm, and detention prior to trial or without trial is the carefully limited exception.” The Court has repeatedly emphasized that detention must be the least restrictive means necessary to achieve a compelling government interest. *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. at 129.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
