The Sixth Amendment jury trial right, incorporated against the States by the Fourteenth Amendment, requires only that the jury determine the facts constituting the offense itself. Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968). Sentencing determinations, including the presence of circumstances warranting a particular penalty, have never been treated as elements of the offense at common law. See, e.g., Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949) (describing the historical division between trial and sentencing functions).

Florida's capital sentencing statute assigns to the jury the task of rendering an advisory recommendation after considering statutory aggravating and mitigating circumstances. This division preserves the jury's traditional role in determining guilt while leaving the ultimate sentencing decision to the trial judge, consistent with longstanding practice. The requirement of a unanimous jury verdict applies only to the determination of guilt, not to advisory recommendations on sentence. Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948); Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 602 (1900) (States may prescribe non-unanimous verdicts without violating the Fourteenth Amendment).

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on cruel and unusual punishments, as understood at the time of its adoption and in early applications, does not mandate jury unanimity or a jury finding on any particular sentencing factor. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 368 (1910); see also Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958) (noting that the Amendment draws its meaning from historical standards). Mental capacity evidence may be considered as mitigation, but the Constitution does not require that a jury make a conclusive determination on that issue as a bar to punishment. The Florida procedure therefore satisfies both constitutional guarantees.
