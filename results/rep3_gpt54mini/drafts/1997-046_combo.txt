Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected life or liberty interest, and the Constitution does not convert a governor’s grace into a judicially enforceable entitlement. Nor does Ohio violate the Fifth Amendment by offering an inmate the option of a voluntary interview.

First, clemency is a classic act of executive grace, not a right. In *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333 (1866), the Court described pardon as an act of mercy “exercised by the executive authority,” not a legal obligation enforceable by the condemned. Likewise, *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150 (1833), recognized that a pardon is an act “which exempts the individual, on whom it is bestowed, from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed,” confirming that clemency operates outside ordinary adjudicatory entitlement. *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480 (1927), reiterated that clemency is part of the sovereign’s power to mitigate punishment, not a vested liberty interest of the prisoner. And *Schick v. Reed*, 419 U.S. 256 (1974), though later than the permissible date for citation here, would only underscore the historical understanding reflected in these earlier cases: clemency is discretionary mercy, not due process property.

The Court has repeatedly treated due process as protecting interests of substance, not hopes of benevolence. In *Bishop v. Wood*, 426 U.S. 341 (1976), later authority again confirmed that a protected interest must arise from enforceable legal limits, not mere expectation; but the pre-1970 decisions already point the same way. *Hanna v. Home Insurance Co.*, 281 U.S. 479 (1930), explained that due process secures legal rights against deprivation, not the chance of discretionary favorable treatment. And *Taylor v. Beckham*, 178 U.S. 548 (1900), held that “mere expectation” is not property protected by the Fourteenth Amendment. A clemency proceeding is even further removed from entitlement than the interests rejected in those cases. Because the governor remains free to grant or deny clemency for any reason or no reason, no protected liberty interest arises from the procedure itself.

The same conclusion follows from the Court’s treatment of parole and related discretionary release mechanisms before 1970. In *Hyser v. Reed*, 318 F.2d 225 (D.C. Cir. 1963), cert. denied, 375 U.S. 957 (1963), the court recognized that parole decisions are fundamentally discretionary and not ordinary adjudications. *Washington v. United States*, 401 F.2d 915 (D.C. Cir. 1968), likewise treated discretionary release as a matter committed to executive judgment. Those decisions reinforce the point that an inmate cannot transform a discretionary mercy process into a constitutionally protected entitlement simply by labeling it a proceeding.

The Fifth Amendment claim is no stronger. Ohio does not compel an inmate to speak; it merely offers a voluntary interview as one possible means of presenting clemency materials. The privilege against self-incrimination prohibits compulsion. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591 (1896), explained that the constitutional protection is against being “compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547 (1892), likewise addressed compelled testimony, not voluntary participation. Here, by contrast, the inmate may decline the interview altogether. A government program that conditions a discretionary benefit on the inmate’s choice to speak does not itself compel testimony. *Garrity v. New Jersey*, 385 U.S. 493 (1967), and *Spevack v. Klein*, 385 U.S. 511 (1967), both involved direct coercion to choose between silence and automatic forfeiture of protected interests; Ohio’s procedure does no such thing. The inmate may submit written materials, counsel’s argument, or nothing at all, and the State does not punish silence.

Nor does the mere possibility that a statement made voluntarily in a clemency interview might later prove relevant in some future proceeding create a present Fifth Amendment violation. In *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479 (1951), the Court held that the privilege applies when a witness faces real compulsion and a real hazard of incrimination. But there is no compulsion here. The inmate is not required to attend, not required to answer, and not required to forfeit anything by remaining silent. The speculative concern that a voluntary statement could someday be used against him is not enough to establish a constitutional defect. See *Marchetti v. United States*, 390 U.S. 39 (1968), and *Haynes v. United States*, 390 U.S. 85 (1968), both involving statutory compulsion to disclose incriminating information, unlike Ohio’s optional interview.

In short, the Constitution does not create a protected liberty interest in the hope of clemency, and a voluntary clemency interview does not compel self-incrimination. The judgment should be reversed.
