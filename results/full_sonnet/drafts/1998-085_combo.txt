ARGUMENT

I. Punitive damages have always required proof of egregious conduct, not merely a culpable state of mind.

Since the mid-nineteenth century, this Court has recognized that punitive damages are an extraordinary remedy reserved for extraordinary wrongs. In Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363 (1851), the Court explained that a jury awarding exemplary damages looks to "the enormity of [the defendant's] offence rather than the measure of compensation to the plaintiff." Id. at 371. That formulation makes clear that the trigger for punitive liability is the character of the wrongful conduct itself—its enormity—not simply the presence of some culpable mental state. A defendant may act with intent, or even with ill will, and yet the underlying conduct may fall short of the enormity the common law has always demanded before punishment, rather than compensation, is warranted.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in Philadelphia, Wilmington & Baltimore Railroad Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. (21 How.) 202 (1858), where exemplary damages were tied to conduct of an aggravated character, and again in Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes, 115 U.S. 512 (1885), which described punitive awards as penal in nature—designed to punish and deter conduct of a kind society condemns as intolerable, not merely to redress an actor's subjective disregard of another's rights. See id. at 521. Similarly, in Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), the Court treated exemplary damages as compensation-plus-punishment appropriate only where the wrong was attended by circumstances of aggravation, underscoring that the objective quality of the defendant's conduct—not simply proof of intent or recklessness—has always been the touchstone.

Nowhere is the distinction between mental state and egregiousness more sharply drawn than in Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101 (1893). There, this Court held that a corporation could not be subjected to punitive damages for the wrongful act of its conductor absent proof that the corporation itself had authorized, participated in, or ratified conduct of a sufficiently aggravated character; the mere fact that the agent may have acted with some improper motive did not suffice to visit punishment on the principal. See id. at 108–09. Prentice thus confirms two related propositions critical here: first, that punitive liability turns on the gravity of the conduct assessed as a whole, and second, that an employer's derivative exposure to punitive damages cannot rest on an agent's state of mind alone, divorced from the seriousness of what was actually done and the employer's own relationship to it.

Huntington v. Attrill, 146 U.S. 657 (1892), reinforces this understanding of the penal character of punitive damages. The Court there distinguished remedial awards, which compensate, from penal awards, which punish and deter—recognizing that the penal label attaches only where the conduct at issue is of the kind the community regards as warranting punishment, not wherever a jury might find some improper motive. See id. at 667–68.

II. Congress is presumed to have legislated against this common-law backdrop when it authorized punitive damages under Title VII.

When Congress enacted 42 U.S.C. § 1981a, it did not write on a blank slate. It employed terms—"malice" and "reckless indifference"—that carry a long common-law pedigree. This Court has repeatedly instructed that "where Congress borrows terms of art in which are accumulated the legal tradition and meaning of centuries of practice, it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word in the body of learning from which it was taken." Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). Because the common-law tradition surrounding punitive damages—reflected in Day, Quigley, Humes, Barry, Prentice, and Huntington—consistently demanded conduct of an aggravated, egregious character before punishment was permitted, this Court should presume Congress intended the same limitation when it used the vocabulary of malice and reckless indifference in § 1981a. A construction that permits punitive damages upon proof of intentional discrimination alone, without any independent showing that the conduct was sufficiently outrageous to warrant punishment, would sever the statutory terms from the very tradition that gives them content and would convert every garnvariety intentional-discrimination verdict into a potential punitive-damages case—a result the common law never tolerated and Congress gave no clear indication of intending.

III. Vicarious punitive liability against an employer requires still more.

Finally, even if an individual supervisor's state of mind could satisfy § 1981a's malice or reckless-indifference standard, imputing that state of mind to an employer for purposes of punishment demands independent justification. This Court's decision in New York Central & Hudson River Railroad Co. v. United States, 212 U.S. 481 (1909), recognized that corporate liability for the wrongful acts of agents depends on whether those acts were committed within the scope of the agent's authority and in furtherance of the corporation's business—not simply on the agent's personal culpability. See id. at 493–95. Read together with Prentice, that principle confirms that an employer's exposure to the penal sanction of punitive damages cannot rest on an isolated employee's state of mind alone; it must be tethered to conduct, and to the employer's own connection to that conduct, sufficiently egregious to justify visiting punishment on the organization itself.

For these reasons, an award of punitive damages under Title VII requires proof that the employer's conduct was egregious, independent of the actor's state of mind.
