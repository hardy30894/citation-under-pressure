I. The Indictment Failed to Allege an Official Act Within the Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C).

Mrs. Parker’s duties as a clerk consisted solely of assisting ALJ Aronson before and during hearings, recording notes, and typing and mailing decisions. She possessed no authority to approve benefits or to sign the ALJ’s name. An indictment under section 201(b)(2)(C) must charge that the accused was induced to perform an act “in violation of [her] official duty.” The statute reaches only those decisions or actions that fall within the settled scope of an official’s authority.

The Supreme Court has long held that duties “not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery.” United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231 (1914). Where an employee’s settled practice excludes the power to grant or deny benefits, conduct outside that practice cannot constitute an official act. The same limitation appears in the Court’s earlier construction of bribery statutes: the forbidden act must be one the officer is “authorized to do.” Burton v. United States, 196 U.S. 283, 303 (1905).

Because the indictment alleged only that Mrs. Parker forged approvals she had no power to issue, it failed to charge any act within her official duty. The convictions therefore cannot stand.
