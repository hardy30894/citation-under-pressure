# ARGUMENT

## I. The Tenth Amendment Protects the States' Reserved Powers, Not Individual Rights, and a Private Criminal Defendant Therefore Lacks Standing to Invoke It.

The Tenth Amendment provides that "[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. This Court has long recognized that the Amendment operates to protect the States as sovereign entities within our federal structure, not to confer a personal constitutional right enforceable by any individual who happens to be prosecuted under a federal statute.

This principle is not novel. In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies challenged a federal agency's operations as an intrusion on matters reserved to the States. This Court rejected the claim, holding that the private companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. That holding was not incidental to the Court's analysis—it was an independent and sufficient basis for disposing of the claim, and the Court had "no occasion" to address the merits once it determined the private litigants could not raise the issue at all. *See id.*

The rationale for this rule is straightforward: the Tenth Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It is the States, as the retaining sovereigns, who possess the interest the Amendment protects. A private citizen has no cognizable stake in the abstract allocation of power between the state and federal governments distinct from any other citizen's interest in that same allocation—precisely the kind of "generalized grievance" that this Court has repeatedly held insufficient to support standing. *See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 573–74 (1992) (a plaintiff raising only a generally available grievance about government "does not state an Article III case or controversy").

## II. The Weight of Circuit Authority Confirms That Private Parties Lack Standing to Raise Tenth Amendment Claims.

Consistent with *Tennessee Electric Power*, the majority of the courts of appeals to consider the question have held that private litigants—including criminal defendants—lack standing to challenge federal statutes on Tenth Amendment grounds. The First Circuit's decision in *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25 (1st Cir. 2005), is illustrative. There the court explained that "the Tenth Amendment protects the states, not private parties, and only the states, their officers, or agents may raise a Tenth Amendment claim." *Id.* at 35. The court further explained that this rule is not merely academic, because "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." *Id.*

The Eighth, Ninth, Second, and Tenth Circuits are in accord. *See United States v. Hacker*, 565 F.3d 522, 525–27 (8th Cir. 2009); *Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009); *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006); *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004). This near-uniform body of authority reflects the settled understanding, rooted in *Tennessee Electric Power*, that federalism-based limitations on congressional power exist for the benefit of the States as sovereigns and may be waived or asserted by them alone.

## III. Permitting Individual Defendants to Raise Tenth Amendment Claims Would Displace the States' Own Judgment About How to Vindicate Their Sovereign Interests.

Allowing a criminal defendant to invoke the Tenth Amendment as a defense to a federal prosecution would improperly substitute the defendant's litigation strategy for the State's own considered judgment regarding the proper allocation of authority between it and the federal government. A State may conclude, for its own reasons, that federal prosecution of conduct also punishable under state law is unobjectionable, or even welcome, notwithstanding the existence of a parallel or overlapping state offense. Indeed, no State has objected to Bond's prosecution under 18 U.S.C. § 229, nor has the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania asserted any sovereign interest adverse to the statute's application here. Where, as here, "there is no objection to the [federal government's] operations by the states," a private party has no standing to manufacture a controversy the sovereign itself has not raised. *Tenn. Elec. Power Co.*, 306 U.S. at 144.

This limitation is not a mere formality; it reflects the sound principle, reiterated in *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment*, 523 U.S. 83 (1998), that federal courts must ensure that a genuine case or controversy—brought by a party with a concrete stake in the outcome—exists before reaching the merits of a constitutional claim. *Id.* at 101–02. Bond's asserted interest is nothing more than a generalized preference that she be prosecuted by state rather than federal authorities—an interest indistinguishable from that of any other person subject to a valid federal criminal statute, and one that does not implicate any personal right the Tenth Amendment was designed to secure.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Tenth Amendment protects the reserved sovereignty of the States, and because Bond, a private criminal defendant, asserts no interest distinct from the State's own sovereign prerogative, she lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
