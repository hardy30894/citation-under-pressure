The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. 

The Sixth Amendment claim is based on the Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), which held that a jury must find the aggravating factors necessary for the imposition of the death penalty. However, Florida's sentencing scheme is distinct from Arizona's in that it requires a jury to make a recommendation regarding the sentence, whereas Arizona's scheme allowed a judge to make the necessary findings alone. 

Although Ring overruled Walton v. Arizona, 497 U.S. 639 (1990), in relevant part, it did not directly address the constitutionality of Florida's death penalty scheme. The Supreme Court has not held that a jury's recommendation must be unanimous to satisfy the Sixth Amendment. In fact, the Court has previously upheld non-unanimous jury verdicts in certain contexts.

Moreover, Florida's scheme allows the trial judge to make the ultimate sentencing decision, weighing the aggravating and mitigating circumstances. This is consistent with the Supreme Court's precedent, as the Court has held that a judge may make the final determination regarding the imposition of the death penalty, as long as the jury has made the necessary factual findings. See, e.g., Schriro v. Smith, 546 U.S. 6 (2005) (per curiam) (explaining that a state may require a judge to make a determination regarding a defendant's mental retardation after a jury has made the necessary factual findings).

The Eighth Amendment claim is also unavailing. While the Supreme Court has held that the execution of mentally retarded defendants is unconstitutional, see Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), it has left it to the states to determine the manner in which this restriction is enforced. Florida law sets forth a clear three-pronged test for determining mental retardation, and the trial court properly applied this test in Hurst's case.

Furthermore, Florida's outlier status among states that impose the death penalty is not dispositive. The Supreme Court has not held that a state's death penalty scheme must conform to the majority of other states to be constitutional. While Justice Pariente's concurrence in the lower court opinion noted the potential Eighth Amendment implications of Florida's outlier status, this concern is not sufficient to render the scheme unconstitutional.

In conclusion, Florida's death sentencing scheme, which requires a jury recommendation but not unanimity, and allows a judge to make the ultimate sentencing decision, is constitutional under both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona does not require a different result, and the state's scheme is consistent with the Court's precedent regarding the imposition of the death penalty.
