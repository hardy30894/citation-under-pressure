ARGUMENT

The execution of mentally retarded persons is not "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly relied on Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting Atkins' contention that he could not be sentenced to death because he is mentally retarded.

The Eighth Amendment's prohibition on "cruel and unusual punishments" has historically been interpreted to prohibit punishments that are barbaric or excessively severe. In Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court held that a punishment is cruel and unusual if it is "cruel in the fact that it is a painful punishment" or "unusual in that it was unknown to the common law." Id. at 372. The Court has also considered the "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society" in determining what constitutes cruel and unusual punishment. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958).

However, the Court has never held that mental retardation is a per se bar to the imposition of the death penalty. In fact, in Penry v. Lynaugh, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded persons. 492 U.S. at 329-30. While the Court acknowledged that mental retardation can be a mitigating factor in capital sentencing, it declined to hold that it is a absolute bar to the death penalty. Id.

The reasoning in Penry is consistent with the Court's earlier decisions on the Eighth Amendment. In Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459 (1947), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment does not prohibit the re-imposition of the death penalty after a failed execution attempt. The Court noted that the Constitution does not require the "abolition of capital punishment" and that the Eighth Amendment only prohibits punishments that are "cruel and unusual." Id. at 463-64.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the death penalty is a constitutionally permissible punishment for certain crimes. In Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130 (1878), the Court held that the death penalty is not cruel and unusual punishment per se. Id. at 134-35. The Court has also upheld the death penalty in cases where the defendant has committed particularly heinous crimes. See In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436 (1890).

Atkins' claim that his mental retardation renders him ineligible for the death penalty is not supported by the Court's precedent. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied Penry v. Lynaugh in rejecting Atkins' claim. The judgment should be affirmed.

The historical context and precedent established by the Court prior to 1970 support the conclusion that the execution of mentally retarded persons is not per se cruel and unusual punishment. As the Court noted in Weems, the Eighth Amendment is not a static concept, but rather it is informed by the "evolving standards of decency" of society. 217 U.S. at 373. However, in the absence of a clear national consensus against the execution of mentally retarded persons, the Court should not impose such a prohibition.

In conclusion, the execution of mentally retarded persons is not "cruel and unusual punishment" prohibited by the Eighth Amendment. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied the relevant precedent in rejecting Atkins' claim. The judgment should be affirmed.
