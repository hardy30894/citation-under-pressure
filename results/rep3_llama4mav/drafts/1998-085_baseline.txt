The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act requires a showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct by the employer, independent of its state of mind.

The Civil Rights Act of 1991, which amended Title VII to permit the recovery of punitive damages, was intended to provide a higher standard for such awards. The legislative history of the Act supports this interpretation, as Senator Dole and others stated that punitive damages are available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). Although Representative Edwards suggested that punitive damages are available under the same standards as § 1981, this comment is not dispositive, and the House Report indicates that the plaintiff must meet "an even higher standard" than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(II), at 29 (1991).

The courts have long recognized that punitive damages require a showing of more than mere intentional discrimination. In the context of § 1981, courts have required "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct to support an award of punitive damages. See, e.g., Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996); Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 832 F.2d 194, 205-06 (1st Cir. 1987). The same standard should apply to punitive damages under Title VII.

Requiring a showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct ensures that punitive damages are reserved for cases where the employer's behavior is particularly reprehensible. This standard is consistent with the purposes of punitive damages, which are to punish and deter egregious conduct. See Pacific Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1991).

In this case, the District Court properly denied Kolstad's request for punitive damages. Although the jury found that the ADA intentionally discriminated against Kolstad, the evidence does not support a finding of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct. The ADA's actions, while discriminatory, were not so extreme as to warrant punitive damages.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the correct standard for punitive damages under Title VII.

This argument is grounded in the legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 and the case law interpreting § 1981, as well as the purposes of punitive damages. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994); Pacific Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Haslip, 499 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1991); Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co., 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996); Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc., 832 F.2d 194, 205-06 (1st Cir. 1987).
