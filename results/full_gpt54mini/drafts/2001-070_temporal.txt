The Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders because the Amendment draws its meaning from “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), and those standards are reflected in “objective indicia” of societal judgment, Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 378 (1910). But no pre-1970 authority established a constitutional rule barring capital punishment for persons with mental retardation. To the contrary, this Court has long recognized that legislatures, not courts, define the class of persons subject to the death penalty, subject only to the outer limits of the Eighth Amendment. In In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), the Court explained that the Amendment forbids punishments that are “cruel in the sense of that word as used in the constitution,” not all punishments that are harsh, and in Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 135-36 (1879), the Court upheld a death sentence while identifying only methods of execution that transgress “the dignity of man.”

Under those principles, Atkins’s claim fails because the historical record does not show a settled constitutional judgment that mentally retarded offenders may not be executed. The common-law tradition permitted individualized consideration of a defendant’s “idiotcy” or “lunacy,” but it did not announce a categorical exemption from capital punishment. See M’Naghten’s Case, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (H.L. 1843) (insanity as a defense); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 485 (1895) (insanity inquiry turns on “mental disease or defect”). That longstanding distinction matters: the law has treated insanity as a bar to criminal responsibility, not intellectual disability as a blanket immunity from punishment. See id.; Youtsey v. United States, 97 F. 937, 940 (6th Cir. 1899).

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require the Court to constitutionalize a rule that the States have not adopted. In Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514, 531 (1968), the Court rejected an attempt to derive a broad constitutional limitation on punishment from “fundamental principles,” emphasizing the absence of a clear, objective consensus. The same restraint controls here. The Constitution does not permit the judiciary to replace the States’ legislative judgments with a newly announced categorical exemption absent a demonstrable national consensus rooted in historical practice. See id.; Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962) (Eighth Amendment condemnation limited to punishment of status as “a narcotic addict,” not every condition affecting culpability).

Atkins’s reliance on proportionality principles is also unavailing. Weems recognized that punishment may be cruel when it is “greatly disproportionate to the offense charged,” 217 U.S. at 367, but the Court has never held that intellectual disability alone renders a capital sentence disproportionate per se. Indeed, in Powell, 392 U.S. at 533, the plurality refused to extend Robinson into a constitutional rule excusing an offender based on a condition affecting conduct and culpability, warning against converting the Eighth Amendment into “an instrument for the judicial imposition of a social policy.” That caution is especially apt in capital cases, where the Court has consistently deferred to the States’ administration of punishment within constitutional bounds. See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463-64 (1947).

The proper constitutional inquiry is whether the sentence imposed is within the historical and societal understanding of “cruel and unusual,” not whether the Court believes a different policy would be preferable. See Trop, 356 U.S. at 100-01; Weems, 217 U.S. at 378. Before 1970, no decision of this Court had held that the execution of mentally retarded persons violates the Eighth Amendment. Because the Amendment’s text, history, and pre-1970 precedent do not support a categorical bar, the judgment below should be affirmed.
