**ARGUMENT**

**I.  Montana has specific personal jurisdiction over Ford because its contacts with the state relate to the claims at issue.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the defendant’s contacts with the forum state are not random, fortuitous, or attenuated, but instead arise out of or relate to the defendant’s activities within the state. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945). Here, Ford purposefully availed itself of the Montana market by advertising and selling parts within the state. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985). These activities constitute a “stream of commerce” that foreseeably led to the vehicle’s presence in Montana and the subsequent accident. *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980). Therefore, the claims “arise out of” Ford’s Montana activities.

Ford’s decision to advertise and sell parts in Montana demonstrates a purposeful direction of its activities toward the forum. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 251 (1958). Ford did not merely sell a finished vehicle to a dealer in Washington; it established a commercial presence in Montana by engaging in the sale of its products to Montana residents. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475. This continuous course of dealing with Montana customers creates a sufficient connection between Ford and the forum to support jurisdiction.

Furthermore, the claims in this case arise out of Ford’s activities in Montana. The accident occurred in Montana, and the vehicle was purchased in Montana. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297. Ford’s contacts with Montana are not random or fortuitous; they are the very activities that made the vehicle’s presence in Montana possible. *Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 919 (2011). Ford’s Montana sales foreseeably led to the vehicle being driven on Montana highways, where the alleged defect manifested. Consequently, the claims are directly related to Ford’s purposeful availment of the Montana market.

**II.  The Due Process Clause is satisfied because the burden on Ford is not disproportionate to the benefits Montana receives.**

The Due Process Clause requires that the defendant’s burden in defending the suit in the forum state not be so great as to outweigh the plaintiff’s interest in obtaining relief. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. Here, Ford is a global corporation with substantial resources, and the burden of litigating in Montana is minimal compared to the benefits Montana receives from adjudicating claims involving its residents. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475.

Montana has a strong interest in adjudicating disputes involving its residents and the safety of its highways. By allowing Ford to be sued in Montana, the state ensures that its citizens have access to a forum where the accident occurred and where the evidence is located. *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297. The burden on Ford is not disproportionate to this interest, as Ford has already established a commercial presence in the state through its sales of parts.

**III.  Montana does not need to exercise general jurisdiction over Ford.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state to exercise general jurisdiction over a foreign corporation only if the corporation’s “affairs are so completely rooted in the State’s affairs that it is essentially at home” there. *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 139 (2014). Ford is incorporated in Delaware and maintains its headquarters in Michigan. It does not have a substantial presence in Montana. Therefore, general jurisdiction does not apply, and specific jurisdiction is the appropriate standard.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, Montana has specific personal jurisdiction over Ford. The claims arise out of Ford’s purposeful availment of the Montana market through the sale of parts, and the Due Process Clause is satisfied. The Montana Supreme Court correctly affirmed the district court’s decision.
