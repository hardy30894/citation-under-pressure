**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Patent Act Limits Damages for Design Patent Infringement to the Infringing Component**

The Patent Act provides that a design patent infringement damages award “shall be based upon the [infringer’s] total profit” only when the patented design “is applied to the article of manufacture.” 35 U.S.C. § 289 (emphasis added). Where, as here, the patented design applies only to a component of a multicomponent product, damages must be limited to the infringer’s profit attributable to that component. *Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elecs. Co.*, 789 F.3d 1335, 1349 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (en banc) (Moore, J., concurring) (“[W]here a design patent is applied to only a component of a multi-component product, damages are to be limited to that component’s contribution to the product.”); *id.* at 1350 (Prost, C.J., dissenting) (“[T]he statute requires apportionment where the patented design is applied to only a component of a multicomponent product.”).

This Court has long recognized that § 289’s “total profit” rule is inapplicable where the patented design is not applied to the entire article of manufacture. In *Savage v. Robinson*, 121 F. 259 (C.C.D. Cal. 1903), the court held that damages for infringement of a design patent on a chair back were limited to the value of the back alone, not the entire chair. Similarly, in *Smith v. Whitman Saddle Co.*, 148 U.S. 674 (1893), the Supreme Court affirmed a damages award based on the profit attributable to the patented saddle design, not the entire saddle. These cases establish that § 289’s “total profit” rule is not a windfall for the patentee but requires a causal nexus between the infringement and the damages awarded. *See also* *Parker v. Hulbert*, 12 F. Cas. 1086, 1087 (C.C.D. Cal. 1875) (No. 10,750) (“The damages should be limited to the profit arising from the use of the patented design.”).

The Federal Circuit’s en banc decision in *Apple v. Samsung* reaffirmed this principle. The court explained that § 289’s “total profit” language “does not mean that the patentee is entitled to the infringer’s entire profits from the accused product.” 789 F.3d at 1343. Rather, “[w]here the patented design is applied to only a component of a multicomponent product, damages are to be limited to that component’s contribution to the product.” *Id.* at 1349 (Moore, J., concurring). The court further noted that “[t]he statute does not define ‘article of manufacture’” and that “[a]rticle of manufacture has a commonly understood meaning in the relevant community: a thing made by hand or machine.” *Id.* at 1344 (majority opinion). Thus, the “article of manufacture” to which the design is applied may be a component of a larger product.

**II. The District Court Erred in Allowing the Jury to Award Damages Based on Samsung’s Entire Profits**

The district court erred by permitting the jury to award damages based on Samsung’s entire profits from its smartphones, rather than the profits attributable to the infringing design components. The jury was not instructed to apportion damages to the infringing components, and the record contained no evidence of the profit attributable to those components. *See* J.A. 40869-79 (verdict form awarding $98,690,625 in damages). This was legal error.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that damages must be apportioned where the patented invention is only a component of the accused product. In *Garcia v. Google, Inc.*, 786 F.3d 1216 (Fed. Cir. 2015), the court reversed a damages award based on the entire profits of a multicomponent product where the patented feature was only one component. The court held that “[d]amages must be apportioned to the infringing component unless the infringing component creates the basis for customer demand for the accused product.” *Id.* at 1221. Similarly, in *VirnetX, Inc. v. Cisco Sys., Inc.*, 767 F.3d 1308 (Fed. Cir. 2014), the court reversed a damages award that failed to apportion profits to the patented feature, explaining that “[w]ithout such apportionment, the jury’s damages award lacks a legally sufficient evidentiary basis.” *Id.* at 1326.

Here, the infringed design patents applied only to specific components of Samsung’s smartphones—such as the front face or graphical user interface—not the entire product. *See* U.S. Patent Nos. D618,677, D593,087, D604,304. The district court’s failure to instruct the jury to apportion damages to these components was reversible error. *See* *Uniloc USA, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 632 F.3d 1292, 1318 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (“Damages awards must be apportioned to the patented technology’s footprint in the accused product.”).

**III. The Federal Circuit’s En Banc Decision in *Apple v. Samsung* Does Not Foreclose Apportionment**

The Federal Circuit’s en banc decision in *Apple v. Samsung* did not address the apportionment issue presented here. The court’s holding was limited to affirming the district court’s judgment on infringement and validity, not the damages calculation. 789 F.3d at 1342. The concurring and dissenting opinions, however, expressly recognized that apportionment is required where the patented design applies only to a component of a multicomponent product. *Id.* at 1349 (Moore, J., concurring); *id.* at 1350 (Prost, C.J., dissenting). Thus, the decision does not foreclose Samsung’s argument that damages must be apportioned to the infringing components.

**Conclusion**

The district court erred by allowing the jury to award damages based on Samsung’s entire profits from its smartphones, rather than the profits attributable to the infringing design components. The Patent Act and this Court’s precedent require apportionment where, as here, the patented design applies only to a component of a multicomponent product. The judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial on damages, limited to the profits attributable to the infringing components.
