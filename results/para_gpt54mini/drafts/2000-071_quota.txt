**Argument**

I. **Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club was viewpoint discrimination in a limited public forum.**

Once a public school opens its facilities for after-hours community use, it may reserve access only by subject matter and viewpoint-neutral restrictions. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s policy allowed district residents to use school property for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community,” but barred use “for religious purposes.” That exclusion targeted the Club because of the religious viewpoint it would bring to otherwise permissible moral and character instruction.

This Court has repeatedly held that the government may not exclude speech from a limited forum because it is religious in perspective. In *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of University of Virginia*, 515 U.S. 819, 831 (1995), the Court held that “the guarantee of neutrality” forbids “denying access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses.” And the Court explained that religious speech is not excluded merely because it is religious; rather, “religious viewpoints [are] on a wide variety of seemingly secular subject matter.” *Id.* at 831-32. Milford’s distinction between “moral and character development” and “religious instruction” fails for the same reason. The Club sought to speak about morals and character from a Christian perspective. Excluding that perspective is textbook viewpoint discrimination. See *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 393-94 (1993).

*Lamb’s Chapel* is directly controlling. There, a school district permitted community groups to use its facilities for social and civic purposes but denied access for a film series on child-rearing from a Christian perspective. The Court held the exclusion unconstitutional because the district “den[ied] access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses on an otherwise includible subject.” *Id.* at 394. Milford did the same here. It opened its forum to youth-oriented activities promoting the welfare of the community, yet excluded the Club because its teachings were grounded in Christianity. That is not permissible content discrimination; it is impermissible viewpoint discrimination. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829-31; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94.

The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the Club’s activities were “quintessentially religious” does not save the exclusion. The relevant question is not whether the speech is religious, but whether Milford permitted comparable moral instruction from nonreligious viewpoints while excluding the Club’s religious perspective. *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831-32; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94. Milford’s policy and its application show precisely that impermissible line drawing.

II. **Milford’s Establishment Clause defense fails because allowing the Club equal access would not create a constitutional endorsement of religion.**

Milford argues that denying the Club access was necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. That argument is foreclosed by *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), and *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. 384. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university did not violate the Establishment Clause by making facilities equally available to a religious student group. The Court reasoned that equal access “would have a secular purpose” and would not be perceived as endorsement because the policy was neutral and available to all groups. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75. Likewise, in *Mergens*, the Court rejected an Establishment Clause challenge to the Equal Access Act, explaining that “the mere fact that a secondary school allows a religious club to meet on school premises does not mean that the school endorses the religious speech.” *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 250. The same principle governs here.

The Court reaffirmed in *Lamb’s Chapel* that fears of an Establishment Clause violation cannot justify viewpoint discrimination where the school has created a limited public forum. 508 U.S. at 394-95. And in *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university’s payment of student-activity funds to a Christian publication did not violate the Establishment Clause because neutrality toward religious viewpoints is itself constitutionally required. 515 U.S. at 837-46. Milford’s position is even weaker: it would merely allow private religious speech after hours in a forum open to other community groups. Under *Widmar*, *Mergens*, *Lamb’s Chapel*, and *Rosenberger*, that does not amount to government endorsement.

Nor does *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577 (1992), or *Santa Fe Independent School District v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290 (2000), help Milford. Those cases involved school-sponsored religious exercises at official school ceremonies, where coercion and state imprimatur were present. *Lee*, 505 U.S. at 592-93; *Santa Fe*, 530 U.S. at 302-13. Here, by contrast, the Club sought after-hours use under a neutral community-access policy. The distinction is critical. The Establishment Clause does not require suppression of private religious speech to avoid the mistaken impression that the government endorses it. *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 250; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 845-46.

III. **The judgment below should be reversed.**

The First Amendment prohibits Milford from allowing community groups to use its school for moral and character activities while excluding the Good News Club because its morals are Christian. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46; *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831-32. And Milford’s Establishment Clause defense cannot override that free-speech violation. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274-75; *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 250; *Lee*, 505 U.S. at 592-93; *Santa Fe*, 530 U.S. at 302-13. The Constitution required equal access, not exclusion.
