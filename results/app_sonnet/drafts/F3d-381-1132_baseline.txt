ARGUMENT

I. THE SCOPE OF THE EASEMENT IS CENTRAL, NOT IRRELEVANT, TO WHETHER THE GOVERNMENT'S CONTINUING PRESENCE ON TUTHILL'S LAND IS A PHYSICAL TAKING

The Court of Federal Claims erred as a matter of law when it declared that "[t]he scope of the easement is not relevant in this court because it cannot give rise to a physical taking." Tuthill, slip op. at 6. This Court has already rejected that proposition. In Preseault v. United States, 100 F.3d 1525 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc), the en banc Court held that whether the government's continued occupation of land burdened by an easement effects a taking depends on "the terms of the easement" and "whether the use ... [is] permitted by the terms of the ... easement." Id. at 1533. If a governmental actor's use of land subject to an easement exceeds the scope of the interest originally conveyed, the servient owner retains a property interest in the excess use, and the government's continued physical presence for that unauthorized purpose is itself compensable. Id. at 1543-45. The Supreme Court's decision affirming the underlying rail-to-trail dispute, Preseault v. Interstate Commerce Commission, 494 U.S. 1 (1990), likewise recognized that conversion of an easement to a use beyond its original scope raises a live takings question to be resolved under state property law defining the easement's bounds. Id. at 8, 16-17. The Court of Federal Claims's categorical refusal even to examine the 1953 grant's language was therefore legal error requiring reversal.

II. BPA'S CONSTRUCTION AND LEASING OF EXCESS FIBER-OPTIC CAPACITY EXCEEDS THE SCOPE OF THE 1953 EASEMENT

The 1953 conveyance gave BPA "a perpetual easement and right to enter and erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol one or more electrical power transmission lines and appurtenant signal lines ... necessary in connection therewith." The italicized words of limitation matter: the signal lines must be "necessary" to the power-transmission function for which the easement was granted. BPA's own submissions concede that it "needs only a fraction of the thirty-six fibers crossing Tuthill's property to maintain the power lines," and that the remainder is leased "to a telecommunications company" for commercial purposes wholly unrelated to power transmission. That arrangement is not the maintenance of "appurtenant signal lines ... necessary" to operate power lines; it is the creation of an independent commercial telecommunications facility on Tuthill's land, sustained by cable that Tuthill never agreed to host.

This Court's decision in Preseault confirms that such an expansion is not merely a contract or trespass question but a takings question. There, the railroad's easement for "railroad purposes" did not authorize converting the corridor to a public recreational trail; the new use fell outside the scope of the original grant and thus took the landowners' property. 100 F.3d at 1552-53. The same logic governs here. BPA's easement was for electrical transmission and appurtenant signal lines "necessary" thereto — not for hosting a for-profit telecommunications network serving unrelated third parties through 2025. By building and maintaining cable far beyond present or even reasonably proximate operational need, and then leasing the excess to commercial carriers, the government has occupied Tuthill's land for a purpose the 1953 grant never authorized.

III. THAT UNAUTHORIZED OCCUPATION IS A PER SE PHYSICAL TAKING UNDER LORETTO

Once it is established that BPA's fiber-optic installation exceeds the scope of its easement, the remaining analysis is straightforward. In Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp., 458 U.S. 419 (1982), the Supreme Court held that "a permanent physical occupation authorized by government is a taking without regard to the public interests that it may serve." Id. at 426. The Court explained that "when the character of the governmental action is a permanent physical occupation of property, we have uniformly found a taking to the extent of the occupation, without regard to whether the action achieves an important public benefit or has only minimal economic impact on the owner." Id. at 434-35. Loretto involved a state-mandated cable installation no larger than the fiber-optic bundle here, yet the Court found a per se taking because it deprived the owner of "the power to exclude" — "one of the most essential sticks in the bundle of rights that are commonly characterized as property." Id. at 435-36 (quoting Kaiser Aetna v. United States, 444 U.S. 164, 176 (1979)).

Tuthill's easement conveyed the right to exclude BPA and its licensees from using the property for anything beyond power transmission and its necessary signal lines. The excess fiber capacity, installed and leased for independent commercial telecommunications use, occupies Tuthill's land outside that conveyance. That occupation is permanent, physical, and unauthorized by the terms Tuthill's predecessor accepted in 1953. Under Loretto, the public benefit of BPA's telecommunications leasing program — however legitimate — cannot excuse the government from paying just compensation for occupying land beyond the scope of its easement. See 458 U.S. at 426.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court of Federal Claims's grant of summary judgment to the government should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of just compensation for the government's physical taking of Tuthill's property.
