**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer Zone Law Is a Valid Content-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation That Satisfies the First Amendment.**

The First Circuit correctly held that Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2 is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation that leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). The statute does not regulate speech based on its content or viewpoint; it applies equally to all persons who “knowingly enter or remain on a public way or sidewalk adjacent to a reproductive health care facility” within 35 feet of an entrance, exit, or driveway. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute as content-neutral because it “places no restrictions on—and expressly does not prohibit—any particular message”). The Massachusetts law’s exemptions for persons entering or leaving the facility, employees, emergency personnel, and those passing through further confirm its neutrality. *See id.* at 725.

The statute is narrowly tailored to serve the Commonwealth’s significant interests in protecting public safety, ensuring access to healthcare facilities, and preserving the orderly movement of pedestrians. *See id.* at 715 (recognizing that “the State’s interests in protecting the health and safety of its citizens” and “ensuring the free flow of traffic” are “unquestionably legitimate”). The 35-foot buffer zone is far less restrictive than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill*, and it does not ban speech entirely; it merely moves speakers a modest distance from clinic entrances. *See id.* at 726-27. The First Circuit correctly found that the law “leaves open ample alternative channels of communication,” including oral speech, amplified sound, signs, literature distribution, and prayer. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 179 (1st Cir. 2009). Petitioners have demonstrated that they continue to engage in sidewalk counseling, display signs, and distribute literature—and have even persuaded women to forgo abortions. *See McCullen v. Coakley*, 844 F. Supp. 2d 206, 213-15 (D. Mass. 2012). The Constitution requires only that “ample” channels remain, not that speakers achieve their preferred level of effectiveness. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802.

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls This Case and Should Not Be Limited or Overruled.**

This Court’s decision in *Hill v. Colorado* squarely governs the constitutionality of the Massachusetts statute. In *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado law that created a 100-foot buffer zone around healthcare facility entrances, prohibiting any person from “knowingly approach[ing]” within eight feet of another person for purposes of “oral protest, education, or counseling” without consent. 530 U.S. at 707. The Court held that the statute was content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and left open ample alternative channels. *Id.* at 725-30. The Massachusetts law is even less restrictive: it imposes a 35-foot fixed buffer zone, does not regulate the distance from which speakers may approach individuals, and does not prohibit any specific form of speech. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 176-77.

Petitioners’ request to limit or overrule *Hill* is unwarranted. *Hill* was a 6-3 decision that has been repeatedly cited and applied by lower courts. *See, e.g., United States v. Dinwiddie*, 76 F.3d 913, 923 (8th Cir. 1996) (upholding a similar injunction). The decision rests on well-established principles of time-place-manner regulation and does not conflict with this Court’s later decisions in *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), or *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010). Those cases involved content-based restrictions on commercial speech and corporate political speech, respectively—not content-neutral regulations of public sidewalk conduct. *See Sorrell*, 564 U.S. at 571 (striking down a law that “disfavor[ed]” specific speakers and messages); *Citizens United*, 558 U.S. at 340 (invalidating a ban on corporate independent expenditures). *Hill* remains good law and is directly on point.

**III. The First Circuit Correctly Applied the Law of the Case Doctrine.**

The First Circuit properly declined to revisit its prior holding that the Act is facially constitutional. The law of the case doctrine bars relitigation of issues “explicitly or implicitly decided by an earlier appellate decision in the same case.” *United States v. Matthews*, 643 F.3d 9, 13 (1st Cir. 2011). Petitioners’ reliance on *Sorrell*, *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), and *Citizens United* does not demonstrate a “dramatic change” in controlling legal authority. *Matthews*, 643 F.3d at 14. Those cases address distinct First Amendment contexts and do not undermine *Hill* or the facial validity of the Massachusetts law. The district court and the First Circuit correctly applied the doctrine to avoid piecemeal litigation.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
