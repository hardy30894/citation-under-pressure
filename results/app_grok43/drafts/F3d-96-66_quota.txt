The active operations duty requires a vessel to exercise reasonable care over areas under its control during stevedoring operations. Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156, 167 (1981). This duty encompasses hazards created by the vessel’s own equipment and crew actions. Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co., 512 U.S. 92, 98 (1994). The M/V Atlantic Universal’s snatch blocks remained under the exclusive control of Bora’s crew, who alone bore responsibility for repositioning them after opening hatch covers. Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026, 1029 (3d Cir. 1992).

A vessel breaches this duty when it leaves equipment in a dangerous position that interferes with cargo operations and fails to correct the condition. Davis v. Portline Transportes Maritime Internacional, 16 F.3d 532, 540 (3d Cir. 1994). Here, the block rested horizontally atop cargo in the wing, blocking both removal of the fruit and the hookup man’s escape route. The crew’s failure to return the block to vertical position after loading in Chile created precisely the hazard Scindia requires vessels to eliminate before turning cargo spaces over to longshoremen. Scindia, 451 U.S. at 167.

The district court erred in holding that Serbin assumed the risk by attempting the move himself. Assumption of risk is not a defense under section 905(b). Federal Marine Terminals, Inc. v. Burnside Shipping Co., 394 U.S. 404, 415 (1969). Longshoremen may take reasonable steps to mitigate obvious hazards when the vessel has neglected its duty. Bjaranson v. Botelho Shipping Corp., 873 F.2d 1204, 1208 (9th Cir. 1989). Serbin’s decision to free the cargo without awaiting the crew was consistent with industry practice of minimizing delay in competitive fruit operations. Hill v. Texaco, Inc., 674 F.2d 447, 451 (5th Cir. 1982).

Genuine issues of material fact exist concerning whether the block constituted an open and obvious danger that the crew should have anticipated longshoremen would encounter. Helaire v. Mobil Oil Co., 709 F.2d 1031, 1036 (5th Cir. 1983). The fixed metal stoppers on these blocks distinguished them from portable equipment longshoremen customarily handled, creating a latent risk. Theriot v. Bay Drilling Corp., 783 F.2d 527, 535 (5th Cir. 1986). Summary judgment was therefore inappropriate. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).

The record permits a finding that Bora’s crew retained active control over the hatch-cover system throughout unloading. This ongoing involvement triggered the duty to intervene when the misplaced block became known. Scindia, 451 U.S. at 175. Reversal and remand for trial are required.
