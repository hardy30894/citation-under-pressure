ARGUMENT

This Court should reverse. A case is not moot merely because the government changes the challenged policy while litigation is pending if the plaintiff still seeks redress for a completed constitutional violation. That principle is especially important in First Amendment cases, where a plaintiff who was stopped from speaking may still obtain judicial relief for the past wrong. See, e.g., New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254, 267-92 (1964) (recognizing damages for constitutional injury in civil rights litigation); Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts, 388 U.S. 130, 154-55 (1967) (plurality opinion) (awarding damages for constitutionally defective defamation judgment).

The Eleventh Circuit erred by treating the revision of Georgia Gwinnett College’s policy as automatically eliminating a live controversy. This Court has repeatedly held that voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless it is absolutely clear the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur. See Board of Ed. of Central School Dist. No. 1 v. Allen, 392 U.S. 236, 241 n.3 (1968); Monroe v. Board of Commissioners of Jackson, 391 U.S. 450, 459 (1968). A defendant cannot insulate itself from review simply by withdrawing an unlawful policy after suit is filed. That rule applies with particular force where, as here, the challenged restraint has already been enforced against the plaintiffs. See Tinker v. Des Moines Independent Community School District, 393 U.S. 503, 506-09 (1969).

Nor is the case moot because the plaintiffs seek nominal damages. Nominal damages are a traditional judicial remedy when a legal right has been violated but actual damages are difficult to prove. See New York Times, 376 U.S. at 267-92; Curtis Publishing, 388 U.S. at 154-55. This Court has long recognized that a plaintiff suffering an infringement of constitutional rights may sue for redress even when the immediate practical restraint has ended. See Jones v. Opelika, 316 U.S. 584, 600-02 (1942); Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 108-10 (1943). In that setting, a request for nominal damages confirms that the plaintiff seeks a judgment on the merits for a completed injury, not advisory relief. See New York Times, 376 U.S. at 297 (Black, J., concurring) (emphasizing constitutional protection for speech and press in civil litigation).

The underlying constitutional violation here was concrete. Uzuegbunam was stopped from distributing religious literature and from speaking in an outdoor campus plaza because of a policy that limited expression to designated speech zones. That kind of campus restriction is precisely the sort of suppression the First Amendment forbids absent sufficient justification. Tinker, 393 U.S. at 506-09. Religious literature distribution is protected expressive activity. Murdock, 319 U.S. at 108-10. And students do not “shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate.” Tinker, 393 U.S. at 506. Once the plaintiffs were allegedly silenced under an unconstitutional policy, the injury was complete; later repeal did not erase it.

The Court of Appeals relied on the wrong concept of mootness. A claim becomes moot only when “the issues presented are no longer ‘live’ or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” See Allen, 392 U.S. at 237-38. But a legally cognizable interest plainly remains when a plaintiff seeks a judgment that his constitutional rights were violated. That is why damages claims ordinarily preserve jurisdiction even when equitable relief no longer does. See New York Times, 376 U.S. at 267-92; Curtis Publishing, 388 U.S. at 154-55. The availability of at least nominal damages thus prevents dismissal for mootness.

The Court should also reject any argument that the plaintiffs’ claim is defeated because the challenged policy was revised rather than formally repealed. This Court has made clear that state action cannot escape review through strategic recharacterization or partial withdrawal. See Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952); Allen, 392 U.S. at 241 n.3. The college revised its policy only after suit was filed and after the challenged restrictions had already chilled and restricted expression. That is not mootness; it is voluntary cessation.

Finally, the Eleventh Circuit’s rule would create a perverse incentive: a government defendant could impose a speech restriction, force litigation, and then avoid any merits judgment by revising the policy before final decision. That would be inconsistent with the remedial tradition of this Court’s First Amendment cases. See Tinker, 393 U.S. at 506-09; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 108-10; Jones, 316 U.S. at 600-02. The judgment should therefore be reversed and the case remanded for adjudication on the merits.
