**Argument**

I. Title VII’s text does not require a separate showing that the employer’s conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the state of mind Congress specified.

The question presented is answered first and foremost by the statutory text. Congress authorized punitive damages under Title VII when the plaintiff “demonstrates that the respondent engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Nothing in that language adds an independent requirement that the discriminatory conduct itself be “egregious” or “outrageous.” Where Congress has chosen a mental-state standard, courts are not free to superimpose a separate threshold of reprehensibility. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994) (describing the Civil Rights Act of 1991 as expanding monetary relief, including punitive damages, available for intentional discrimination).

This Court’s cases likewise tie punitive liability to the defendant’s state of mind, not to an additional “egregiousness” element. In St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502 (1993), the Court explained that once the factfinder discredits the employer’s stated reasons, “no additional proof of discrimination is required.” Id. at 511. That principle matters here because punitive damages under § 1981a depend on proof of intentional discrimination plus the required mental state; they do not turn on a separate judicial assessment that the underlying discrimination was especially extreme. See id.; 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1).

II. The court of appeals correctly rejected the view that punitive damages are unavailable unless the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious.”

The D.C. Circuit recognized that Congress enacted § 1981a to provide “additional remedies” for unlawful intentional discrimination. Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n, 139 F.3d 958, 969 (D.C. Cir. 1998) (en banc). The statute caps punitive damages by employer size, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), but Congress did not condition those capped awards on proof of conduct beyond “malice” or “reckless indifference.” See Kolstad, 139 F.3d at 968-70. The court therefore properly refused to graft onto the statute a free-floating “egregiousness” requirement. See id.; see also Harsco Corp. v. Renner, 209 F.3d 154, 159-61 (3d Cir. 2000) (punitive damages under § 1981a require the statutory mental state, not an additional showing of aggravated conduct).

That understanding is consistent with the ordinary meaning of punitive damages in federal civil-rights law. The Court has long treated punitive awards as aimed at punishment and deterrence where the defendant acts with the requisite culpability. See Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (holding punitive damages available under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 when defendant acts with “reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others”). Congress used materially similar language in § 1981a(b)(1), and the better reading is that it incorporated a mental-state requirement, not a second, undefined severity requirement. See id.; 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1).

III. The contrary rule would improperly narrow Congress’s remedial scheme and conflict with the structure of § 1981a.

Congress knew how to limit punitive damages by objective features when it wanted to do so. It imposed dollar caps, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), and excluded certain employers and claims from coverage elsewhere in the Act. It did not add an “egregiousness” test. Courts should not rewrite a statute’s remedial standard by importing language from legislative snippets that Congress did not enact. See Connecticut Nat’l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) (“Courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.”).

Nor does the legislative history compel the additional requirement. The operative statutory text is clear, and where text is clear, resort to contrary legislative history is improper. See Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135, 147-48 (1994). The D.C. Circuit was correct to give effect to the enacted language rather than to impose an extra-statutory “outrageousness” threshold. Kolstad, 139 F.3d at 969-70.

IV. At minimum, the case should be remanded for application of the correct standard, not affirmed on an erroneous “egregiousness” rule.

Because the district court denied punitive damages on the premise that punitive relief required something more than the statutory showing of malice or reckless indifference, the judgment cannot stand under the correct legal standard. See Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n, 912 F. Supp. 13, 15-16 (D.D.C. 1996), aff’d in part, vacated in part, 139 F.3d 958 (D.C. Cir. 1998). The proper course is to allow the factfinder to decide whether the employer acted with the state of mind Congress identified in § 1981a(b)(1). See Smith, 461 U.S. at 56; St. Mary’s Honor Center, 509 U.S. at 511.

**Conclusion**

The Court should hold that punitive damages under Title VII do not require proof that the employer’s conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous,” and it should affirm the court of appeals’ rejection of that extra-statutory limitation.
