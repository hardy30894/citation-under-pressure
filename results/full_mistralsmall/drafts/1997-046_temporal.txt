**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN RELEASE FROM CUSTODY, WHICH TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment forbids a state from depriving any person of liberty without due process of law. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 570-71 (1972). Clemency is the “historic remedy for preventing miscarriages of justice” where a defendant’s continued confinement would be “harsh and unjust.” *Ex parte Grossman*, 277 U.S. 498, 505 (1928). When clemency is the only means by which a prisoner may obtain release, the decision to grant or deny it implicates a protected liberty interest. *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 18 (1979) (recognizing that parole release hearings, though discretionary, create a liberty interest where state law makes release “possible”).

Ohio’s clemency scheme, by permitting the Governor to commute a death sentence, plainly creates such an interest. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St. 3d 513, 520 (1994) (noting that the Governor’s clemency power is “almost unfettered” but subject to constitutional constraints). Because clemency is the final avenue for relief before execution, the process must comport with due process. *See Dumschat v. Connecticut Bd. of Pardons*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) (recognizing that clemency decisions are “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review” but not foreclosing due process protections where a liberty interest exists).

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A “CHOICE” BETWEEN PARTICIPATION IN CLEMENCY AND THE RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person “shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 6 (1964). A state may not condition a benefit—such as clemency—on the waiver of constitutional rights. *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 597 (1972) (holding that a state may not deny a benefit based on an employee’s refusal to waive First Amendment rights).

Here, Ohio’s clemency process forces inmates to choose between participating in an interview and asserting their Fifth Amendment privilege. The regulation permitting interviews states that any statement made may be considered in the clemency decision. Ohio Adult Parole Authority Reg. § 4(C)(2). This creates an unconstitutional Hobson’s choice: an inmate who remains silent risks appearing uncooperative, while one who speaks may incriminate himself. *See Lefkowitz v. Cunningham*, 431 U.S. 801, 808 (1977) (invalidating a state law that penalized a political party’s refusal to waive Fifth Amendment rights).

**III. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN HOLDING THAT CLEMENCY IS NOT AN INTEGRAL PART OF THE ADJUDICATORY PROCESS.**

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that clemency is too remote from trial to warrant due process protections misapprehends the nature of clemency. *See Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 18 (1956) (noting that due process attaches to “all steps” in the adjudicatory process). Clemency is the final safeguard against wrongful execution. *See Ex parte Grossman*, 277 U.S. at 505. To hold otherwise would permit states to evade constitutional scrutiny by relegating constitutional violations to the clemency stage.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that Ohio’s clemency process violates the Due Process and Fifth Amendment rights of condemned inmates.
