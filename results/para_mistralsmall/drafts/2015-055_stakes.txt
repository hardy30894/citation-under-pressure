**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE JOHNSON RULE ANNOUNCING THAT THE ACCA’S RESIDUAL CLAUSE IS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “places ‘certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.’” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion) (quoting *Mackey v. United States*, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments)). The Court has further clarified that a new rule applies retroactively if it “was dictated by precedent existing at the time the defendant’s conviction became final.” *Id.* at 301. The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), is unconstitutionally vague, satisfies both prongs of *Teague*.

First, *Johnson* places certain conduct beyond the reach of the criminal law by invalidating the residual clause in its entirety. The Court held that the residual clause “fails to give ordinary people fair notice of the conduct it prohibits” and “authorizes arbitrary enforcement.” *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557. Thus, the rule in *Johnson* is a “bedrock procedural element” that implicates the fundamental fairness of the criminal justice system. *See Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 352 (2004) (holding that rules implicating the fundamental fairness of criminal proceedings apply retroactively).

Second, *Johnson* was dictated by precedent. The Court’s decision in *Johnson* relied on its prior holdings in *United States v. Begay*, 553 U.S. 137 (2008), and *James v. United States*, 550 U.S. 192 (2007), which emphasized the need for clarity in defining violent felonies under the ACCA. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The Court reiterated that the residual clause’s “serious potential risk” standard was unconstitutionally vague because it failed to provide the requisite notice and guidance. *Id.* at 2557. Thus, *Johnson* did not announce a new rule but rather clarified the constitutional limits of the residual clause in light of existing precedent.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply *Johnson* retroactively. The court’s reliance on *Welch v. United States*, 604 F.3d 1229 (11th Cir. 2010), which predated *Johnson*, was misplaced. *Welch* did not address the constitutional infirmity of the residual clause and therefore cannot bar retroactive application of *Johnson*. *See Chaidez v. United States*, 568 U.S. 342, 355 (2013) (holding that a new rule applies retroactively even if it was not foreshadowed by prior precedent).

II. **PETITIONER’S PRIOR CONVICTION FOR FLORIDA ROBBERY DOES NOT QUALIFY AS A “VIOLENT FELONY” UNDER THE ACCA**

Even if *Johnson* did not apply retroactively, Petitioner’s prior conviction for Florida robbery does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as a crime that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another” or that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). Under the categorical approach, courts must look to the elements of the prior offense, not the facts underlying the conviction. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990).

Florida’s robbery statute, Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1), criminalizes the taking of property from a person with the use of “force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” The Florida Supreme Court has held that mere snatching—such as grabbing an item from a person’s hand—does not constitute sufficient force to support a robbery conviction. *Goldsmith v. State*, 573 So. 2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d DCA 1991); *A.J. v. State*, 561 So. 2d 1198, 1198 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990). The Eleventh Circuit has recognized that Florida’s robbery statute requires “some quantum of force greater than that inherent in the snatching of an item from a person’s grasp.” *United States v. Lockley*, 632 F.3d 1238, 1246 (11th Cir. 2011).

Because Florida’s robbery statute criminalizes conduct that does not necessarily involve violent force, it does not categorically qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA’s elements clause. *See Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010) (holding that a prior conviction must have “as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force”). Moreover, the residual clause’s “serious potential risk” standard is unconstitutionally vague and cannot be applied to salvage Petitioner’s sentence. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557.

III. **PETITIONER’S SENTENCE UNDER THE ACCA WAS UNLAWFULLY ENHANCED**

Petitioner’s sentence was enhanced under the ACCA based on a prior conviction that does not qualify as a “violent felony.” Because the ACCA’s residual clause is unconstitutionally vague and Petitioner’s prior conviction does not satisfy the elements clause, the district court erred in imposing a 15-year mandatory minimum sentence. *See Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 260 (2013) (holding that sentencing courts must apply the categorical approach to determine whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit and remand for resentencing consistent with *Johnson* and the categorical approach.
