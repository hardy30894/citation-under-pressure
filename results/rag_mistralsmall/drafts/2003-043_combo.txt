**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection from them.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a “taxpayer” within three years of the filing of the taxpayer’s return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The term “taxpayer” includes “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” is defined to include “an individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). Thus, the IRS must assess each individual partner separately to extend the limitations period for collection from that partner. *See Coson v. United States*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that an assessment must identify the taxpayer and that an assessment against a partnership does not constitute an assessment against individual partners); *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 562 (7th Cir. 1995) (noting that the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners, extending the limitations period for both).

The IRS’s argument that an assessment against the partnership suffices to extend the limitations period for the partners is foreclosed by the plain text of the statute and this Court’s precedents. *See Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280, 286 (1946) (recognizing that the IRS must look beyond form to substance in determining whether a partnership exists for tax purposes, but not altering the requirement that each taxpayer be assessed individually); *Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 303 U.S. 493, 496 (1938) (holding that the tax treatment of partners depends on their individual liability, not the partnership’s assessment).

**II. The IRS cannot circumvent the assessment requirement by relying on state law to impose liability on the partners.**

Under California law, partners are jointly and severally liable for the debts of the partnership. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). However, this liability does not obviate the need for an assessment or judgment against the individual partners. *See Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 264 U.S. 499, 502 (1924) (holding that a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners and that partners must be individually named and served to reach their personal assets); *Forsyth v. Woods*, 78 U.S. 484, 490 (1871) (noting that partnership debts are distinct from individual debts and that the rule setting apart partnership assets for partnership debts would be undermined if individual partners could be held liable without a judgment).

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the court merely held that a partner who had paid his share of the partnership’s tax was not entitled to a refund because he was liable for the debt under state law. *Id.* at 910. The court did not hold that the IRS could collect the tax from the partner without an assessment or judgment. *Id.* at 910-11. Thus, *Young* does not support the IRS’s position.

**III. The IRS’s failure to obtain a judgment against the partners within the limitations period further bars collection.**

Alternatively, the IRS could have brought a judicial action to collect the tax from the partners within three years of the filing of the partnership’s return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). However, the IRS did neither. *See Goldston v. United States (In re Goldston)*, 104 F.3d 1198, 1200-01 (10th Cir. 1997) (holding that an action to collect taxes must be brought within the limitations period). The IRS’s failure to assess or sue the partners within the limitations period bars collection from them.

**IV. The bankruptcy court properly disallowed the IRS’s claims.**

The Bankruptcy Code permits a creditor to file a claim only if the creditor has a “right to payment.” 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). The IRS’s claims against the partners are unenforceable because the IRS failed to assess or obtain a judgment against the partners within the limitations period. *See Liberty Nat. Bank of Roanoke, Va. v. Bear*, 276 U.S. 215, 220 (1928) (holding that a creditor’s claim must be enforceable under applicable law). Thus, the bankruptcy court did not err in disallowing the IRS’s claims. *See Francis v. McNeal*, 228 U.S. 695, 700 (1913) (holding that a creditor’s claim must be enforceable against the debtor).

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection from them. The IRS cannot circumvent this requirement by relying on state law to impose liability on the partners. The IRS’s failure to obtain a judgment against the partners within the limitations period further bars collection. The bankruptcy court properly disallowed the IRS’s claims.
