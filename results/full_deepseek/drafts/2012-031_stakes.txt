**ARGUMENT**

The district court abused its discretion by certifying a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The court accepted a damages model that did not isolate the specific antitrust injury alleged—deterrence of overbuilder entry—and thus failed to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement. This Court’s precedents demand a rigorous analysis ensuring that common evidence can prove class-wide damages attributable to the theory of liability. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 131 S. Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011); *General Telephone Co. v. Falcon*, 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982).

**I. The District Court Failed to Conduct the Required Rigorous Analysis of the Damages Model**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). Predominance cannot be satisfied unless the plaintiffs demonstrate that damages are capable of proof through common evidence. *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 311–12 (3d Cir. 2008). The district court must resolve factual disputes relevant to certification, even when they overlap with the merits. *Id.* at 317. Here, the court accepted plaintiffs’ expert Dr. McClave’s model without ensuring it measured damages from the sole theory of liability—deterrence of overbuilders. That was clear error.

**II. The Damages Model Was Not Tied to the Alleged Antitrust Violation**

Plaintiffs’ theory of liability was that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder entry, causing supra-competitive prices. The district court expressly limited proof of antitrust impact to that theory. App. 00032. Yet Dr. McClave’s model measured damages from all anticompetitive conduct, including alleged exclusionary acts against RCN and other conduct not tied to overbuilder deterrence. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 175–76 (E.D. Pa. 2010). A damages model that does not isolate the specific antitrust injury alleged cannot support class certification. *In re New Motor Vehicles Canadian Export Antitrust Litig.*, 522 F.3d 6, 28–29 (1st Cir. 2008) (reversing certification where damages model failed to measure impact of alleged conspiracy). The model’s inability to attribute damages to the overbuilder theory meant that individual inquiries would be required to determine whether each class member’s injury was caused by the alleged violation. That defeats predominance.

**III. The District Court’s Finding That the Model Was Common Evidence Was Clearly Erroneous**

The district court found that Dr. McClave’s model provided common evidence to measure damages. But the model compared Comcast’s prices in the Philadelphia DMA to prices in other markets where Comcast had not engaged in clustering. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 174. This comparison did not account for the fact that Comcast’s prices might have been higher for reasons unrelated to overbuilder deterrence—such as market-specific costs, demographics, or other competitive conditions. A model that fails to isolate the effect of the alleged violation cannot prove that all class members suffered the same injury. *Wal-Mart*, 131 S. Ct. at 2551 (commonality requires that class members “have suffered the same injury”). The district court’s acceptance of this model was “completely devoid of minimum evidentiary support.” *Krasnov v. Dinan*, 465 F.2d 1298, 1302 (3d Cir. 1972).

**IV. The District Court’s Error Was Not Harmless**

Without a valid class-wide damages model, the predominance inquiry collapses. The district court’s error forced Comcast to face a class action where individual issues of causation and damages would overwhelm common questions. This Court has repeatedly warned against certifying classes where damages cannot be measured on a class-wide basis. *See, e.g., Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177–78 (1974) (requiring that class actions be “manageable”). The district court’s decision to certify despite the model’s flaws was an abuse of discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court’s certification order should be reversed. A class cannot be certified without admissible evidence that damages are capable of class-wide proof tied to the theory of liability. The Third Circuit’s contrary decision should be vacated.
