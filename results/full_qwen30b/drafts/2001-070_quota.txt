**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits the infliction of “cruel and unusual punishments.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958). This prohibition is not static; it evolves in accordance with “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that executing individuals with intellectual disability (then referred to as “mental retardation”) violates the Eighth Amendment. The Court’s decision was grounded in a comprehensive analysis of national consensus, the nature of the punishment, and the culpability of the offender. The respondent respectfully submits that the execution of Daryl Atkins—whose intellectual disability was established by expert testimony and confirmed by the Virginia Supreme Court—constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment, and that the judgment below must be affirmed.

First, the national consensus against executing individuals with intellectual disability is overwhelming and well-documented. As the Court observed in *Atkins*, “a majority of States have enacted statutes prohibiting the execution of persons with intellectual disability.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 319. At the time of the decision, 18 states had explicitly barred such executions, and 14 others had done so through judicial interpretation. Id. at 319 n.14. This consensus is not merely legislative; it reflects a broader societal understanding that individuals with intellectual disability possess diminished capacity for rational decision-making, moral understanding, and self-control. *See Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 330 (1989) (plurality opinion) (“[T]he jury must be able to consider and give effect to the fact that the defendant’s capacity for moral culpability is significantly impaired.”). The fact that Virginia, like the vast majority of states, has not enacted a statutory bar does not negate the existence of a national consensus. *See *Graham v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 48, 70 (2010) (“[T]he Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is not limited to the text of the Constitution but is informed by the evolving standards of decency.”).

Second, the execution of individuals with intellectual disability is disproportionate to their moral culpability. The Eighth Amendment’s proportionality principle requires that punishment be “not grossly disproportionate” to the offense. *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 965 (1991). In *Atkins*, the Court emphasized that “the execution of persons with intellectual disability is grossly disproportionate because such individuals are less culpable than average criminals.” *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 321. This is due to their diminished capacity for planning, understanding consequences, and resisting impulses. *See *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 307 (1972) (Stewart, J., concurring) (“The basic concept underlying the Eighth Amendment is nothing less than the dignity of man.”). The Virginia Supreme Court correctly recognized that Atkins’ intellectual disability significantly impaired his ability to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct and to conform his behavior to the law. *See *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 330 (plurality opinion) (recognizing that intellectual disability “may affect the defendant’s capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct”).

Third, the execution of individuals with intellectual disability fails to serve the penological goals of retribution and deterrence. *See *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 183 (1976). Retribution requires that punishment be proportionate to the offender’s blameworthiness. *See *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277, 292 (1983). Because individuals with intellectual disability are less able to understand the nature and consequences of their actions, they are less morally blameworthy than others who commit similar crimes. *See *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 321. Moreover, deterrence is ineffective where the individual lacks the cognitive capacity to comprehend the threat of punishment. *See *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986) (noting that “the purpose of punishment is defeated when the individual is incapable of understanding the reason for it”).

Fourth, the Virginia Supreme Court’s reliance on *Penry v. Lynaugh* was misplaced. *Penry* did not hold that intellectual disability is irrelevant to capital sentencing; rather, it held that juries must be permitted to consider such evidence in mitigation. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 330 (plurality opinion). The Court in *Atkins* explicitly rejected the notion that *Penry* permitted the execution of intellectually disabled individuals. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 320 (“We conclude that the execution of persons with intellectual disability is inconsistent with the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment.”). The Virginia Supreme Court’s failure to apply *Atkins*’s holding—that intellectual disability is a bar to execution—constitutes a clear error of constitutional law.

Fifth, the execution of individuals with intellectual disability is inconsistent with the principles of justice and human dignity. *See *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. The Eighth Amendment is not merely a procedural safeguard; it is a substantive limitation on the state’s power to inflict punishment. *See *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 667 (1962) (holding that punishment for a condition—addiction—violates the Eighth Amendment). The execution of individuals with intellectual disability is not a punishment for a crime, but a punishment for a disability. *See *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 321. This is fundamentally unjust.
