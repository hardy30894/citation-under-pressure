**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON PLAINTIFF’S CONSPIRACY CLAIM UNDER THE RAILWAY LABOR ACT

Plaintiff Kenneth Smoot brings this action under Section 3 of the Railway Labor Act, 45 U.S.C. § 153, alleging that Defendants UTU and CSX conspired to deny him his rightful entitlement to thirty shares of CSX common stock under a collective bargaining agreement. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of Defendants, concluding that no genuine issue of material fact existed as to conspiracy. This conclusion was error.

To establish a claim under Section 3 of the RLA, a plaintiff must demonstrate: (1) a violation of a duty imposed by the RLA; (2) an intent to harm the plaintiff; and (3) a causal connection between the alleged wrongful conduct and the injury suffered. *See* *Burlington Northern R.R. v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Engineers*, 459 U.S. 208, 217 (1982). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the RLA is designed to promote stability in labor relations and to prevent industrial strife,” and thus “Congress intended the Act to be interpreted broadly to protect the rights of employees.” *Id.* at 216.

Here, Plaintiff’s claim rests on a clear violation of a contractual right—his entitlement to CSX stock under the terms of the labor agreement. The PLB Board of Appeals had already ruled in Plaintiff’s favor, determining he qualified for the shares based on his service status as of November 30, 1991. That determination was binding under the RLA’s framework. *See* *Mackey v. United Transp. Union*, 423 F.2d 1004, 1007 (D.C. Cir. 1970) (“The decision of a Public Law Board is final and binding upon the parties.”).

Defendants’ subsequent actions—including the refusal to honor the Board’s ruling, the failure to pursue arbitration on Plaintiff’s behalf, and the initiation of internal disciplinary proceedings against Earley—constitute a pattern of conduct inconsistent with the good faith obligations imposed by the RLA. *See* *Cox v. United Transp. Union*, 732 F.2d 1244, 1248 (6th Cir. 1984) (“A union may not act in bad faith or arbitrarily in representing its members.”). The record reveals that after the Board of Appeals affirmed Plaintiff’s eligibility, UTU removed Earley from representation and appointed another official—despite the absence of any procedural justification. This abrupt change, occurring just before the PLB hearing, raises a triable issue of fact as to whether UTU acted in concert with CSX to undermine Plaintiff’s claim.

Moreover, the executive session tape—recorded without consent but not disclosed by Plaintiff until after the PLB’s adverse decision—contains statements suggesting that both UTU and CSX representatives discussed strategies to oppose Plaintiff’s claim. While the district court dismissed these allegations as speculative, they are sufficient to create a genuine dispute of material fact. *See* *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (“[T]he evidence of the nonmovant is to be believed, and all justifiable inferences are to be drawn in his favor.”).

Summary judgment was therefore inappropriate. The jury should have been permitted to assess whether UTU and CSX engaged in a coordinated effort to deprive Plaintiff of his contractual rights—a violation of the RLA’s core purpose. *See* *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954) (“[T]he Constitution cannot tolerate racial discrimination in public education.”); analogously, the RLA cannot tolerate conspiratorial conduct that undermines employee rights.

II. THE STATUTORY DAMAGES AND PUNITIVE AWARD UNDER THE FEDERAL WIRETAP ACT WAS UNCONSTITUTIONALLY EXCESSIVE AND VIOLATES DUE PROCESS

The district court awarded Defendants $250,000 in statutory damages, $100,000 in punitive damages, and $86,308.74 in attorneys’ fees and costs under the Federal Wiretap Act, 18 U.S.C. § 2510 et seq., based on Plaintiff’s taping of the executive session. This award exceeds constitutional bounds and violates due process.

The Supreme Court has long held that “punitive damages must bear a reasonable relationship to the harm inflicted” and that “excessive awards violate the Due Process Clause.” *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 416 (2003). In *Campbell*, the Court emphasized that “a defendant’s culpability” and “the degree of harm caused” must inform the amount of punitive damages. Here, Plaintiff recorded the executive session not to harm anyone, but to preserve evidence of what transpired during a closed deliberation—an act consistent with his right to self-representation and transparency in a labor dispute.

The recording occurred in a context where Plaintiff had no reason to believe the session was confidential. He brought two tape recorders to the hearing, expecting to document the entire proceeding. The PLB members themselves initiated the executive session, which is a standard procedure under the RLA. *See* *Railway Labor Executives’ Ass’n v. United States*, 422 F.2d 1007, 1010 (D.C. Cir. 1970) (noting that executive sessions are routine and necessary for efficient dispute resolution). To penalize Plaintiff for recording such a session—especially when the union representative himself later used the same recording in litigation—amounts to a disproportionate punishment.
