The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a "class of one" where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group. The Seventh Circuit's decision in this case, holding that a plaintiff can allege an equal protection violation by asserting that state action was motivated solely by a "spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective," is an unwarranted expansion of equal protection jurisprudence.

The Equal Protection Clause is traditionally understood to protect against discrimination based on membership in a particular class or group. See, e.g., Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954) (striking down segregation in public schools); Loving v. Virginia, 388 U.S. 1 (1967) (invalidating laws prohibiting interracial marriage). The Clause is not intended to provide a cause of action for individualized grievances unrelated to class-based discrimination.

In Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), this Court established that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits state action that discriminates against individuals based on their membership in a particular class. The Court held that a San Francisco ordinance, although neutral on its face, was unconstitutional because it was administered in a discriminatory manner against Chinese laundry owners. Id. at 373-74. This decision and others like it demonstrate that the Equal Protection Clause is concerned with protecting against class-based discrimination, not individualized animus.

The Seventh Circuit's decision below ignores this fundamental principle. By allowing a "class of one" to bring an equal protection claim, the court effectively transforms the Clause into a federal tort law, permitting individuals to litigate in federal court what are essentially state law claims. See, e.g., Paul v. Davis, 424 U.S. 693 (1976) (holding that the Due Process Clause does not provide a cause of action for defamation, a state law tort).

Other circuits have rejected the "class of one" theory, recognizing that it is inconsistent with the traditional understanding of the Equal Protection Clause. See, e.g., Campagna v. Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection, 334 F.3d 150 (1st Cir. 2003); Jennings v. City of Stillwater, 383 F.3d 1199 (10th Cir. 2004). These decisions demonstrate that the Seventh Circuit's holding is an outlier and should be reversed.

Furthermore, allowing "class of one" claims would lead to an unwarranted expansion of federal judicial power, permitting courts to second-guess the decisions of state and local officials. See, e.g., FCC v. Beach Communications, Inc., 508 U.S. 307 (1993) (holding that rational basis review is a deferential standard that requires only a plausible justification for the challenged action).

In addition, the "class of one" theory is inconsistent with the Court's decision in Snowden v. Hughes, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), which held that an equal protection claim requires a showing of intentional discrimination against a particular class or group. Id. at 8. Olech does not allege that she is a member of a particular class or group; rather, she claims that the Village's actions were motivated by a personal vendetta against her. This is not a cognizable equal protection claim.

The Court's decision in Personnel Administrator of Massachusetts v. Feeney, 442 U.S. 256 (1979), also supports this conclusion. In Feeney, the Court held that a state law that gave preference to veterans in hiring for state jobs did not violate the Equal Protection Clause, even though it had a disproportionate impact on women. Id. at 279-80. The Court emphasized that the law was not intended to discriminate against women, but rather to reward veterans. Id. Similarly, in this case, Olech does not allege that the Village's actions were motivated by a discriminatory intent against a particular class or group.

In Village of Willowbrook v. Olech, the Seventh Circuit's decision should be reversed because it misinterprets the Equal Protection Clause. The Clause is not a catch-all provision for individualized grievances, but rather a safeguard against class-based discrimination. See, e.g., McGowan v. Maryland, 366 U.S. 420 (1961) (upholding a Sunday closing law against an equal protection challenge because it did not discriminate against a particular class). By rejecting the "class of one" theory, this Court can reaffirm the traditional understanding of the Equal Protection Clause and prevent an unwarranted expansion of federal judicial power.
