# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) CONFIRMS THAT NPS RETAINS REGULATORY AUTHORITY OVER THE NATION RIVER

Statutory construction begins and ends with the text where, as here, the language is unambiguous. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (holding that "where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise"). Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that "[n]o lands which, before, on, or after [ANILCA's enactment], are conveyed to the State, to any Native Corporation, or to any private party shall be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units." The statute's structure draws a critical distinction: it exempts non-federal *lands* from regulations applicable *solely* to public lands, but says nothing about withdrawing the boundaries of conservation system units themselves or stripping NPS of authority to regulate activities—such as hovercraft use—that are not limited to public lands alone.

This Court has long held that statutory exemptions must be construed with reference to their precise terms, not expanded beyond their text. *Phillips Co. v. Walling*, 324 U.S. 490, 493 (1945) (exemptions from remedial statutes are to be narrowly construed, since "if broad exemptions were to be granted by mere implication, the achievement of the statute's purposes could be virtually defeated"). Sturgeon's interpretation—that § 103(c) categorically bars NPS from regulating any activity on non-federal land within a unit—would require the Court to read the phrase "regulations applicable solely to public lands" as though it said "regulations applicable to public lands," excising the word "solely" from the statute. But courts must give effect to every word Congress used. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955) (it is a court's duty "to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute"). The hovercraft ban, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), applies uniformly to all lands and waters administered by NPS, federal and non-federal alike; it is therefore not a regulation "applicable solely to public lands" and falls outside § 103(c)'s narrow carve-out.

## II. CONGRESS DID NOT INTEND TO DIMINISH THE BOUNDARIES OR INTEGRITY OF CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS ESTABLISHED UNDER ANILCA

Even if the text permitted some ambiguity, the broader statutory scheme confirms that Congress intended NPS to retain authority necessary to protect the integrity of park units as coherent ecological and recreational systems. Courts must interpret individual provisions in light of the statute as a whole. *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940) ("[I]n expounding a statute, we [are not] guided by a single sentence or member of a sentence, but look to the provisions of the whole law, and to its object and policy."). ANILCA's purpose, as declared in its findings, was to preserve "unrivaled scenic and geological values" and to protect "wildlife habitats" within newly created conservation system units. Reading § 103(c) to strip NPS of authority over rivers, waterways, and other non-federal inholdings scattered throughout these units would fragment federal management authority in a manner inconsistent with that congressional purpose.

This reading is reinforced by ANILCA § 1319 (codified at 16 U.S.C. § 3207), which expressly preserves "existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to... exercise licensing or regulatory functions." Where Congress intends to modify or repeal existing regulatory authority, it says so specifically; ANILCA contains no specific provision divesting NPS of authority to regulate activities affecting navigable waters within park boundaries. The canon against implied repeals applies with full force here. *Posadas v. National City Bank*, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936) ("The intention of the legislature to repeal must be clear and manifest.").

## III. THE UNITED STATES RETAINS AUTHORITY OVER NAVIGABLE WATERS WITHIN PARK BOUNDARIES BY VIRTUE OF ITS NAVIGATIONAL SERVITUDE AND COMMERCE POWER, INDEPENDENT OF STATE TITLE TO THE STREAMBED

Even assuming the State holds title to the submerged lands beneath the Nation River pursuant to the Submerged Lands Act and the equal-footing doctrine, that title does not divest the federal government of its paramount authority over navigation. This Court has consistently held that state ownership of a riverbed is subordinate to the federal navigational servitude. *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967) (per curiam) (the "dominant power" of the federal government to control navigable waters "is a power to control for purposes of navigation," and just compensation need not be paid for its exercise even where the State holds title to the bed). Likewise, in *United States v. Twin City Power Co.*, 350 U.S. 222, 224–25 (1956), the Court held that the federal navigational servitude is "a dominant one, not to be displaced by state law," and that the United States may regulate activities on navigable waters "without being disabled by the fact of state ownership of the riverbed."

This principle traces to *Gilman v. Philadelphia*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 713, 724–25 (1866), where the Court recognized that "[c]ommerce includes navigation" and that the power to regulate navigable waters extends to the whole of a state's navigable rivers to the extent they affect interstate commerce, irrespective of who holds title to the submerged lands. The same principle undergirds *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871), which held that a "river is navigable in fact when it is used, or is susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce," and that Congress's power to regulate such commerce is plenary.

Thus, the source of NPS's regulatory authority over hovercraft use on the Nation River need not rest solely on § 103(c) or on federal ownership of the riverbed. Congress's Commerce Clause authority, and the correlative navigational servitude, independently support the hovercraft ban regardless of who holds title to the submerged lands. See also *United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. 53, 62 (1913) (the United States' "dominant right" over navigable waters "is the servitude which... may be asserted to the exclusion of any competing or conflicting one").

## IV. STURGEON'S CONSTITUTIONAL ARGUMENT UNDER THE PRIVILEGES OR IMMUNITIES CLAUSE DOES NOT LIMIT CONGRESS'S POWER TO REGULATE NAVIGATION FOR CONSERVATION PURPOSES

To the extent Sturgeon invokes a right to navigate derived from *The Slaughter-House Cases*, 83 U.S. (16 Wall.) 36, 79 (1872), that right has never been understood as absolute or as displacing Congress's authority to impose reasonable, non-discriminatory regulations on the use of navigable waters in furtherance of legitimate federal purposes, including conservation. The right recognized in *Slaughter-House* was the right "to use the navigable waters of the United States," not a right to use any particular vessel or mode of transport free from regulation. Reasonable equipment and use restrictions imposed uniformly, such as the hovercraft ban, are consistent with the government's traditional authority to regulate the manner of navigation. See *Gilman*, 70 U.S. at 724–25 (Congress's power over navigable waters includes the power "to determine what specific plans shall be adopted" for their use).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed as to Sturgeon.
