**Argument**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt” for money, property, or services obtained by “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The text is broad, and the Supreme Court has directed that discharge exceptions for fraud be read to prevent a wrongdoer from escaping the consequences of fraud through bankruptcy. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991). That principle controls here.

This Court should affirm because Cohen’s debt was incurred through actual fraud and the Bankruptcy Code makes nondischargeable not only the value wrongfully obtained, but the entire debt arising from the fraud, including statutory treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs.

I. **Cohen’s debt falls squarely within § 523(a)(2)(A).**

The bankruptcy court found that Cohen charged rents in excess of what the ordinance allowed and did so without learning the legal limits, consulting counsel, or disclosing to the tenants that the rents were unlawful. The Third Circuit correctly held that charging an illegal rent carried an implicit representation that the amount demanded was lawful. A debtor need not make an express oral or written lie to commit “actual fraud.” See Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 69 (1995) (discussing common-law fraud principles incorporated into § 523(a)(2)(A)).

The Third Circuit also correctly held that reckless disregard for the truth satisfies the scienter requirement. That conclusion is consistent with this Court’s instruction that § 523(a)(2)(A) tracks the common-law understanding of fraud, and with the rule that fraudulent intent may be inferred from the totality of the circumstances. Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87; Field, 516 U.S. at 69; In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995). Cohen was notified his rents were unlawful, ignored the matter, failed to perfect his appeal, and never reimbursed the tenants. Those findings support actual fraud, not mere negligence.

II. **Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts the entire debt arising from fraud, not merely the amount initially obtained.**

The text excepts “any debt” for money or property obtained by fraud, “to the extent obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). That language does not limit nondischargeability to the value initially transferred to the debtor. When Congress used the word “debt,” it referred to liability on a claim, not just the principal amount wrongfully acquired. Pennsylvania Dep’t of Pub. Welfare v. Davenport, 495 U.S. 552, 558 (1990). Once a debt is created by fraud, the resulting liability is nondischargeable.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213 (1998), resolves this case. There, the Court held that § 523(a)(2)(A) bars discharge of “any liability arising from” fraud, including treble damages and attorney’s fees awarded under state law. Id. at 218-23. The Court rejected the argument that only the amount directly obtained by the fraud is excepted from discharge, explaining that “any debt” encompasses “all liability arising from” the fraudulent conduct. Id. at 220-21. It also held that the phrase “to the extent obtained by” modifies “money, property, services, or . . . credit,” not “any debt,” so once the debtor obtains money by fraud, all consequential liability is nondischargeable. Id. at 218-19.

That is dispositive here. Cohen’s fraud gave rise to the tenants’ entire judgment, including the treble-damage component mandated by the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, as well as fees and costs. Under Cohen, those sums are part of the nondischargeable debt. 523 U.S. at 223.

III. **Allowing discharge would defeat the Bankruptcy Code’s policy.**

The Bankruptcy Code’s “fresh start” is reserved for the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87. Congress did not intend to confer a fresh start on a debtor who obtained money through fraud and then invoked bankruptcy to minimize the civil consequences. Field, 516 U.S. at 69; Cohen, 523 U.S. at 217-18. Exempting only the base overcharge while discharging the statutory remedies would allow fraudulent debtors to externalize the full cost of their misconduct and would undercut the deterrent purpose of consumer fraud statutes. Cohen, 523 U.S. at 222-23.

The judgment below should therefore be affirmed. Cohen’s debt, including the treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs awarded to the tenants, is nondischargeable under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A).
