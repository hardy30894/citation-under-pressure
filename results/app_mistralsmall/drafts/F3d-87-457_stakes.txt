**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATUTE’S DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS ARE FACIALLY OVERBROAD AND UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

The Florida statute’s disclosure requirements sweep far too broadly, chilling constitutionally protected lobbying activity. Facial overbreadth challenges are permissible where, as here, a statute’s “substantial overbreadth” threatens First Amendment rights. *Board of Trustees of State Univ. of N.Y. v. Fox*, 492 U.S. 469, 484-85 (1989) (recognizing facial challenges where statute’s overbreadth is "substantial"). The statute’s mandatory disclosure of expenditures by category—including vague terms like "research" and "communication"—fails to provide clear notice of what conduct is prohibited, violating due process. *Papachristou v. Jacksonville*, 405 U.S. 156, 162 (1972) (holding that laws must "give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited").

The statute’s lack of definitional clarity exacerbates its overbreadth. The terms "lobbying" and "principal" are defined so broadly as to encompass incidental contacts with legislators, including mere discussions of policy. *See* Fla. Stat. § 11.045(1)(a) (defining "lobbying" as "influencing or attempting to influence legislative action"). This risks capturing core First Amendment activity—petitioning the government—without a compelling justification. *See City of Madison v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Comm’n*, 429 U.S. 167, 174 (1976) (protecting "the right of individuals to petition their government").

**II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN IMPOSES AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL RESTRICTION ON LOBBYISTS’ PROFESSIONAL SPEECH.**

The statute’s prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying directly burdens protected speech by conditioning compensation on legislative outcomes. This restriction fails strict scrutiny because it is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest. *See Riley v. National Fed’n of the Blind of N.C.*, 487 U.S. 781, 795 (1988) (holding that content-based restrictions on speech must be narrowly tailored).

The state’s asserted interests—preventing corruption and ensuring transparency—are insufficient to justify the ban. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected prophylactic bans on speech where less restrictive alternatives exist. *See United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709, 726-27 (2012) (striking down a ban on false statements where alternatives like penalties for actual fraud were available). Here, the state could instead require disclosure of contingency-fee arrangements, as it does for expenditures, without banning them outright.

Moreover, the Court’s prior decisions do not support the statute’s blanket prohibition. *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), upheld disclosure requirements for lobbyists but did not address contingency fees. The Court has since cautioned against extending *Harriss* to impose categorical bans on speech. *See Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 365 (2010) (rejecting categorical restrictions on corporate speech).

**III. THE STATUTE CANNOT BE SUSTAINED UNDER A FACIAL CHALLENGE BECAUSE IT LACKS A SAVING CONSTRUCTION.**

A facial challenge succeeds where no set of circumstances exists under which the statute is valid. *Salerno*, 481 U.S. at 745. Here, the statute’s overbreadth and vagueness render it unconstitutional in all applications. The state’s argument that the statute’s severability clause saves it is unavailing, as the Court has refused to sever unconstitutional provisions where they are "inextricably intertwined" with valid ones. *Ayotte v. Planned Parenthood of N. New Eng.*, 546 U.S. 320, 330 (2006).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in granting summary judgment to the state. The statute’s disclosure provisions are facially overbroad and vague, and the contingency-fee ban violates the First Amendment. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
