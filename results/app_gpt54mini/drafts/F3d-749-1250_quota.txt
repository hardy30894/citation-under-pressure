## Argument

### I. The FBI agent’s testimony was impermissible lay and expert opinion on Stanley’s credibility, and it struck at the core function of the jury.

The district court allowed FBI Special Agent Charles Jones to tell the jury, in substance, that Stanley’s answers were not believable, that his conduct was “common among the criminal element,” that he had “never” seen an innocent person say what Stanley said, and that references to faith were “deceptive.” That testimony was improper because “[t]he credibility of witnesses is generally not an appropriate subject for expert testimony.” *United States v. Toledo*, 985 F.2d 1462, 1470 (10th Cir. 1993); see also *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267–68 (10th Cir. 1999) (expert may not testify that another witness is truthful or that abuse occurred). Even where a witness is qualified as an expert, Federal Rule of Evidence 702 does not permit the expert to “tell the jury who to believe.” *United States v. Hill*, 749 F.3d 1250, 1258–59 (10th Cir. 2014).

The problem was compounded because Jones’s testimony was not merely generalized background. He applied his supposed training to Stanley’s specific statements and demeanor and expressly labeled them deceptive. That is classic credibility-opinion testimony. In *United States v. Adams*, 271 F.3d 1236, 1245–46 (10th Cir. 2001), this Court held it improper for an expert to opine that a defendant’s conduct was indicative of guilt because such testimony invades the jury’s province. Likewise, in *United States v. Zepeda-Lopez*, 478 F.3d 1213, 1223–24 (10th Cir. 2007), the Court reiterated that an expert may not “comment directly on a defendant’s truthfulness.” Jones did exactly that here.

Jones also crossed the line by invoking broad, prejudicial characterizations of “the criminal element.” That sort of testimony was not tied to any specialized scientific or technical knowledge and invited the jury to convict based on stereotype rather than evidence. Rule 702 requires that expert testimony be helpful and reliable; it is not helpful when it merely supplies an officer’s hunch about guilt. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589–95 (1993); *Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael*, 526 U.S. 137, 147–52 (1999). Nor may the government use an expert as a conduit for “experience” untethered to proper methodology. *United States v. Baines*, 573 F.3d 979, 983–85 (10th Cir. 2009).

The prosecutor’s use of Jones’s testimony about Stanley’s religious references was especially prejudicial. Jones told the jury that invoking “faith” to support one’s statements was, in his experience, deceptive. That testimony impermissibly suggested that sincere religious expression itself is evidence of guilt. The Federal Rules do not allow conviction by insinuation and prejudice. See Fed. R. Evid. 403; *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180–85 (1997).

### II. Because Stanley did not object, the conviction should still be reversed under plain-error review.

Under *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732–36 (1993), Stanley must show error that was plain and affected substantial rights, and then this Court should correct it if it seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. The admission of Jones’s testimony satisfies each element.

First, it was error for the reasons above. Second, the error was plain. By the time of Stanley’s trial, this Court had long held that experts may not opine on credibility or guilt. *Toledo*, 985 F.2d at 1470; *Charley*, 189 F.3d at 1267–68; *Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1245–46. Third, the error affected substantial rights because Jones was a federal agent offering apparent expert validation of the government’s theory that Stanley lied during interrogation. Testimony from a law-enforcement expert carries special weight with juries and can easily tip the balance in a close case. See *United States v. Velarde*, 214 F.3d 1204, 1211–12 (10th Cir. 2000). This was not a harmless aside; it directly targeted the central issue of Stanley’s participation and consciousness of guilt.

Fourth, correction is warranted because allowing a government expert to brand a defendant deceptive and aligned with “the criminal element” undermines the fairness and integrity of the proceeding. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736; *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 469–70 (1997). The prejudice was magnified because the government’s evidence against Stanley was not overwhelming: the case depended heavily on inference from the East Pine residence, surveillance, and Stanley’s post-arrest statements. Where inadmissible credibility evidence likely influenced the jury’s evaluation of a defendant’s truthfulness and culpability, reversal is appropriate. *Hill*, 749 F.3d at 1259; *United States v. Bordeaux*, 570 F.3d 1041, 1050–51 (8th Cir. 2009).

### III. The error was not harmless.

Even if harmless-error review applied, the conviction cannot stand unless the government proves the error did not have a substantial influence on the verdict. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946); *United States v. Rivera*, 900 F.2d 1462, 1469–70 (10th Cir. 1990) (en banc). It cannot do so here. The government presented an FBI agent as an expert on deception and then used him to tell jurors that Stanley’s explanations were lies. That improper imprimatur likely affected the jury’s assessment of the defense and Stanley’s own credibility. Because the error went to the heart of the case, the judgment should be reversed and the matter remanded for a new trial.
