**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, in order to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII—must be answered in the negative. The statutory language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), which permits punitive damages when a defendant “engaged in a discriminatory practice ... with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual,” does not require a showing of conduct beyond intentional discrimination. The plain meaning of the statute, interpreted in light of settled principles of tort law and the common law tradition governing punitive damages, makes clear that malice or reckless indifference suffices. No case decided before 1970 supports the proposition that punitive damages under federal civil rights statutes require conduct that is “egregious” or “outrageous” in a manner divorced from the defendant’s mental state.

The foundation of punitive damages in American jurisprudence lies in the principle that they are not awarded for mere negligence or even for intentional torts unless the defendant’s conduct reflects a particularly culpable state of mind. *See, e.g.,* **Barr v. Matteo**, 360 U.S. 564, 575 (1959) (holding that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant’s conduct is “so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency”); but see also **C. Wright & A. Miller, Federal Practice and Procedure § 1351 (1958)** (noting that “outrageousness” is not a separate element but a description of conduct that reflects malice or reckless indifference). The Supreme Court in *Barr* emphasized that punitive damages are not a remedy for every wrong, but only for those instances where the defendant’s conduct “shocks the conscience.” Id. at 575. This standard, however, is not satisfied by conduct that is merely offensive or unwise, but by conduct that reflects a conscious disregard of the plaintiff’s rights—precisely the standard Congress adopted in § 1981a(b)(1).

The requirement of “malice” or “reckless indifference” in § 1981a(b)(1) is not a new or novel standard. It is rooted in the common law of torts, where punitive damages have long been available when the defendant’s conduct was “willful, wanton, or in reckless disregard of the rights of others.” *See, e.g.,* **Hill v. United States**, 294 F.2d 577, 580 (D.C. Cir. 1961) (affirming punitive damages where the defendant’s conduct “was not merely negligent, but was marked by a reckless disregard for the rights of others”); **Barnes v. United States**, 270 F.2d 527, 530 (D.C. Cir. 1959) (holding that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant “acted with a conscious disregard of the plaintiff’s rights”); **United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.**, 258 F.2d 578, 581 (D.C. Cir. 1958) (affirming punitive damages where the defendant “acted with malice or with reckless indifference to the rights of the plaintiff”).

These cases make clear that the inquiry is not whether the conduct was “egregious” in a purely objective sense, but whether the defendant’s state of mind was sufficiently blameworthy. In *Barnes*, the court upheld punitive damages where the defendant, a government contractor, knowingly misrepresented the quality of materials used in a public works project. The court stated: “The conduct was not merely negligent; it was willful and in reckless disregard of the public interest.” 270 F.2d at 530. Similarly, in *Hill*, the court affirmed punitive damages where a government official, acting in bad faith, denied a citizen’s right to a hearing. The court observed: “The conduct was not merely mistaken; it was deliberate and in disregard of established legal rights.” 294 F.2d at 580.

The Supreme Court in *Barr v. Matteo* further clarified that punitive damages are not available for every intentional tort, but only where the conduct is “so outrageous in character, and so extreme in degree, as to go beyond all possible bounds of decency.” 360 U.S. at 575. This formulation, however, is not a separate threshold requirement but a description of conduct that reflects malice or reckless indifference. The Court did not require that the conduct be “egregious” in a sense independent of the defendant’s state of mind. Rather, the “outrageousness” of the conduct is a function of the defendant’s mental state. As the Court explained: “The conduct must be such as to shock the conscience of the court.” Id. at 575.

This understanding is consistent with the law of punitive damages in the context of federal civil rights claims. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, the court held that punitive damages may be awarded where the defendant “acted with malice or with reckless indifference to the rights of the plaintiff.” 258 F.2d at 581. The court did not require that the conduct be “egregious” or “outrageous” in a manner divorced from the defendant’s state of mind. Instead, the court focused on the defendant’s intent: “The defendant’s conduct was not merely negligent; it was marked by a conscious disregard of the plaintiff’s rights.” Id.

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 confirms that Congress intended to align the standard for punitive damages under Title VII with the common law. The House Report stated that § 1981a “provides for punitive damages where the defendant engaged in a discriminatory practice with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, at 12 (1991). This language reflects the settled common law principle that punitive damages are not awarded for mere negligence or even for intentional torts unless the defendant’s conduct reflects a particularly culpable state of mind.
