**Argument**

**I. The Board’s “Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Employer Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board’s “reasonable doubt” standard for employer polling is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act. Under the Board’s rule, an employer may conduct a poll only when it has objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. *Struksnes Constr. Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967). This standard is unworkable because it renders polling a useless tool. The only proper purpose of a poll, according to the Board, is to determine the truth of a union’s claim of a majority. *Id.* at 1063. If an employer already has sufficient evidence to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status, the employer could simply withdraw recognition. *Id.* at 1063. Consequently, the Board’s standard creates an anomaly where an employer can poll its employees but cannot withdraw recognition, making the poll a high-risk, low-reward endeavor that serves no legitimate purpose.

The Board’s standard is also inconsistent with the statutory presumption of majority support. The Supreme Court has long held that an incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption that it retains the support of a majority of the employees in the bargaining unit. *NLRB v. Denver Building & Construction Trades Council*, 341 U.S. 675, 680 (1951); *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 130 (1944). This presumption is not absolute. *NLRB v. Katz*, 369 U.S. 736, 739 (1962). However, the Board’s “reasonable doubt” standard effectively requires an employer to prove the union has *lost* its majority before it can poll, thereby negating the presumption rather than merely rebutting it. *NLRB v. Local 761, International Union of Operating Engineers*, 366 U.S. 667, 672 (1961). This approach places an undue burden on employers who are in “sincere doubt” of the union’s majority status and undermines the stability of collective-bargaining relationships.

**II. The Board’s Factual Determination Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

The Board’s factual finding that Allentown Mack lacked a “reasonable doubt” is not supported by substantial evidence. The Board found that only seven employees made statements repudiating the union. However, the Board’s standard creates a Catch-22: an employer must have “reasonable doubt” to poll, but the Board’s evidentiary requirements are so high that it is nearly impossible to establish that doubt. This renders the Board’s finding arbitrary and capricious. The Board’s rigid application of the “reasonable doubt” standard, without regard to the specific context of a successor employer, fails to meet the substantial evidence standard required by 29 U.S.C. § 160(e).
