**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Refusing to Clarify the Ambiguous Verdict and in Entering Judgment That Nullified the Jury’s Award.**

When a verdict is ambiguous or inconsistent, the trial court has a duty to clarify the jury’s intent before discharging the jury. *Seminole Oil & Gas Corp. v. Allen*, 82 F.2d 833, 835 (10th Cir. 1936). The jury’s verdict here was facially ambiguous: on Form 1 it awarded Unit $70,768.02 for breach of contract, and on Form 3 it awarded Enron a set-off of $86,494.30. The natural inference is that the jury already subtracted the set-off from the contract price of $157,262.36 to reach the net award. That inference is reinforced by the arithmetic: $157,262.36 minus $86,494.30 equals $70,768.06—a difference of only four cents, easily attributable to rounding. Yet the district court interpreted the forms independently, subtracted the set-off from the damages, and entered judgment for zero dollars, effectively disregarding the jury’s award.

The court’s refusal to send the jury back for clarification was reversible error. This Court has long held that when a verdict is “so uncertain and ambiguous that the court cannot ascertain what the jury intended,” the jury should be resubmitted. *Huntsman v. Perry*, 204 F.2d 301, 303 (10th Cir. 1953); *see also Taylor v. Gladden*, 188 F.2d 757, 759 (7th Cir. 1951) (reversible error to refuse to permit jury to correct ambiguous verdict). The ambiguity here was patent, and Unit specifically requested clarification before the jury was discharged. The court’s refusal deprived Unit of its right to a coherent verdict.

Furthermore, the court’s interpretation violated the fundamental principle that a verdict must be construed to give effect to the jury’s intention, if that intention can be reasonably ascertained. *Union Pac. R.R. Co. v. Hadley*, 246 U.S. 13, 21 (1918) (“[V]erdicts are to have a reasonable intendment and are not to be avoided except from necessity.”); *McNiel v. Ziegler*, 68 F.2d 209, 210 (10th Cir. 1933) (“The verdict should be construed in the light of the evidence and the instructions and given effect if possible.”). The jury was instructed that if it found substantial performance, it should award the unpaid invoices “less the cost to Enron of correcting any defects.” That cost was precisely the basis for Enron’s set-off claim. The jury’s use of both forms unambiguously indicates that it performed that subtraction once, not twice. The district court’s contrary construction required it to assume double counting, which no instruction authorized.

Finally, the court’s refusal to question the jury violated the long-settled rule that when a verdict appears inconsistent on its face, the court should “send the jury back for further deliberation” rather than “guess” at the intended meaning. *Bayamon Canning Co. v. Porto Rican-American Tobacco Co.*, 73 F.2d 399, 402 (1st Cir. 1934); *see also Brennan v. Luehrs*, 235 F.2d 123, 125 (10th Cir. 1956) (observing that the court “should have returned the jury for reconsideration”). The district court’s failure to do so cannot be deemed harmless, because the resulting judgment—zero dollars—contradicts the jury’s clear intent to award Unit $70,768.02.

**II. The Jury Instructions, Even If Not Properly Objected To, Compound the Error.**

Although Unit acknowledges that its objection to the instructions was not specific enough to preserve the error under Fed. R. Civ. P. 51, the confusion engendered by the verdict forms highlights the fundamental unfairness of the district court’s approach. The instructions permitted the jury to reduce Unit’s damages by the cost of defects and then to award that same cost as a set-off. Without a clarifying instruction that double reduction was prohibited, the jury’s verdict was inherently ambiguous. The district court’s refusal to cure that ambiguity by questioning the jurors or by adopting the only construction consistent with a reasonable jury—that the set-off had already been deducted—was error.

**III. Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to enter judgment for Unit in the amount of $70,768.02, or in the alternative, to conduct a new trial on damages.
