# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 523(a)(2)(A) RENDERS NONDISCHARGEABLE THE ENTIRE DEBT ARISING FROM FRAUD, INCLUDING TREBLE DAMAGES, ATTORNEY'S FEES, AND COSTS

### A. The Plain Language of § 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts "Any Debt" Traceable to Fraud, Not Merely the Amount of the Fraudulently Obtained Money

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge "any debt . . . for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by . . . false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This Court has consistently held that "the starting point for interpreting a statute is the language of the statute itself." Consumer Product Safety Comm'n v. GTE Sylvania, Inc., 447 U.S. 102, 108 (1980). The phrase "to the extent obtained by" modifies the *character* of the debt—identifying which debts qualify as fraud debts—not the *quantum* of damages recoverable once a debt is properly so characterized.

Once money is "obtained by" fraud, the entire debt traceable to that fraud, including all liability arising from it, is nondischargeable. As the Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized in the decision the Third Circuit adopted below, the statute's focus is on "debt," a term this Court has defined broadly as "liability on a claim," 11 U.S.C. § 101(12), and "claim" itself is defined to include a "right to payment, whether or not such right is reduced to judgment . . . disputed, undisputed, legal, equitable, secured, or unsecured." 11 U.S.C. § 101(5)(A). See In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 679 (11th Cir. 1993). Nothing in this capacious definitional structure suggests that a "debt" splits into dischargeable and nondischargeable components depending on whether particular dollars trace to compensatory versus statutory or punitive elements of liability.

### B. This Court's Precedents Confirm That Once Fraud Is Established, the Entire Resulting Liability Is Nondischargeable

This Court's decision in *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), forecloses any contrary reading. There, this Court held that the "fresh start" policy underlying the Bankruptcy Code is "reserved for the 'honest but unfortunate debtor,'" *id.* at 286-87, and that Congress intended § 523(a)(2)(A) to prevent debtors "guilty of fraud" from obtaining the benefits of discharge for debts so incurred. *Id.* at 287. *Grogan* applied a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard to *all* dischargeability determinations under § 523(a)(2)(A) precisely because "the fresh start policy does not extend" to protect fraudfeasors from *any* liability flowing from their misconduct. If the "fresh start" is unavailable altogether to defrauders as to the underlying debt, it is equally unavailable as to the full measure of liability—including punitive and multiplied damages—that the debtor's fraud has legally occasioned.

The Third Circuit below properly relied on this reasoning, holding that "a debtor liable for punitive damages because of his or her fraud should not be permitted to have that debt discharged." Pet. App. (quoting *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 680). That conclusion follows ineluctably from *Grogan*'s holding that Congress designed § 523(a)(2)(A) to ensure that "perpetrators of fraud" do not enjoy the "new opportunity in life" that discharge affords honest debtors. 498 U.S. at 286-87.

### C. The Statutory Text Draws No Distinction Between Compensatory and Non-Compensatory Damages

Respondent's position—and the reasoning of the Ninth Circuit in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), which the courts below correctly rejected—would require reading into § 523(a)(2)(A) a limitation Congress did not enact. *Levy* held that only the amount "actually obtained by" fraud is nondischargeable, rendering associated punitive damages freely dischargeable. 951 F.2d at 198. But this atomized approach cannot be squared with the statutory text, which excepts the *debt*, not merely the *principal sum* fraudulently acquired. Congress could have written § 523(a)(2)(A) to except only "money . . . obtained by fraud," full stop—but instead it excepted "any debt . . . to the extent obtained by" fraud, language directing courts to ask whether the debt as a whole is fraud-based, not to parse the debt into recoverable and dischargeable slices.

Moreover, treble damages and attorney's fees awarded under statutes like the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not free-floating punishments untethered from the fraud; they are the *legal consequence* the State has attached to the very fraudulent conduct Congress targeted in § 523(a)(2)(A). As the Third Circuit observed, because such damages are "codified," they are "entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations," and thus are properly understood "as the debt he incurred through his conduct, rather than as punishment." Pet. App. This is not a case of a discretionary jury punitive award divorced from the underlying fraud; it is a case where the State's chosen remedial mechanism for fraud—trebling plus fees—defines the debt itself.

### D. Petitioner's Conduct Falls Squarely Within the Class of Fraud Congress Intended to Reach

The Bankruptcy Court's findings, affirmed at every level below, establish all elements of actual fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A): Cohen made material misrepresentations regarding lawful rent, exhibited reckless indifference to their falsity, intended to deceive, and Respondents reasonably relied to their detriment. See *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995) (intent to deceive may be inferred from "the totality of the circumstances, including the debtor's reckless disregard for the truth"). Having engaged in fraud within the meaning Congress intended to police, Cohen cannot now invoke the Code's fresh-start policy to shield himself from the full legal consequences—including the treble damages and fees New Jersey law attaches to that fraud—of his own misconduct.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
