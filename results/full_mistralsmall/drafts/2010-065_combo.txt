**ARGUMENT**

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO ASSERT A TENTH AMENDMENT CHALLENGE TO THE STATUTE.**

A criminal defendant may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge a federal statute on federalism grounds. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 143–44 (1939) ("The [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment."); *Frothingham v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485 (1923) (taxpayer lacked standing to challenge federal spending as beyond enumerated powers); *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447, 485 (1923) (same). The Tenth Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941) (quoting *United States v. Sprague*, 282 U.S. 716, 733 (1931)). It does not confer rights upon individuals but instead "states the division of power between the Federal Government and the States." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 108 (1945). Thus, only a state whose sovereignty is directly impaired may raise a Tenth Amendment claim. *Tennessee Electric Power*, 306 U.S. at 144; *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998) (jurisdictional limits bind federal courts).

Bond’s reliance on *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693 (7th Cir. 1999), is misplaced. That case permitted a private party to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to a federal statute, but it did so without analyzing *Tennessee Electric Power* or *Frothingham*, and it conflicts with the weight of authority. *See United States v. Parker*, 362 F.2d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 1966) (denying standing); *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.2d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 1965) (same). This Court should adhere to the Supreme Court’s clear holdings that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims.

**II. THE STATUTE IS A VALID EXERCISE OF CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER.**

Congress may enact legislation to implement valid treaties, even if that legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432–33 (1920) ("If the treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government."); *United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.*, 299 U.S. 304, 318 (1936) ("The treaty power is vested in the President and Senate, and its scope is not limited by the enumerated powers of Congress."). The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, and § 229 is a necessary and proper means of implementing it. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 432–33.

Bond’s argument that § 229 lacks a federal interest element is unavailing. The Necessary and Proper Clause permits Congress to regulate intrastate activity if it is "conducive" to the treaty’s enforcement. *Holland*, 252 U.S. at 435; *Perez v. Brownell*, 356 U.S. 44, 57 (1958) (Congress may regulate local activity if it is "appropriate" to effectuate a valid federal power). The statute’s application to Bond’s conduct is a reasonable means of ensuring compliance with the Convention’s prohibition on chemical weapons. *See Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 782 (1948) (Congress may act "in areas where the scope of the constitutional power is uncertain" if the means are "plainly adapted" to a legitimate end).

**III. THE STATUTE IS NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE.**

A statute is void for vagueness only if it fails to provide "fair warning" of prohibited conduct. *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 387, 391 (1926); *United States v. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921). Section 229 defines "chemical weapon" and "toxic chemical" with sufficient clarity to apprise ordinary persons of the prohibited conduct. *See Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (statute must "define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited"). The statute’s exceptions for peaceful and lawful purposes further cabin its scope. *See United States v. Wurzbach*, 280 U.S. 396, 403 (1930) (statute is not vague if it "marks boundaries sufficiently distinct").

Bond’s claim that § 229 is overbroad is equally meritless. The statute targets only the knowing use of toxic chemicals as weapons, a narrow and well-defined class of conduct. *See United States v. Ragen*, 314 U.S. 513, 523–24 (1942) (statute is not overbroad if it reaches only "a class of cases within the statute’s unquestionably constitutional applications"). The statute does not criminalize innocent or expressive conduct, and Bond’s reliance on *United States v. Robel*, 389 U.S. 258 (1967), is misplaced, as that case involved a First Amendment challenge to a statute that broadly restricted associational rights. *See also Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 515 (1951) (statute is not vague if it targets "clear and present danger" conduct).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
