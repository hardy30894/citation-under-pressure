**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due Process Requires the Government to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That the Defendant Remained a Member of the Conspiracy Within the Limitations Period.**

The Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). A timely prosecution is not a procedural nicety; it is itself a fact necessary to the government's case, for a conviction obtained outside the statute of limitations is void. *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 87, 114–15 (1970). Once a defendant produces evidence that he withdrew from the conspiracy before the limitations window began, the question whether he remained a culpable member during that window becomes an element the prosecution must establish, not a defense the accused must disprove.

This Court has repeatedly refused to let States or the federal government relabel elements of the offense as "affirmative defenses" so as to shift the burden of persuasion to the accused. In *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), the Court held that Maine could not require a defendant to prove heat-of-passion by a preponderance of the evidence where that fact bore directly on the degree of culpability, because "the Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged." *Id.* at 704 (quoting *Winship*). Although *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), permitted the State to place the burden of an affirmative defense on the defendant, it did so only because the defense there—extreme emotional disturbance—did not negate any element of the offense itself. *Id.* at 206–07. Withdrawal is different in kind. It does not excuse conduct that otherwise satisfies every element of conspiracy; it denies that the defendant continued to be a member of the conspiracy at all during the time the government must prove membership existed.

**II. Withdrawal Negates an Element of the Offense Rather Than Excusing It.**

Conspiracy is a continuing offense. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912) ("[T]he offense of conspiracy is continuous… it is renewed as to all its members every day"). Membership, not merely formation, is what the government must prove existed within the limitations period. *United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270, 274 (2003) (conspiracy "does not end… until the conspirators… achieve their goal or… have abandoned the enterprise"). Where a defendant has come forward with evidence of withdrawal, he is not raising a justification or excuse; he is disputing the very fact—continued membership—that the indictment must establish to satisfy the statute of limitations. *See Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 396–97 (1957) (recognizing that the limitations period runs from the last act in furtherance of the conspiracy, and that withdrawal terminates a defendant's liability for subsequent acts).

This Court's decision in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422 (1978), confirms this understanding of withdrawal as evidence bearing on continued participation rather than as a freestanding affirmative defense that exculpates otherwise complete criminal conduct. The Court there treated withdrawal as relevant to whether the defendant remained part of the conspiracy for purposes of vicarious liability for co-conspirators' acts, not as a matter separable from the government's continuing burden to prove membership. *Id.* at 464–65.

**III. Once Evidence of Withdrawal Is Produced, Any Burden-Shifting Scheme Risks Relieving the Government of Its Constitutional Burden.**

This Court's presumption cases counsel caution before permitting the government to rely on an inference of continued membership once a defendant has introduced evidence to the contrary. In *Leary v. United States*, 395 U.S. 6, 36 (1969), the Court struck down a statutory presumption because it was not clear "beyond a reasonable doubt" that the presumed fact flowed from the proven fact. The same logic applies here: allowing the jury to presume continued membership—and thus timeliness—once the defendant has produced evidence of an earlier withdrawal effectively relieves the prosecution of proving an element beyond a reasonable doubt, in tension with *Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364, and with this Court's admonition in *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 477 (2000), that "the domain of the jury" includes every fact that determines the permissible scope of punishment, here including whether the prosecution itself is timely.

Nor does *Martin v. Ohio*, 480 U.S. 228 (1987), rescue the government's position. There, the Court permitted Ohio to place the burden of proving self-defense on the defendant only because the jury instructions required the prosecution to independently establish every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt notwithstanding the affirmative defense. *Id.* at 233–34. No comparable safeguard exists here: if the defendant bears the burden of proving withdrawal, the jury may convict without ever requiring the government to prove, as to the limitations period itself, the very membership that defines the offense.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, due process requires that the burden of persuasion remain with the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The judgment below should be reversed.
