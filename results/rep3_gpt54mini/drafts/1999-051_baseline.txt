**ARGUMENT**

This Court should hold that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the petitioner first establishes both “cause and prejudice” for the ineffective-assistance default itself. That rule follows directly from this Court’s procedural-default cases and preserves the state procedural rules those cases are designed to respect. See *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986); *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 729-30 (1991).

A federal habeas petitioner generally may not obtain review of a claim that was not properly presented to the state courts. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 86-87 (1977). A federal court will excuse the default only if the petitioner shows cause for the default and actual prejudice. *Id.* at 87; *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 485. Ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause, but only if the ineffective-assistance claim itself is properly preserved. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488-89. As this Court explained, “the exhaustion doctrine... generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” *Id.* at 489.

That principle controls here. Carpenter’s sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is defaulted. He seeks to excuse that default by asserting ineffective assistance of appellate counsel. But that ineffective-assistance claim was itself dismissed by the state court as untimely. Under *Coleman*, where a petitioner has “failed to exhaust state remedies” and the claim is now procedurally barred, the claim is “technically exhausted” but defaulted. 501 U.S. at 732. In that posture, the claim cannot serve as cause unless the petitioner can also demonstrate cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffective-assistance claim itself. *Id.* at 750. Otherwise, the federal court would permit a defaulted federal claim to bootstrap review of another defaulted claim, undermining the very rule that state procedural defaults ordinarily foreclose federal habeas review. *Id.* at 750; *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 492.

Petitioner cannot evade *Coleman* by contending that ineffective assistance is an exception to the cause-and-prejudice requirement. This Court rejected that approach in *Murray* and reaffirmed it in *Coleman*: attorney error rising to constitutional ineffective assistance may be cause, but ordinary attorney error is not, and a constitutionally ineffective-assistance claim must itself be properly presented or independently excused. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488-89; *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-53. The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule would create an anomaly: a petitioner who defaulted the ineffective-assistance claim in state court would gain federal review of that claim whenever it is repackaged as cause for some other defaulted claim. That is incompatible with *Coleman*’s instruction that federal review is barred when “the independent and adequate state procedural ground” rests on the petitioner’s own failure to comply with state law. 501 U.S. at 729-30.

Nor does this case fall within the narrow circumstance where external impediment excuses a default. Cause must be something external to the defense. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488. Carpenter identifies no objective factor external to the defense that prevented him from timely raising his ineffective-assistance claim in state court. His present theory depends entirely on his own counsel’s earlier performance, but that is precisely the sort of claim that must itself be preserved under state procedures before it may serve as cause. *Id.* at 489; *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752-53.

Finally, this rule does not leave habeas petitioners without a remedy. A petitioner may still establish cause and prejudice for the ineffective-assistance default, or in an extraordinary case may show a fundamental miscarriage of justice. *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 496; *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750. Carpenter has made no such showing. Because his ineffective-assistance claim is itself procedurally defaulted and unexcused, it cannot excuse the default of his sufficiency claim.

The judgment of the court of appeals should therefore be reversed.
