The exclusion of the Good News Club from meeting after hours at Milford Central School did not violate the Club's First Amendment free speech rights. The school's community use policy prohibits use of its facilities for religious purposes, and the Club's activities, which include singing songs, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying, fall squarely within this prohibition.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the government may restrict speech in certain contexts, including in public forums that are not open to all forms of expression. In Perry Education Assn. v. Perry Local Educators' Assn., 460 U.S. 37 (1983) is not available, however,  the Court's earlier decision in  Adderley v. Florida, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), established that the government may limit access to public property based on the subject matter of the speech. Here, Milford's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on the use of its facilities, and the Club's activities are not entitled to special protection simply because they involve the expression of religious views.

The Court of Appeals correctly held that the Club's activities were "quintessentially religious" and not merely the teaching of morals from a religious perspective. As the Court has noted, "the line between permissible and impermissible state activity is not always easy to discern" (Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 16 (1947)), but here, the Club's activities involve explicit religious instruction and prayer, which go beyond the bounds of permissible discussion of moral values.

The Club's argument that its activities represent a "viewpoint" on the subject of morality is unpersuasive. While the Supreme Court has recognized that the government may not discriminate against speech based on its viewpoint (Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940)), it has also noted that the government may restrict speech that is "utterly without redeeming social importance" (Roth v. United States, 354 U.S. 476, 484 (1957)). Here, the Club's activities, while potentially valuable in a religious context, are not entitled to protection as a "viewpoint" on a secular subject.

Furthermore, permitting the Club to use the school's facilities would raise Establishment Clause concerns. The Court has long recognized that the government may not provide direct support to religious institutions or activities (McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203 (1948); Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306 (1952)). Allowing the Club to meet at the school would effectively endorse the Club's religious activities, which could be seen as a form of state sponsorship.

In conclusion, Milford Central School did not violate the Good News Club's First Amendment free speech rights by excluding it from meeting after hours at the school. The school's community use policy is a reasonable restriction on the use of its facilities, and the Club's activities are not entitled to special protection. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
