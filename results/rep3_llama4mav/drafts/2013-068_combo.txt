The petitioners argue that the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics violates their First Amendment rights. However, this Court has long recognized that the government has the authority to regulate the time, place, and manner of speech in public forums. See Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574-576 (1941); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304-305 (1940); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 160-161 (1939); Lovell v. Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 451-452 (1938).

The buffer zone at issue here is a content-neutral regulation that serves a significant government interest in ensuring public safety and order. See Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87-88 (1949); Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948); Cox v. Louisiana, 336 U.S. 106, 112-113 (1965) (Black, J., dissenting, but not on this point). The law does not prohibit speech entirely, but rather regulates the location and manner in which it may be expressed. See Poulos v. New Hampshire, 345 U.S. 395, 405-406 (1953); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-273 (1951).

The petitioners claim that the buffer zone is not narrowly tailored to serve the government's interest, but this argument is unpersuasive. The law is designed to prevent congestion and ensure safe passage around the clinics, and the 35-foot buffer zone is a reasonable means of achieving this goal. See Cameron v. Johnson, 390 U.S. 611, 616-617 (1968); Cox v. Louisiana, 336 U.S. at 112-113.

Moreover, the petitioners have alternative means of communicating their message. They may still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, and display signs outside the buffer zone. See Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572-573 (1942); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. at 162. The law does not prohibit all forms of expression, but rather regulates the manner in which it may be expressed in a specific location.

The First Circuit correctly applied the law of the case doctrine in refusing to revisit its earlier decision upholding the law on its face. See Messenger v. Anderson, 225 U.S. 436, 444 (1912); Thompson v. Maxwell, 168 U.S. 451, 456 (1897). The petitioners have not shown that there has been a significant change in the law or facts that would justify reconsideration of the issue.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts law creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech. It serves a significant government interest in ensuring public safety and order, and leaves open alternative means of communication. The judgment of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
