ARGUMENT

Sieben satisfied its burden under the McDonnell Douglas framework by articulating legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons for Feltmann’s probation and termination. McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Those reasons—persistently low gross profits per vehicle, repeated violation of the demo-car geographic restriction, and unauthorized guest drives—were supported by undisputed evidence and were not shown to be pretextual. Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981).

The single vulgar remark by a non-supervisory employee at another franchise, promptly investigated and remedied, did not create a hostile environment. Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986). Isolated incidents that are not “sufficiently severe or pervasive” cannot support liability. Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc., 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993).

Feltmann’s complaint about the remark likewise fails to establish retaliation. The owners reprimanded the offender, offered to terminate him, and invited further reports; no further harassment occurred. Absent evidence that the decision-makers’ stated reasons were false or that retaliation was the true motive, judgment for the employee cannot stand. St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993).

The decision to place Feltmann on probation alongside a male colleague for identical sales deficiencies, and the refusal to extend a sales trip after higher-ranked consultants declined, are consistent with neutral performance criteria. Furnco Constr. Corp. v. Waters, 438 U.S. 567, 577 (1978). Title VII does not require employers to grant every female employee preferential treatment or to ignore documented policy violations. Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins, 490 U.S. 228, 242 (1989) (plurality).

Finally, Feltmann’s continued weekly 300-mile trips after written notice of the restriction supplied an independent, non-retaliatory ground for discharge. Because she produced no evidence that similarly situated male consultants were treated more leniently after comparable violations, the judgment rests on speculation rather than proof. Watson v. Fort Worth Bank & Trust, 487 U.S. 977, 986 (1988).

The record therefore compels reversal.
