The Fourteenth Amendment affords no right, independent of an insanity defense, to introduce expert evidence of mental condition for the purpose of negating the mens rea element of a state offense. States possess authority to prescribe the elements of crimes, the scope of defenses, and the rules governing the admission of evidence. Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516 (1884). That authority includes the power to confine mental-health testimony to an insanity claim on which the defendant bears the burden of proof. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952).

The Due Process Clause “does not impose upon the States a duty to adopt any particular rule of evidence or procedure.” Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). A state procedure violates due process only when it “offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Arizona’s limitation on the use of expert testimony satisfies this standard. It leaves the prosecution to prove every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt while permitting the defendant to establish legal insanity under the state’s chosen standard. The restriction therefore preserves the essential demands of fairness without compelling the state to restructure its substantive criminal law or its evidentiary framework. Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900).

Because the trial court’s ruling rested on a permissible allocation of proof and evidence, the conviction does not contravene the Fourteenth Amendment.
