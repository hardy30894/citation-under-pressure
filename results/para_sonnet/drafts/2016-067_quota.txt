# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 1692a(6) TURNS ON THE IDENTITY OF THE DEBT'S OWNER AT THE TIME OF COLLECTION, NOT ON THE DEBT'S DEFAULT STATUS AT ACQUISITION.

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as "any person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This Court has already recognized that this second definitional clause is the operative test for entities like Santander that are neither loan-servicing agencies nor businesses whose "principal purpose" is debt collection. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (noting that "[t]he Act's definition of the term 'debt collector' includes a person 'who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another'"). The critical inquiry under that clause is whether the collector is acting on behalf of *another* party or on its own account. Santander purchased petitioners' loans outright from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collected those debts for itself. It was not collecting "debts owed or due . . . another"; it was collecting debts owed to itself.

Petitioners attempt to escape this straightforward textual conclusion by arguing that "another" should be read to include the *original* creditor, so that any debt once serviced for CitiFinancial Auto remains subject to FDCPA regulation forever after, even once Santander purchased it outright. That reading cannot be squared with the statute's temporal focus on the *collection activity itself*. As the Eleventh Circuit held in rejecting an identical argument, "our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether [the purchaser] regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to [the purchaser]; our inquiry is whether [the purchaser] regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection." *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015). The Ninth Circuit reached the identical conclusion, explaining that "[t]he statute is not susceptible to the [debtors'] interpretation that 'owed or due another' means 'originally owed or due another.'" *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, NA*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013). Congress regulated present-tense collection conduct, not the historical pedigree of the underlying obligation.

## II. THE STATUTORY EXCLUSIONS CANNOT BE USED TO MANUFACTURE INCLUSION WITHIN THE DEFINITION OF "DEBT COLLECTOR."

Petitioners' argument depends on inverting the statute's structure: rather than first asking whether Santander satisfies any of the *inclusive* definitions of "debt collector" in § 1692a(6), they leap directly to the *exclusions*—specifically § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which removes from the definition "any person collecting or attempting to collect any debt . . . owed or due another . . . which was not in default at the time it was obtained by such person." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). But an exclusion has no work to do until a person first falls within the general definition. See *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314 ("[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a 'debt collector' as a matter of course. [Instead] . . . he must satisfy the Act's substantive requirements."). This Court's own interpretive practice confirms that exclusionary clauses are construed against the backdrop of the operative definition they modify, not used to expand it. See *Paroline v. United States*, 572 U.S. 434, 447 (2014) (explaining that a modifying clause is properly read as "applicable" to the terms it follows) (quoting *Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920)). Because Santander collected debts owed to itself, it never entered the category of persons collecting debts "owed or due another," and the (F)(iii) exclusion is simply irrelevant to it.

Some circuits have reasoned, as petitioners urge here, that because § 1692a(4)'s definition of "creditor" excludes a person who receives an assigned debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another," anyone who purchases defaulted debt must default into "debt collector" status. See *Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012); *Ruth v. Triumph P'ships*, 577 F.3d 790, 796–97 (7th Cir. 2009); *FTC v. Check Investors, Inc.*, 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007). But that reasoning conflates the definitions of "creditor" and "debt collector" rather than analyzing whether Santander satisfies the affirmative statutory test for a debt collector in the first place. The two terms are not perfect mirror images requiring that every non-creditor collector be a debt collector; each must be assessed under its own textual criteria.

## III. PETITIONERS' RULE WOULD PRODUCE RESULTS CONTRARY TO CONGRESS'S STATED PURPOSE.

Congress enacted the FDCPA "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors," 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e)—that is, by third-party agents lacking a stake in the underlying debt, whose incentives Congress viewed as uniquely prone to abuse. A consumer finance company that purchases a loan portfolio and thereafter collects on its own account bears the same incentives as any creditor collecting its own accounts receivable. Treating such an entity as a "debt collector" simply because the debt happened to be in default at the moment of sale would sweep an enormous category of ordinary commercial creditors—purchasers of charged-off credit-card debt, automobile deficiency balances, and similar receivables—into a regulatory regime Congress reserved for third-party collection agents. Nothing in the statute's text or purpose supports that result.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
