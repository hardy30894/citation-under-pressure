# ARGUMENT

## I. Section 289's "Article of Manufacture" Does Not Mean the Entire End Product When the Patented Design Applies Only to a Component

The Patent Act provides that one who applies a patented design "to any article of manufacture" is liable "to the extent of his total profit." 35 U.S.C. § 289. The Federal Circuit held below that this "total profit" award must always be calculated on the entire end product sold to consumers, reasoning that consumers could not separately purchase the components. That per se rule cannot be reconciled with the statutory text or with this Court's precedent.

The term "article of manufacture" is not synonymous with "end product." This Court has long recognized that an "article of manufacture" encompasses components of multi-component products, not merely finished consumer goods. See *Deepsouth Packing Co. v. Laitram Corp.*, 406 U.S. 518, 528 (1972) (treating unassembled components as embodying the patented invention). Nothing in § 289's text confines "article of manufacture" to the whole smartphone rather than, say, the front face or the graphical user interface to which Apple's design patents actually apply. Indeed, the same phrase—"article of manufacture"—appears in 35 U.S.C. § 171, which this Court has confirmed protects the "design of an article," not the article's every possible commercial embodiment. See *Gorham Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. 511, 525 (1871) (design patent protects the "appearance," not the underlying utilitarian article).

## II. Traditional Principles of Causation Require Apportioning Profits to the Infringing Component

This Court has repeatedly held that damages remedies in intellectual property cases must be tied to the value the infringement actually contributed—not the value of the entire commercial product in which the infringing feature is embedded. In *Aro Manufacturing Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476, 512 (1964), this Court explained that a patentee's remedy must be limited to "the use which is made of the invention," refusing to permit recovery premised on the value of an entire replacement product. Although *Aro* arose in the utility-patent damages context, its animating principle—that a defendant should disgorge profits attributable to the infringement, not profits attributable to unrelated components—applies with equal force here.

This apportionment principle finds its clearest articulation in the utility patent damages context under § 284, where this Court has insisted that damages be tied to "the smallest salable patent-practicing unit," rejecting entire market value calculations except in narrow circumstances. *See Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884) (patentee "must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant's profits and the patentee's damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features"). *Garretson*'s apportionment mandate—decided under a predecessor damages statute—was never repealed by Congress when it enacted § 289's "total profit" language for design patents. To the contrary, § 289's use of "total profit" refers to the total profit on the relevant *article of manufacture*, which itself may be a component rather than the whole product. Reading § 289 to eliminate apportionment entirely revives exactly the windfall recovery this Court disapproved in *Garretson*.

## III. The Federal Circuit's Categorical Rule Improperly Substitutes a Legal Presumption for Case-Specific Factual Analysis

The Federal Circuit's rule below rests on the proposition that because Samsung did not sell the infringing components separately, the "article of manufacture" must be the whole phone. That reasoning collapses two distinct questions—consumer purchasing patterns and statutory interpretation—into one, and it does so in a manner this Court has cautioned against in analogous contexts. In *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.*, 547 U.S. 388, 393 (2006), this Court rejected "expansive principles suggested by [a] general adaptability of the traditional [remedy] language," and instead required courts to apply traditional, case-specific analysis. The same instruction should govern here: whether the relevant article of manufacture is the whole product or a component is a question of fact to be determined by traditional means, not resolved by an inflexible presumption favoring entire-product profits.

This Court's decision in *Petrella v. Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, Inc.*, 572 U.S. 663, 667 n.1 (2014), further illustrates that even where Congress uses seemingly categorical remedial language, courts must look to the equitable principles surrounding that remedy's historical application rather than adopting the most expansive possible reading. Just as *Petrella* examined the historical operation of laches rather than assuming a categorical statutory bar, courts applying § 289 must examine which article—the whole or the part—actually incorporates the patented design, rather than assuming the answer is always the whole.

## IV. A Multi-Factor Test Properly Effectuates Congressional Intent

Petitioner urges this Court to adopt a totality-of-the-circumstances test asking, among other things: (1) the scope of the design claimed in the patent, including the drawing and written description; (2) the relative prominence of the design within the product as a whole; (3) whether the design is conceptually distinct from the product's overall look; and (4) the physical relationship between the patented design and the rest of the product, including whether it can be sold separately.

This approach honors the statutory text of § 289 while preventing the disgorgement of profits wholly unrelated to the design innovation—precisely the concern this Court articulated in *Aro* and *Garretson*. It also respects the deferential-but-bounded factfinding role assigned to juries and district courts, consistent with the "substantial evidence" framework this Court has long applied when reviewing damages verdicts. *See Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB*, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938) (substantial evidence must be "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion").

## CONCLUSION

The Federal Circuit's categorical rule that "article of manufacture" always means the end product cannot be squared with the statutory text of § 289, the historical apportionment principles of *Garretson* and *Aro*, or this Court's insistence on case-specific remedial analysis in *eBay* and *Petrella*. The judgment below should be reversed.
