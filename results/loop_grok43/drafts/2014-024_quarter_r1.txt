ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred because 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. Criminal statutes are construed to incorporate the common-law requirement of mens rea. See Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 500 (1951).

The First Amendment demands subjective intent before speech may be punished as a threat. The clear-and-present-danger test protects expression unless the speaker intends to produce imminent lawless action. Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919). "The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent." Id. An objective standard would criminalize protected advocacy. Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925).

Punishment of speech without regard to the speaker's purpose chills expression the Amendment safeguards. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931); Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson, 283 U.S. 697, 713-714 (1931). "The safeguarding of free speech . . . demands that the state show a clear and present danger" arising from the speaker's intended meaning. Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 311 (1940). An objective test fails this requirement. Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941).

Even "fighting words" require that the speaker intend to provoke violence. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). The same principle governs threats under § 875(c). Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 503 (1951). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand.
