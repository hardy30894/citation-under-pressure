**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), which governs the choice of law on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that state wage and hour laws may apply on the OCS so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute and undermines the comprehensive federal regulatory scheme Congress designed for offshore operations. The correct interpretation is that state law applies only when federal law is silent or inadequate—i.e., when a “gap” exists. The Court should reverse.

### I. The OCSLA’s Choice of Law Provision Requires a Gap in Federal Law Before State Law May Apply

The text of § 1333(a)(2)(A) is clear: state law applies “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” (Emphasis added.) The phrase “to the extent that” is not a mere formality—it is a limiting clause that conditions the application of state law on the existence of a federal vacuum. This construction is consistent with the Act’s foundational purpose: to provide a coherent legal regime for the OCS, not to invite piecemeal state law supplementation where federal law already governs.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the presence of a comprehensive federal regulatory scheme precludes the application of state law that would interfere with or duplicate federal regulation.” *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941). In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 366 (1969), the Court held that Louisiana’s wrongful death law applied on the OCS because the federal High Seas Act did not provide a remedy for deaths occurring on fixed platforms—there was a “gap” in federal law. The Court emphasized that the High Seas Act “no more applies to these accidents actually occurring on the islands than it would to accidents occurring in an upland federal enclave.” Id. at 366. The absence of a federal remedy was the *reason* state law applied. The Court did not hold that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law already governs.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation—reading “not inconsistent” as a standalone threshold—ignores this foundational principle. It would allow state law to apply even where federal law is fully operative, creating a regime of overlapping and potentially conflicting standards. That is not what Congress intended. As the Court explained in *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Ass’n*, 505 U.S. 88, 102 (1992), “Congress may not have intended to allow state law to supplement federal law in areas where federal law is already comprehensive.” The FLSA is such a scheme.

### II. The Fair Labor Standards Act Is a Comprehensive and Exclusive Regulatory Scheme

The FLSA is not a mere floor; it is a detailed, self-contained system of wage and hour protections. It establishes minimum wage, overtime pay, recordkeeping, and child labor standards, and it includes a savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—that explicitly permits states to impose *more protective* standards. But that clause does not mean state law applies *in addition* to federal law when federal law already governs. Rather, it allows states to *supersede* federal minimums, not to coexist with them.

The Department of Labor has consistently interpreted the FLSA as a comprehensive framework. See *Brigham v. Eugene Water & Elec. Bd.*, 357 F.3d 931, 940 (9th Cir. 2004) (citing DOL regulations). The FLSA’s regulations define “workweek,” “hours worked,” and “compensable time” with precision. For example, the DOL has held that time spent on “controlled standby” is compensable if the employee is required to remain on the premises and is not free to use the time for personal purposes. 29 C.F.R. § 785.18. This is precisely the situation here: Newton worked twelve-hour shifts on a fixed platform, was not free to leave, and was on call. The FLSA already governs this issue.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that California’s meal period law is “not inconsistent” with the FLSA is legally flawed. The FLSA does not require meal periods. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 207. California’s law, by contrast, mandates a 30-minute meal period for every five hours worked. That is a *more protective* standard, but it is not “not inconsistent”—it is *inconsistent* in the sense that it imposes a *different* obligation than the FLSA. The FLSA’s savings clause does not permit state law to *add* obligations where federal law already provides a complete remedy. *See* *Barnes v. Costle*, 565 F.2d 975, 980 (7th Cir. 1977) (state law cannot impose additional requirements where federal law already provides a full remedy).

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Approach Would Create Legal Chaos on the OCS

The Ninth Circuit’s rule—state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent”—would allow a patchwork of state laws to govern offshore employment, depending on the state of the adjacent coast. A worker on a platform off California could claim under California’s meal period law, while a worker on a similar platform off Louisiana could not, even if the work conditions were identical. This undermines the uniformity Congress sought.
