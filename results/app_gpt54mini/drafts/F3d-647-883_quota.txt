**ARGUMENT**

I. **Buckles’s § 2255 motion was timely because the Ninth Circuit’s recall of the mandate restarted the certiorari clock.**

A conviction becomes final for § 2255 purposes when the time for seeking certiorari expires, or when certiorari is denied. *Clay v. United States*, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003). The Supreme Court’s certiorari deadline ordinarily runs from the denial of rehearing. Sup. Ct. R. 13(1), 13(3). Here, however, the Ninth Circuit recalled its mandate on June 12, 2007, and Buckles reasonably understood that action to reopen the time for certiorari.

That understanding was supported by the court’s own practice. A court of appeals has power to recall a mandate in extraordinary circumstances. *Calderon v. Thompson*, 523 U.S. 538, 550 (1998). When a court recalls a mandate, it necessarily reasserts jurisdiction over the case. *United States v. Foumai*, 910 F.2d 617, 621 (9th Cir. 1990). And where a lower court “appropriately entertains” a rehearing petition, the certiorari period runs from the later denial. Sup. Ct. R. 13(3). Buckles’s motion for appointment of certiorari counsel was filed under Ninth Circuit Rule 4-1(e), a rule designed to preserve access to Supreme Court review for indigent former retained-counsel clients. Construing Buckles’s April 24, 2007 letter as that motion was therefore consistent with the rule’s text and purpose.

At a minimum, the June 12 order created a reasonable basis for believing that the time to seek certiorari had been extended. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that procedural traps should not defeat review where the litigant acts in reliance on court action. See *Houston v. Lack*, 487 U.S. 266, 270-76 (1988); *Houston v. Lack*, 487 U.S. 266, 271 (1988) (pro se prisoner filing deemed filed when delivered to prison officials). Buckles filed his petition within 90 days of June 12. His petition was therefore timely, and his § 2255 motion filed within one year of finality was timely as well. *Clay*, 537 U.S. at 532.

II. **Even if the certiorari petition was untimely, equitable tolling applies because Buckles diligently relied on erroneous information supplied by the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office.**

Section 2255’s limitation period is subject to equitable tolling. *Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 645 (2010); *United States v. Buckles*, 647 F.3d 883, 890 (9th Cir. 2011). A petitioner must show diligence and extraordinary circumstances that stood in the way of timely filing. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 649; *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005). Buckles can make that showing.

First, he acted diligently. He promptly asked retained counsel on April 24, 2007 to assist in filing a Rule 4-1(e) motion, copied the Clerk, then followed up with a pro se “supplemental” motion on May 3 after counsel failed to respond. That conduct is far from “sleeping on his rights.” *Pace*, 544 U.S. at 419.

Second, misinformation from the court can constitute an extraordinary circumstance. The Ninth Circuit has held that equitable tolling may be warranted when a court or its clerk misleads a litigant about filing requirements. *Spitsyn v. Moore*, 345 F.3d 796, 801-02 (9th Cir. 2003); *Ramirez v. Yates*, 571 F.3d 993, 1001 (9th Cir. 2009). The Supreme Court has likewise recognized that misleading official action may justify tolling where it causes late filing. *Holland*, 560 U.S. at 651-52. Buckles declared that a Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office employee told his sister that “the timeline for filing the Writ of Certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the Petition for Appointment of Counsel and a new mandate date would be issued.” If credited, that statement directly explains why Buckles filed his petition on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of the recalled mandate, but after the July 2 date later identified by the Supreme Court Clerk.

This Court has also recognized that equitable tolling is appropriate where a prisoner relies on inaccurate information from an official source. *Sossa v. Diaz*, 729 F.3d 1225, 1236-37 (9th Cir. 2013). And the Supreme Court has held that procedural deadlines may be relaxed where the litigant is affirmatively misled. *Irwin v. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 498 U.S. 89, 96 (1990). Buckles’s declaration, if true, presents exactly that circumstance.

III. **The district court’s untimeliness ruling should be reversed.**

Under *Clay*, finality turns on the end of direct-review time. Under *Holland*, *Pace*, and *Spitsyn*, equitable tolling protects diligent prisoners misled by extraordinary circumstances. Buckles satisfies both paths to relief. His § 2255 motion should be deemed timely, or at minimum the matter should be remanded for factual development concerning the Clerk’s alleged misinformation. See *Schlueter v. Varner*, 384 F.3d 69, 76-78 (3d Cir. 2004) (equitable tolling requires factual inquiry where petitioner alleges misleading official conduct).
