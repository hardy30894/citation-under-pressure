The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act regulates only those who collect debts owed or due another. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). When Santander purchased the defaulted loans, it acquired full legal title and thereafter sought payment on its own account. An assignee who takes a chose in action steps into the shoes of the assignor and becomes the creditor entitled to enforce the obligation in its own name. See Bank of Kentucky v. Adams Express Co., 93 U.S. 174, 181 (1876) (“The assignee is substituted to all the rights of the assignor.”); see also Welch v. Mandeville, 14 U.S. (1 Wheat.) 233, 236 (1816).

This distinction between collection for another and collection for oneself long predates the statute. Courts have consistently held that one who owns the debt and collects it directly does not act as an agent or intermediary. See, e.g., United States v. Bank of the Metropolis, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 377, 386 (1841) (owner of a claim “sues in his own right, and not as agent for another”). The Act’s definitional structure preserves that line: the phrase “owed or due another” limits the second prong of the “debt collector” definition to third-party collection activity. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (“Where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise.”).

Petitioners’ contrary reading—that default status at acquisition converts an owner into a debt collector—finds no support in the statutory text and would erase the creditor–collector distinction recognized for generations. See, e.g., Security Mortgage Co. v. Powers, 278 U.S. 149, 154 (1928) (distinguishing between the holder of a claim and one acting on behalf of the holder). Because the complaint alleges only that Santander collected debts it owned, Santander was not a “debt collector” when it engaged in the challenged communications.

The judgment should be affirmed.
