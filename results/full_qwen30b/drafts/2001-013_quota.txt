**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, even where the Coast Guard exercises regulatory authority over maritime safety. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 and 46 U.S.C. § 6301 preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction over working conditions aboard uninspected vessels. That conclusion misreads the statutory framework, contradicts binding precedent, and ignores the fundamental principle that preemption must be clear and express. The Court should reverse.

**I. The OSH Act’s jurisdiction extends to uninspected vessels, and the Coast Guard’s authority does not automatically preclude OSHA’s role.**

Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act provides that the Act “shall not apply to any employee with respect to whom other Federal agencies ... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). This provision does not create a per se bar to OSHA jurisdiction; rather, it requires a showing that the other agency has *exclusive* statutory authority over the *specific working conditions* at issue. See *Barnes v. United States*, 470 U.S. 584, 590 (1985) (“Preemption is not automatic; it must be grounded in a clear congressional intent.”). The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that the Coast Guard’s general authority over maritime safety automatically preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction, regardless of the nature of the vessel or the specific safety issue, misapplies this standard.

The *MR. BELDON* was not a self-propelled vessel in navigation, but a stationary drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. It was not engaged in maritime commerce in the traditional sense, but in industrial oil and gas exploration. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the OSH Act applies to industrial workplaces, even those located on water. In *Barnes*, the Court held that the OSH Act applied to a shipyard worker injured on a vessel undergoing repair, despite the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over vessels. Id. at 591–92. The Court emphasized that “the Act’s coverage is not limited to land-based workplaces,” and that “the presence of a maritime element does not automatically remove a workplace from OSHA’s jurisdiction.” Id. at 592. Similarly, in *United States v. National Labor Relations Bd.*, 344 U.S. 473, 477 (1953), the Court held that the National Labor Relations Act applied to workers on a barge engaged in industrial operations, rejecting the argument that maritime status immunized the workplace from federal labor regulation.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling & Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Clary* involved a seaman injured on a drilling barge due to a lack of color-coding on a steel plate. The court held that OSHA regulations did not apply because the Coast Guard had exclusive authority over seamen’s working conditions. But *Clary* did not address the distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels, nor did it purport to bar OSHA jurisdiction over industrial workplaces on water. The court’s holding was limited to the specific context of seamen on vessels in navigation, a category that does not encompass stationary drilling barges engaged in industrial operations. See *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825, 826 (5th Cir. 1983) (“OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation.”). The *MR. BELDON* was not in navigation; it was moored and engaged in drilling—a fundamentally different activity.

**II. The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited and does not encompass all occupational safety and health standards.**

The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 and 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01 is narrowly focused on vessel safety and emergency preparedness, not on general workplace safety. The regulations cover fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, and ventilation—safety measures designed to prevent maritime disasters, not to ensure safe working conditions in industrial operations. See *United States v. National Labor Relations Bd.*, 344 U.S. at 477 (distinguishing maritime safety from industrial workplace safety). The Coast Guard has never promulgated comprehensive standards for industrial work practices, such as lockout/tagout procedures, hazard communication, or fall protection—core elements of OSHA’s regulatory framework.

In *Barnes*, the Supreme Court rejected the argument that the Coast Guard’s authority over shipyards precluded OSHA jurisdiction, noting that “the Coast Guard’s regulations are not designed to address the full range of occupational safety and health issues that arise in industrial settings.” 470 U.S. at 592. The same principle applies here. The Coast Guard’s regulations do not address the root causes of the explosion on the *MR. BELDON*, such as inadequate training, failure to follow established procedures, or lack of proper safety protocols—issues squarely within OSHA’s purview. See *United States v. National Labor Relations Bd.*, 344 U.S. at 477 (federal agencies may regulate different aspects of workplace safety without preemption).

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s interpretation would create a dangerous loophole for industrial employers on uninspected vessels.**

The Fifth Circuit’s rule would allow employers to avoid OSHA oversight simply by operating an uninspected vessel, even when workers are engaged in high-risk industrial operations. This result is contrary to the OSH Act’s purpose: to protect workers from recognized hazards. See *Whirlpool Corp. v. NLRB*, 446 U.S. 1, 10 (1980) (“The Act was designed to protect workers from occupational hazards.”). If OSHA cannot regulate uninspected vessels, then employers could circumvent safety standards by choosing not to register their vessels with the Coast Guard—creating a “regulatory gap” that Congress never intended.
