**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on a single, dispositive legal principle: once a party has entered into a judicially approved settlement resolving its liability for a response action under a federal environmental statute, it may no longer pursue a cost-recovery action under CERCLA § 107(a). Instead, it must proceed under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), which provides a right of contribution to a settling party, but only within a three-year statute of limitations. The District Court erred in concluding otherwise. The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA resolved Guam’s liability for the Ordot Dump’s remediation, thereby triggering § 113(f)(3)(B) and extinguishing any right to pursue a § 107(a) cost-recovery claim. This conclusion is compelled by settled principles of statutory construction, administrative law, and the doctrine of equitable estoppel, all of which were firmly established in the jurisprudence of this Court and the lower federal courts before 1970.

**I. A judicially approved settlement that imposes obligations for response actions constitutes a “settlement” under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), regardless of a disclaimer of liability.**

The plain language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The term “resolved” is not limited to adjudications or findings of liability. Rather, it encompasses any binding agreement that extinguishes or terminates a party’s legal obligation to respond to a hazardous substance release. This interpretation is consistent with the settled understanding of “settlement” in federal administrative law prior to 1970.

In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 303 U.S. 610 (1938), the Court held that a settlement agreement between the government and a contractor, even without an admission of liability, “constitutes a final determination of the rights of the parties” and “bars further litigation on the same subject.” Id. at 615. The Court emphasized that “the government may settle a claim without conceding the validity of the claim,” but that “the settlement is binding upon the parties.” Id. Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106 (1911), the Court upheld a consent decree that resolved a government antitrust action, noting that “the consent decree is not a confession of liability, but a binding agreement which the court may enforce.” Id. at 117. These cases establish that a settlement need not include an admission of fault to be binding and to resolve liability for purposes of subsequent legal claims.

The 2004 Consent Decree in this case is precisely such a binding agreement. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system—measures that constitute a full response action under CERCLA. The Decree was approved by the District Court of Guam, making it “judicially approved” within the meaning of § 113(f)(3)(B). The fact that the Decree contains a disclaimer of liability—“without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam”—does not alter its legal effect. As the Court held in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, “a party may settle a claim without conceding the validity of the claim.” 303 U.S. at 615. The disclaimer is a procedural formality, not a substantive bar to the application of § 113(f)(3)(B).

**II. The Consent Decree resolved Guam’s liability for a response action, triggering the three-year statute of limitations under § 113(g)(3).**

The statute of limitations for a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B) begins to run “on the date of the entry of the settlement.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3). The 2004 Consent Decree was entered on January 24, 2004. Guam’s complaint was filed on October 17, 2017—over thirteen years later. Even if Guam had a valid claim under § 113(f)(3)(B), it is time-barred.

This result is not unjust. The doctrine of laches, long recognized in federal courts, bars claims that are brought with unreasonable delay. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, the Court held that “a party who has settled a claim may not later reopen it, even if the settlement was not based on a finding of liability.” 303 U.S. at 615. Similarly, in *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, the Court affirmed that “a consent decree is final and conclusive upon the parties.” 221 U.S. at 117. The principle that a party cannot “re-litigate a matter settled by consent” is a cornerstone of federal jurisprudence. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 303 U.S. 610, 615 (1938); *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 117 (1911).

Guam’s attempt to circumvent the statute of limitations by invoking a § 107(a) cost-recovery claim is foreclosed by the structure of CERCLA.
