# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering a complex regulatory scheme. This Court settled the controlling test more than sixty years ago: "if Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) meets that test many times over.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). That is precisely the kind of policy and criterion to govern executive action whose *absence* doomed the statutes in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). Those decisions turned on the total absence of standards, not on the mere fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action; Section 811(h) supplies exactly the standards those statutes lacked.

The Court has consistently sustained delegations far less confined than this one when Congress faced a fast-moving regulatory problem. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix "fair and equitable" wartime prices, recognizing that the pace of change in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same is true here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new "designer drugs" faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond, which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment proceedings. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), the Court held that the "public interest, convenience, or necessity" standard was an adequate guide for licensing broadcast stations, and in *Federal Power Commission v. Hope Natural Gas Co.*, 320 U.S. 591, 600 (1944), the Court approved the similarly open-ended "just and reasonable" standard for setting natural-gas rates. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

Nor is the absence of exhaustive technical detail fatal. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a conservatorship statute containing scant express standards because the field involved standards that were already familiar and generally accepted, on which the delegatee could draw; the same is true of the pharmacological and law-enforcement criteria Congress specified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture "excessive profits" — a standard with no fixed numerical content — because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to fill in details Congress itself could not practically anticipate. The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here.

Finally, judicial review of the *constitutionality* of the delegation remains available, satisfying the concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952). Although § 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, it does not — and could not — foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself, as the court below correctly recognized. That conclusion is consistent with the longstanding principle, recognized in *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), that Congress may not transfer to another branch the legislative power vested in it by Article I — a principle that safeguards the structure of government but does not require this Court to strike down a delegation, like this one, that is accompanied by meaningful substantive standards. That structural check remains intact.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a simpler reason: Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) of Title 21 permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Controlled Substances Act to officers and employees of the Department of Justice. That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization for the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. Cf. *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (upholding delegated authority to an executive department to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted, and the delegation to the DEA was therefore proper.
