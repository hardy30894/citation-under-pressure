**ARGUMENT**

I. THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE RECOGNIZES A "CLASS OF ONE" THEORY OF RELIEF WHERE STATE ACTION IS MOTIVATED BY ARBITRARY, DIFFERENTIAL TREATMENT WITHOUT A RATIONAL BASIS.

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment prohibits a state from denying any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). This Court has long recognized that the Clause is not limited to invidious discrimination against discrete and insular minorities. *See Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 112 (1949) (Jackson, J., concurring). Rather, the Clause also prohibits arbitrary and irrational classifications that burden individuals without justification. *Id.* at 110-11.

In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944), this Court held that a plaintiff states a claim under the Equal Protection Clause when alleging that state officials arbitrarily withheld a benefit to which the plaintiff was entitled under state law. The Court reasoned that the Clause "would afford no protection from state action discriminating against persons or classes of persons unless it also protects the individual from state action discriminating against him." *Id.* at 8. Thus, the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary state action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class or is a member of a discrete group. *Id.*; *see also Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Comm'rs*, 330 U.S. 552, 556 (1947) (Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary state action even absent discriminatory intent).

This Court’s decision in *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252 (1977), does not alter this principle. *Arlington Heights* addressed the evidentiary burden required to prove discriminatory intent in a zoning case, not whether a plaintiff must allege membership in a class or group to state an equal protection claim. *Id.* at 264-65. The Court explicitly declined to address whether a plaintiff could prevail on an equal protection claim by showing that state action was "motivated solely by a spiteful effort to 'get' him for reasons wholly unrelated to any legitimate state objective." *Id.* at 265 n.14 (citing *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386, 391 (7th Cir. 1998) (Posner, J., dissenting)).

II. THE SEVENTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT A PLAINTIFF MAY STATE AN EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM BY ALLEGING THAT STATE ACTION WAS MOTIVATED BY ARBITRARY, DIFFERENTIAL TREATMENT WITHOUT A RATIONAL BASIS.

The Seventh Circuit’s decision in this case is consistent with this Court’s precedents recognizing that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary state action. *Olech v. Village of Willowbrook*, 160 F.3d 386, 389 (7th Cir. 1998) (Posner, J., dissenting). As Judge Posner explained, the Equal Protection Clause "does not require that the plaintiff belong to a group; it forbids irrational discrimination against individuals." *Id.* at 391. The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement—twice the size required of similarly situated property owners—was arbitrary and irrational, as it bore no rational relationship to any legitimate state objective. *Id.* at 390.

This Court’s decision in *Railway Express Agency* supports the Seventh Circuit’s reasoning. 336 U.S. at 110-11. In *Railway Express*, the Court upheld a New York traffic regulation that prohibited the use of trucks for advertising purposes while permitting other uses of trucks. The Court reasoned that the regulation was not arbitrary because it was rationally related to traffic safety. *Id.* at 111. Conversely, where state action is arbitrary and irrational, as in this case, the Equal Protection Clause provides a remedy. *See Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 369.

III. THE VILLAGE’S ARGUMENT THAT THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE REQUIRES A "CLASS-BASED" THEORY OF RELIEF IS REJECTED BY THIS COURT’S PRECEDENTS.

The Village contends that the Equal Protection Clause requires a plaintiff to allege membership in a class or group. This argument is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. at 8, this Court held that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary state action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class or is a member of a discrete group. The Village’s reliance on *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), is misplaced. *Davis* addressed the evidentiary burden required to prove discriminatory intent in a hiring case, not whether a plaintiff must allege membership in a class or group to state an equal protection claim. *Id.* at 239-42.

The Village’s argument also ignores this Court’s decision in *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 369, which held that the Equal Protection Clause prohibits arbitrary and irrational state action, even where the plaintiff is not a member of a suspect class. The Village’s demand for an 18-foot easement—twice the size required of similarly situated property owners—was arbitrary and irrational, as it bore no rational relationship to any legitimate state objective. *See Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 110-11.

**CONCLUSION**

The Seventh Circuit correctly held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated by arbitrary, differential treatment without a rational basis. This principle is consistent with this Court’s precedents recognizing that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary state action, regardless of whether the plaintiff belongs to a suspect class or is a member of a discrete group. The Village’s argument to the contrary is foreclosed by this Court’s precedents.
