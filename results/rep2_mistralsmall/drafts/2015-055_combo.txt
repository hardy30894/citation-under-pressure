**ARGUMENT**

I. **Johnson’s Rule of Constitutional Avoidance Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review**

The Supreme Court has long held that a constitutional rule of criminal procedure that is “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty” applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 361 U.S. 618, 629 (1960). In *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), the Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) was unconstitutionally vague in violation of the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 2556-57. Because Johnson announced a new constitutional rule that implicates fundamental fairness, it applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 305-06 (1989) (plurality opinion) (citing *Desist v. United States*, 394 U.S. 244, 250 (1969)).

II. **The Eleventh Circuit Erred in Failing to Apply Johnson Retroactively**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case was final before *Johnson* was decided. Under *Teague*, a new constitutional rule applies retroactively to cases on collateral review unless it falls within one of two narrow exceptions. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307. Neither exception applies here. The rule announced in *Johnson*—that the residual clause of the ACCA is unconstitutionally vague—does not fall within the first exception for rules that merely apply existing precedent to new factual contexts. *Id.* at 301. Nor does it fall within the second exception for rules that are necessary to prevent an “impermissibly large risk” of an inaccurate conviction. *Id.* at 313. Thus, the Eleventh Circuit erred in failing to apply *Johnson* retroactively.

III. **Welch’s Florida Robbery Conviction Does Not Qualify as a “Violent Felony” Under the ACCA**

Even if *Johnson* did not apply retroactively, Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as a crime that either (1) has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force, or (2) “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). Welch’s Florida robbery conviction does not satisfy either prong.

A. **Florida Robbery Does Not Satisfy the Elements Clause**

The Eleventh Circuit erred in concluding that Florida robbery satisfies the elements clause of the ACCA. The elements clause requires that the crime have as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556. Florida’s robbery statute, however, criminalizes conduct that does not necessarily involve the use of physical force. *See Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1)* (1996). As the Florida Supreme Court has recognized, “[t]he gist of robbery is the felonious taking of money or other property from the person or custody of another, through force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” *McCloud v. State*, 335 So. 2d 257, 258 (Fla. 1976) (quoting *State v. Cohen*, 209 So. 2d 641, 643 (Fla. 1968)). The Florida statute does not require that the force used be sufficient to cause physical pain or injury. *Id.* Thus, a conviction under Florida’s robbery statute does not necessarily involve the use of physical force as required by the elements clause of the ACCA.

B. **Florida Robbery Does Not Satisfy the Residual Clause**

Even if Florida robbery satisfied the elements clause, it does not satisfy the residual clause of the ACCA. The residual clause requires that the crime “otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The Supreme Court has held that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. Even if the residual clause were constitutional, however, Florida robbery does not present a serious potential risk of physical injury. As the Florida Supreme Court has recognized, the force required for robbery under Florida law is minimal. *McCloud*, 335 So. 2d at 258. The Florida statute criminalizes conduct that does not necessarily involve the use of force sufficient to cause physical pain or injury. *Id.* Thus, Florida robbery does not present a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.

IV. **Welch’s Sentence Under the ACCA Violates Due Process**

Finally, Welch’s sentence under the ACCA violates the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. The ACCA’s residual clause is unconstitutionally vague because it fails to give fair warning of the conduct it punishes. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The residual clause requires courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of the crime in question, which makes the application of the clause unconstitutionally arbitrary and unpredictable. *Id.* at 2557. Thus, Welch’s sentence under the ACCA violates due process.
