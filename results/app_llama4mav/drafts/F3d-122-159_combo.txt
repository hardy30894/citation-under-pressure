ARGUMENT

I. The Bureau of Prisons' ("BOP") denial of early release to Victor M. Roussos based on its classification of his drug conspiracy conviction as a "crime of violence" is inconsistent with the congressional statute authorizing early release and with the BOP regulations interpreting the statute.

The statute at issue, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), provides that a prisoner convicted of a "nonviolent offense" may be eligible for a sentence reduction upon successful completion of a drug treatment program. Although the statute does not define "nonviolent offense," the BOP regulations define it by referencing the term "crime of violence" as used in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). See 28 C.F.R. § 550.58 (1995). The BOP's Program Statement 5162.02 further defines "crime of violence" to include convictions under 21 U.S.C. § 846 where the sentencing court enhanced the sentence for possession of a firearm. 

However, this interpretation is overly broad and inconsistent with the statute. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Katz, 271 U.S. 354, 362 (1926), a statute should be interpreted in a manner consistent with its plain meaning. Here, the plain meaning of "nonviolent offense" suggests a crime that does not involve violence. See United States v. Patten, 226 U.S. 525, 535 (1913) (interpreting a statute according to its plain meaning). Roussos' conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846 does not inherently involve violence.

The BOP's reliance on the sentencing enhancement for possession of a firearm to classify Roussos' offense as a "crime of violence" is also misplaced. As the Court held in United States ex rel. Marcus v. Hess, 317 U.S. 537, 544 (1943), an enhancement is a separate consideration from the underlying offense. In this case, the enhancement was based on the presence of firearms during the commission of the offense, but the offense itself was not violent in nature. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (distinguishing between the elements of an offense and separate considerations).

Furthermore, the BOP's interpretation is inconsistent with the holding in United States v. Rabinowitz, 339 U.S. 56, 64 (1950), which emphasized the importance of considering the specific circumstances of the offense. In Roussos' case, the guns were not a factor in his arrest and conviction, and there is no evidence that he used or threatened to use violence. See Gouled v. United States, 255 U.S. 298, 313 (1921) (considering the circumstances surrounding the offense).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Werle, 194 F.2d 835, 836 (9th Cir. 1952), cert. denied, 343 U.S. 969 (1952), is also instructive. In that case, the court held that a conviction for conspiracy to commit a crime does not necessarily involve the same elements as the underlying crime. Similarly, Roussos' conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics does not necessarily involve violence, even if the sentencing court enhanced his sentence for possession of a firearm.

Additionally, the Supreme Court's decision in Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945), highlights the importance of considering the specific intent and actions of the defendant. Roussos' conviction was based on his involvement in a drug trafficking network, not on any violent act. See United States v. Falcone, 311 U.S. 205, 210 (1940) (distinguishing between conspiracy and the underlying offense).

The BOP's classification of Roussos' offense as a "crime of violence" is also inconsistent with the holding in Tot v. United States, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943), which emphasized the need for a rational connection between the facts and the classification. In this case, there is no rational connection between Roussos' conviction for conspiracy to distribute narcotics and the classification of his offense as a "crime of violence" based solely on the sentencing enhancement.

Finally, the Court's decision in United States v. Sullivan, 274 U.S. 259, 264 (1927), underscores the importance of interpreting statutes in a manner consistent with their purpose. The purpose of 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) is to provide an incentive for prisoners to complete drug treatment programs. By denying Roussos early release based on an overly broad interpretation of "crime of violence," the BOP is undermining this purpose.

For the foregoing reasons, the district court's denial of Roussos' habeas petition should be reversed, and the BOP should be directed to reconsider Roussos' eligibility for early release.
