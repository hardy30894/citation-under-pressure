**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Claims Because She Accumulated Sufficient Hours to Qualify as an Eligible Employee.**

A. Under the FMLA, an employee is eligible if she has worked “at least 1,250 hours of service” during the preceding 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). The Third Circuit has held that “[t]he burden is on the employer to show that the employee did not work the required 1,250 hours.” *Hodgens v. Gen. Dynamics Corp.*, 144 F.3d 151, 161 (1st Cir. 1998) (cited with approval in *Wascura v. Carver,* 169 F.3d 683, 686 (3d Cir. 1999)). Here, Nationwide failed to meet this burden.

B. The record shows that Erdman regularly worked extra hours, which Nationwide either paid or allowed as “comp” time. Patty Sarno, Erdman’s supervisor, acknowledged that Erdman “regularly worked extra hours outside the office” and that these hours were authorized. This evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Erdman, creates a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether she accumulated 1,250 hours. *SeeMcTernan*, 564 F.3d at 646.

C. Nationwide’s subsequent policy change prohibiting “comp” time does not retroactively erase hours already worked. The FMLA’s eligibility requirement is based on hours *actually worked*, not hours *authorized* in the future. *SeeHodgens*, 144 F.3d at 161. The Third Circuit has recognized that “[a]n employer cannot avoid FMLA liability by asserting that it later adopted a policy prohibiting the very conduct that gave rise to the employee’s eligibility.” *Wascura*, 169 F.3d at 686 (citing *Brungart v. BellSouth Telecommunications, Inc.*, 231 F.3d 791, 798 (11th Cir. 2000)).

D. The District Court’s reliance on *Hodgens* is misplaced. In *Hodgens*, the employer presented evidence that the employee had not worked the required hours, whereas here, Nationwide has not met its burden. *SeeHodgens*, 144 F.3d at 161. The mere assertion that Erdman’s hours were “unapproved” is insufficient to rebut the evidence of actual hours worked. *SeeWascura*, 169 F.3d at 686.

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s ADA “Association” Claim.**

A. The ADA prohibits discrimination against an employee “because of the known disability of an individual with whom the qualified individual is known to have a relationship or association.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(b)(4). The Third Circuit has held that this provision protects employees who care for disabled family members. *SeeCleveland v. Policy Mgmt. Sys. Corp.*, 526 U.S. 795, 803 (1999) (citing *Tyndall v. Nat’l Educ. Ctrs., Inc.*, 31 F.3d 209, 213 (4th Cir. 1994)).

B. Erdman’s undisputed care for her daughter Amber, who has Down Syndrome, brings her within the ADA’s protection. *SeeCleveland*, 526 U.S. at 803. The District Court erred in granting summary judgment on this claim, as Erdman has presented sufficient evidence to create a genuine dispute of material fact. *SeeMcTernan*, 564 F.3d at 646.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the District Court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed. Erdman has presented sufficient evidence to create genuine disputes of material fact as to both her FMLA eligibility and her ADA “association” claim. The case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
