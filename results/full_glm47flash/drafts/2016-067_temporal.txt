**ARGUMENT**

I.  Santander was a creditor collecting on its own account, not a “debt collector,” when it attempted to collect the defaulted loans.

The FDCPA distinguishes between “debt collectors” and “creditors” based on whether the collection activity is for one’s own account or for another. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). A “debt collector” is defined as one who collects debts “owed or due another.” Id. Conversely, a creditor collects debts “for its own account.” *Hertz Corp. v. Friend*, 339 U.S. 564, 568 (1950). The Supreme Court explained that an agent acts “for the principal” and not “for his own account.” Id. at 568. This distinction is the statutory framework for determining whether an entity is a debt collector or a creditor.

Here, Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial. By purchasing the debt, Santander became the creditor to whom the debt was owed. It did not act as an agent for CitiFinancial. *Miller v. Universal C.I.T. Credit Corp.*, 390 F.2d 533, 535 (2d Cir. 1968). The Second Circuit held that a creditor collecting its own debt is not a “debt collector” under the Act. Id. at 535. The court noted that the Act regulates the collection of debts owed to another, not debts owed to oneself. Id. at 535.

Because Santander collected the debts for its own account after purchasing them, it was a creditor, not a debt collector, and thus not subject to the FDCPA.
