**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The Controlled Substances Act (“CSA”) does not violate the non-delegation doctrine because Congress provided the Attorney General with an “intelligible principle” to guide his discretion in temporarily scheduling substances. *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928) (holding that Congress may delegate authority when it provides “an intelligible principle” to guide the delegatee). The CSA explicitly directs the Attorney General to consider whether a substance poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and requires that he base his decision on factors such as the substance’s history of abuse, current pattern of abuse, and risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These statutory standards are sufficiently definite to prevent arbitrary or unconstrained action. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935) (invalidating a delegation where Congress failed to provide any standards or criteria to guide the President’s discretion).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress has provided even minimal guidance. *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911) (upholding a delegation to the Secretary of Agriculture to issue regulations governing the use of public grazing lands, where the statute provided that such regulations must be “consistent with the purposes” of the enabling legislation). Similarly, in *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892), the Court upheld a delegation to the President to suspend tariff reductions if he found that foreign countries were imposing unjust discriminatory duties on American goods. The Court reasoned that the President’s discretion was guided by the statute’s requirement that he act only when he found such discrimination to exist. *Id.* at 694. The temporary scheduling authority under the CSA is no less constrained. The statute’s explicit criteria and the requirement that the Attorney General consider the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ scientific and medical evaluation ensure that his discretion is cabined by legislative standards. *See Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372-73 (1989) (upholding a delegation to the Sentencing Commission where Congress provided detailed standards and goals to guide the Commission’s work).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. In *Schechter Poultry*, the Court invalidated a delegation because the statute provided no standards whatsoever to guide the President’s approval of industry codes of fair competition. *Id.* at 530. The CSA, by contrast, provides detailed criteria and procedures that the Attorney General must follow. The temporary scheduling provision is thus far more analogous to the delegations upheld in *Field* and *Grimaud* than to the unconstrained delegation struck down in *Schechter Poultry*.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL’S DELEGATION OF AUTHORITY TO THE DEA IS AUTHORIZED BY CONGRESS.**

The CSA explicitly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the Act. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) (providing that the Attorney General “may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice”). This authority is consistent with longstanding precedent upholding subdelegation where the enabling statute expressly permits it. *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 364 (1942) (holding that where a statute expressly authorizes delegation, the head of an agency may subdelegate authority to subordinate officers). The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator is thus valid.

The Toubys’ argument that the delegation is improper because it involves the creation of criminal offenses is foreclosed by *United States v. Doremus*, 249 U.S. 86 (1919). In *Doremus*, the Court upheld a delegation to the Commissioner of Internal Revenue to determine which drugs could be sold only pursuant to a written order, with violations punishable as crimes. *Id.* at 90-91. The Court rejected the argument that the delegation was unconstitutional because it involved the creation of criminal offenses, holding that the statute provided sufficient standards to guide the Commissioner’s discretion. *Id.* at 93-94. The temporary scheduling provision of the CSA is similarly valid, as it provides explicit standards and procedures to guide the Attorney General’s discretion.

**III. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE.**

The Toubys argue that the temporary scheduling provision violates the Due Process Clause because it allows the Attorney General to criminalize conduct without sufficient notice. This argument is foreclosed by *Wichita Railroad & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Commission of Kansas*, 260 U.S. 48, 59 (1922) (holding that due process does not require that a statute provide notice of its penal provisions where the statute is otherwise constitutional). The CSA provides clear notice of the substances that may be temporarily scheduled and the criteria governing such scheduling. The temporary scheduling provision is thus consistent with due process. *See District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co.*, 346 U.S. 100, 107 (1953) (holding that a statute is not unconstitutionally vague if it provides sufficient notice of the conduct it proscribes).

**CONCLUSION**

The CSA does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General, and the Attorney General’s delegation of authority to the DEA is authorized by Congress. The judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
