**ARGUMENT**

I. THE STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION BEYOND THE REMOVAL PERIOD

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not permit the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable. *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001). The Supreme Court construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns. *Id.* at 690-91. The Court held that "once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is no longer authorized by the statute." *Id.* at 701.

This construction is compelled by the Due Process Clause, which prohibits arbitrary or indefinite detention. *See Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896) ("A statute authorizing indefinite detention without trial would raise serious constitutional questions."). The Court in *Zadvydas* recognized that indefinite detention would violate substantive due process, as it "would risk unjustifiably infringing on the liberty of aliens." 533 U.S. at 692. The statute must therefore be read to avoid this constitutional infirmity. *See id.* at 690-91.

II. THE HABEAS COURT MUST DETERMINE WHETHER DETENTION REMAINS REASONABLE

The habeas court’s task is to assess whether detention exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 704. The Court established a framework for this inquiry:

> The habeas court must ask whether the detention in question exceeds a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. It should measure reasonableness primarily in terms of the statute’s basic purpose, namely assuring the alien’s presence at the moment of removal. Thus, if removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the court should hold continued detention unreasonable and no longer authorized by statute.

*Id.* at 704 (emphasis added). The Court further explained that "what counts as the ‘reasonably foreseeable future’ conversely would have to shrink" as the period of detention grows. *Id.* at 705.

This framework ensures that detention does not become punitive or arbitrary. *See Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 545 (1952) (Jackson, J., dissenting) ("Executive imprisonment has been considered oppressive and lawless since John, at Runnymede, pledged that no free man should be imprisoned, dispossessed, or outlawed, save by the judgment of his peers or by the law of the land."). The habeas court must apply this standard to prevent indefinite detention.

III. PETITIONER’S DETENTION EXCEEDS THE REASONABLE TIME LIMITATION

Petitioner has been detained for over six years beyond the removal period, with no realistic prospect of removal. The government concedes that no country will accept him, yet continues to detain him. This indefinite detention violates the statute’s reasonable time limitation. *See Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701.

The government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. In *Mezei*, the alien was an excludable nonresident at the border, not a lawful permanent resident with ties to the United States. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 700-01. The Court distinguished *Mezei* on this basis, noting that Petitioner’s detention raises "serious constitutional concerns" that *Mezei* did not. *Id.* at 700.

Moreover, the government’s argument that Petitioner’s detention is justified by the risk of flight or danger to the community is unavailing. The statute permits detention only "pending removal," and once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention must cease. *Id.* at 701. The government cannot rely on generalized risk assessments to justify indefinite detention. *See United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987) (preventive detention must be narrowly tailored and justified by compelling circumstances).

IV. THE STATUTE’S RELEASE CONDITIONS DO NOT AUTHORIZE INDEFINITE DETENTION

The government argues that Petitioner may be released under supervised conditions, but this does not cure the constitutional infirmity of indefinite detention. The statute permits detention only for a reasonable time, and once that time has passed, release—even with conditions—is required. *Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 704. The Court explicitly rejected the government’s argument that detention could continue indefinitely so long as release conditions were imposed. *Id.* at 701-02.

The government’s reliance on 8 U.S.C. § 1226(c) (detention of criminal aliens) is similarly misplaced. That statute applies only to aliens "when the alien is released" from criminal custody, not to aliens like Petitioner who have long since completed their sentences. *See Demore v. Kim*, 538 U.S. 510, 523 (2003) (upholding § 1226(c) as a "narrow provision" limited to the post-release context).

**CONCLUSION**

The statute does not authorize Petitioner’s indefinite detention. The habeas court correctly determined that his continued detention violates the Constitution. The judgment below should be affirmed.
