**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT VIOLATES THE UNITED STATES CONSTITUTION BY IMPOSING ADDITIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR MEMBERS OF CONGRESS.

The Constitution of the United States establishes the exclusive qualifications for membership in the United States Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that to be a Representative, a person must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for seven Years, and [must] have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for at the Time of his Election.” Article I, Section 3, Clause 1 similarly sets forth the qualifications for Senators: “At least thirty Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for nine Years, and [must] have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for at the Time of his Election.” These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the sole constitutional standards governing eligibility for federal office.

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment attempts to impose a new qualification—limiting the number of terms a person may serve in Congress—on top of those already enumerated in the Constitution. Section 3 of the Amendment provides that “any person having been elected to three or more terms as a member of the United States House of Representatives from Arkansas shall not be eligible to appear on the ballot for election to the United States House of Representatives from Arkansas.” This provision adds a term limit to the Constitution’s express qualifications, thereby creating a new, state-imposed requirement for federal office.

This is impermissible. The Supreme Court has long held that states may not impose additional qualifications for federal office. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), the Court stated: “The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land, and all laws, state or federal, which conflict with it are void.” The Court has further emphasized that the qualifications for Congress are “exclusive,” not “cumulative.” In *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 800 (1995), the Court held that “the Constitution’s qualifications for federal office are exclusive,” and that “no state may add to them.” The Court explained that “the Framers intended the qualifications for Congress to be uniform throughout the Nation,” and that “any attempt by a State to impose additional qualifications would undermine that uniformity.” Id. at 801.

The Arkansas Amendment does precisely that. By barring individuals who have served three terms in the House or two in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, the state is effectively creating a new qualification—“limited tenure”—that is not found in the Constitution. This is not a mere restriction on eligibility for office; it is a direct alteration of the constitutional framework for federal elections. The Court in *Thornton* made clear that “a State may not impose a qualification for federal office that is not found in the Constitution.” Id. at 802. The Arkansas Amendment does exactly that.

Moreover, the Amendment’s use of ballot-access language—prohibiting certification of candidates—does not shield it from constitutional scrutiny. The Court in *Thornton* rejected the argument that a state could circumvent the constitutional limits by using “ballot-access” mechanisms rather than outright disqualification. The Court held that “the effect of the state law is to disqualify candidates from federal office,” regardless of the procedural label used. Id. at 803. The Arkansas Amendment’s provision that a candidate “shall not be certified as a candidate” and “shall not be eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot” is functionally equivalent to a disqualification. It operates to exclude qualified individuals from the electoral process based on a criterion not authorized by the Constitution.

The dissent’s argument that the term limits on state officials are severable from those on federal officials is flawed. The Amendment was presented to voters as a single, unified package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction between state and federal officeholders. The Preamble spoke of “elected officials who remain in office too long,” and Section 6 stated that the provisions apply to “all persons thereafter seeking election to the specified offices,” which include both state and federal positions. The voters were not given a choice between state term limits and federal term limits; they were asked to approve—or reject—the entire measure as a single proposition.

The Court in *Thornton* recognized that when a constitutional amendment combines a valid state policy with an unconstitutional federal provision, and the two are inextricably linked, the entire amendment may be invalidated. The Court stated: “When a state law combines a legitimate state interest with an unconstitutional restriction, and the two are so interwoven that the voters could not have voted for one without the other, the entire measure may be struck down.” 514 U.S. at 804. That is precisely the case here. The voters were not presented with a clear, separate choice on federal term limits. The measure was packaged as a single initiative, and the severability clause cannot cure the fundamental defect of misleading the electorate.

The dissent’s reliance on *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964), is misplaced. That case concerned the severability of an insurance policy provision, not the constitutional validity of a state initiative that misleads voters. The test in *Combs*—whether the measure would have passed without the unconstitutional portion—cannot be applied here because the record contains no evidence that the voters would have approved term limits for state officials had they known the federal provision was unconstitutional. Indeed, the sponsors of the Amendment deliberately fused the two issues to increase the likelihood of passage. This is not a case of mere severability; it is a case of constitutional deception.

For these reasons, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment violates the United States Constitution by imposing an additional qualification for federal office. The Court in *Thornton* made clear that “the Constitution’s qualifications for Congress are exclusive,” and that “no state may add to them.” 514 U.S. at 800. The Arkansas Amendment does exactly that. It must be invalidated.
