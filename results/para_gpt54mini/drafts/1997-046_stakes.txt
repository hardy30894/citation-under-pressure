Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected life or liberty interest, and the availability of a voluntary interview does not itself compel self-incrimination in violation of the Fifth Amendment.

I. No protected life or liberty interest arises from clemency proceedings.

The Due Process Clause protects interests that the Constitution or state law actually secures. But executive clemency is different. In Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that a prisoner has no constitutional entitlement to commutation and that the denial of clemency is ordinarily not a judicially reviewable deprivation of liberty. Id. at 464-67. The Court explained that “pardon and commutation decisions” have “not traditionally been the business of courts.” Id. at 464.

That rule controls here. Ohio’s clemency power is lodged in the Governor’s discretion. As the Sixth Circuit recognized below, Ohio law does not constrain that discretion with substantive standards requiring any particular outcome. A procedure that merely creates a possibility of mercy does not create a protected liberty interest. See Dumschat, 452 U.S. at 465-66; Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex, 442 U.S. 1, 7-11 (1979) (state-created expectation arises only where the statutory structure meaningfully limits official discretion).

Nor does the fact that clemency follows judicial proceedings change the analysis. The Court has recognized due process protection for procedures that are an integral part of adjudication, but only where the State has created a process that functions as part of the judicial system. Evitts v. Lucey, 469 U.S. 387, 393-405 (1985), involved a first appeal as of right, not an act of executive grace. The Court has never extended Evitts to clemency, and doing so would conflict with Dumschat’s holding that commutation decisions are not ordinarily subject to due process review. See also Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard, 523 U.S. 272, 280-85 (1998) (opinion of Rehnquist, C.J.) (rejecting a due process entitlement in clemency procedures).

At most, a condemned prisoner is entitled to the most minimal protection against wholly arbitrary state action. Woodard, 523 U.S. at 289 (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment); id. at 289-90 (Stevens, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). But Ohio’s procedure—providing notice, an opportunity to submit materials, and a voluntary interview—comfortably exceeds that floor.

II. A voluntary clemency interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compulsion, not all strategic choices. Baxter v. Palmigiano, 425 U.S. 308, 316-18 (1976), held that adverse consequences may follow from a person’s voluntary silence or testimony in a noncriminal setting without creating unconstitutional compulsion. The key inquiry is whether the State requires self-incrimination.

Ohio does not. The regulation at issue permits, but does not require, an interview. As the Sixth Circuit noted, the prisoner is free to decline the interview, and if he participates, he may say “as much or as little” as he chooses. A voluntary choice among options, one of which may be more advantageous than another, is not compulsion. See McKune v. Lile, 536 U.S. 24, 41-47 (2002) (plurality opinion) (not every difficult choice amounts to unconstitutional compulsion); id. at 48-54 (O’Connor, J., concurring in the judgment) (compulsion requires penalty of constitutional dimension).

This case is even weaker than Baxter. There, the Court considered a prison disciplinary proceeding with direct penalties. Baxter, 425 U.S. at 314-18. Here, by contrast, the interview is optional and serves only as one source of information for a clemency decision the Governor may grant or deny for any reason. No statement is compelled merely because a prisoner may choose to speak in hopes of obtaining mercy.

Petitioner’s “unconstitutional conditions” theory fares no better. The Constitution is not violated whenever the government offers a benefit conditioned on a choice whether to speak; the question is whether the condition exacts compelled self-incrimination. See Wyman v. James, 400 U.S. 309, 317-24 (1971) (no unconstitutional compulsion where participation remained voluntary); Minnesota v. Murphy, 465 U.S. 420, 427-34 (1984) (privilege against self-incrimination is violated only by compelled testimonial disclosure). Ohio imposes no penalty for silence and no sanction for refusing the interview. There is thus no Fifth Amendment violation.

In short, clemency remains an act of executive grace, not a protected entitlement, and Ohio’s voluntary interview option gives the prisoner a choice, not a compulsion. The judgment should be affirmed.
