**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered Before Its Effective Date**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) provides that the reinstatement provision applies when an alien “has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed or having departed voluntarily, under an order of removal.” Congress used the present perfect tense—“has reentered”—which ordinarily describes conduct occurring after the statute’s enactment. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 270 (1994) (noting that statutes generally apply prospectively absent clear contrary intent). The provision was added by § 305(a) of the Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 and took effect on April 1, 1997. Nothing in the text or legislative history indicates that Congress intended § 241(a)(5) to reach back and attach new legal consequences to illegal reentries that occurred years—even decades—before the statute’s enactment. Applying it to pre-enactment reentries would contradict the “presumption against retroactive legislation” that courts must apply unless Congress “has expressly prescribed the statute’s proper reach.” *Id.* at 263–64.

The Tenth Circuit held that the statute applies because petitioner “reentered illegally after having been removed.” But that reasoning ignores the temporal dimension of “has reentered.” If Congress had intended to reach all prior reentries regardless of when they occurred, it would have used language such as “any alien who ever reentered illegally after a removal order.” It did not. The presumption against retroactivity requires a “clear statement” from Congress before a statute is applied to conduct predating its enactment. *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001). No such statement exists here.

**II. Even If the Statute Applies, Its Application to Petitioner Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

If this Court concludes that § 241(a)(5) governs pre-enactment reentries, the statute must be assessed under the *Landgraf* retroactivity analysis. A statute has retroactive effect when it “impairs rights a party possessed when he acted, increases a party’s liability for past conduct, or imposes new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Section 241(a)(5) does all three.

At the time petitioner reentered the United States in 1982, the governing law permitted him to seek discretionary relief from removal, including adjustment of status. *See* former 8 U.S.C. § 1255 (1982). The new statute stripped that relief entirely for aliens who had reentered after a prior removal—no matter how long ago. Applying § 241(a)(5) here “attaches a new disability” to a completed act—petitioner’s 1982 reentry—and eliminates a benefit that he could have sought at the moment he reentered. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269. That is the “paradigmatic case of retroactivity.” *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States*, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997).

The government may argue that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting status because he reentered illegally. But this Court has recognized that even aliens who enter unlawfully may acquire important rights and expectations over time, including the ability to adjust status upon marriage to a U.S. citizen. *See St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321–22 (holding that alien who pleaded guilty to a crime before changes in immigration law had a “reasonable expectation” that relief would remain available). Petitioner married a U.S. citizen in 2001, before any removal proceedings began. The reinstatement statute foreclosed a form of relief that had been available continuously from the time of his reentry until 1997. That sudden elimination of a long-standing avenue for relief is precisely the kind of “retroactive effect” that *Landgraf* forbids absent clear congressional intent.

Finally, the government bears the burden of demonstrating that Congress clearly intended such retroactive application. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 272–73. Because the statute is silent on its temporal reach and because its retroactive consequences are severe—barring all relief for aliens who have lived in the United States for decades—application to pre-enactment reentries violates fundamental fairness and this Court’s retroactivity jurisprudence.

**CONCLUSION**

The petition for review should be granted. Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date, and even if it did, applying it to petitioner would be impermissibly retroactive.
