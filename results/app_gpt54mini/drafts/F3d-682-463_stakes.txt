**Argument**

This case presents a straightforward question under Title VII and the EL-CRA: whether a male employee who was repeatedly subjected to sexual touching, sexual remarks, and humiliating conduct can prove harassment “because of sex” even where the harasser also engaged in generalized bullying. He can. The district court’s contrary conclusion reads the record too narrowly and conflicts with controlling authority recognizing that same-sex harassment is actionable when the evidence permits an inference of sex-based animus. *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc.,* 523 U.S. 75, 79-80 (1998); *Parker v. Reema Consulting Servs., Inc.,* 915 F.3d 297, 300-02 (4th Cir. 2019); *Williams v. Gen. Motors Corp.,* 187 F.3d 553, 565-66 (6th Cir. 1999).

Under Title VII, an employee need not prove that harassment arose from romantic desire or overt hostility toward men as a class. The Supreme Court held in *Oncale* that “sex discrimination consisting of same-sex sexual harassment is actionable” so long as the plaintiff can show the conduct occurred because of sex. 523 U.S. at 79. The Court identified several ways to make that showing, including evidence that the harasser was motivated by sexual desire, that the harassment was framed in sex-specific terms, or that the harasser treated members of one sex differently. *Id.* at 80-81. This Court likewise has recognized that hostile-environment claims turn on the totality of the circumstances and may be proven by sexually charged conduct and comments that allow the factfinder to infer sex-based motivation. *Williams,* 187 F.3d at 565-66; *Hafford v. Seidner,* 183 F.3d 506, 512-13 (6th Cir. 1999).

The record here easily permits that inference. Ottobre did not merely “bully” Wasek in the abstract. He repeatedly grabbed Wasek’s buttocks, poked him in the rear with a hammer handle and then a sucker rod, and accompanied that conduct with comments such as “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” Those are not neutral workplace insults; they are explicit sexualized acts and remarks. Courts have repeatedly held that sexual touching and sexualized commentary are powerful evidence that harassment occurred because of sex. *Oncale,* 523 U.S. at 80-81; *Williams,* 187 F.3d at 565-66; *Radtke v. Everett,* 442 Mich. 368, 398-99, 501 N.W.2d 155 (1993).

The district court also erred by treating Wasek’s belief that Ottobre was bisexual as legally irrelevant. It was not. The issue is not whether Wasek could prove Ottobre’s sexual orientation with precision; it is whether the evidence as a whole supports a reasonable inference that Ottobre targeted Wasek with sexualized conduct. *Oncale* expressly rejected categorical limits on same-sex harassment claims and instructed courts to examine whether the evidence supports sex-based discrimination. 523 U.S. at 79-80. Here, the physical sexualized conduct and remarks do exactly that.

Nor does the existence of some nonsexual workplace conflict defeat Wasek’s claim. A hostile-environment plaintiff need not show that every abusive act was sexual in isolation; the question is whether sex-based conduct was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment. *Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson,* 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986); *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.,* 510 U.S. 17, 21-23 (1993). The record shows persistent conduct over time, repeated touching of intimate parts, sexually degrading remarks, and managerial indifference. A reasonable jury could find that this conduct was both severe and pervasive. *Williams,* 187 F.3d at 563-66; *Hafford,* 183 F.3d at 512-13.

Arrow Energy may argue that Wasek’s dispute with Ottobre was mutual horseplay or ordinary friction on a rough crew. But the Supreme Court has cautioned that same-sex harassment does not become nonactionable simply because the workplace is crude or because the harasser also acts out generally. *Oncale,* 523 U.S. at 80-81. The question is whether the conduct was “because of sex,” not whether the workplace was universally unpleasant. *Id.* The record here permits a finding that Ottobre’s conduct was sexually charged and targeted at Wasek in a way that went well beyond ordinary bullying.

The company’s response only reinforces liability. When Wasek complained, Tripp laughed, warned him not to “make waves,” and later Strong responded to Wasek’s report by telling him to “just kick [Ottobre’s] ass” and “duke it out.” An employer that knows of ongoing harassment and fails to take prompt corrective action is liable under Title VII and the EL-CRA. *Hafford,* 183 F.3d at 513-14; *Radtke,* 442 Mich. at 396-97. That is exactly what happened here.

The same result follows under the EL-CRA, which is construed consistently with federal Title VII precedent. *Radtke,* 442 Mich. at 378-79, 501 N.W.2d 155; *Betz v. Coachman Concepts, Inc.,* 334 Mich. App. 1, 25-27, 964 N.W.2d 862 (2020). Because the evidence supports a finding that Wasek was harassed through sexual touching, sexual comments, and managerial indifference, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
