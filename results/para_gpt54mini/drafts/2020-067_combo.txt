Respondent’s position is correct: plain-error review is not confined to the four corners of the trial transcript when the question is whether an error affected substantial rights or seriously undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Plain-error doctrine has always been a discretionary, appellate, and context-sensitive inquiry. In United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), the Court held that an appellate court may notice “[p]lain errors” affecting substantial rights even though not brought to the trial court’s attention. Atkinson recognized that the court’s obligation is not mechanical; it exists to prevent miscarriages of justice. Id. at 160. That principle was reaffirmed in Silber v. United States, 370 U.S. 717 (1962), where the Court treated plain-error review as an exceptional corrective power rather than a rigid evidentiary rule. Likewise, in United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), the Court explained that Rule 52(b) requires a showing that the error affected substantial rights and that the court should exercise discretion only when the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” Id. at 736 (quoting United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1 (1985)); but the relevant point here is the Court’s longstanding recognition that the inquiry is functional, not formalistic.

That functional approach necessarily permits consideration of reliable materials beyond the trial proof when assessing prejudice. In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court formulated the governing harmless-error inquiry by asking whether the error had a substantial and injurious effect on the judgment, a standard that requires the reviewing court to look to the full record to determine whether the conviction likely rested on the error. Id. at 765. The Court repeated that appellate judges must assess “the whole record” in Harrington v. California, 395 U.S. 250 (1969), in deciding whether an error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Id. at 254. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18 (1967), likewise made clear that harmlessness turns on whether the State can show, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the error did not contribute to the verdict. Id. at 24. Those decisions would be impossible to administer if appellate courts were limited to the evidence actually presented to the jury and barred from considering the rest of the record.

The same is true in plain-error review specifically. In United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152 (1982), the Court held that plain-error review is reserved for “particularly egregious errors,” id. at 163, and that relief depends on whether the error “has prejudicially affected the outcome of the district court proceedings,” id. at 170. Determining whether an error was outcome-determinative requires examining the record as a whole, not isolating the formal trial proof from undisputed surrounding facts. The Court’s decision in Johnson v. United States, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), reinforces that point: the Court assessed whether an omission from the jury instructions affected substantial rights by considering the trial context and the strength of the evidence, id. at 469-70, and then declined to exercise its discretion because the error did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Id. at 470. That is exactly the sort of holistic review the Eleventh Circuit performed here.

Historical practice confirms this understanding. In Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632 (1896), the Court recognized that appellate courts may notice plain error apparent from the record to prevent injustice. Id. at 658. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), likewise stressed that appellate review exists to ensure a fair trial and conviction based on competent proof. Id. at 84. United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 310 U.S. 150 (1940), also demonstrates that courts may examine the full record to determine whether asserted error mattered to the verdict. Id. at 239-40. And in Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), the Court took a record-based approach in assessing whether error prejudiced the defendant’s right to a fair trial. Id. at 67-68.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would distort plain-error review into a fiction. A court asked whether an error affected substantial rights must ask what actually happened in the case, not merely what the jury heard in a vacuum. That is particularly true where, as here, the omitted element concerns an undisputed status that is established elsewhere in the record. Under Atkinson, Kotteakos, Frady, Johnson, and the other authorities above, the Eleventh Circuit properly considered the entire record in deciding that Greer could not show prejudice or a serious threat to the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings.

The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
