ARGUMENT

The Massachusetts statute creates a content-neutral time, place, and manner restriction that satisfies the First Amendment. A regulation of speech in a traditional public forum is valid if it is “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech,” “narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest,” and “leave[s] open ample alternative channels for communication.” Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (quoting Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984)). Sidewalks remain quintessential public forums, Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983), yet the State may impose reasonable restrictions to protect access to medical facilities, Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc., 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994).

The Act is content neutral on its face. It applies equally to all speakers and does not “draw content-based distinctions on its face.” Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719 (2000). Exemptions for clinic employees and emergency personnel are based on conduct, not viewpoint. See id. at 719-20. Recent decisions addressing speaker-based or content-based laws in dissimilar contexts therefore do not control. See Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc., 564 U.S. 552, 565 (2011); Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 340 (2010).

The buffer zones are narrowly tailored to the significant interest in ensuring safe access to reproductive-health facilities. The State need not adopt the “least restrictive or least intrusive means,” Ward, 491 U.S. at 798, and may “regulate the time, place, and manner of . . . expressive activities” to protect unwilling listeners from targeted approaches, Hill, 530 U.S. at 716-17; see also Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of W. N.Y., 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997); Burson v. Freeman, 504 U.S. 191, 210-11 (1992). Fixed zones of thirty-five feet are materially indistinguishable from the zones upheld in Hill. The record confirms that the zones address documented obstruction without suppressing all expression.

Finally, the Act leaves open ample alternative channels. Petitioners may speak at normal conversational volume, display signs, distribute literature, and pray outside the zones. Frisby v. Schultz, 487 U.S. 474, 483-84 (1988). These avenues have permitted continued sidewalk counseling and have produced the very communications petitioners claim to have achieved. Because the statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation under controlling precedent, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
