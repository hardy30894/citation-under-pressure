# ARGUMENT

## I. GUAM'S SECTION 107 COST-RECOVERY ACTION REMAINS TIMELY BECAUSE THE 2004 CONSENT DECREE DID NOT RESOLVE GUAM'S CERCLA LIABILITY

### A. CERCLA's Two Remedial Provisions Serve Distinct Purposes and Must Not Be Conflated

Congress designed CERCLA to enable the "prompt cleanup of hazardous waste sites and to ensure that responsible parties foot the bill." CERCLA directs that any potentially responsible party "shall be liable" for costs associated with hazardous substance releases. 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a). Section 107 permits any party that has itself incurred cleanup costs to recover those costs from other responsible parties—full stop. Section 113(f), by contrast, was added later to address a distinct problem: allocating costs among joint tortfeasors after liability has been resolved through litigation or settlement. See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161-62 (2004) (explaining that Congress added § 113 because "CERCLA contained no provision expressly providing for a right of action for contribution").

These provisions are not interchangeable. Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies only where a party "has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action." The word "resolved" is not surplusage—it requires a genuine, substantive determination of liability, not merely any agreement touching on environmental compliance.

### B. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not "Resolve" Guam's Liability Within the Meaning of Section 113(f)(3)(B)

The Consent Decree at issue arose from a Clean Water Act enforcement action, not a CERCLA action. Critically, the Decree contains an express disclaimer: it was entered "based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." This Court should give that language its plain effect. Just as this Court has instructed that "where a statute is clear, the courts are not at liberty to construe the statute other than according to its terms," the same principle applies with equal force to contractual instruments like consent decrees—their unambiguous terms control.

The D.C. Circuit's reliance on Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996 (6th Cir. 2015), actually supports Guam: that court found no resolution of liability triggering § 113(f)(3)(B) "in part because the plaintiff had not conceded the question of its liability." Guam likewise never conceded liability—it expressly disclaimed it. The lower court's suggestion that this disclaimer is mere boilerplate that "cannot overcome the Consent Decree's substantive provisions" inverts the proper analysis: where parties have unambiguously stated they are not resolving liability, courts should not manufacture a resolution the parties disclaimed.

### C. The Consent Decree Arose Under the Clean Water Act, Not CERCLA, and Cannot Trigger CERCLA's Contribution Machinery

Section 113(f)(3)(B) contemplates settlements resolving liability "for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action"—terminology drawn from CERCLA itself. See 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24) (defining "remedial action"). The 2002 enforcement action was brought exclusively under the Clean Water Act's permitting requirements for "discharging pollutants... into waters of the United States without obtaining a permit." This Court has recognized that different environmental statutes serve different purposes and provide different remedies. See Meghrig v. KFC Western, Inc., 516 U.S. 479 (1996) (contrasting RCRA's citizen-suit remedies with CERCLA's cost-recovery provisions, noting "Congress thus demonstrated in CERCLA that it knew how to provide for the recovery of cleanup costs").

A Clean Water Act consent decree addressing unpermitted discharges is not the functional equivalent of a CERCLA settlement resolving liability for a "response action." Guam never resolved any CERCLA liability because no CERCLA claim was ever asserted against it in that proceeding.

### D. Practical and Equitable Considerations Support Guam's Position

The consequences of the D.C. Circuit's rule are severe and inequitable. Under that court's reasoning, Guam—having entered into a Clean Water Act settlement containing no CERCLA-specific liability admission—finds itself time-barred from recovering the costs of contamination that, on the well-pleaded allegations this Court must accept as true, the Navy caused through decades of disposing of "munitions and chemicals, allegedly including... DDT and Agent Orange." As the court below itself acknowledged, "the result we reach today is harsh" and leaves "the United States depositing dangerous munitions and chemicals at the Ordot Dump for decades and le[aving] Guam to foot the bill."

This colonial history matters. The United States governed Guam as a military possession—literally treating it as "the USS Guam"—for decades, constructed and operated the very dump at issue, and continued using it to dispose of military waste throughout the Korean and Vietnam Wars, all while retaining ultimate sovereign authority over the territory. See 48 U.S.C. § 1421a (Guam remains an "unincorporated territory of the United States"). Guam's unique constitutional status as a territory whose governmental structure was itself created by Congress, see Guam Organic Act, and whose courts and jurisdiction Congress has closely regulated, see Chase Manhattan Bank v. South Acres Development Co., 434 U.S. 236 (1978); Limtiaco v. Camacho, 549 U.S. 483 (2007), counsels against reading ambiguous statutory triggers to extinguish Guam's remedies against the very sovereign that created the hazard and controls Guam's governmental architecture.

### E. Even If Some Uncertainty Exists, Section 107's Six-Year Limitations Period Should Control

Where, as here, a settlement does not clearly and unambiguously resolve CERCLA liability, the party's cause of action should be governed by § 107's six-year statute of limitations for remedial actions, not § 113(f)'s three-year window. This approach honors Congress's structure: § 113(f)(3)(B) is triggered only upon genuine resolution of liability, and absent such resolution, the injured party retains its independent § 107 cause of action. Any other rule would allow parties opportunistically to invoke settlements never intended to resolve CERCLA liability, thereby stripping injured parties of remedies through interpretive sleight-of-hand rather than genuine legal resolution.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit and hold that Guam's Section 107 cost-recovery action against the United States remains timely.
