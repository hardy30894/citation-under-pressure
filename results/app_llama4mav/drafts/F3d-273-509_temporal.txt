I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC on the Grounds that the Release Bars Bancorp's Claims

The district court incorrectly concluded that the release executed by Bancorp on June 6, 1988, bars its claims against the FDIC. The language of the release does not cover the claims asserted by Bancorp in the instant action. The release only covers claims "arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985." 

The claims asserted by Bancorp, however, relate to the FDIC's actions as receiver, including its payment of post-insolvency interest to itself, its sale of Bank assets, and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate. These claims do not arise from or relate to the OCC's decision to close the Bank. As the court held in Johnson v. United States, 112 F.Supp. 345, 348 (D.Mass.1953), a release is to be construed in accordance with its plain meaning, and the plain meaning of the release here does not encompass Bancorp's claims.

Furthermore, the court in Dorchy v. Jones, 68 F.2d 863, 866 (2d Cir. 1934), held that a release should be interpreted narrowly, and that it will not be construed to bar claims that are not explicitly mentioned. Since Bancorp's claims are not explicitly mentioned in the release, they are not barred.

II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment to the FDIC on the Grounds that the Statute of Limitations Bars Bancorp's Claims

The district court also erred in concluding that New York's six-year statute of limitations bars Bancorp's claims. The statute of limitations for claims of unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste under N.Y. C.P.L.R. § 213(7) does not begin to run until the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995.

As the court held in City of New York v. New York & S.B.R. Co., 231 N.Y. 18, 23 (1921), a cause of action accrues when the plaintiff has a complete and present cause of action. In this case, Bancorp's claims did not accrue until the termination of the receivership, as it was only then that Bancorp could determine the full extent of its damages.

Moreover, the court in Matter of People (Bond & Mtge. Guar. Co.), 303 N.Y. 423, 431 (1951), held that a receivership is a trust, and that the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the termination of the trust. Since the FDIC's actions as receiver constituted a trust, the statute of limitations did not begin to run until the termination of the receivership on November 1, 1995. Therefore, Bancorp's claims, filed on October 31, 1995, are timely.
