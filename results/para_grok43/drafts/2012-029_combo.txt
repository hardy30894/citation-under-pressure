The federal courts lack exclusive jurisdiction over this malpractice action. A state-law claim falls within 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) only when federal patent law creates the cause of action or supplies a necessary element of a well-pleaded claim. See Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897); American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). The complaint here alleges only common-law negligence; the experimental-use exception appears solely as a defense to an anticipated on-sale-bar argument. Such an anticipated defense cannot confer arising-under jurisdiction. Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908); The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co., 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913).

Jurisdiction likewise fails when the federal question is merely incidental or collateral. Shoshone Mining Co. v. Rutter, 177 U.S. 505, 507-08 (1900); Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Resolution of the malpractice claim turns on whether counsel’s omission proximately caused injury under Texas tort principles, not on any construction of the Patent Act itself. See Moore v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co., 291 U.S. 205, 214-15 (1934). Even if a patent issue must be examined to decide the “case within a case,” that examination does not transform the action into one arising under federal law. Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 115 (1936).

The Constitution’s grant of patent power does not automatically federalize every suit touching patents. Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 823 (1824), requires that the federal question form an original ingredient of the claim; it does not extend to every remote or hypothetical patent question. Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 379 (1821), likewise limits federal jurisdiction to cases in which the federal right is directly and necessarily in issue. Here the patent validity question is neither.

State courts routinely adjudicate title, contract, and tort disputes that incidentally involve patents without ousting state jurisdiction. See, e.g., Pratt, 168 U.S. at 259; American Well Works, 241 U.S. at 260. Allowing federal exclusivity whenever a patent defense might arise would “disturb the balance” long maintained between state and federal courts. Gully, 299 U.S. at 117. Because the well-pleaded complaint presents no substantial, disputed question of patent law as an essential element, the state courts retain jurisdiction.
