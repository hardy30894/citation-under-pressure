ARGUMENT

The Qualifications Clauses prescribe the exclusive criteria for congressional service. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; art. I, § 3, cl. 3. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court concluded after exhaustive review of text, history, and structure that “the Constitution fixes the qualifications for membership in the Congress, and that the qualifications are exclusive.” Id. at 548. Arkansas Amendment 73 adds a new disqualification—prior service beyond specified terms—thereby enlarging the constitutional list. That addition is forbidden.

The same principle of uniformity precludes state variation. Members of Congress “partake of the same national character” as the President; they enact laws affecting the entire Union. Allowing each state to engraft its own eligibility rules would produce the “hodge-podge of qualifications” the Framers rejected. See also McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 405 (1819) (states may not “retard, impede, burden, or in any manner control” the operation of federal institutions). The Arkansas ballot-access mechanism, though framed as a restriction on certification rather than an outright bar, operates as a de facto qualification and is therefore equally invalid. See Anderson v. Celebrezze, 460 U.S. 780, 792-93 (1983) (state election rules that effectively exclude candidates must satisfy constitutional limits).

Because Section 3 is unconstitutional, the severability clause cannot rescue the remainder. The Amendment was presented to voters as a single package linking federal and state term limits; nothing in the record shows that voters would have approved the state-office provisions standing alone. See Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co., 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964). The entire Amendment therefore falls.
