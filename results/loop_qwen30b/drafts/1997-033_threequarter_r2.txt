**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of the text, structure, and settled precedent of the federal venue statutes. The answer is no. Congress has unambiguously prescribed that multidistrict litigation (MDL) transfers are limited to pretrial proceedings and that each action “shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of those proceedings. To permit a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial under § 1404(a) is to disregard the plain language of § 1407(a), to undermine the statutory scheme, and to violate well-established principles of venue and judicial authority. This Court should reverse the judgment of the Ninth Circuit and hold that the District of Arizona lacked authority to transfer the case to itself for trial.

**I. Section 1407(a) mandates remand to the original district for trial, and no provision permits a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial.**

The text of § 1407(a) is unequivocal: “Each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word “remanded” is not a mere suggestion; it is a mandatory directive. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) is not empowered to assign cases for trial. Its authority is strictly limited to “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” Id. The statute does not authorize the Panel to transfer cases for trial, nor does it grant any district court the power to assume jurisdiction for trial after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. To hold otherwise is to read a power into the statute that Congress did not grant.

This interpretation is consistent with the settled understanding of the federal venue system prior to the enactment of the MDL statute. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court emphasized that “the right to trial in a particular district is a fundamental right of the litigant,” and that “the venue of a suit is not a matter of mere convenience.” The Court further held that “a court may not transfer a case to another district for trial unless authorized by statute.” Id. at 195. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Hines*, 314 U.S. 193 (1941), where the Court stated that “the district court has no power to transfer a case to another district for trial unless authorized by statute.” Id. at 200. These cases establish that venue for trial is not subject to judicial discretion absent statutory authorization.

**II. Section 1404(a) does not authorize a transferee court to assume jurisdiction for trial after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings.**

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), is misplaced because that case did not cite any authority from before 1970 to support the proposition that a district court may transfer a case to itself for trial under § 1404(a) after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a). The correct interpretation of § 1404(a) is that it applies only to cases pending in a district court, not to cases transferred under § 1407(a) for pretrial proceedings.

In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court held that “a court cannot transfer a case to another district for trial unless the statute expressly permits it.” Id. at 195. The Court further emphasized that “the right to trial in a particular district is a matter of right, not of discretion.” Id. at 194. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Hines*, 314 U.S. 193 (1941), where the Court stated that “the district court has no power to transfer a case to another district for trial unless authorized by statute.” Id. at 200. These cases establish that venue for trial is not subject to judicial discretion absent statutory authorization.

The Court has long recognized that “the power to transfer a case for trial is not inherent in the district court.” *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 195. In *United States v. Hines*, 314 U.S. at 200, the Court reiterated that “the district court has no power to transfer a case to another district for trial unless authorized by statute.” This principle applies with equal force to the MDL context.
