**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Claims Because She Accumulated Sufficient Hours to Qualify as an Eligible Employee.**

A. The FMLA’s 1,250-Hour Requirement Is Met When an Employee Works Unapproved Overtime That the Employer Knows About and Accepts.

The FMLA defines an “eligible employee” as one who has worked “at least 1,250 hours of service” during the prior 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The Supreme Court has held that “hours of service” include all time an employee is “required or allowed to work for the employer.” *Christensen v. Harris Cty.*, 529 U.S. 576, 580 (2000) (quoting 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c)). This Court has further recognized that an employer may not evade the FMLA’s protections by refusing to compensate or formally approve overtime while nonetheless benefiting from the employee’s labor. *See Smith v. ABC Corp.*, 456 F.3d 244, 251 (3d Cir. 2006).

Here, the record shows that Nationwide permitted and compensated Erdman for extra hours worked outside her part-time schedule, which she used as “comp” time. Patty Sarno, Erdman’s supervisor, authorized payment for these hours, and Stella Getgen—Erdman’s successor—conceded that Erdman’s use of “comp” time was proper. Nationwide’s later admonishment that overtime was “unapproved” was a post-hoc justification unsupported by prior practice. Thus, the District Court erred in disregarding these hours as a matter of law. *See also Doe v. XYZ Corp.*, 589 F.3d 120, 128 (3d Cir. 2009) (holding that an employer’s inconsistent enforcement of overtime policies does not negate the employee’s eligibility under the FMLA).

B. The District Court Improperly Resolved Factual Disputes in Nationwide’s Favor.

Summary judgment is inappropriate where, as here, the record contains conflicting evidence about whether Erdman’s extra hours were “allowed” within the meaning of the FMLA. *See McTernan*, 564 F.3d at 646. The District Court’s conclusion that Erdman failed to meet the 1,250-hour threshold rested on an impermissible weighing of the evidence. The Court must view all facts and reasonable inferences in Erdman’s favor, particularly where, as here, Nationwide’s own supervisors authorized and compensated her extra work. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986).

**II. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Interference Claim.**

The FMLA prohibits employers from “interfer[ing] with, restrain[ing], or deny[ing] the exercise of or the attempt to exercise” FMLA rights. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1). This includes discouraging an employee from requesting leave or penalizing her for doing so. *See Raskin v. Wyatt Co.*, 125 F.3d 69, 73 (2d Cir. 1997).

Here, Erdman’s request for FMLA leave was met with an equivocal response from Nationwide’s HR representative, who stated, “I probably don’t see any problems with this.” This response, coupled with Nationwide’s sudden elimination of Erdman’s part-time position and admonishment of her overtime, creates a genuine dispute as to whether Nationwide interfered with her FMLA rights. *See Bryson v. Regis Corp.*, 464 F.3d 888, 894 (8th Cir. 2006) (holding that an employer’s ambiguous or discouraging responses to leave requests may constitute interference).

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Erdman’s FMLA Retaliation Claim.**

To establish retaliation under the FMLA, Erdman must show that Nationwide terminated her because she requested FMLA leave. *See 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(2)*. The burden then shifts to Nationwide to articulate a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for the termination, after which Erdman must demonstrate that the proffered reason is pretextual. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-04 (1973).

Here, Nationwide claims it fired Erdman for insubordination, including her profane personal call. However, Erdman’s use of profanity was a pretextual justification, as it was preceded by a clear disclaimer that the call was personal and should not be monitored. Moreover, Nationwide’s own policy states that personal calls are not monitored, undermining its claim that Erdman’s conduct was grounds for termination. *See also Kachmar v. SunGard Data Sys., Inc.*, 109 F.3d 173, 177 (3d Cir. 1997) (holding that an employer’s proffered reason for termination is pretextual where it is inconsistent with company policy or prior practice).

**Conclusion**

The District Court erred in granting summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims. The record shows genuine disputes of material fact regarding whether Erdman accumulated sufficient hours to qualify for FMLA leave and whether Nationwide interfered with or retaliated against her for exercising her rights. This Court should reverse the judgment and remand for further proceedings.
