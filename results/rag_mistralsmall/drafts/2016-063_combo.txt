**ARGUMENT**

**I. The D.C. Circuit erred in transferring this case to the Federal Circuit because the jurisdictional dismissal of Perry’s mixed-case appeal is reviewable in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).**

The D.C. Circuit held that *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), controls and requires transfer to the Federal Circuit because the Board dismissed Perry’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction. However, *Powell* is distinguishable and has been undermined by intervening Supreme Court precedent. The jurisdictional dismissal here arose from a mixed case—Perry alleged discrimination in connection with an adverse action appealable to the Board. When the Board dismisses a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds, judicial review lies in district court under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). *See Levers v. Anderson*, 326 U.S. 219, 223–24 (1945) (holding that where a statutory scheme provides for review in a particular court, that designation controls even when the agency dismisses on jurisdictional grounds).

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is misplaced. In *Powell*, the employee did not allege discrimination in connection with an appealable action; her claim was purely a discrimination claim without a jurisdictional predicate. Here, by contrast, Perry’s discrimination claim was inextricably intertwined with his appeal of an adverse action that the Board otherwise had jurisdiction to review. *See Local No. 207, International Ass’n of Bridge, Structural & Ornamental Iron Workers Union v. Perko*, 373 U.S. 701, 706 (1963) (explaining that where a claim arises from conduct that could be subject to Board jurisdiction, the statutory scheme governs the review mechanism).

**II. The jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case does not divest the district court of jurisdiction under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2).**

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that a jurisdictional dismissal removes the case from the mixed-case exception is contrary to the text and structure of the Civil Service Reform Act. Section 7703(b)(2) provides for district court review of cases in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and ... alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). The phrase “which [she] may appeal” refers to the type of action, not whether the Board ultimately concluded jurisdiction existed. *See Hussey v. United States*, 222 U.S. 88, 95 (1911) (explaining that jurisdiction is determined by the nature of the claim, not the agency’s erroneous dismissal).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that jurisdictional statutes must be construed to avoid rendering rights nugatory. *See Beadles v. Smyser*, 209 U.S. 393, 409 (1908) (holding that jurisdictional statutes should be interpreted to ensure that rights are not lost due to technical errors). Here, construing § 7703(b)(2) to exclude mixed cases dismissed on jurisdictional grounds would effectively nullify the discrimination claims of federal employees who settle disputes under coercion. Such a result is inconsistent with the remedial purposes of the anti-discrimination laws.

**III. The Federal Circuit lacks jurisdiction over this mixed-case appeal dismissed on jurisdictional grounds.**

The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1) is limited to cases in which the Board reaches the merits of an appealable action. Where, as here, the Board dismisses a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds, the case does not fall within the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction. *See Elgin, Joliet & Eastern Railway Co. v. Burley*, 325 U.S. 711, 722 (1945) (holding that where an agency dismisses a claim on jurisdictional grounds, the reviewing court must determine whether the claim otherwise falls within its jurisdiction).

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell* is further undermined by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Glaspell v. Northern Pacific Railroad*, 144 U.S. 211, 217 (1892), which teaches that jurisdictional dismissals do not alter the underlying nature of the claim. Here, Perry’s claim is a mixed case regardless of the Board’s jurisdictional ruling. The district court, not the Federal Circuit, is the proper forum for review.

**IV. The D.C. Circuit’s transfer order conflicts with the remedial purposes of the anti-discrimination laws.**

The anti-discrimination provisions of the Civil Service Reform Act were enacted to provide meaningful judicial review for federal employees alleging discrimination. *See Chessman v. Teets*, 354 U.S. 156, 165 (1957) (explaining that procedural safeguards must be construed to protect constitutional and statutory rights). The D.C. Circuit’s holding frustrates that purpose by channeling mixed-case appeals dismissed on jurisdictional grounds to the Federal Circuit, which lacks jurisdiction to adjudicate discrimination claims.

This Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s transfer order and remand with instructions to transfer the case to the appropriate district court for review.
