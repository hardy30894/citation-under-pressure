ARGUMENT

The district court erred in dismissing Foley’s claims for breach of the settlement agreement as a third-party beneficiary, violation of Massachusetts General Laws chapter 93A, and violation of chapter 244. Each count states a plausible claim under governing precedent, and none is preempted.

I. The complaint adequately alleges that Foley is an intended third-party beneficiary of the Pick-a-Payment settlement agreement.

Under Massachusetts law, a contract creates enforceable rights in a third party when the parties intend to confer a benefit on that party. Rae v. Air-Speed, Inc., 386 Mass. 187, 195 (1982). The settlement agreement expressly places Settlement Class B members, including Foley, first in line for HAMP consideration and then for MAP2R consideration through a prescribed waterfall. These provisions are not incidental; they impose specific servicing commitments “in order to ensure that Borrowers are appropriately considered for a MAP2R Modification in a timely manner.” The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo ignored these obligations for more than a year, proceeding instead to foreclosure. That conduct states a claim for breach. See also Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 302 (1981) (adopted in Massachusetts).

II. The same conduct states a claim under chapter 93A.

A practice is unfair or deceptive under chapter 93A when it violates “a statute, rule, regulation or common law duty” intended to protect consumers. Commonwealth v. DeCotis, 366 Mass. 234, 244 (1974). The settlement agreement imposed precisely such duties. Wells Fargo’s failure to apply the required waterfall, its repeated misrepresentations that only HAMP was available, and its initiation of foreclosure while modification review remained pending constitute the sort of “rascality” that chapter 93A condemns. See also Kattar v. Demoulas, 433 Mass. 1, 13 (2000). Foley’s allegations of resulting harm—loss of his home and emotional distress—satisfy the injury requirement. Hershenow v. Enterprise Rent-A-Car Co., 445 Mass. 790, 798 (2006).

III. Federal law does not preempt these state-law claims.

The Home Owners’ Loan Act and its implementing regulations do not displace state contract and consumer-protection statutes that merely require a lender to honor its own voluntary undertakings. See Barnett Bank of Marion Cnty., N.A. v. Nelson, 517 U.S. 25, 33 (1996) (preemption occurs only when state law “prevents or significantly interferes with” federal objectives). The settlement agreement is a privately negotiated contract; enforcing it does not impose new substantive requirements on federal savings associations. See also Fid. Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass’n v. de la Cuesta, 458 U.S. 141, 155 (1982) (preemption analysis turns on whether the state rule conflicts with federal authority). Foley’s claims therefore survive.

The judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
