The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates the conduct of "debt collectors," not "creditors." The distinction between the two is crucial in determining whether Santander Consumer USA, Inc. (Santander) is subject to the FDCPA. The petitioners argue that Santander is a "debt collector" because it acquired their defaulted debts from CitiFinancial Auto. However, this argument is flawed.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector" as a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed or due another. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The key phrase here is "owed or due another." The Supreme Court has long held that the plain meaning of a statute should be given effect. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). In this case, the plain meaning of "owed or due another" suggests that a debt collector is one who collects debts on behalf of another, not for itself.

The Fourth Circuit correctly applied this interpretation, holding that Santander was not a "debt collector" when it collected on the petitioners' debts because it was collecting for its own account, not on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. See Henson v. Santander Consumer USA, Inc., 817 F.3d 131 (4th Cir. 2016). This interpretation is consistent with the holding in Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920), which established that a clause should be read as applicable to all the words it follows.

The petitioners' argument relies heavily on the fact that Santander acquired their debts after they had defaulted. However, the FDCPA's definition of "debt collector" does not turn on the default status of the debt. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). Instead, it focuses on whether the person collecting the debt is doing so on behalf of another. The Seventh Circuit has held that a person who collects debts for itself is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA, even if it also collects debts for others. See Schlosser v. The Supreme Court has also emphasized the importance of considering the statutory context in interpreting a statute. See United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542-43 (1940). In this case, the FDCPA's definitions of "creditor" and "debt collector" are mutually exclusive, and a person can be either one or the other in a particular transaction. See FTC v. Wurzbach, 280 U.S. 396 (1930), which highlights the importance of clear statutory definitions.

Furthermore, the FDCPA's purpose is to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors. See 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). The Act is not intended to regulate creditors collecting their own debts. See id. The Fourth Circuit's interpretation is consistent with this purpose, as it limits the FDCPA's application to those who collect debts on behalf of others.

In conclusion, Santander is not a "debt collector" under the FDCPA because it was collecting on the petitioners' debts for its own account, not on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto. The Fourth Circuit's decision should be affirmed.
