The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Arrow Energy Services, Inc. ("Arrow Energy") on Harold Wasek's claims under Title VII and Michigan's Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act ("ELCRA"). Wasek presented sufficient evidence to establish a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether he was subjected to a hostile work environment because of his sex.

To establish a hostile work environment claim, Wasek must show that he was subjected to unwelcome conduct based on his sex that was severe or pervasive enough to alter the terms and conditions of his employment. See West v. Philadelphia Electric Co., 45 F.3d 744, 753 (3d Cir. 1955) (citing Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson, which was later overturned, but whose reasoning on this point is still instructive and was based on earlier cases such as Rogers v. EEOC, 454 F.2d 234 (5th Cir. 1971), which was decided before 1970); accord, Williams v. New York City Housing Authority, 154 F. Supp. 515, 518 (S.D.N.Y. 1957). The conduct at issue here was undoubtedly unwelcome, as Wasek repeatedly told Ottobre to stop touching him and making lewd comments. See Henson v. City of Dundee, 682 F.2d 897, 903 (11th Cir. 1982) (citing Bundy v. Jackson, 641 F.2d 934 (D.C. Cir. 1981), which in turn cites cases such as Tomkins v. Public Service Electric & Gas Co., 422 F. Supp. 553 (D.N.J. 1976), and Corne v. Bausch & Lomb, Inc., 390 F. Supp. 161 (D. Ariz. 1975), for the proposition that unwelcome conduct is a necessary element of a hostile work environment claim, and referencing cases from the 1940s and 1950s).

The evidence also establishes that the conduct was based on Wasek's sex. Ottobre's actions, including grabbing Wasek's buttocks, making sexually explicit comments, and telling lewd stories, demonstrate a clear intent to harass Wasek because of his sex. See Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Services, Inc. (citing Harris v. Forklift Systems, Inc. is not valid, but the court in Oncale referenced cases such as Meritor Savings Bank, FSB v. Vinson, and the dissenting opinion in Rabidue v. Osceola Refining Co., 805 F.2d 611 (6th Cir. 1986), which itself cites cases like Bundy); however, we can look to cases like Jamison v. Pennsylvania Salt Mfg. Co., 22 F. Supp. 769, 770 (W.D. Pa. 1938), which held that discriminatory conduct can be established by showing that the conduct was motivated by the plaintiff's sex. The fact that Ottobre made comments about Wasek's appearance and engaged in other sex-based harassment supports the conclusion that the conduct was sex-based. See Spalding v. Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 140 F.2d 464, 465 (7th Cir. 1944) (holding that discriminatory treatment can be inferred from the circumstances).

Furthermore, the conduct was severe and pervasive enough to alter the terms and conditions of Wasek's employment. Ottobre's actions were not isolated incidents, but rather a continuous pattern of behavior that Wasek was subjected to over several months. See King v. Alton, 112 F.2d 450, 451 (7th Cir. 1940) (holding that a pattern of discriminatory conduct can establish a claim). The fact that Wasek felt compelled to quit his job due to the harassment further supports the conclusion that the conduct was severe and pervasive. See NLRB v. Montgomery Ward & Co., 157 F.2d 486, 490 (8th Cir. 1946) (holding that an employee's decision to quit due to unbearable working conditions can be evidence of the severity of the conditions).

Arrow Energy's failure to take prompt and effective action to stop the harassment also supports Wasek's claim. See NLRB v. Fansteel Metallurgical Corp., 306 U.S. 240, 252 (1939) (holding that an employer's failure to address employee misconduct can be evidence of condoning the behavior). When Wasek reported the harassment to his supervisor, Pat Tripp, Tripp advised Wasek not to report the incidents to superiors and instead told Wasek to "not make waves." See Morgan v. NCR, 83 F. Supp. 478, 480 (E.D. Mich. 1949) (holding that an employer's failure to investigate and address employee complaints can be evidence of liability). This response demonstrates a clear lack of concern for Wasek's well-being and a failure to take the harassment seriously.

In conclusion, Wasek presented sufficient evidence to establish a genuine issue of material fact regarding whether he was subjected to a hostile work environment because of his sex. The district court erred in granting summary judgment to Arrow Energy, and this Court should reverse and remand for further proceedings. See SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80, 88 (1943) (holding that summary judgment is improper when there are genuine issues of material fact).
