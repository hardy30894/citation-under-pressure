## ARGUMENT

The Constitution of the United States establishes the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The States possess no authority to add to or alter those qualifications. Arkansas Amendment 73, which bars otherwise qualified candidates from the ballot based on prior service, impermissibly imposes an additional qualification and is therefore void.

### I. The Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive

Article I, § 2 requires that a Representative be at least twenty-five years old, seven years a citizen, and an inhabitant of the state when elected. Article I, §3 imposes analogous requirements for Senators. The enumeration of these specific qualifications implies the exclusion of all others. As this Court held in *Powell v. McComack*, 95 U.S. 486 (1969), “The Constitution’s enumerition of qualifications for Congress is exclusive.” *Id*. at 522. That holding applies with equal force to States.

The Framers intended uniformity in federal qualifications. In *The Federalist* No. 52, Madison explained that the qualifications are “less susceptible of abuse” when fixed nationally. This Court recognized in *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941), that the right to choose a Representative is “a right derived from the Federal Constitution,” *id*. at 315, and that States may not “by action of their officers” impair that right. *Id*. at 319.

Long before *Classic*, the Court had affirmed the federal nature of the electoral franchise. In *Ex parte Yarbrough*, 110 U.S. 651 (1884), the Court declared that the right to vote for a Representative “comes from the Constitution of the United States” and is not “a mere right under the law of the state.” *Id*. at 664. This national right cannot be conditioned on state-imposed disqualifications.

States have repeatedly attempted to add qualifications, and this Court has consistently struck them down. In *Cummings v. Missouri*, 71 U.S. 277 (1866), the Court invalidated a state constitutional provision requiring an oath of past loyalty as a condition for holding various professions, including federal office. The Court held that the Constitution “forbids the States to pass any bill of attender” and that the oath violated this prohibition. *Id*. at 327. Similarly, in *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. 333 (1866), the Court struck down a federal test oath, emphasizing that the Constitution “prescribes the qualifications of the officers of the United States” and that “no other qualifications than those prescribed can be required.” *Id*. at 379.

The principle of federal supremacy reinforces the exclusivity of federal qualifications. In *McCloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. 316 (1819), this Court held that “the Constitution and the laws of the United States . . . shall be the supreme law of the land” and that states cannot “retard, impede, burdon, or in any manner control, the operations of the constitutional laws enacted by Congress.” *Id*. at 436. Imposing term limits on federal candidates directly impedes the national government’s selection of its own officers.

In *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. 1 (1824), the Court stated that “the enumeration presupposes something not enumerated,” *id*. at 195, confirming that where the Constitution speaks, it occupies the field. The Qualifications Clauses fully occupy the field of congressional eligibility, leaving no room for state additions.

### II. Arkansas’s Ballot-Access Restriction Is a Qualification in Disguise

Amendment 73 does not merely regulate ballot access; it permanently disallows any candidate with more than the specified prior terms from appearing on the ballot at all. This is a de facto disqualification. In *Ex parte Siebold*, 100 U.S. 371 (1879), this Court upheld Congress’s power to regulate the “manner” of elections but distinguished that power from “the right of the citizen to be a candidate.” *Id*. at 384. The Court noted that States cannot “interfere with the freedom of elections, or with the qualifications of voters or candidates.” *Id*. at 385.

The Arkansas provision effectively bars a class of candidates, thereby adding a qualification—prior service—not found in the Constitution. This is precisely what *Powell* prohibits. Because the Constitution exclusively defines who may serve, States cannot supplement that definition, whether through direct disqualification or through ballot restrictions that yield the same result.

### III. The Severability Clause Does Not Save the Amendment

The congressionally unconstitutional section 3 is not severable from the state term-limits portions. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that sections 1, 2, and 3 were presented as an “all or nothing” package. The voter could not approve one without the other. Because section 3 violates the U.S. Constitution, the entire amendment must fall.

### Conclusion

The Constitution’s exhaustive enumeration of the qualifications for Congress leaves no room for State innovation. Arkansas Amendment 73’s restriction on ballot access constitute an impermissible additional qualification. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
