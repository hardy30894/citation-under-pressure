**ARGUMENT**

**I. The OCSLA Adopts State Law Only To Fill A Genuine Gap In Federal Law.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act’s choice-of-law provision, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), incorporates adjacent-state law only “[t]o the extent that” it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. The Ninth Circuit read “applicable” as a mere relevance test: if state law pertains to the subject matter and does not conflict with federal law, it becomes federal law on the Shelf. That reading is irreconcilable with the statute’s text, this Court’s precedent, and the Fifth Circuit’s settled construction.

The word “applicable” cannot mean merely “pertinent.” If it did, “to the extent that” would be surplusage, because every state law that “pertains” would apply in full unless separately preempted. Congress instead used “applicable” to ask a functional question: whether the state rule can coherently operate as surrogate federal law without disrupting a federal scheme. That is why this Court has consistently described the OCSLA as a gap-filling statute.

In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), state wrongful-death law applied only after the Court found that the federal Death on the High Seas Act did not reach fixed platforms. *Id.* at 365-66. In *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97 (1971), the Court applied a state limitations period because the federal compensation statute provided none. *Id.* at 105. The Court has thus never treated § 1333(a)(2)(A) as a general grant of authority to import state law whenever federal law is silent or less protective.

The Fifth Circuit’s long-standing construction is the same. In *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), the court held that state law becomes federal law on the OCS only “where necessary to fill a significant void or gap.” That principle remains the law of the circuit. *See Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Engineering, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990); *Breton Energy, L.L.C. v. Mariner Energy Res., Inc.*, 764 F.3d 394, 398 (5th Cir. 2014). The Ninth Circuit’s rejection of the gap requirement creates a direct split on the meaning of “applicable.” Its interpretation would make every state statute a candidate for federal adoption, subject only to a constitutional-style preemption analysis—a result Congress never intended.

**II. The FLSA Is A Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap For State Wage Law.**

The Fair Labor Standards Act is a detailed, comprehensive regulatory scheme governing minimum wage, overtime, recordkeeping, and child labor. It leaves no “void or gap” on the OCS that California wage and hour law could fill. The district court correctly so held.

Newton’s contrary argument rests on the FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), which allows states to set more protective wage standards. But that clause operates only where a state actually exercises jurisdiction. The OCS is not within California’s territory; California wage law reaches the Shelf only if the OCSLA adopts it. The savings clause does not, and cannot, alter the OCSLA’s gap-filling limitation. It merely disclaims preemption of state laws *within state territory*. *See Pac. Merch. Shipping Ass’n v. Aubry*, 918 F.2d 1409, 1425 (9th Cir. 1990) (FLSA savings clause applies where states have jurisdiction); *see also Cal. Div. of Labor Standards Enforcement v. Dillingham Constr., N.A., Inc.*, 519 U.S. 316, 330 (1997) (savings clauses “are not a grant of jurisdiction”). The savings clause’s existence is therefore not an invitation to borrow state law on the OCS; it is a decision by Congress to leave state law in force where the state already has authority.

Treating the savings clause as a “gap” would also create absurd results. If the FLSA’s savings clause opened the door to all state wage laws on the OCS, then every adjacent coastal state’s wage regime—including conflicting and contradictory standards—would become federal law for its portion of the Shelf. The OCSLA’s requirement that borrowed state law be “not inconsistent” with “other Federal laws” cannot be satisfied by a savings clause that merely disclaims preemption. A disclaimer is not an affirmative incorporation. *See Paul v. United States*, 371 U.S. 245, 264 (1963) (federal enclaves are governed by federal law, and state law applies only by explicit congressional adoption). *See also Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 218-19 (1986) (OCSLA does not displace federal admiralty law absent a clear congressional command).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding effectively rewrites § 1333(a)(2)(A). It would permit state law to apply whenever it is not preempted, rather than whenever it is needed to fill a gap in federal law. That is not the statute Congress wrote.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
