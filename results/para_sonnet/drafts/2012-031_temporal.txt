# ARGUMENT

## I. The District Court's Certification Order Rests on Factual Findings That Are Entitled to Deference and Are Not Clearly Erroneous

The District Court conducted a four-day evidentiary hearing, considered thirty-two expert reports, and issued an eighty-one-page opinion before concluding that Respondents had shown, by a preponderance of the evidence, a common methodology for measuring class-wide damages. Such findings may not be disturbed unless this Court is "left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed." United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co., 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). Petitioner asks this Court to substitute its own weighing of competing expert testimony for that of the trial court that heard it live. But the Gypsum standard exists precisely to prevent appellate courts from re-trying factual disputes that the district court was uniquely positioned to resolve. Where, as here, the record contains competent evidentiary support for the court's conclusion, the inquiry ends.

## II. Antitrust Law Has Long Permitted Damages to Be Proven by Reasonable Approximation Rather Than Mathematical Certainty, and That Flexibility Applies with Equal Force on a Class-wide Basis

Petitioner's central premise—that damages must be calculable with precise, individualized exactitude before a class may be certified—cannot be reconciled with this Court's antitrust jurisprudence. This Court has repeatedly held that a defendant whose own unlawful conduct created the uncertainty in measuring injury may not use that uncertainty as a shield.

In Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co., 282 U.S. 555 (1931), this Court held that "the wrongdoer is not entitled to complain that they cannot be measured with the exactness and precision that would be possible if the case, which he alone is responsible for making, were otherwise." Id. at 563. The Court explained that "the rule which precludes the recovery of uncertain damages applies to such as are not the certain result of the wrong, not to those which are the certain result, but uncertain in amount." Id.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc., 327 U.S. 251 (1946), holding that "the jury may make a just and reasonable estimate of the damage based on relevant data, and render its verdict accordingly." Id. at 264. The Court further explained that "in such circumstances juries are allowed to act on probable and inferential, as well as direct and positive, proof," id., because "the most elementary conceptions of justice and public policy require that the wrongdoer shall bear the risk of the uncertainty which his own wrong has created." Id. at 265.

This same tolerance for reasonable approximation traces to Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co., 273 U.S. 359 (1927), where the Court held that "it will be enough if the evidence show the extent of the damages as a matter of just and reasonable inference, although the result be only approximate." Id. at 379. And in Continental Ore Co. v. Union Carbide & Carbon Corp., 370 U.S. 690 (1962), the Court cautioned that antitrust plaintiffs are not held "to a standard of proof unrealistic in light of the difficulty of ascertaining the actual damages" caused by anticompetitive conduct. Id. at 702.

Most recently before the class-certification decisions below, this Court in Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc., 395 U.S. 100 (1969), reaffirmed that a plaintiff "should not be denied a recovery merely because certain elements of damage cannot be measured with the same precision as others," id. at 123, and that damages calculations may rest on reasonable inference drawn from probative evidence rather than mathematical certainty.

These precedents establish the governing framework: a damages methodology need only supply a reasonable and probative basis for approximation, not an exact individualized accounting. Dr. McClave's regression model, credited by the District Court after extensive hearings, satisfies exactly that standard. Petitioner's demand for individualized precision imposes a burden this Court's antitrust decisions have consistently rejected—one that would allow a defendant whose unified anticompetitive scheme injured an entire class to escape liability simply because it, not the plaintiffs, created the evidentiary complexity.

## III. The Uniform, Class-wide Nature of Petitioner's Anticompetitive Conduct Supports Common Proof of Impact

Petitioner's clustering scheme was not directed at individual customers but at the market structure itself—the deterrence of overbuilder competition throughout the Philadelphia region. This Court has recognized that anticompetitive schemes producing market-wide effects—rather than discrete, individualized transactions—are properly analyzed through common evidence of the scheme's operation. See Klor's, Inc. v. Broadway-Hale Stores, Inc., 359 U.S. 207, 213 (1959) (recognizing that concerted anticompetitive conduct injures "the public" through its effect on market structure, not merely through isolated individual transactions). Where, as here, the alleged injury flows from a single, market-wide course of conduct affecting all subscribers uniformly through elevated prices, common proof of impact is not merely permissible but the natural consequence of the violation alleged.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
