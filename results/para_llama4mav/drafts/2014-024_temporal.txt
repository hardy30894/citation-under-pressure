The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because the trial court's jury instruction on the standard for a "true threat" was erroneous. The instruction given to the jury stated that a statement is a true threat when a defendant intentionally makes a statement in a context or under such circumstances that a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat. However, this objective standard is inconsistent with the requirement that a defendant must have had a subjective intent to threaten.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects speech, even speech that is unpopular or disturbing, unless it falls within a narrowly defined exception. In Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), the Court held that speech may be restricted if it poses a "clear and present danger" of causing harm. However, the Court also emphasized that the mere expression of opinion or idea, without more, does not constitute a clear and present danger. Id. at 52. Similarly, in Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296 (1940), the Court held that a statute prohibiting the breach of the peace could not be applied to speech that was merely annoying or disturbing, but rather required a showing of a "clear and present danger" of violence. Id. at 308.

To convict Elonis under § 875(c), the government must prove that he transmitted a communication containing a threat to injure another person. However, the statute does not define what constitutes a "threat." In the absence of a statutory definition, the courts have developed a common law understanding of the term. As the Court held in Frohwerk v. United States, 249 U.S. 204 (1919), a companion case to Schenck, the mere expression of a desire or wish for harm to come to someone is not necessarily a threat. Id. at 206. Rather, the context and circumstances surrounding the statement must be considered to determine whether it constitutes a true threat.

The trial court's instruction to the jury, which focused on whether a reasonable person would foresee that Elonis' statements would be interpreted as threats, was insufficient to ensure that Elonis' First Amendment rights were protected. The instruction did not require the jury to find that Elonis subjectively intended to threaten the recipients of his Facebook posts. As a result, Elonis' conviction should be reversed, and the case should be remanded for a new trial with a jury instruction that requires a finding of subjective intent to threaten.
