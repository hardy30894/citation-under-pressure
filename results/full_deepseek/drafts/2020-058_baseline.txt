**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an “admission” for purposes of adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act compel the opposite conclusion.

**I. Section 1254a(f)(4) Deems TPS Recipients to Be in Lawful Nonimmigrant Status, Which Necessarily Includes Admission**

Congress expressly provided that an alien granted TPS “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This deeming provision is not merely a statement about ongoing presence; it is a legal fiction that places TPS recipients in the same position as nonimmigrants who were lawfully admitted. As the Ninth Circuit recognized, “[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.” *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017). The statutory definition of “admission” is “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). When Congress deems a TPS recipient to be in lawful nonimmigrant status, it necessarily deems that the alien has been lawfully admitted, because nonimmigrant status is predicated on admission. *See Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 554 (6th Cir. 2013) (holding that TPS recipients “are considered to have been ‘inspected and admitted’ for purposes of § 1255(a)”).

The Third Circuit’s distinction between “admission” as a physical event and “status” as a legal condition is overly formalistic. The INA itself uses “admission” in contexts that do not require a new physical entry. For example, an adjustment of status under § 1255 “generally constitutes an admission.” *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011). If a purely administrative grant of lawful permanent residence can be an admission, then a grant of TPS—which confers lawful nonimmigrant status—can likewise be an admission. The Third Circuit’s reading would render § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless for the very aliens Congress intended to benefit: those who entered without inspection. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (statutes should be construed to avoid rendering provisions superfluous).

**II. The Statutory Context Confirms That TPS Can Serve as an Admission**

Congress knows how to distinguish between admission and status when it wishes. In § 1255(k), it refers to “lawful admission” and “lawful status” as separate requirements. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(k)(2). But in § 1254a(f)(4), Congress did not merely say TPS recipients are in lawful status; it said they are “considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” The phrase “as a nonimmigrant” is critical. Nonimmigrants are defined as aliens who are “admitted” into the United States. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(15). By deeming TPS recipients to be nonimmigrants, Congress necessarily deemed them to have been admitted.

The Third Circuit relied on the fact that Congress created explicit exceptions to the admission requirement for special immigrants and visa-eligible aliens in § 1255(h) and (i), but not for TPS recipients. *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (presumption that Congress acts intentionally when it includes language in one section but omits it in another). That argument misses the point. Congress did not need to create a separate exception for TPS recipients because it already provided a deeming provision that equates TPS with lawful nonimmigrant status—and thus with admission. The exceptions in § 1255(h) and (i) apply to aliens who are not otherwise deemed admitted; TPS recipients are already deemed admitted by operation of § 1254a(f)(4).

**III. The Purpose of TPS Supports Petitioner’s Interpretation**

TPS is designed to protect nationals of countries experiencing armed conflict, environmental disasters, or other extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). The Third Circuit’s interpretation forces TPS recipients who entered illegally to choose between remaining in the United States indefinitely without a path to permanent residence or returning to dangerous home countries to seek lawful entry. That result undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. *See Ramirez*, 852 F.3d at 964 (noting that requiring TPS recipients to leave and reenter would “undermine the purpose of TPS”). Congress intended TPS to provide a stable, lawful status that allows recipients to integrate into American society, including the possibility of adjustment of status for those who meet other requirements.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on § 1254a(h)—which requires a supermajority for special legislation adjusting TPS recipients’ status—is misplaced. That provision addresses *ad hoc* legislative adjustments, not individual applications under existing law. Nothing in § 1254a(h) limits the effect of the deeming provision in § 1254a(f)(4). *See Flores*, 718 F.3d at 556 (rejecting similar argument).

**IV. The Third Circuit’s Decision Creates an Unwarranted Circuit Split**

The Third Circuit acknowledged that its holding conflicts with the Sixth and Ninth Circuits. *See Flores*, 718 F.3d 548; *Ramirez*, 852 F.3d 954. Those courts correctly recognized that § 1254a(f)(4) equates TPS with lawful nonimmigrant status, which in turn satisfies § 1255(a)’s admission requirement. The Third Circuit’s contrary reading introduces confusion and inequity: TPS recipients in the Third Circuit cannot adjust status even if they have lived in the United States for decades, while similarly situated recipients in the Sixth and Ninth Circuits can. This Court should resolve the conflict and adopt the interpretation that best effectuates Congress’s intent.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
