# ARGUMENT

## I. Due Process Requires Only That the Suit Not Offend Traditional Notions of Fair Play, Not That the Defendant’s In-State Conduct Be the Cause of the Plaintiff’s Injury.

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction has never been mechanical causation. In *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), this Court abandoned the rigid territorial formalism of *Pennoyer v. Neff*, 95 U.S. 714 (1878), in favor of a standard asking whether the defendant has such contacts with the forum that maintenance of the suit does not offend “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Int’l Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. The Court explained that jurisdiction turns on “the quality and nature” of the defendant’s activity in relation to the forum, not on a but-for chain running from that activity to the injury. *Id.* at 319. Nothing in *International Shoe* ties jurisdiction to strict causation; it ties jurisdiction to the defendant’s purposeful and systematic engagement with the state in which suit is brought.

This Court confirmed that flexible approach in *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457 (1940), holding that due process is satisfied so long as there has been a fair opportunity to defend and the state has a legitimate interest in the litigation, not by application of a rigid causal test. *Id.* at 463. And in *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352 (1927), the Court sustained Massachusetts’ jurisdiction over a nonresident motorist based on his use of the state’s highways—use that created risk to Massachusetts residents generally, not merely a causal link traceable to any single act. *Id.* at 356. The animating principle of *Hess* is precisely the one at issue here: a defendant who avails itself of a state’s roads, and the safety and economic benefits that flow from operating within that state, may be sued there for injuries arising from that activity, regardless of where the specific instrumentality of harm was first placed into commerce.

## II. Ford’s Extensive, Purposeful Activities Directed at Montana Satisfy Due Process Even Though the Explorer Was Not Originally Sold There.

Ford’s continuous and substantial course of dealing in Montana—advertising, selling, and servicing the very models at issue—readily satisfies the standard this Court has applied when a defendant systematically exploits a state’s market. In *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), the Court upheld jurisdiction over an insurer whose only contact with California was a single reinsurance contract with a forum resident. The Court emphasized that “it is sufficient for purposes of due process that the suit was based on a contract which had substantial connection with that State,” *id.* at 223, and that California had a “manifest interest in providing effective means of redress for its residents.” *Id.* at 223. Montana has the identical interest in providing a forum for its own citizen, injured on a Montana highway, against a manufacturer that has cultivated the Montana market for decades.

*Travelers Health Assn. v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), likewise sustained jurisdiction based on a defendant’s solicitation of business within the forum, holding that mail-order solicitation of policies from Virginia residents subjected the association to Virginia’s jurisdiction over disputes arising from that solicited business. *Id.* at 647. Ford’s presence in Montana is far more extensive than the correspondence at issue in *Travelers Health*: Ford maintains dealerships, advertises, and distributes replacement parts throughout the state, thereby purposefully cultivating a market for the very vehicle model that injured Ms. Gullett.

That extensive activity also satisfies the “doing business” principle recognized in *International Harvester Co. v. Kentucky*, 234 U.S. 579 (1914), where the Court held that a corporation conducting business through agents within a state renders itself subject to that state’s jurisdiction for controversies arising from business of that character. *Id.* at 587. And *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952), confirms that even a foreign corporation’s continuous and systematic activity in a forum—there, wartime management operations conducted from Ohio—can support jurisdiction, because the “course of business” conducted in the forum matters more than any single causal event. *Id.* at 445–48.

Finally, *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235 (1958), does not require a different result. There, the defendant trustee’s sole contact with Florida was the unilateral act of a third party, the settlor, who happened to move to Florida; the trustee itself had done nothing to avail itself of Florida law. *Id.* at 253. Ford’s situation is the inverse: Ford itself, not a third party, deliberately created and maintained a market for its vehicles throughout Montana, including for the Explorer model driven by Ms. Gullett. Ford’s own purposeful conduct—not the unilateral act of a stranger—connects it to the forum.

## III. Conclusion

Because Ford purposefully and continuously availed itself of the Montana market for the very type of vehicle that caused Ms. Gullett’s death, and because due process under *International Shoe* and its progeny requires only fair relatedness between forum contacts and the litigation—not strict causation—the Montana Supreme Court correctly sustained jurisdiction.
