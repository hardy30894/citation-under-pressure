**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling employees is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.

The Board’s polling standard contravenes the Act’s policy favoring employee free choice and the use of elections to test union support. *NLRB v. Friedman-Harry Marks Clothing Co.*, 310 U.S. 584, 593 (1940) (“The Board’s function is to provide a method by which the true desires of the employees may be ascertained.”). The Board’s rule permits polling only when an employer already has objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about a union’s majority status. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987) (citing *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990)). This standard renders polling superfluous, as an employer could simply withdraw recognition or seek an RM election under the same standard. *Friedman-Harry Marks Clothing Co.*, 310 U.S. at 593.

The Board’s approach conflicts with the Act’s remedial purpose. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 42 (1937) (“The fundamental principle of the Act is ‘to encourage the practice and procedure of collective bargaining and to protect the exercise by workers of full freedom of association.’”). By imposing a higher threshold for polling than for withdrawal of recognition, the Board discourages employers from using polls to verify employee sentiment, thereby undermining stability in labor relations. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Tel. Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 346 (1938) (“The Board is empowered to restrain any action which it deems inimical to the Act’s purposes.”).

II. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.

The Board erred in discounting employee statements and other objective indicia of waning union support. The Act does not require employers to prove a union’s loss of majority status with mathematical precision. *NLRB v. Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. 469, 479 (1941) (“The Board is not required to establish its case by evidence which would be admissible in a court of law.”). Here, Allentown Mack presented substantial evidence—including employee statements and a secret ballot poll showing 19 employees opposed to the union—that raised a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.

The Board improperly excluded certain employee statements from consideration. For example, the Board refused to credit statements made during job interviews where employees were told the company would remain non-union. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (“The Board may consider circumstantial evidence and draw reasonable inferences from it.”). Such statements, though made in a non-coercive context, were probative of employee sentiment. *NLRB v. Link-Belt Co.*, 311 U.S. 702, 710 (1940) (“The findings of the Board are conclusive where supported by substantial evidence.”).

The Board also erred in disregarding the poll results themselves. The poll was conducted under strict procedural safeguards, supervised by a neutral third party, and yielded a clear anti-union majority. *Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. at 479. The Board’s refusal to credit the poll undermines the Act’s goal of ascertaining true employee desires. *Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. at 42.

III. The Board’s standard is arbitrary and capricious.

The Board’s polling standard lacks a rational basis in the Act or its legislative history. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944) (“The construction of the Act by the Board is entitled to great weight.”). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling can serve a legitimate purpose in verifying employee sentiment, yet it imposes a standard that renders polling meaningless. *Virginia Elec. & Power Co.*, 314 U.S. at 479.

The Board’s approach is inconsistent with prior judicial decisions upholding employer polls based on lesser evidence. *NLRB v. Sands Mfg. Co.*, 306 U.S. 332, 344 (1939) (“The Board’s order must be enforced if supported by substantial evidence.”). The Board’s refusal to defer to its own precedent in this case is arbitrary and capricious. *Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. at 131.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s polling standard is irrational and inconsistent with the Act. The Board’s factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. The Court should reverse the Board’s order and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
