# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of § 523(a)(2)(A) Excepts From Discharge the Full Debt "Arising From" Fraud, Not Merely the Amount of Money or Property the Debtor Originally Obtained

Section 523(a)(2)(A) provides that a discharge does not extinguish "any debt . . . for money, property, services, or . . . credit, to the extent obtained by . . . false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The critical phrase "to the extent obtained by" modifies the term "debt," not the antecedent list of "money, property, services." The most natural reading of the statute therefore excepts from discharge the debt that arises from the fraud—including all liability that flows from the fraudulent conduct—not simply the value of what the debtor originally received.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that "debt" under the Bankruptcy Code is a broad term encompassing any "liability on a claim," and "claim" is in turn defined broadly to include a "right to payment, whether or not such right is reduced to judgment . . . contingent, . . . disputed, . . . [or] equitable." 11 U.S.C. § 101(5), (12); see *Johnson v. Home State Bank*, 501 U.S. 78, 83 (1991) (Congress intended by this language "to adopt the broadest available definition of 'claim'"). Once a debtor's fraud has given rise to liability, the entire liability—compensatory damages, treble damages, attorney's fees, and costs awarded on account of that fraud—constitutes a single "debt" that was "obtained by" the fraud, because it is the fraud that gave rise to the legal obligation in the first place.

The Third Circuit correctly rejected the Ninth Circuit's contrary approach in *In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), which parsed the "to the extent obtained by" language to permit discharge of any damages exceeding the amount fraudulently acquired. Pet. App. 12a. As the court of appeals recognized, that approach improperly divorces the concept of "debt" from the liability actually imposed by a court of law for the fraudulent conduct. The court instead adopted the reasoning of the Eleventh Circuit in *In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), which held that a "debt" for purposes of § 523(a)(2)(A) includes "a judgement requiring the payment of both compensatory and punitive damages." Pet. App. 13a (citing *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 679).

## II. The Bankruptcy Code's "Fresh Start" Policy Is Reserved for the "Honest but Unfortunate Debtor," Not the Fraudfeasor

This Court has long recognized that the "fresh start" afforded by discharge is not unlimited, but is calibrated to distinguish between debtors deserving of a second chance and those who are not. In *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), this Court held that the burden of proof for establishing nondischargeability under § 523(a) is only the ordinary preponderance-of-the-evidence standard—not the heightened clear-and-convincing standard—precisely because "the exceptions to discharge . . . reflect a congressional judgment that the equitable, fresh-start policies underlying the Bankruptcy Code are outweighed by the creditors' interest in recovering full payment of debts resulting from" the enumerated categories of misconduct, including fraud. *Id.* at 287. This Court explained that Congress intended the "fresh start" to be available only to the "honest but unfortunate debtor." *Id.* at 286–87.

The court of appeals properly relied on this principle, holding that "this opportunity for a completely new beginning may only be availed by the honest but unfortunate debtor," and that "a debtor liable for punitive damages because of his or her fraud should not be permitted to have that debt discharged." Pet. App. 13a (quoting *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 680) (internal quotation marks omitted). Petitioner Cohen, who was found to have committed actual fraud against his tenants by charging rents he impliedly represented as lawful when he had made no effort to ascertain what the law permitted, is not the sort of debtor Congress intended to protect through discharge. Trans. at 51–54, 70–73. His conduct falls squarely within the category of misconduct the fresh-start policy does not shield.

## III. Treating the Full Judgment as Nondischargeable Accords With the Purpose and Structure of § 523(a)(2)(A)

Congress's use of mandatory treble-damages provisions, such as the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, N.J. Stat. Ann. § 56:8-1 *et seq.*, at issue here, illustrates why an "extent obtained" limitation focused solely on the initial fraudulent gain makes little sense. As the court of appeals observed, the treble-damages award in this case was not a matter of judicial discretion but was mandated by statute for any violation of the Act—rendering it "entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations to the plaintiffs." Pet. App. 14a. In that sense, the enhanced damages are properly understood "as the debt he incurred through his conduct, rather than as punishment." *Id.*

This reasoning is consistent with the broader purpose of § 523(a)(2)(A): to ensure that a debtor's fraud victims recover the full measure of harm flowing from that fraud, undiminished by the shelter of bankruptcy. A rule permitting discharge of everything but the bare principal amount fraudulently obtained would perversely reward the *most* deceptive and injurious frauds—those that predictably trigger enhanced statutory remedies, litigation costs, and consequential damages—by allowing the defrauder to discharge the very liability created by the egregiousness of the fraud. Nothing in the text, structure, or purpose of the Bankruptcy Code supports such a result. Consistent with *Grogan*'s admonition that the fresh start is reserved for the honest debtor, and with the plain text of § 523(a)(2)(A), this Court should affirm the judgment below and hold that the entire debt arising from Cohen's fraud—including the treble damages, attorney's fees, and costs—is nondischargeable.
