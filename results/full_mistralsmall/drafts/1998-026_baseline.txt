**ARGUMENT**

**I. A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) is subject to harmless-error review.**

The Supreme Court has long held that not all procedural errors require automatic reversal. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993), the Court explained that an error is harmless unless it "affected the substantial rights" of the defendant. This principle applies with equal force to Rule 32(a)(2) violations. *See United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74, 80 (2004) (applying harmless-error review to Rule 32 violations).

The Third Circuit correctly applied this standard in affirming the district court’s denial of habeas relief. *See United States v. Peguero*, 172 F.3d 48, 52 (3d Cir. 1999). The court reasoned that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2)—ensuring defendants are informed of their appellate rights—was satisfied where Peguero was already aware of his right to appeal. *Id.* at 53. This conclusion aligns with the Supreme Court’s guidance that harmless-error review focuses on whether the error prejudiced the defendant, not merely whether it occurred. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 8 (1999).

**II. The district court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was harmless because he was aware of that right.**

The record establishes that Peguero knew of his right to appeal despite the court’s omission. The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero was aware of his appellate rights at the time of sentencing. *See Peguero v. United States*, No. 96-483, 1997 WL 713555, at *2 (E.D. Pa. Nov. 14, 1997). This factual finding is entitled to deference under *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 318 (1963), and is supported by the record.

The Third Circuit properly concluded that the Rule 32(a)(2) violation did not prejudice Peguero because he did not seek to appeal. *Peguero*, 172 F.3d at 53. The Supreme Court has recognized that a defendant’s knowledge of his rights mitigates the harm of a procedural error. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 63 (2002) (holding that a defendant’s awareness of his rights reduces the likelihood of prejudice from a Rule 11 violation). Here, Peguero’s awareness of his right to appeal—combined with his decision not to pursue one—demonstrates that the error was harmless.

**III. The harmless-error standard applies to collateral review of Rule 32(a)(2) violations.**

The Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996 (AEDPA) does not alter this analysis. AEDPA requires habeas petitioners to show that a state court’s adjudication "resulted in a decision that was contrary to, or involved an unreasonable application of, clearly established Federal law." 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)(1). The Supreme Court has held that harmless-error review is a fundamental principle of federal habeas law. *See Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 628 (1993). Thus, the Third Circuit’s application of harmless-error review to Peguero’s claim was consistent with clearly established Supreme Court precedent.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless because Peguero was aware of that right and chose not to appeal. The Third Circuit correctly applied harmless-error review, and its decision should be affirmed.
