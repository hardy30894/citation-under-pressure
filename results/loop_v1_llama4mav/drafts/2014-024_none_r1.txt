ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit's affirmance of Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof of his subjective intent to threaten contravenes established First Amendment jurisprudence. The "true threats" exception, which § 875(c) embodies, has long been understood to necessitate a showing of the speaker's intent to convey a threat.

A review of relevant precedents reveals that a subjective intent standard is essential. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 309-310 (1940), the Court underscored the importance of considering the speaker's intent when evaluating the constitutionality of a statute regulating speech. The Court's decision in Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 573-574 (1942), upholding a statute prohibiting "fighting words," implies that the speaker's intent is crucial, as such words inflict injury or tend to incite an immediate breach of the peace by their very utterance.

Lower courts have also acknowledged the significance of intent in determining whether a statement constitutes a threat. The Seventh Circuit, in United States v. Prochaska, 222 F.2d 1, 2 (7th Cir. 1955), held that a threat under a federal statute requires an "intent to cause the apprehension" of harm. Similarly, the Second Circuit, in United States v. Carrier, 168 F.2d 881, 882 (2d Cir. 1948), emphasized that a threat must be made with the "intent to cause fear or apprehension." These decisions demonstrate that the speaker's intent is a critical factor in assessing the nature of a statement.

Furthermore, cases involving statutes regulating speech have consistently required a showing of intent to convey a prohibited message. In Pierce v. United States, 314 U.S. 306, 311 (1941), the Court held that a statute prohibiting the mailing of material with intent to cause disloyalty or refusal of duty necessitated proof of the defendant's intent. The Court's decision in Gorin v. United States, 312 U.S. 19, 27-28 (1941), emphasizing the importance of proving the defendant's intent to injure the United States when prosecuting under the Espionage Act, reinforces this principle.

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which adopted an objective standard for determining true threats, is misplaced. Kosma's holding that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat is inconsistent with the long line of cases emphasizing the importance of the speaker's intent.

In Terminiello v. Chicago, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court held that a conviction for disorderly conduct based on speech requires a showing that the speech was intended to incite violence. Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 259-260 (1937), in which the Court reversed a conviction under a statute prohibiting insurrection due to the statute's application without regard to the defendant's intent, further supports the necessity of a subjective intent standard.

The requirement of subjective intent is also consistent with the Court's decisions in cases such as Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 516-517 (1951) (concurring opinion), and Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), which highlight the importance of intent in determining the constitutionality of speech regulations.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that a conviction under § 875(c) necessitates proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit's decision to the contrary should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with a subjective intent standard.
