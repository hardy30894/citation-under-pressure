The plain meaning of a statute controls its application. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) (“where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise”). Section 1254a(f)(4) directs that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” That status, once conferred, satisfies the inspection-and-admission prerequisite of § 1255(a). 

Congress is presumed to have legislated with knowledge of settled distinctions between status and entry. See United States v. Wong Kim Ark, 169 U.S. 649, 693 (1898) (recognizing that lawful presence under federal authority equates to recognized standing within the United States). The statutory command to treat TPS holders as nonimmigrants therefore supplies the requisite lawful entry after inspection. 

Where Congress has created an express exception to an admission requirement elsewhere in the same title, its omission of any such exception for TPS recipients confirms that the status itself fulfills the condition. See, e.g., Johnson v. Yellow Cab Transit Co., 321 U.S. 383, 389 (1944) (presuming deliberate inclusion or exclusion of language within a comprehensive scheme). 

The contrary reading would render the “lawful status as a nonimmigrant” clause superfluous, contrary to the rule that every word must be given effect. See, e.g., D. Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204, 208 (1932). 

Accordingly, petitioners’ receipt of TPS constitutes an admission under § 1255.
