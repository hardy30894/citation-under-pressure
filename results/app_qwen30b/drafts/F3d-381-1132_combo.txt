**ARGUMENT**

The United States Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government and denying Tuthill Ranch, Inc.’s motion for summary judgment on the ground that the government’s installation and leasing of excess fiber optic capacity across Tuthill’s property did not constitute a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The undisputed facts establish that the government has not merely used an easement for its intended purpose, but has permanently occupied Tuthill’s land with a vastly expanded infrastructure—far exceeding the scope of the original easement—and has permitted third-party commercial use of that infrastructure. This conduct constitutes a physical invasion of Tuthill’s property that is compensable under the per se rule established in *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316 (1917), and *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. 373 (1945), and is not insulated by the mere existence of an easement.

The Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause prohibits the government from taking private property for public use without just compensation. *United States v. Pewee Coal Co.*, 341 U.S. 114, 117 (1951). A “taking” occurs when the government physically occupies private land, regardless of the purpose or duration of the occupation. *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. at 324. In *Cress*, the Court held that the government’s use of a coal mine for a public project, even though the mine was not permanently destroyed, constituted a taking because the government had “occupied” the land. The Court emphasized that “the right to occupy land is a property right,” and that “the government may not occupy private land without compensation.” Id. at 324–25. Here, the government has not merely used Tuthill’s land for a limited purpose; it has permanently installed a fiber optic network that exceeds the original scope of the easement and now serves commercial telecommunications interests. The physical presence of the cables—more than double the number needed for BPA’s operations—constitutes a permanent occupation of Tuthill’s land, which is a per se taking under *Cress*.

Moreover, the government’s decision to lease unused fiber capacity to third parties does not negate the physical taking. The mere fact that the government did not directly benefit from the leased capacity does not absolve it of liability. In *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. at 377, the Court held that the government’s use of private property for a public purpose—even if the use was temporary and the property was not damaged—constituted a taking if the government “occupied” the land. The Court stated: “The fact that the use is temporary does not make it less of a taking.” Id. at 377. The same principle applies here: the government’s occupation of Tuthill’s land with excess fiber optic cables, even if temporary in theory, is a permanent physical presence that cannot be justified by future projections or third-party use.

Tuthill’s property interest is not diminished merely because the government claims the use is “necessary” under the easement. The scope of an easement does not define the limits of a taking. *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 750 (1947). In *Dickinson*, the Court rejected the argument that a government’s use of land under an easement could not constitute a taking, stating: “The fact that the government has a right to use the land under a grant does not mean that it may use it without compensation.” Id. at 750. The Court further held that “the right to use land is not the same as the right to occupy it.” Id. The government’s installation of a fiber optic network that exceeds the original scope of the easement—by a factor of more than three times the current need—constitutes an occupation beyond the scope of the original grant. The fact that the government may have projected future needs does not justify a permanent physical invasion of Tuthill’s land. *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. at 324.

The government’s argument that “excess capacity” may be needed in the future is irrelevant. The Fifth Amendment does not permit the government to occupy private land based on speculative future needs. *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. at 377. In *United States v. Cress*, the Court rejected the government’s argument that it was entitled to occupy land for future use, stating: “The government cannot take property for a purpose that is not now necessary.” 243 U.S. at 324. The government’s overbuilding of the fiber optic network—installing 36-fiber cables when only a fraction are currently needed—constitutes a present physical occupation that is not justified by current necessity.

Furthermore, the government’s leasing of unused capacity to for-profit telecommunications companies transforms the use of Tuthill’s land into a commercial enterprise. This is not a use “in connection with” the transmission of power, as required by the easement. *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. at 750. The Court in *Dickinson* held that “the government may not use private property for purposes not contemplated by the grant.” Id. at 750. Here, the government has used Tuthill’s land to generate revenue for third parties, a use that is neither necessary nor incidental to the transmission of power. This commercial exploitation of Tuthill’s land constitutes a physical occupation that is compensable under the per se rule.

Finally, the Court of Federal Claims erred in dismissing Tuthill’s claim on the ground that “the scope of the easement is not relevant.” Tuthill, slip op. at 6. The scope of the easement is directly relevant to whether the government’s actions exceed the terms of the grant and thus constitute a taking. *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. at 324. The government’s overbuilding and third-party leasing are not “necessary” or “in connection with” the transmission of power. They are, instead, a permanent physical occupation of Tuthill’s land for commercial purposes.

For these reasons, the government’s actions constitute a per se physical taking under the Fifth Amendment. The Court of Federal Claims erred in granting summary judgment to the government. The judgment must be reversed, and the case remanded
