**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution prohibits “cruel and unusual punishments.” In *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238, 278 (1972) (Black, J., concurring), the Court recognized that the Amendment’s prohibition is not static but evolves in response to “the progress of a maturing society.” This principle has long informed the Court’s interpretation of the Eighth Amendment, particularly in the context of capital punishment. In *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958), the Court emphasized that the Amendment must be construed “in the light of our evolving standards of decency,” a standard that reflects “the progress of a maturing society.” The Court has consistently held that the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is not confined to the practices of the 18th century but must be interpreted in light of contemporary values and understandings.

This evolving standard of decency has been applied to the death penalty in a series of landmark decisions. In *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 188 (1976), the Court reaffirmed that the death penalty is not per se unconstitutional, but it must be administered in a manner that is consistent with the Eighth Amendment’s requirements. The Court has since refined those requirements, holding that the death penalty must not be imposed in a manner that is arbitrary, capricious, or disproportionate. In *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977), the Court struck down the death penalty for the crime of rape, concluding that such a punishment was “grossly disproportionate” to the offense. Similarly, in *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407, 417 (2008), the Court held that the death penalty for the non-homicide rape of a child violated the Eighth Amendment, stating that “the death penalty is reserved for the most serious crimes—those involving the taking of human life.”

The Court has also recognized that the Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of individuals whose mental capacity is so diminished that they lack the moral culpability required for capital punishment. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 328 (1989), the Court acknowledged that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment must be interpreted in light of the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” The Court held that a jury must be able to consider the mitigating evidence of a defendant’s mental retardation in a capital sentencing proceeding. However, the Court did not hold that such individuals are categorically exempt from the death penalty. Instead, it required that the jury be given a meaningful opportunity to consider the mitigating effect of mental retardation.

This case presents a more fundamental question: whether the execution of individuals with intellectual disabilities—those who are mentally retarded—violates the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The answer, grounded in the evolving standards of decency, is yes.

The United States Supreme Court has long recognized that individuals with intellectual disabilities possess diminished moral culpability. In *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 409 (1986), the Court held that the execution of a person who is insane violates the Eighth Amendment. The Court reasoned that “the execution of a person who is insane is cruel and unusual punishment because such a person lacks the moral responsibility necessary for punishment.” The same principle applies to individuals with intellectual disabilities. As the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities (AAIDD) has stated, individuals with intellectual disabilities “have significantly reduced cognitive and adaptive functioning,” which impairs their ability to understand the consequences of their actions, to conform their behavior to societal norms, and to participate meaningfully in the legal process. This diminished capacity is not a mere matter of degree but a fundamental difference in moral responsibility.

Moreover, the national consensus against executing individuals with intellectual disabilities is overwhelming. As of the time of this argument, 38 states and the District of Columbia have enacted laws prohibiting the execution of individuals with intellectual disabilities. The remaining states have either adopted similar prohibitions through judicial decision or have not carried out such executions in practice. The American Bar Association, the American Psychiatric Association, and the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers have all issued formal positions opposing the execution of individuals with intellectual disabilities. This widespread consensus reflects a national understanding that such executions are inconsistent with the principles of justice and human dignity.

The Court has previously recognized that the Eighth Amendment must be interpreted in light of “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. at 101. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311 (2002), the Court held that the execution of individuals with intellectual disabilities constitutes cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment. The Court concluded that “the execution of mentally retarded persons is grossly disproportionate and excessive punishment” and that such executions “violate the basic concept of human dignity.” The Court emphasized that “the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment must be interpreted in light of the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” Id. at 311.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied this precedent in affirming Atkins’ death sentence. The Court recognized that *Penry* required the jury to consider mitigating evidence of mental retardation, but it did not require a categorical exemption from the death penalty. However, the Court’s reliance on *Penry* was misplaced in light of the subsequent decision in *Atkins*. The Virginia Supreme Court’s decision to affirm the death sentence in the face of *Atkins* was erroneous and must be reversed.

For the foregoing reasons, the execution of Daryl Renard Atkins, a man with intellectual disabilities, violates the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s holding in *Atkins v. Virginia*.
