# ARGUMENT

## I. The Act Is a Valid, Content-Neutral Regulation of the Time, Place, and Manner of Speech on Public Ways.

Petitioners do not dispute that the Act applies equally to every speaker, regardless of viewpoint, within a fixed thirty-five-foot radius of clinic entrances. That neutrality is decisive. This Court has long recognized that "[c]ivil liberties, as guaranteed by the Constitution, imply the existence of an organized society maintaining public order without which liberty itself would be lost in the excesses of unrestrained abuses." *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941). *Cox* upheld a licensing scheme for parades on public streets precisely because it "d[id] not entrust... officials with arbitrary power" and served only to coordinate the "time, place and manner" of assembly with competing public needs. *Id.* at 576. The Act does no more.

It is true that streets and sidewalks occupy a special place in First Amendment jurisprudence. As Justice Roberts explained in *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496 (1939), such places "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public and, time out of mind, have been used for purposes of assembly, communicating thoughts between citizens, and discussing public questions." *Id.* at 515. But *Hague* did not hold that this privilege was unbounded; rather, it recognized that the use of public ways "must be exercised in subordination to the general comity and convenience of the public in the use of streets." *Id.* A buffer zone of thirty-five feet, applicable to speakers of every persuasion, is exactly the kind of subordination *Hague* contemplated.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), sustaining a municipal ordinance conditioning public-park assemblies on a license, because the ordinance was "nothing more than a reasonable, non-discriminatory" regulation of the use of public property, not a suppression of speech. *Id.* at 408. Likewise, in *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), the Court upheld an ordinance banning sound trucks that emitted "loud and raucous noises" on public streets, explaining that municipalities retain broad authority to regulate the manner in which speech is delivered in public spaces so long as ample channels for communication remain. *Id.* at 82, 86-87. The buffer zone here is far less restrictive than the outright ban on a mode of communication sustained in *Kovacs*: petitioners may still speak, sing, pray, hold signs, and distribute literature—merely from a short distance away.

## II. The Commonwealth's Interest in Ensuring Unobstructed Access to Medical Facilities Justifies the Modest Restriction Imposed.

This Court has consistently sustained restrictions on speech-related conduct near sensitive locations when the State's interest in maintaining unimpeded access and order is substantial. In *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536 (1965), the Court upheld a statute forbidding picketing "in or near" a courthouse with the intent of influencing judicial proceedings, holding that the statute regulated only "the place and manner" of the demonstration and left "an alternative channel" of expression open. *Id.* at 558. The Court recognized that a State may protect the integrity and functioning of an institution from the disruptive effects of congregated protest without offending the First Amendment. The interest Massachusetts asserts here—unobstructed ingress and egress to reproductive healthcare facilities, and protection of patients and staff from congestion, harassment, and confrontation at the clinic door—is at least as substantial as the interest in unimpeded access to a courthouse.

*Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), confirms that the State's authority to manage access to property used for particular ends is not diminished merely because the objecting parties wish to communicate a message there. As the Court put it, "[t]he State, no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated." *Id.* at 47. Massachusetts has not closed the sidewalks to petitioners; it has drawn a narrow, marked zone around clinic entrances so that the facilities can serve their lawful medical purpose without obstruction—precisely the sort of "regulation in the interest of all" contemplated by *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 304-05 (1940), which distinguished evenhanded regulations protecting "the peace, good order, and comfort of the community" from impermissible licensing schemes vesting officials with unbridled discretion over content.

## III. The Act's Neutrality Distinguishes It from Regulations This Court Has Struck Down.

Unlike the ordinances invalidated in *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), and *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88 (1940), the Act does not target the content of speech or vest officials with discretion to discriminate among messages. *Schneider* struck down anti-littering ordinances because they swept up all handbill distribution without regard to a legitimate, narrowly drawn interest, while *Thornhill* invalidated a statute that criminalized picketing based on its communicative purpose. Massachusetts, by contrast, restricts a small zone of ground regardless of what is said there. That the petitioners' message is pro-life is irrelevant to the Act's operation—the same buffer applies to clinic escorts, singers, and silent observers alike.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
