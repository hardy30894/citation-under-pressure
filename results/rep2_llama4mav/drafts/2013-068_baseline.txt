The Massachusetts statute creating a 35-foot buffer zone around abortion clinics is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation. As the First Circuit correctly held, the law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve significant government interests, and leaves open adequate alternative channels of communication.

The buffer zone is a reasonable measure to ensure public safety and facilitate access to healthcare facilities. The Supreme Court has previously upheld a similar Colorado statute in Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which prohibited certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances. The Court in Hill recognized that sidewalks and areas outside healthcare clinics are "quintessential public forums" but held that the buffer zone was a valid time, place, and manner regulation.

The petitioners argue that the law is not content-neutral because it disproportionately affects their "pro-life" message. However, the First Circuit correctly applied the law of the case doctrine, which precludes relitigation of issues already decided in McCullen I. The petitioners' reliance on recent Supreme Court decisions, such as Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), is misplaced, as those cases addressed different factual scenarios and did not undermine the Hill decision.

The buffer zone is narrowly tailored to serve the significant government interests in public safety and access to healthcare facilities. The Massachusetts legislature enacted the law to address problems of congestion, intimidation, and harassment outside abortion clinics. The law exempts certain individuals, such as patients, employees, and law enforcement officers, and allows for alternative means of communication, including oral speech, distribution of literature, and displays of signage.

The petitioners claim that the buffer zone prevents them from effectively communicating with their intended audience. However, the First Circuit correctly found that adequate alternative channels of communication remain available, including oral speech of varying degrees of volume and amplification, distribution of literature, and displays of signage and symbols. The buffer zone does not render the petitioners' speech ineffective, as they can still engage with patients and distribute literature outside the buffer zone.

The Attorney General's guidance letter, which provides interpretation of the law, does not render the statute unconstitutional. The First Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the petitioners leave to amend their complaint to challenge the letter, as the petitioners had ample time to raise the issue and failed to do so in a timely manner.

In conclusion, the Massachusetts statute is a constitutional time, place, and manner regulation that serves significant government interests in public safety and access to healthcare facilities. The First Circuit correctly upheld the law, and its decision should be affirmed. The Supreme Court's ruling in Hill v. Colorado remains controlling, and there is no need to limit or overrule it.
