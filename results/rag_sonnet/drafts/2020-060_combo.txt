# ARGUMENT

## I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not "Resolve" Guam's Liability Within the Meaning of CERCLA Section 113(f)(3)(B), and Guam's Section 107 Action Therefore Remains Timely.

A settlement's legal effect must be measured by what its text actually accomplished, not by what a later litigant wishes it had accomplished. This Court has long insisted that the scope of an agreement between a sovereign and another party is defined by its actual terms, not by implication. In *Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), the Court refused to read into a cession agreement any grant of authority beyond what the parties expressly bargained for, explaining that "[n]othing remained to the United States, according to the terms of the agreement, but the public lands." *Id.* at 224. The same interpretive discipline governs here. The 2004 Consent Decree was explicit that it was entered "without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." That express reservation is the functional equivalent of the narrow grant at issue in *Pollard's Lessee*—it limits what passed under the instrument to precisely what the parties wrote, and no more.

This Court has likewise cautioned that documents drafted by the government itself should be construed according to their operative language, expansively where the language commands expansiveness and narrowly where it does not. In *Kern-Limerick, Inc. v. Scurlock*, 347 U.S. 110 (1954), the Court gave full effect to contract language directing that certain terms "be construed in the broadest manner possible" precisely because the parties' own text commanded that result. *Id.* at 113. Here, the opposite textual command controls: the Consent Decree's disclaimer of liability commands a narrow construction, one that forecloses treating a decree requiring closure and a civil penalty as an adjudication—or even a voluntary resolution—of Guam's ultimate liability for the Navy's contamination. Because the decree did not resolve liability, it did not trigger the three-year contribution clock, and Guam's cost-recovery claim survives.

## II. The Historical Relationship Between the Navy and Guam Confirms that Responsibility for the Ordot Dump Cannot Be Shifted to Guam by Default.

Guam's status is not that of an ordinary landowner who happened to inherit a contaminated site from a private predecessor. For nearly half a century, the United States exercised exclusive sovereign and military dominion over the island. As this Court recognized in construing the very treaty by which the United States acquired Guam, the occupying power "will . . . assume and discharge the obligations that may under international law result . . . from the fact of its occupation, for the protection of life and property." *Pearcy v. Stranahan*, 205 U.S. 257, 259 (1907). The Navy's construction and continuous operation of an unlined, uncapped landfill—used to dispose of munitions and chemicals through two subsequent wars—occurred entirely within that zone of exclusive federal responsibility.

That the United States later transferred civil governance to Guam through the Organic Act of 1950 does not erase obligations the Navy incurred while it alone controlled the island. This Court has held that a change in sovereignty does not, by itself, extinguish the legal consequences of the predecessor sovereign's conduct; rather, "that great body of . . . law which regulates private and domestic rights continues in force until abrogated or changed by the new ruler." *Vilas v. City of Manila*, 220 U.S. 345, 357 (1911). If anything, that principle cuts against the Navy here: the obligations attached to its decades-long operation of the Ordot Dump did not simply evaporate because Guam's government assumed nominal civilian authority in 1950 while the Navy kept using the site.

This Court has also recognized that territories governed under military rule occupy a distinct constitutional posture that ordinary civil-military assumptions do not neatly capture. *See Duncan v. Kahanamoku*, 327 U.S. 304, 324 (1946) (protecting the rights of individuals "not connected with the armed forces to have their guilt or innocence determined in courts of law which provide established procedural safeguards, rather than by military tribunals"); *Gonzales v. Williams*, 192 U.S. 1 (1904) (describing the extensive federal administrative and judicial apparatus imposed on Guam's sister territory following cession from Spain). And this Court has warned against permitting military prerogatives to override statutory or constitutional protections without a genuine showing of necessity: "the statutory rights of citizens are not to be readily cut down on pleas of military necessity, especially pleas" divorced from any actual operational requirement. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 331 U.S. 416, 424–25 (1947). Because the relationship between civilian and military authority in a territory like Guam is, in this Court's words describing an analogous civil-military interface, "sui generis," *Burns v. Wilson*, 346 U.S. 137, 140 (1953), courts should be especially reluctant to let a technical construction of a settlement's caption dictate which sovereign bears the cost of a hazard the Navy alone created.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
