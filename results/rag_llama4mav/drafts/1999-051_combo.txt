The question presented is whether a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The respondent argues that it cannot.

The doctrine of procedural default is a well-established principle in habeas corpus law, which holds that a federal court will not review a state court's decision if the petitioner failed to comply with a state procedural rule. See Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200 (1950). In this case, Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was procedurally defaulted because his application to reopen his direct appeal was dismissed as untimely.

Carpenter attempts to excuse this default by alleging that his appellate counsel was constitutionally ineffective in not raising the sufficiency claim on direct appeal. However, this ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim is itself procedurally defaulted. The respondent argues that a procedurally defaulted claim cannot serve as cause to excuse another procedural default.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a habeas corpus petitioner must exhaust all available state remedies before seeking federal relief. See Price v. Johnston, 334 U.S. 266 (1948). This includes raising all claims, including those related to ineffective assistance of counsel, in a timely manner. See Holiday v. Johnston, 313 U.S. 342 (1941). Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse another procedural default would undermine the exhaustion doctrine and create an exception that would swallow the rule.

Furthermore, the Court has emphasized the importance of comity and finality in habeas corpus proceedings. See Long v. District Court of Iowa, Lee Cty., 385 U.S. 192 (1966). Permitting a procedurally defaulted claim to excuse another default would disregard the state court's judgment and create an unnecessary tension between federal and state courts.

In United States Ex Rel. McCann v. Adams, 320 U.S. 220 (1943), the Court held that a habeas corpus petitioner must be given an opportunity to establish a claim that was not previously adjudicated on its merits. However, this does not mean that a procedurally defaulted claim can be used to excuse another default. In fact, the Court has consistently held that a petitioner's failure to comply with state procedural rules can bar federal habeas corpus review. See Newsom v. Smyth, 365 U.S. 604 (1961); Walker v. Wainwright, 390 U.S. 335 (1968).

In Peyton v. Rowe, 391 U.S. 54 (1968), the Court expanded the scope of habeas corpus review, but did not address the issue of procedurally defaulted claims. However, the Court's decision in Nowakowski v. Maroney, 386 U.S. 542 (1967), suggests that a habeas corpus petitioner must demonstrate that their claims were properly presented to the state courts before seeking federal relief.

Finally, the Court's decision in Smith v. Bennett, 365 U.S. 708 (1961), emphasizes the importance of providing a state prisoner with a meaningful opportunity to present their claims to the state courts. Carpenter had such an opportunity, but failed to comply with state procedural rules.

In conclusion, a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim. The doctrine of procedural default, the exhaustion doctrine, and the principles of comity and finality all support this conclusion. The Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and uphold the procedural default of Carpenter's sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.
