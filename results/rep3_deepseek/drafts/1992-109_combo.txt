**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The proper constitutional standard is the one this Court has consistently applied to all competency determinations: whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This standard governs competency to stand trial, to plead guilty, and to waive counsel alike.

**I. The Dusky Standard Applies to All Competency Determinations**

This Court has never distinguished between competency to stand trial and competency to waive constitutional rights. In *Dusky*, the Court articulated a single test for competency: whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings.” *Id.* That test was reaffirmed in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 386 (1966), which held that a trial court must conduct a competency hearing whenever it has a “bona fide doubt” as to the defendant’s competence. *Pate* did not suggest a higher standard for waivers. *See also Bishop v. United States*, 350 U.S. 961, 961 (1956) (per curiam) (remanding for competency determination under *Dusky* standard).

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” requirement finds no support in this Court’s pre-1970 decisions. In *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court noted that the issue of competency to waive counsel “may be separate” from competency to stand trial, but it did not articulate a different standard. *Westbrook* simply remanded for a hearing; it did not impose a heightened mental-capacity requirement. *See also Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105, 108 (1954) (holding that a defendant who is “insane” cannot waive counsel, but applying the same basic competency inquiry).

**II. The Knowing and Intelligent Waiver Requirement Is Distinct from Competency**

The Ninth Circuit conflated the competency inquiry with the separate requirement that a waiver of constitutional rights be “knowing and intelligent.” *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”). That requirement addresses the voluntariness and understanding of the waiver, not the defendant’s mental capacity. *See Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942) (waiver of jury trial valid if defendant “knows what he is doing”). A defendant may be competent under *Dusky* yet still make an unknowing waiver; conversely, a competent defendant may knowingly waive rights even if his choices seem unwise. *See Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173, 175 (1946) (defendant’s “choice of a bad defense” does not render waiver invalid).

The Court has applied the same competency standard to guilty pleas. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242 (1969), the Court required that a guilty plea be “intelligent and voluntary,” but it did not impose a higher competency threshold. *See also McCarthy v. United States*, 394 U.S. 459, 466 (1969) (plea must be “voluntary and intelligent” under the *Dusky* standard). The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” test improperly elevates the competency inquiry into a substantive review of the wisdom of the defendant’s decision.

**III. The Trial Court Properly Applied the Dusky Standard**

The record shows that the trial court had no “bona fide doubt” about Moran’s competency. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 385. Two court-appointed psychiatrists had examined Moran and found him competent to stand trial. Their reports—which the trial court reviewed—indicated that Moran understood the charges, could assist counsel, and was aware of the penalties. *See Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. The fact that Moran was taking medication does not, without more, create a good faith doubt. *See Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 313 (1966) (per curiam) (competency determination may consider medication effects, but no heightened standard). The trial court’s colloquy with Moran, though brief, confirmed his understanding of his rights. *See Johnson*, 304 U.S. at 464.

**IV. The State Post-Conviction Finding Was Correct Under the Proper Standard**

The state court’s post-conviction finding that Moran was competent to waive counsel and plead guilty is entitled to deference. *See 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d)*. The court relied on the psychiatric evaluations, which satisfied the *Dusky* standard. The Ninth Circuit erred by requiring the state court to apply a “reasoned choice” test that this Court has never adopted. Because the state court applied the correct constitutional standard, its factual findings are presumptively correct.

**CONCLUSION**

The Due Process Clause does not require a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. The *Dusky* standard governs all competency determinations. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
