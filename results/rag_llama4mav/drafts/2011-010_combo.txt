The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) extends workers' compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act (LHWCA) to employees injured "as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources of the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain language of the statute requires that the operations be conducted on the outer Continental Shelf, but it does not explicitly state that the injury must also occur on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Supreme Court has provided guidance on the interpretation of the OCSLA in several cases. In Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court noted that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed." Id. at 219. Although this statement is dictum, it suggests that the location of the operations is the primary factor in determining OCSLA coverage.

The Third Circuit has held that the OCSLA does not require a situs-of-injury test, instead applying a "but for" test to determine whether the injury occurred "as the result of" operations on the outer Continental Shelf. Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988). This interpretation is supported by the language of the statute, which focuses on the causal connection between the operations on the outer Continental Shelf and the injury.

In contrast, the Fifth Circuit has adopted a situs-of-injury requirement, holding that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer Continental Shelf platform or on the waters above the outer Continental Shelf. Mills v. Director, Office of Workers' Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). This interpretation is not supported by the plain language of the statute and is inconsistent with the Supreme Court's guidance in Tallentire.

The LHWCA, which is incorporated into the OCSLA, provides compensation for injuries occurring on navigable waters. 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Supreme Court has interpreted this provision to require that the injury occur on navigable waters, but not necessarily on the outer Continental Shelf. See Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson, 396 U.S. 212 (1969); Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244 (1942); South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251 (1940); Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford Railroad, 281 U.S. 128 (1930); Northern Coal & Dock Co. v. Strand, 278 U.S. 142 (1928).

Furthermore, the OCSLA's purpose is to provide a uniform system of compensation for workers engaged in extractive operations on the outer Continental Shelf. See United States v. Louisiana, 363 U.S. 1 (1960); Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co., 375 U.S. 34 (1963); Hahn v. Ross Island Sand & Gravel Co., 358 U.S. 272 (1959); Pennsylvania Railroad v. O'Rourke, 344 U.S. 334 (1953). Requiring a situs-of-injury test would undermine this purpose by creating a jurisdictional gap between the OCSLA and state workers' compensation laws.

In Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), the Supreme Court held that the OCSLA's choice-of-law provision applies to injuries occurring on artificial islands and fixed structures on the outer Continental Shelf. This decision supports the idea that the OCSLA's coverage is determined by the location of the operations, rather than the location of the injury.

In conclusion, the OCSLA does not require a situs-of-injury test. The statute's language and the Supreme Court's guidance in Tallentire and other cases support a broader interpretation of the OCSLA's coverage, which is not limited to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case should be affirmed, and the OCSLA benefits should be awarded to the petitioner.
