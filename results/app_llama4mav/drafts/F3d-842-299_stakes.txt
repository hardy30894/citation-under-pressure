## Argument

The district court erred in denying the Pressls' motion to remand this case to state court because it lacked subject matter jurisdiction. The court's jurisdiction was premised on 28 U.S.C. § 1331 and 16 U.S.C. § 825p, but neither statute confers jurisdiction over this declaratory judgment action, which turns solely on the interpretation of a private flowage easement under Virginia law.

### I. The District Court Lacked Federal Question Jurisdiction Under 28 U.S.C. § 1331

The district court's assertion of jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 was improper because the Pressls' complaint does not raise a substantial federal question. The complaint seeks a declaration of the parties' rights under a private easement, a matter governed by state law. See Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals Inc. v. Thompson, 478 U.S. 804, 813-14 (1986) (holding that a state-law claim does not arise under federal law merely because it involves a disputed issue of federal law). The fact that APCO operates the Smith Mountain hydroelectric project under a FERC license does not transform the Pressls' state-law claim into a federal question. See Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing, 545 U.S. 308, 312-13 (2005) (noting that a federal issue is not sufficient to create federal jurisdiction unless it is "substantial"). Here, the dispute centers on the interpretation of the easement, not on any federal law or regulation.

### II. The District Court Lacked Jurisdiction Under 16 U.S.C. § 825p

The district court also erred in asserting jurisdiction under 16 U.S.C. § 825p, which grants jurisdiction over suits arising under the Federal Power Act ("FPA"). However, the FPA does not govern private easement disputes. See 16 U.S.C. § 824(b)(1) (limiting FERC's jurisdiction to "public utilities"). The Pressls' complaint does not challenge any FERC order or regulation; it merely seeks a declaration of their rights under a private easement. See California v. FERC, 495 U.S. 490, 499 (1990) (holding that the FPA does not preempt state law governing private property rights). Therefore, § 825p does not provide a basis for federal jurisdiction.

### III. The Case Should Be Remanded to State Court

Because the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, it should have granted the Pressls' motion to remand the case to state court. See 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c) (mandating remand when the district court lacks subject matter jurisdiction). The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of respecting the limits of federal jurisdiction. See Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of America, 511 U.S. 375, 377 (1994) ("Federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction."). We therefore request that this Court vacate the district court's judgment and remand with instructions to return the case to state court.

By vacating the district court's order and remanding the case, this Court will ensure that the Pressls' state-law claims are adjudicated in the proper forum. See Caterpillar Inc. v. Williams, 482 U.S. 386, 399 (1987) (noting that "the plaintiff is 'master of the claim' and may 'avoid federal jurisdiction by exclusive reliance on state law'").
