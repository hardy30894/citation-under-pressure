ARGUMENT

A state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel cannot constitute cause to excuse the procedural default of a separate federal claim. Under Coleman v. Thompson, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991), “federal habeas review of the claims is barred unless the prisoner can demonstrate cause for the default and actual prejudice as a result of the alleged violation of federal law.” Cause requires “something external to the petitioner, something that cannot fairly be attributed to him.” Id. at 753.

An ineffective-assistance claim may supply cause only when the prisoner has first satisfied the procedural prerequisites for presenting that claim. Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478, 488-89 (1986), held that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause.” Where the ineffective-assistance allegation itself has been defaulted under an independent and adequate state rule, it cannot serve as cause. Id. at 489; see also Keeney v. Tamayo-Reyes, 504 U.S. 1, 9 (1992).

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule—that a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may nevertheless excuse another default—contradicts these principles. It would permit prisoners to circumvent state procedural bars simply by asserting, for the first time in federal court, that prior counsel was ineffective. Such a result would undermine the comity and federalism interests that Coleman and Carrier protect. See also Engle v. Isaac, 456 U.S. 107, 129 (1982) (requiring prisoners to present constitutional claims “at a time when their resolution is still possible” in state court).

The court of appeals’ reliance on the “prejudice” prong of Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984), to bypass the default of the ineffective-assistance claim is misplaced. Prejudice for cause-and-prejudice purposes is distinct from Strickland prejudice; the former presupposes a valid, non-defaulted cause. Carrier, 477 U.S. at 494. Because Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed as untimely under Ohio’s firmly established reopening rule, that claim cannot supply cause.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
