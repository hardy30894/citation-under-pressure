**Argument**

Arizona’s rule did not violate due process. The Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a defendant “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense,” but that guarantee does not disable the States from defining the relationship between insanity, mental illness, and criminal intent. *Crane v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 683, 690 (1986); *Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37, 42-43 (1996) (plurality opinion). Nor does it compel admission of all defense evidence whenever it has some tendency to negate mens rea. States retain broad authority to structure criminal defenses and evidentiary rules, including rules allocating proof on insanity and limiting mental-disease evidence to that defense. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); *Clark v. Arizona*, 548 U.S. 735, 752-54 (2006).

1. **Due process does not confer a freestanding constitutional right to use expert mental-state evidence to rebut mens rea outside the insanity defense.**  
The Constitution does not “abridge” the States’ power to define crimes and defenses. *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201-07 (1977). Under *Patterson*, the State may require the defendant to prove insanity, so long as the prosecution proves the elements it has chosen to make elements of the offense. *Id.* at 210-16. Arizona followed that model. It did not relieve the State of proving intent; rather, it limited a particular category of expert evidence—psychiatric testimony diagnosing a defendant’s mental disease—to the insanity inquiry. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 748-49, 779-80.

This Court has repeatedly rejected claims that due process compels a particular method of proving or disproving culpability when the State has adopted a permissible substantive scheme. In *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445-48 (1992), the Court upheld a state rule placing the burden on the defendant to prove incompetence by a preponderance. In *Egelhoff*, the Court upheld a statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication on the mens rea question, explaining that historical practice and state autonomy permit legislatures to limit evidence bearing on mental state. 518 U.S. at 43-58. Arizona’s rule is even more modest: it permits lay evidence, cross-examination, and all other relevant proof on intent, while channeling expert psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 770-74.

2. **The exclusion was a permissible evidentiary and substantive limitation, not an unconstitutional bar on defense evidence.**  
Due process is offended only when exclusion of evidence is “arbitrary” or “disproportionate” to the rule’s purposes. *Crane*, 476 U.S. at 689-91; *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 324-31 (2006). Arizona’s limitation was neither. The State identified a serious evidentiary danger: psychiatric testimony about diagnosis and capacity can invite jurors to conflate diminished capacity with legal insanity, to overvalue expert opinion, and to relitigate the legal question of culpability through speculative medicine. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 773-79. The Court has recognized that expert evidence may be excluded where its probative value is outweighed by the risk of confusion or misuse. *United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308-12 (1998). That is especially true in the mental-disease context, where the “novel, underdeveloped” nature of psychiatric predictions makes them uniquely vulnerable to misunderstanding. *Ake v. Oklahoma*, 470 U.S. 68, 81-83 (1985).

Arizona’s rule also serves an important structural function: it preserves the State’s chosen insanity doctrine. The Court has long held that the Constitution does not require any particular insanity test. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 752-53; *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514, 536-37 (1968) (plurality opinion); *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 797-99. If a State may require a defendant to prove insanity by clear and convincing evidence, *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 769-70, it may surely prevent expert mental-disease evidence from being used to evade that burden under the guise of “diminished capacity.” Allowing exactly that would collapse the distinction between insanity and mens rea that Arizona permissibly chose to maintain.

3. **The prosecution still bore the burden of proving intent beyond a reasonable doubt.**  
Arizona did not shift the burden on mens rea. The defendant remained free to contest intent with the full range of non-expert evidence, including the circumstances of the shooting, his words and conduct, and other objective facts. Due process requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of each element, but it does not dictate that the State must tolerate every category of expert rebuttal. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970); *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 701-04 (1975). Here, the State proved the offense; Clark’s insanity defense failed; and Arizona’s evidentiary rule merely prevented psychiatric opinion from being repurposed as a partial-responsibility defense the legislature had not recognized. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 779-80.

4. **The Court should defer to Arizona’s historically grounded judgment.**  
The Constitution leaves “wide latitude” to the States in defining the insanity defense and the use of mental-disease evidence. *Clark*, 548 U.S. at 748, 773-80. That judgment is reinforced by history: at common law, insanity was a narrow defense, and states have long regulated or restricted psychiatric evidence on intent. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 797-99; *Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. at 43-45. Because Arizona’s rule is rooted in that tradition and rationally related to preventing confusion and preserving the integrity of its insanity defense, it does not offend due process. *Medina*, 505 U.S. at 445-53; *Scheffer*, 523 U.S. at 308-12.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
