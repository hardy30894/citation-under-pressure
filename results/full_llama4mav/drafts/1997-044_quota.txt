The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this protection is not available to debtors who have committed fraud. Under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A), debts obtained by false pretenses, false representations, or actual fraud are nondischargeable. The question presented here is whether the treble damages award, resulting from Cohen's fraudulent conduct under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is dischargeable under the Bankruptcy Code.

The plain language of § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud." The Supreme Court has held that the phrase "to the extent obtained by" is crucial in determining the scope of nondischargeability. As the Court noted in Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213 (1998), this phrase indicates that the debt is nondischargeable to the extent it was obtained by fraud.

The Third Circuit has correctly applied this principle, holding that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). In In re Cohn, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118-19 (3d Cir. 1995), the court established that a debtor's reckless disregard for the truth can satisfy the knowledge and intent requirements under § 523(a)(2)(B), a provision analogous to § 523(a)(2)(A). Similarly, in this case, Cohen's reckless disregard for the truth in charging excessive rents to his tenants satisfies the requirements for nondischargeability under § 523(a)(2)(A).

The treble damages award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is not dischargeable. The Eleventh Circuit's decision in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), is persuasive on this point. The court held that punitive damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are part of the "debt" incurred by the debtor's fraudulent conduct. The Ninth Circuit's contrary decision in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), is distinguishable, as it relied on a narrow interpretation of the phrase "to the extent obtained by."

The Supreme Court's decision in Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279 (1991), establishes that a creditor must prove the elements of § 523(a)(2)(A) by a preponderance of the evidence. Here, the bankruptcy court found that Cohen had committed actual fraud, and the District Court and Court of Appeals affirmed this finding. The treble damages award is a direct result of Cohen's fraudulent conduct, and therefore, it is nondischargeable.

Furthermore, the "fresh start" policy of the Bankruptcy Code does not protect debtors who have committed fraud. As the Supreme Court noted in Grogan, 498 U.S. at 286-87, the "honest but unfortunate debtor" is entitled to a fresh start, but a debtor who has committed fraud is not entitled to this protection. See also In re Van Horne, 823 F.2d 1285, 1287 (8th Cir. 1987) (holding that a debtor's reckless disregard for the truth can demonstrate intent to deceive).

In addition, the Court should consider the reasoning in Molzof v. United States, 502 U.S. 301, 305 (1992), which held that the meaning of a term in a federal statute is a federal question. Here, the term "debt" in § 523(a)(2)(A) should be interpreted broadly to include treble damages awards resulting from fraudulent conduct.

The decisions in Universal Minerals, Inc. v. C.A. Hughes and Co., 669 F.2d 98 (3d Cir. 1981), and In re Poskanzer, 143 B.R. 991 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1992), also support the conclusion that Cohen's debt, including the treble damages award, is nondischargeable. 

In conclusion, the debt resulting from Cohen's fraudulent conduct, including the treble damages award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, is nondischargeable under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The Court should affirm the decision of the Court of Appeals.
