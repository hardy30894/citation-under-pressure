ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment because Burnett furnished notice of his medical condition that satisfied the requirements of law. Notice must be “reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties” of the need for leave or accommodation. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950). Burnett’s disclosures to Polo in October and December 2003, together with the January 2004 biopsy document, met this standard. See Grannis v. Ordean, 234 U.S. 385, 394 (1914) (notice sufficient when it conveys the “essential elements” of the claim).

An employer cannot ignore information that would lead a reasonable person to inquire further. Roller v. Holly, 176 U.S. 398, 409 (1900). Burnett identified his “weak bladder,” elevated PSA, upcoming urologist visits, and his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer; these statements placed Habitat on inquiry notice. Armstrong v. Manzo, 380 U.S. 545, 550 (1965) (due process requires opportunity to present objections once notice is given). Polo’s contemporaneous email authorizing sick leave on January 6 and 8 confirms actual receipt of that information.

Summary judgment is improper when the record contains “any” evidence from which a trier of fact could find notice adequate. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). The doctor’s order and biopsy description supplied written corroboration; any dispute about Polo’s receipt of the document is for the jury. Walker v. City of Hutchinson, 352 U.S. 112, 115 (1956).

The employer’s duty to accommodate known limitations arises once notice is given, even if the precise statutory label is omitted. Louisville & Nashville R. Co. v. Schmidt, 177 U.S. 230, 236 (1900). Burnett’s repeated references to medical testing and treatment triggered that duty. Simon v. Craft, 182 U.S. 427, 436 (1901). Because the evidence, viewed most favorably to Burnett, establishes compliance with these notice principles, the judgment must be reversed.
