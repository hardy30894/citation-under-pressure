The United States respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute exceeds the federal government's enumerated powers and is inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

As the Third Circuit Court correctly held, Bond's challenge to § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). In Tennessee Electric Power Co., the Court held that private parties lack standing to raise Tenth Amendment claims, stating that "the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144. This holding has been reaffirmed by several circuit courts, including the First, Second, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir. 2005); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir. 2006); United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir. 2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir. 2004).

The rationale underlying this rule is that the Tenth Amendment is a structural provision that protects the sovereignty of the states, and private parties do not have a personal stake in the allocation of power between the federal government and the states. See Printz v. United States, 521 U.S. 898, 935 (1997) (O'Connor, J., concurring) ("The Tenth Amendment ... serves as a reminder that the Federal Government is one of enumerated powers."). As the First Circuit Court explained, "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35.

Moreover, even if Bond had standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge, her claim would fail on the merits. The Supreme Court has long recognized that Congress has the authority to enact laws necessary to implement treaties, even if those laws would otherwise be beyond its enumerated powers. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920) ("If [a] treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government."). The Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998, which includes § 229, is a valid exercise of Congress's authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause. See United States v. Lue, 134 F.3d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1998) ("Congress's authority under the Necessary and Proper clause extends beyond those powers specifically enumerated in Article I, section 8, [and] it may enact laws necessary to effectuate the treaty power.").

Additionally, Bond's vagueness challenge to § 229 is without merit. The statute provides clear notice of the conduct it prohibits, and its application to Bond's specific conduct is not vague. See United States v. Powell, 423 U.S. 87, 92 (1975) ("[V]agueness challenges to statutes which do not involve First Amendment freedoms must be examined in the light of the facts of the case at hand."). The statute's definition of "chemical weapon" and "toxic chemical" is clear, and Bond's use of potassium dichromate to poison Haynes falls squarely within the statute's prohibitions. See 18 U.S.C. § 229F(1)(A), (8)(A).

The search warrants for Bond's home and car were also valid. The affidavit supporting the warrants provided probable cause to believe that evidence of Bond's crime would be found in those locations. See United States v. Hodge, 246 F.3d 301, 306 (3d Cir. 2001) ("It is reasonable to infer that a person involved in [criminal activity] ... would store evidence of that [activity] at his home."). The postal inspectors' surveillance and investigation provided sufficient evidence to establish probable cause. See United States v. Whitner, 219 F.3d 289, 297 (3d Cir. 2000) ("[E]vidence associated with [criminal activity] needs to be stored somewhere, and ... a [perpetrator] will have the opportunity to conceal it in his home.").

In conclusion, Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under § 229 on Tenth Amendment grounds, and her challenges to the statute's constitutionality and the search warrants fail on the merits. The judgment of the Third Circuit Court should be affirmed.
