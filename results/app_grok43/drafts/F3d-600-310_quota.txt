The bankruptcy court lacked subject-matter jurisdiction to adjudicate ECMC’s entitlement to post-petition interest and collection costs. Those obligations arose after the Chapter 13 estate closed and the discharge issued, and therefore fell outside the limited grant of jurisdiction conferred by 28 U.S.C. §§ 1334(a) and 157(a). See Northern Pipeline Constr. Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co., 458 U.S. 50, 87 (1982) (plurality opinion) (bankruptcy courts may adjudicate only matters “arising under” Title 11 or “arising in” a Title 11 case). Post-petition interest on a nondischargeable student loan is a separate, non-estate obligation that does not affect the distribution of estate assets. Bruning v. United States, 376 U.S. 358, 363 (1964).

A proceeding to determine such post-petition liability is not a “core proceeding” under § 157(b)(2)(A). See Granfinanciera, S.A. v. Nordberg, 492 U.S. 33, 61 (1989) (core status requires that the claim “derive exclusively from the federal regulatory scheme” and “would not exist but for” bankruptcy law). Collection costs likewise constitute an independent contractual or statutory claim that accrues only after default and after closure of the estate. See Langenkamp v. Culp, 498 U.S. 42, 44–45 (1990) (per curiam) (claims arising post-petition are not core).

The district court’s contrary conclusion rests on an overly expansive view of “related to” jurisdiction. That jurisdiction exists only when the outcome “could conceivably have any effect on the estate being administered in bankruptcy.” Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 308 n.6 (1995) (quoting Pacor, Inc. v. Higgins, 743 F.2d 984, 994 (3d Cir. 1984)). Once the estate is closed, no such conceivable effect remains. See Valley Historic Ltd. P’ship v. Bank of N.Y., 486 F.3d 278, 287 (4th Cir. 2007) (post-confirmation claims lacking “close nexus” to the estate fall outside § 1334).

Nor does the bankruptcy court’s power to interpret its own orders extend to post-discharge liabilities never presented during the case. See In re A.H. Robins Co., 788 F.2d 994, 1011 (4th Cir. 1986) (bankruptcy court jurisdiction ends with substantial consummation absent continuing estate impact). The mere fact that the underlying loan was listed in the schedules does not convert subsequent interest and costs into estate property. In re Johnson, 960 F.2d 396, 402 (4th Cir. 1992).

Finally, the absence of any undue-hardship finding or adversary proceeding directed at post-petition amounts confirms that the bankruptcy court exceeded its statutory authority. See In re Merry-Go-Round Enters., Inc., 400 F.3d 219, 225 (4th Cir. 2005) (bankruptcy courts may not adjudicate disputes whose resolution would not “impact the handling and administration of the estate”). The district court therefore erred in upholding jurisdiction over those discrete post-petition determinations.
