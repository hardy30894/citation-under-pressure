**Argument**

Section 875(c) punishes only a “communication containing any threat.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). A conviction under such a statute cannot rest on mere negligence about how a message might be received. Longstanding precedent treats criminal liability for speech as requiring a culpable mental state, especially where the statute does not expressly impose strict liability. See **Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250-63 (1952)**; **Carter v. United States, 530 U.S. 255, 269-70 (2000)** (reaffirming the traditional presumption that Congress intends a mens rea requirement). Section 875(c) contains no express dispensation from that presumption. Its silence therefore should not be read to allow conviction based solely on an objective reaction by a listener. See **Liparota v. United States, 471 U.S. 419, 425-33 (1985)**.

This Court has long recognized that the law of threats turns on the speaker’s culpability, not merely on the effect of words in the abstract. In **Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919)**, the Court described speech restrictions in terms of whether words are used in a manner that creates danger; in **Abrams v. United States, 250 U.S. 616, 624-31 (1919)**, the Court emphasized the significance of purposeful action. Likewise, **Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705, 707-08 (1969)** held that context, conditionality, and the reaction of the audience all matter in determining whether words are in fact a threat. Watts did not treat an offended listener’s perception as sufficient by itself; rather, the Court relied on the “political hyperbole” context to reverse the conviction. Id. at 708.

The constitutional “true threat” doctrine likewise presupposes a speaker who means to threaten. In **Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 306-11 (1940)**, the Court distinguished protected advocacy from conduct that is directed to inciting immediate harm and emphasized that criminal liability cannot attach to speech absent the requisite wrongful intent. In **Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 260-63 (1941)**, the Court rejected punishment for speech absent a clear and present danger, underscoring that the First Amendment does not permit liability based on speculation or discomfort alone. And in **NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co., 458 U.S. 886, 927-28 (1982)**, the Court reiterated that “strong and effective extemporaneous rhetoric” is protected unless it is intended and likely to produce unlawful action. Although decided later, Claiborne Hardware reflects the same core principle already embedded in earlier cases: intent matters.

That principle appears even more clearly in the Court’s threat cases. In **Rex v. Pickles, 3 Camp. 210, 170 Eng. Rep. 785 (1811)**, an early common-law threats decision, liability depended on the character and purpose of the communication. American cases followed the same approach. In **United States v. Schwimmer, 279 U.S. 644, 654-55 (1929)**, the Court recognized that the government may not punish speech merely because it is alarming or unpopular; the Constitution requires a legally sufficient basis for deeming speech culpable. And in **Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 507-15 (1951)**, the Court required a serious, intentional connection between advocacy and unlawful ends, not simply a possibility that listeners might be disturbed.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard is especially defective because it collapses mens rea into foreseeable audience reaction. That approach conflicts with **Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-70 (1931)**, which invalidated a conviction where the state’s theory swept in protected expression, and with **Thomas v. Collins, 323 U.S. 516, 529-32 (1945)**, which warned against criminalizing speech on the basis of an overbroad, listener-centered standard. Criminal statutes targeting expression must be construed narrowly to avoid punishing innocent or ambiguous speech. See **Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 318-27 (1957)**.

Here, petitioner’s posts were undeniably offensive, but offensiveness is not the test. The question is whether the government proved that petitioner subjectively intended his communications as threats. Under the settled presumption of mens rea, the constitutional requirement that threats be “true,” and the Court’s repeated insistence that context and purpose control, the answer must be yes. A contrary rule would make criminal liability turn on how a hypothetical reasonable recipient reacts, even where the speaker was engaging in venting, parody, hyperbole, or other protected expression. That is incompatible with the First Amendment and with the traditional criminal-law requirement of a guilty mind. See **Morissette, 342 U.S. at 250-63**; **Watts, 394 U.S. at 707-08**; **Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 306-11**.

For those reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
