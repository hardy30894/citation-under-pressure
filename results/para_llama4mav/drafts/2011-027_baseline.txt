The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the trial court properly applied the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes. The equal footing doctrine, which grants title to the beds of navigable rivers to the state upon admission to the Union, is a well-established principle of constitutional law. Under this doctrine, a river is considered navigable for title purposes if it was navigable at the time of statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); Brewer-Elliott Oil & Gas Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 77, 86-87 (1922).

The test for navigability for title purposes is a factual inquiry that considers whether a river was "used or susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted in the customary modes of trade and travel on water." The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. 557, 563 (1870). The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this test, relying on historical evidence to determine whether the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time of statehood in 1889.

PPL argues that the trial court erred in considering hearsay evidence on summary judgment. However, the Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the historical documents submitted by the State were admissible under various exceptions to the hearsay rule, including the public records and reports exception, M. R. Evid. 803(8), and the ancient documents exception, M. R. Evid. 901(8). See Mont. Petroleum Tank Release Compensation Bd. v. Crumleys Inc., 2008 MT 2, ¶¶ 79-81, 341 Mont. 33, 174 P.3d 948. The State Archivist's affidavit provided a proper foundation for the admission of these documents.

The trial court's determination that the relevant stretches of the rivers were navigable at the time of statehood was supported by the admissible historical evidence. The Montana Supreme Court correctly deferred to the trial court's factual findings, which were not clearly erroneous. See United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (holding that the trial court's determination of navigability is a factual finding that should be given deference).

In conclusion, the Montana Supreme Court correctly applied the constitutional test for determining navigability for title purposes, and the trial court's determination that the relevant stretches of the rivers were navigable at the time of statehood was supported by admissible evidence. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
