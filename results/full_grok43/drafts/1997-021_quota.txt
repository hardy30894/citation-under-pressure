I. The Board’s Uniform “Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the Act.

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continuing majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may rebut that presumption with “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). When such doubt exists, the employer may withdraw recognition, petition for an RM election, or conduct a poll. *Id.* at 778.

The Board requires identical objective evidence for all three options. This equivalence is arbitrary. Three courts of appeals have held that polling may proceed on “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support” even when that evidence would not justify withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). These decisions recognize that a poll, conducted under *Struksnes* safeguards, supplies more reliable information than an employer’s pre-poll assessment and therefore warrants a lower threshold.

The Board’s contrary rule cannot be reconciled with the Act’s policies. The Board itself acknowledges that polling can “avoid making mistakes about the extent of employee support,” yet it permits the practice only when the employer already possesses evidence sufficient to withdraw recognition. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). This renders polling “only marginally useful,” contrary to the statutory interest in accurate determinations of majority status. *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978) (Board must balance competing interests consistently with the Act). Because the Board’s standard is not “rational and consistent with the Act,” it is not entitled to deference. *Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 787.

II. Substantial Evidence Demonstrated Reasonable Doubt.

The Board’s finding that Allentown lacked reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence. Seven unit employees expressly repudiated the union. Three additional employees (McArthur, Wehr, Zoltack) were properly excluded because they were no longer employed on the date Allentown decided to poll. *Fall River*, 482 U.S. at 41 n.8.

Statements by Ridgick and Marsh were correctly discounted under settled precedent holding that expressions made during interviews after the employer announced a nonunion operation are unreliable, *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc. v. NLRB*, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (5th Cir. 1979), and that dissatisfaction with representation does not equate to opposition to continued representation, *Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB*, 882 F.2d 19, 22 (1st Cir. 1989). Bloch’s and Mohr’s reports of others’ views were likewise unreliable because neither witness explained the basis for his assertion. *Thomas Indus.*, 687 F.2d at 868.

The remaining evidence—employee statements, the reduced unit size, and the poll itself conducted under priest supervision—collectively supplied “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt.” *Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 778. The Board’s refusal to credit this evidence was arbitrary.

The petition for review should be granted.
