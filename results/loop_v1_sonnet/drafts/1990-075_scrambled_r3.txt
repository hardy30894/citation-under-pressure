# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests “all legislative powers” in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering a complex regulatory scheme. This Court settled the controlling test more than sixty years ago: “if Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Section 811(h) meets that test many times over.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). That is precisely the kind of policy and criterion whose *absence* doomed the statutes in *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). In *Panama Refining* the Court struck down the delegation because the statute, in the Court's words, "does not declare or indicate any policy" governing the President's exercise of the delegated power. 293 U.S. at 415. Section 811(h) supplies exactly what that statute lacked.

The Court has consistently sustained delegations far less confined than this one when Congress faced a fast-moving regulatory problem. In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix wartime prices according to a general statutory standard, recognizing that the rapidity of change in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same is true here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new “designer drugs” faster than the ordinary scheduling process could respond, which is precisely why it authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment proceedings. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190 (1943), the Court approved the broad public-interest standard governing broadcast licensing, and in *United States v. Rock Royal Co-operative, Inc.*, 307 U.S. 533, 574-77 (1939), the Court sustained the Secretary of Agriculture's authority to fix milk-marketing terms under a similarly open-ended public-interest standard. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

Nor is the absence of exhaustive technical detail fatal. This Court has upheld delegations resting on general administrative standards where Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's access to specialized knowledge that Congress itself could not practically anticipate. *See Opp Cotton Mills, Inc. v. Administrator of Wage & Hour Div.*, 312 U.S. 126, 144-46 (1941) (sustaining broad delegation of wage-fixing authority under the Fair Labor Standards Act). The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here, where scientific and pharmacological judgments about emerging substances are ill-suited to case-by-case congressional determination.

Finally, judicial review of the *constitutionality* of the delegation remains available, satisfying the concern this Court has long recognized that delegations be accompanied by some avenue of judicial oversight. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 56-57 (1932) (emphasizing the availability of judicial review as a safeguard against improper delegation of governmental power to administrative officers). Although § 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, it does not — and could not — foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself, as the court below correctly recognized. The delegation doctrine traces to this Court's early recognition that, while Congress cannot delegate powers that are strictly and exclusively legislative, it "may certainly delegate to others, powers which the legislature may rightly exercise itself." *Wayman v. Southard*, 23 U.S. (10 Wheat.) 1, 43 (1825). That principle is fully vindicated where, as here, constitutional review remains open notwithstanding a bar on review of the agency's discretionary findings.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a simpler reason: Congress expressly authorized it. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) empowers the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” That express statutory authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357, 363-64 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization for the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. Cf. *United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (upholding delegated authority to an executive department to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted, and the delegation to the DEA was therefore proper.
