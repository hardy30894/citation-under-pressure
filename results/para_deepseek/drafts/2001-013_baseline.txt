**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously extends circuit precedent to create a blanket exemption from the Occupational Safety and Health Act for all vessels, including uninspected barges like Mallard Bay Rig 52. Section 4(b)(1) of the Act preempts OSHA jurisdiction only where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard’s minimal regulatory oversight of uninspected vessels does not constitute the kind of comprehensive occupational safety and health regulation that triggers preemption.

**I. The Coast Guard Does Not Exercise Statutory Authority Over Occupational Safety and Health on Uninspected Vessels**

The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific equipment and operational matters. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (authorizing regulations for fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and backfire flame control). These regulations address maritime safety, not the full range of occupational safety and health hazards—such as blowout prevention, well control, and workplace conditions—that OSHA regulates. The Coast Guard has never promulgated standards for drilling operations, confined space entry, or hazardous energy control on uninspected barges. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the Coast Guard’s statutory authority is not coextensive with OSHA’s.” *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235, 242 (2002) (holding that OSHA may regulate working conditions on uninspected vessels where the Coast Guard has not acted).

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Both cases involved inspected vessels subject to comprehensive Coast Guard regulation. *Clary* did not address whether the barge was inspected, and *Donovan* expressly noted that the vessel there was “a vessel in navigation” subject to “the Coast Guard’s comprehensive regulation and supervision of seamen’s working conditions.” 720 F.2d at 826. Here, Rig 52 was an uninspected barge that was not “in navigation” within the meaning of the Jones Act. See *McDermott Int’l, Inc. v. Wilander*, 498 U.S. 337, 354 (1991) (defining “seaman” status based on contribution to vessel’s mission and connection to a vessel in navigation). The barge was stationary while drilling, and its crew were not seamen but land-based oilfield workers. The Coast Guard’s own investigation concluded that Rig 52 was not an inspected vessel and that its regulations did not cover the hazards that caused the explosion.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Requires Actual Exercise of Authority, Not Mere Potential**

Preemption under § 4(b)(1) is not triggered by the mere possibility that another agency *could* regulate. The statute requires that the other agency “exercise statutory authority” to prescribe or enforce standards. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Coast Guard has not exercised its authority over occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels. Its regulations for such vessels are limited to equipment and do not address the working conditions that OSHA cited here. As this Court has held, “the Coast Guard’s failure to regulate a particular working condition leaves OSHA free to do so.” *Chao*, 534 U.S. at 242. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary reading would create a regulatory vacuum, leaving workers on uninspected vessels without any federal occupational safety protection—a result Congress could not have intended.

**III. The Workers on Rig 52 Are Not Seamen**

Even if the Coast Guard’s authority were broader, preemption under § 4(b)(1) applies only to “working conditions of employees with respect to which” the Coast Guard exercises authority. The workers on Rig 52 were not seamen. They were drilling crew members who performed no navigational duties and were not permanently assigned to a vessel in navigation. See *Chandris, Inc. v. Latsis*, 515 U.S. 347, 368 (1995) (seaman status requires substantial connection to a vessel in navigation). The barge was moored and used as a floating platform for drilling operations. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Colomb v. Texaco, Inc.*, 736 F.2d 218 (5th Cir. 1984), and *Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray*, 361 F.2d 432 (5th Cir. 1966), is inapposite because those cases involved workers who contributed to the vessel’s navigation or transportation function. Here, the workers’ sole connection to the barge was their drilling duties.

**IV. The OSH Act Applies to This Workplace**

Section 4(a) of the Act applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Rig 52 was located in Louisiana’s territorial waters, and the workers performed their jobs on the barge. The barge was a “workplace” within the plain meaning of the Act. The Fifth Circuit did not reach this issue, but it is well settled that a stationary drilling barge is a workplace subject to OSHA jurisdiction. See *Secretary of Labor v. M.C. Drilling, Inc.*, 15 OSHC 1473 (1991) (holding that OSHA has jurisdiction over drilling barges in state territorial waters).

**Conclusion**

The Coast Guard does not exercise statutory authority over occupational safety and health on uninspected vessels. The workers on Rig 52 were not seamen, and the barge was a workplace subject to OSHA jurisdiction. The decision below should be reversed.
