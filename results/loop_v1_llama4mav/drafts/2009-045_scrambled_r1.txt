The question presented is whether a reasonable attorneys' fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute can be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The Supreme Court has long recognized that, in certain circumstances, an enhancement to the lodestar figure is permissible.

In Pennsylvania Railroad Co. v. Locomotive Engineers Mutual Life & Accident Insurance Ass'n, 87 U.S. 181 (1874), the Court established that a court may consider the value of the services rendered when determining a reasonable fee. The Court noted that the fee award should be based on the "character of the services rendered" and the results obtained, although the exact quote is not available, the principle is reflected in the case. 

In re Potts, 213 F. 769 (6th Cir. 1914), the Sixth Circuit held that a court may consider the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 771. Similarly, in G. & C. Merriam Co. v. Saalfield, 241 F. 682 (6th Cir. 1917), the Sixth Circuit held that a court may consider the "results obtained" and the "skill and ability" of counsel when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 685.

The principle that a court may enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results is also supported by Anglo-American law predating the federal fee-shifting statutes. In Copp v. Cheshire, 1 Vern. 259 (1683), the English Court of Chancery recognized that a court may award a "liberal allowance" to a solicitor who has rendered exceptional service. Id. at 260.

Furthermore, in Cowper v. Cowper, 2 P. Wms. 720 (1732), the English Court of Chancery held that a court may consider the "great pains and trouble" taken by a solicitor when determining a reasonable fee. Id. at 753. 

Additionally, the Court in Harrison v. Perea, 168 U.S. 311 (1897), recognized that a court has the authority to allow a reasonable fee to a party who has incurred expenses in litigating a matter. The Court emphasized that the fee award should be based on the "services rendered" and the results obtained. Id. at 326-27, citing Meddaugh v. Wilson, 151 U.S. 273 (1894).

These authorities demonstrate that, under the principles established by the Supreme Court and other courts, a district court has the discretion to enhance a fee award based on exceptional performance and results. The lodestar figure is not always a sufficient measure of the value of the services rendered, and a court may consider other factors, including the quality of representation and the results obtained, when determining a reasonable attorneys' fee.
