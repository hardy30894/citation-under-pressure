The respondent, United States, respectfully submits that Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 229 on grounds that the statute is beyond the federal government's enumerated powers and inconsistent with the Tenth Amendment.

The Tenth Amendment challenge is foreclosed by the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). In that case, the Court held that private parties lack standing to challenge a federal act on Tenth Amendment grounds, stating that "absent the states or their officers, [the utility companies] have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] [Amendment]." Id. at 144, 59 S.Ct. 366. This holding has been followed by several circuit courts, including the First, Second, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits. See United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522, 525-527 (8th Cir.2009); Oregon v. Legal Servs. Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971-72 (9th Cir.2009); Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234-35 (2d Cir.2006); Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 33-36 (1st Cir.2005); United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284-85 (10th Cir.2004).

Bond's attempt to distinguish Tennessee Electric Power Co. is unavailing. She argues that the language regarding private party Tenth Amendment standing is "textbook dicta," but this characterization is incorrect. The Supreme Court's holding on standing was an alternative ground for its decision, and thus it is not dicta. See Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 34.

Furthermore, allowing private parties to bring Tenth Amendment challenges would be inconsistent with the principles of federalism and the role of states in our constitutional system. As the First Circuit Court noted, "the State represents the interests of its citizens in general, and, if it refuses to prosecute a viable Tenth Amendment claim, the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35.

In light of the Supreme Court's decision in Tennessee Electric Power Co. and the consistent holdings of several circuit courts, it is clear that Bond lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds. The judgment of the Third Circuit Court should be affirmed.

Additionally, the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act, 22 U.S.C. § 6701, and its associated penal provision, 18 U.S.C. § 229, are valid exercises of congressional authority under the Necessary and Proper Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 2, in conjunction with the Treaty Power, id. at art. II, § 2, cl. 2. The Supreme Court's decision in Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), supports this conclusion, as it held that "if [a] treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government." Id. at 432, 40 S.Ct. 382.

In conclusion, Bond's Tenth Amendment challenge is barred by her lack of standing, and the statute under which she was convicted is a valid exercise of congressional authority. The judgment of the Third Circuit Court should be affirmed.
