**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that due process demands a competency inquiry distinct from the standard for standing trial when a defendant seeks to waive fundamental rights. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871 (1988); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981). This Court has long recognized that competency to stand trial requires only a “rational and factual understanding” of proceedings and the ability to assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975); *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). But waiving counsel or pleading guilty implicates a higher mental threshold: the capacity for “reasoned choice” among available alternatives. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986). This distinction is rooted in the principle that constitutional waivers must be knowing, voluntary, and intelligent. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969).

The Nevada courts erred by conflating these standards. The state relied solely on psychiatric evaluations assessing Moran’s competency to stand trial—focusing on his ability to assist counsel and understand charges—not his capacity to make a reasoned decision to waive counsel and plead guilty. *See* Dr. O’Gorman’s report, which assessed Moran’s “ability to assist his attorney” and “cognizance of penalties,” *O’Gorman Psychiatric Evaluation* (Sept. 12, 1984), at 2—criteria relevant to trial competency, not waiver competency. The state’s post-conviction ruling thus rested on an erroneous legal standard. *See Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963) (federal courts must ensure state courts applied correct legal standards).

II. The record contained substantial evidence triggering a *bona fide* doubt of Moran’s competency to waive rights, obligating the trial court to conduct a competency hearing.

Due process requires courts to initiate a competency hearing whenever they harbor or should harbor a *bona fide* doubt about a defendant’s competency to waive rights. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Seiling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973). Such doubt arises when there is “substantial evidence” of incompetence. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1981), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946 (1982)).

Here, the record revealed multiple red flags. Moran had attempted suicide months before his plea hearing, was heavily medicated with four drugs (including sedatives and antiepileptics), and responded to the court in monosyllables. *See* Reporter’s Transcript of Plea Hearing (Nov. 24, 1984), at 11–12. The trial court’s cursory inquiry—accepting Moran’s waiver without probing the medication’s effects or his decision-making capacity—fell short of constitutional minima. *See Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243 (waivers must be “voluntary in a constitutional sense”).

The state’s post-conviction proceedings did not cure this error. A delayed competency evaluation cannot retroactively validate a flawed waiver. *See Creech v. Arave*, 947 F.2d 873, 879 n.5 (9th Cir. 1991) (noting independent federal review of competency determinations). The state court’s reliance on outdated trial competency evaluations—conducted *before* Moran waived rights—was legally insufficient. *See Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 315.

III. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied the “reasoned choice” standard and found the record inadequate to support Moran’s waiver.

The Ninth Circuit’s holding aligns with this Court’s precedents distinguishing competency to stand trial from competency to waive rights. *See Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172; *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856. The panel properly reviewed the record de novo, as competency determinations are mixed questions of law and fact. *Id.* at 857. Its conclusion that Moran lacked the capacity for reasoned choice is amply supported by the evidence: his medicated state, suicidal history, and minimal responses to the court.

The state’s contention that the Ninth Circuit improperly expanded due process is meritless. The heightened standard protects defendants from unknowing waivers of fundamental rights—a concern this Court has repeatedly emphasized. *See Boykin*, 395 U.S. at 243; *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399 (1993) (recognizing the “utmost solicitude” owed to waivers of constitutional rights).

**Conclusion**
The Due Process Clause demands a heightened competency inquiry when a defendant seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this standard and found the record insufficient to validate Moran’s waivers. This Court should affirm.
