**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Legal Malpractice Claims That Merely Require Application of Federal Patent Law.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim that requires a court to apply federal patent law necessarily "arises under" federal patent law for purposes of 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. The Supreme Court has long held that federal question jurisdiction is not triggered merely because a state-law claim involves a federal issue. *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 313 (2005). Instead, jurisdiction exists only when the federal issue is (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution without disturbing the congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Id.* at 314. Minton's claim fails this test.

**A. The Federal Patent Issue Is Not "Substantial" in the Context of This Malpractice Claim.**

The "substantiality" requirement demands that the federal issue be more than merely important to the parties; it must be "significant to the federal system as a whole." *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 313. Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception applies—is case-specific and fact-bound. It does not implicate the validity or scope of any patent in a manner that would affect future patent rights or the uniformity of patent law. As the dissent below recognized, "the federal issue is not substantial because the outcome of this case will not affect the validity of the '643 patent or any other patent." *Gunn v. Minton*, No. 10-0630, slip op. at 15 (Tex. 2012) (Willett, J., dissenting). The patent has already been declared invalid, and no party seeks to revive it. The only question is whether Minton's attorneys were negligent. That is a quintessential state-law issue.

**B. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance of Federal and State Judicial Responsibilities.**

The Supreme Court has cautioned that federal courts must be "sensitive" to the "potential for upsetting the state-federal line" when considering whether to exercise jurisdiction over state-law claims. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. Legal malpractice is a core area of state tort law. States have a strong interest in regulating the conduct of attorneys and providing remedies for professional negligence. Allowing federal courts to exercise exclusive jurisdiction over malpractice claims that involve patent law would "sweep into federal court a vast number of state-law claims that have only a tangential relationship to federal law." *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 384, 390 (5th Cir. 2008). Indeed, every legal malpractice case arising from patent litigation would become a federal case, regardless of how routine or fact-specific the patent issue. This would "disrupt the careful balance that Congress has struck between state and federal judicial responsibilities." *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

**C. The Federal Circuit's Contrary Precedent Is Inconsistent with Supreme Court Authority.**

The Federal Circuit's decisions in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), are not binding on this Court and are inconsistent with *Grable*. Those cases held that any state malpractice claim requiring proof of patent infringement necessarily raises a "substantial" federal issue. *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1272. But that approach ignores the federalism analysis required by *Grable*. As the dissent below noted, the Federal Circuit "failed to apply the test set forth by the Supreme Court." *Gunn*, slip op. at 17 (Willett, J., dissenting). This Court should not follow precedent that "fails to follow the test set forth by the Supreme Court." *Id.*

**II. The State Courts Properly Exercised Jurisdiction Over This Malpractice Claim.**

Because Minton's claim does not satisfy the *Grable* test, the state courts had jurisdiction to decide it. The Texas Court of Appeals correctly held that "the patent issue in this case is not substantial" and that "federal jurisdiction would disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Minton v. Gunn*, 301 S.W.3d 702, 709 (Tex. App.—Fort Worth 2009). The Supreme Court of Texas erred in reversing that decision and dismissing the case. This Court should reverse and remand for consideration of the merits.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
