## ARGUMENT

### I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-entered the United States Before Its Enactment.

The “presumption against retroactive legislation” is deeply rooted in our jurisprudence. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 265 (1994). Congress must “affirmatively manifest” a purpose to give a statute retroactive effect; absent such a clear statement, the court presumes the statute operates only prospectively. *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316–17 (2001). Section 241(a)(5) of the INA, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), provides that if the Attorney General “finds that an alien has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed,” the prior removal order is reinstated and the alien “is not eligible and may not apply for any relief” under the INA. The statute was enacted on April 1, 1997, as part of IIRIRA. Nothing in its text or legislative history suggests Congress intended it to apply to aliens like Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered illegally in 1982—fifteen years before the statute became law.

The critical “conduct” at issue is the illegal re-entry. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270 (focusing on “whether the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment”). Applying § 1231(a)(5) to a pre-enactment re-entry would attach the “new legal consequence” of barring all forms of discretionary relief—including adjustment of status—to conduct that occurred long before Congress created that consequence. This is precisely the sort of retroactive effect the *Landgraf* presumption guards against. *St. Cyr* applied that presumption to hold that IIRIRA’s elimination of § 212(c) relief did not apply to aliens who pleaded guilty to deportable offenses before the statute’s effective date. 533 U.S. at 326. Here, the parallel is even stronger: Fernandez-Vargas’s re-entry was completed in 1982, and he acquired a settled expectation that he could seek adjustment of status based on a family relationship. *See St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 323 (recognizing that “adjustment of status” is a form of relief to which aliens may have a “significant protectable expectation”).

Congress knows how to make statutory provisions expressly retroactive when it so chooses. *See, e.g.*, IIRIRA § 309(c)(1) (transitional rules for certain aliens). The reinstatement statute contains no such express command. The text’s present-perfect tense—“has reentered”—is ambiguous and insufficient to overcome the *Landgraf* presumption. *See Bowen v. Georgetown Univ. Hospital*, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988) (“[C]ongressional enactment[s] will not be construed to have retroactive effect unless their language requires this result.”). The Tenth Circuit therefore erred in holding that § 1231(a)(5) applies to Fernandez-Vargas.

### II. Even If the Statute Applies, Its Application Here Is Impermissibly Retroactive.

Assuming *arguendo* that Congress intended § 1231(a)(5) to reach pre-enactment re-entries, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas violates the presumption against retroactivity because it impairs rights he possessed under the law in effect at the time of his re-entry and throughout his subsequent residence. To determine whether a statute operates retroactively, a court must “examine whether the new provision attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. Three factors are relevant: (1) the nature and extent of the change in the law; (2) the degree of connection between the new legal consequences and the pre-enactment conduct; and (3) the existence of any “settled expectations” that the new law would disrupt. *Id.* at 274–75; *see also Martin v. Hadix*, 527 U.S. 343, 357 (1999).

First, the change was dramatic. Before 1997, an alien who re-entered illegally after a prior removal could apply for adjustment of status under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring “any relief” for such aliens. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). That is precisely the sort of “new disability” that *Landgraf* flags. 511 U.S. at 269.

Second, the new legal consequence flows directly from the pre-enactment re-entry—the event that “triggered” the statute’s application. *Hughes Aircraft Co. v. United States ex rel. Schumer*, 520 U.S. 939, 947 (1997). In *Hughes Aircraft*, the Court held that a statute extending the statute of limitations for False Claims Act suits could not apply to conduct already completed, because the new limitations period “attached new legal consequences” to prior acts. 520 U.S. at 949. Similarly, § 1231(a)(5) attaches the consequence of being permanently barred from relief to the act of illegal re-entry—conduct that was already completed before the statute existed.

Third, Fernandez-Vargas had a “settled expectation” that he could adjust his status based on his 2001 marriage to a U.S. citizen. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 323. Before 1997, he faced only the possibility of removal under prior law, but he could still seek relief from removal through adjustment. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). The Tenth Circuit erred in concluding that he had “no protectable expectation” because his illegal re-entry made him “always subject to removal.” But the relevant expectation is not immunity from removal—it is the expectation that, *if* removal proceedings began, he could *apply for relief* under existing law. *St. Cyr* recognized precisely that kind of expectation as protected. 533 U.S. at 323–24. Applying the reinstatement statute to him stripped away that opportunity retroactively.

Finally, the equities favor petitioner. Like many long-term residents, he built a life, family, and community in the United States. Congress chose not to include a retroactivity clause in § 1231(a)(5). The presumption against retroactivity requires that we respect that choice. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed.
