The government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. When a defendant withdraws from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period, the government must show that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial, as the government met its burden.

The Supreme Court has long held that the government must prove a defendant's participation in a conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) ("[T]he Government [must] prove beyond a reasonable doubt that [the defendant] became a member of the conspiracy."). The Court has also established that a conspiracy is a continuing offense, and the statute of limitations does not begin to run until the conspiracy is terminated. See Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946) ("A conspiracy is a continuing offense."). 

In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), the Court held that a conspiracy continues until its objectives are achieved or abandoned. See id. at 369-70. The government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the statute of limitations period, and that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during that time. See United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910) ("[T]he crime [of conspiracy] is not committed until the conspiracy is formed, and it is not ended until the purpose is accomplished or abandoned.").

The trial court's instruction to the jury must ensure that the government meets its burden of proof. See Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640, 645 (1946) ("[T]he jury [must] be instructed that they must find beyond a reasonable doubt that [the defendant] was a member of the conspiracy."). The jury must be convinced beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. See Lott v. United States, 309 F.2d 115, 119-20 (5th Cir. 1962) (citing Fiswick and Hyde).

In the present case, the government's evidence established that the conspiracy continued into the valid statute of limitations period. The trial court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial was proper, as the government met its burden of proof. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed this decision, relying on established precedent. See Braverman v. United States, 317 U.S. 49, 53 (1942) ("[T]he precise nature and extent of the conspiracy must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt."); see also Morrison v. California, 291 U.S. 82, 93-94 (1934) (discussing the burden of proof in conspiracy cases).

The petitioners' argument that the government failed to prove their membership in the conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period is unfounded. The government's evidence, as presented at trial, was sufficient to establish the defendants' participation in the conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt. The trial court and the D.C. Circuit correctly applied the relevant law, as established in cases such as Fiswick, Hyde, and Kissel.

In conclusion, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant was a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period. The D.C. Circuit correctly affirmed the lower court's denial of the defendants' motions for a new trial, as the government met its burden. The Court should affirm the judgment of the D.C. Circuit.
