ARGUMENT

I. This Court Has Jurisdiction Notwithstanding the Timing of the Notice of Appeal

Appellants' notice of appeal, filed October 19, 1994, preceded the district court's March 1, 1995 order disposing of the Bozé Plaintiffs' claims. That sequence does not deprive this Court of jurisdiction. A notice of appeal filed before entry of a truly final judgment ripens once that judgment is entered, so long as no intervening prejudice results. See Dickinson v. Petroleum Conversion Corp., 338 U.S. 507, 511 (1950) (holding that a premature appeal was not fatal where the final judgment was subsequently entered while the appeal remained pending). The final-judgment requirement of 28 U.S.C. § 1291 exists "to avoid the delay and expense" of piecemeal appellate review, Cobbledick v. United States, 309 U.S. 323, 325 (1940), and to ensure that appellate courts review only decisions that "end[] the litigation on the merits," Catlin v. United States, 324 U.S. 229, 233 (1945). Here, the March 1, 1995 order supplied precisely that finality, and no party was prejudiced by the timing of the notice. This Court should therefore treat the appeal as timely and reach the merits.

II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Striking Plaintiffs' Experts as a Discovery Sanction

The wholesale exclusion of four expert witnesses is tantamount to dismissal of Plaintiffs' claims, and such severe sanctions are reserved for cases of willfulness, bad faith, or fault attributable to the party, not mere delay attributable to counsel's scheduling difficulties. See Societe Internationale v. Rogers, 357 U.S. 197, 212 (1958) (Rule 37 sanctions may not be imposed where noncompliance is not due to "willfulness, bad faith, or any fault" of the party). Due process likewise constrains a court's power to penalize a litigant so severely that the sanction operates as a "conclusive presumption" against the merits of the claim. Hammond Packing Co. v. Arkansas, 212 U.S. 322, 350-51 (1908). While courts possess inherent authority to manage their dockets, that authority is not unlimited; dismissal-equivalent sanctions require a searching inquiry into the offending party's culpability and the availability of lesser sanctions. Link v. Wabash R.R. Co., 370 U.S. 626, 630-31 (1962) (recognizing the "inherent power" of courts to dismiss for want of prosecution but cautioning that such power must be exercised soundly). The record here reflects a discovery dispute rooted in the experts' need for additional time, not a scheme of willful defiance. The district court's blanket order striking four experts, without individualized findings of bad faith as to each witness, exceeded the bounds of permissible discretion.

III. The District Court Erred in Excluding Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's Testimony

Even under traditional principles governing the admissibility of expert testimony, the opinions of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson should have reached the jury. The touchstone of admissibility has long been whether the expert's methodology rests on principles sufficiently established in the relevant field, not whether the court itself finds the conclusion persuasive. Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923) (expert testimony is admissible where the underlying principle has gained "general acceptance" in the particular field). Once that threshold is met, disputes about the weight or persuasiveness of an expert's conclusions are matters for the jury, not grounds for exclusion. Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp., 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (holding that conflicts among expert witnesses present questions for the trier of fact, and a court may not resolve such conflicts by disbelieving one side's expert as a matter of law). The district court's exclusion of these two experts effectively substituted its own judgment on causation for that of the jury, a role our tradition does not permit.

IV. Summary Judgment Was Improper Because Genuine Factual Disputes Remained

Because the excluded expert testimony should have been admitted, Defendants' summary judgment motions rested on an artificially truncated record. Summary judgment is a "lethal weapon" and courts must be cautious in its use, particularly where the case turns on complex causation questions requiring expert analysis. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary procedures should be used sparingly where motive and inference are decisive). Where competing expert opinions exist, or would have existed but for the erroneous exclusions here, "the jury was entitled to determine which of the two conflicting theories it would accept." Sartor, 321 U.S. at 627. Had Plaintiffs' experts been permitted to testify, the record would have presented precisely the sort of factual dispute concerning exposure and causation that precludes summary disposition.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the orders striking Plaintiffs' experts and excluding Dr. Schroeder's and Dr. Watson's testimony, reverse the grant of summary judgment, and remand for further proceedings.
