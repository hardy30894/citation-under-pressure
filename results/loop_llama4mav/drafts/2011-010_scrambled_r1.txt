ARGUMENT

I. The OCSLA Workers’ Compensation Provision Requires a Situs-of-Injury Test

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends workers’ compensation coverage under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) to injuries occurring “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources . . . of the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The plain meaning of this provision requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. The Supreme Court has noted that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 is not directly on point, but the Court's statement that OCSLA has a "situs requirement" is instructive, see id. at 219. 

The Fifth Circuit has adopted a situs-of-injury test for OCSLA claims, requiring that the injury occur on an outer continental shelf platform or on the waters above the outer continental shelf. Mills v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs, 177 F.2d 356 is not available; however, the en banc decision is available at 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc); see also Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, 314 U.S. 244 (1941); Nogueira v. New York, N.H. & H.R. Co., 281 U.S. 128 (1930); Swanson v. Marra Bros., 328 U.S. 1 (1946). This interpretation is consistent with the LHWCA, which requires that the injury occur on “navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a); see also State Industrial Comm’n v. Nordenholt Corp., 259 U.S. 263 (1922); London Guarantee & Accident Co. v. Indus. Accident Comm’n, 279 U.S. 109 (1929). The LHWCA's situs requirement has been consistently applied by the courts.

II. La Conchita is Not a Maritime Situs

The LHWCA requires that the injury occur on a maritime situs, defined as “navigable waters of the United States, including any adjoining pier, wharf, dry dock, terminal, building way, marine railway, or other adjoining area customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). La Conchita does not meet this definition, as it is not adjacent to navigable waters and is not used for maritime purposes. See e.g., T. Smith & Son, Inc. v. Taylor, 276 U.S. 179 (1928); Minnie v. Port Huron Terminal Co., 295 U.S. 647 (1935). The facility's proximity to the coastline is not sufficient to establish it as a maritime situs, as it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks. The Ninth Circuit has applied a "functional relationship" test to determine whether a facility is a maritime situs, considering factors such as the site's suitability for maritime uses, the proximity to the waterway, and the site's use as an integral part of longshoring operations. Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron, 568 F.2d 137 (9th Cir. 1978). Under this test, La Conchita is not a maritime situs, as it is not used for maritime purposes and is not adjacent to navigable waters. The primary purpose of La Conchita is to process crude oil slurry, a non-maritime activity, see Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 766 F.2d 898 (5th Cir. 1985) (citing Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985)).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should affirm the BRB's denial of benefits under the OCSLA and LHWCA.
