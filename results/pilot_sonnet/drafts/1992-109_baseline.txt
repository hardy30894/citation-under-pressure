# ARGUMENT

## THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR COMPETENCE TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL

## I. The Competency Standard for Standing Trial Is the Same Standard That Applies to Pleading Guilty or Waiving Counsel

This Court has held that due process requires that a criminal defendant be competent to stand trial. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966); *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975). The test is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This standard reflects the constitutional minimum necessary to ensure that the criminal process functions fairly.

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a higher standard of competence—the capacity for "reasoned choice"—applies when a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive the right to counsel. Nothing in this Court's precedents supports bifurcating the competency inquiry in this manner. The *Dusky* standard already requires that a defendant possess a "reasonable degree of rational understanding" of the proceedings, *id.*, a standard that encompasses the mental capacity necessary to make the fundamental decisions attendant to criminal proceedings, including whether to plead guilty and whether to accept counsel's assistance.

The decision to plead guilty and the decision to waive counsel are integrally related to the competency inquiry already required at trial. A defendant who is competent to stand trial—meaning one who can understand the charges, comprehend the proceedings, and communicate with counsel—is competent to make the same category of decisions, including the decision to enter a plea or to proceed without counsel. There is no reason, grounded in the Constitution, to require a distinct and more demanding showing of mental acuity merely because the defendant elects to forgo a full trial.

## II. The "Reasoned Choice" Standard Improperly Conflates Competency with the Requirement That a Waiver Be Knowing and Voluntary

The Ninth Circuit's error stems from a conflation of two analytically distinct inquiries: (1) whether a defendant is *competent* to make decisions, and (2) whether the defendant's *decision* was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary. This Court's waiver jurisprudence, beginning with *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), and continuing through *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), addresses the latter question separately from the competency determination.

In *Faretta*, this Court held that a defendant has a constitutional right to waive counsel and proceed pro se, provided the waiver is knowing and intelligent. 422 U.S. at 835. The *Faretta* inquiry asks whether the defendant was "made aware of the dangers and disadvantages of self-representation," *id.* at 835, not whether the defendant possesses some heightened quantum of mental capacity beyond that required to stand trial. Similarly, in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), this Court required that a guilty plea be entered "voluntarily and knowingly," *id.* at 242, examining whether the defendant understood the rights being relinquished and the consequences of the plea—not whether the defendant met some separate, elevated competency threshold.

The Ninth Circuit's approach improperly imports the "knowing and voluntary" analysis into the competency determination itself, creating a standard untethered from this Court's precedents. The state trial court's duty is to ensure both that the defendant is competent under *Dusky* and that any waiver of rights satisfies the voluntariness requirements of *Boykin* and *Faretta*. These are separate but coexisting inquiries; conflating them into a single "reasoned choice" competency standard finds no support in the constitutional text or this Court's precedents.
