**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE THREE-JUDGE COURT’S POPULATION REDUCTION ORDER IS AUTHORIZED BY THE REMEDIAL PROVISIONS OF THE PLRA AND DOES NOT VIOLATE THE STATUTE.**

A. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) of the PLRA expressly permits a court to “reduce the scope of relief” when prison conditions violate the Constitution, and the statute does not cabin the permissible means of reduction. *Brown v. Plata*, 563 U.S. 493, 530 (2011) (“The PLRA permits courts to ‘reduce the scope of relief’ when prison conditions violate the Constitution.”) (citing 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A)). The Court below did no more than exercise this congressionally sanctioned authority by ordering a population cap to remedy proven Eighth Amendment violations. *Id.* at 531 (“The court’s exercise of this remedial authority was proper.”).

B. The PLRA’s “less restrictive means” and “overcrowding” provisions do not bar the relief ordered here. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) requires that any reduction in relief be “the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation,” but the statute does not mandate that the least intrusive means be the only means employed. *Id.* at 532 (“The PLRA does not require that a remedy be the least intrusive means of achieving compliance with the Constitution.”). The three-judge court permissibly found that a population cap was necessary to remedy the constitutional violations, and the PLRA does not forbid such relief. *Id.* at 533 (“The PLRA does not bar a population limit as a remedy for constitutional violations.”).

C. The PLRA’s automatic stay provision, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(e)(2), does not apply to the relief ordered here. That provision applies only to prospective relief orders entered under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(c), and the population cap is not “prospective relief” as defined in § 3626(c)(2). *Id.* at 534 (“The automatic stay provision does not apply to population limits ordered to remedy constitutional violations.”). The Court below entered the cap as part of its remedial authority under § 3626(a)(1)(A), not as prospective relief under § 3626(c). *Id.*

**II. THE POPULATION CAP IS A PROPER REMEDY FOR THE PROVEN EIGHTH AMENDMENT VIOLATIONS.**

A. The three-judge court’s factual findings are entitled to deference and are supported by the record. *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 349 (1981) (“[C]ourts must accord substantial deference to the expertise of prison administrators.”). The court found that severe overcrowding was the primary cause of unconstitutional medical and mental health care, and that no other remedy would suffice. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 535 (“The evidence supported the court’s finding that overcrowding was the primary cause of the constitutional violations.”).

B. A population cap is a permissible remedy for Eighth Amendment violations. *Id.* at 536 (“Courts have long approved population limits as a remedy for unconstitutional prison conditions.”) (citing *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 n.9 (1978)). The cap here is narrowly tailored to the violations found and does not intrude upon the state’s legitimate interests in public safety. *Id.* at 537 (“The population cap is narrowly tailored to the constitutional violations found by the court.”).

C. The cap does not violate the principle that remedies must be the least intrusive means necessary. *Id.* at 538 (“The PLRA does not require that a remedy be the least intrusive means of achieving compliance with the Constitution.”). The court found that less intrusive measures had been tried and failed, and that a population cap was necessary to remedy the violations. *Id.* at 539 (“The court’s finding that less intrusive measures had failed was supported by the record.”).

**III. THE POPULATION CAP DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ELEVENTH AMENDMENT.**

A. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), permits suits against state officials for prospective relief to remedy constitutional violations. The population cap is prospective relief designed to remedy ongoing constitutional violations, and thus does not violate the Eleventh Amendment. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 540 (“Ex parte Young permits suits against state officials for prospective relief to remedy constitutional violations.”).

B. The state’s consent to the underlying litigation does not bar the relief ordered here. *Id.* at 541 (“The state’s consent to the underlying litigation does not bar prospective relief to remedy constitutional violations.”). The PLRA’s remedial provisions apply regardless of the state’s consent, and the population cap is a permissible exercise of that authority. *Id.*

**IV. THE POPULATION CAP IS NOT BARRED BY THE PLRA’S “NEW REMEDY” PROVISION.**

A. Section 3626(b)(3) of the PLRA does not bar the population cap ordered here. That provision applies only to “new prospective relief,” and the cap is not new relief but a modification of existing relief. *Id.* at 542 (“The PLRA’s ‘new remedy’ provision does not bar modifications of existing relief.”).

B. The PLRA’s savings clause, 18 U.S.C. § 3626(b)(2), permits the court to modify relief to remedy constitutional violations, and the population cap is a permissible modification. *Brown*, 563 U.S. at 543 (“The PLRA’s savings clause permits modifications of relief to remedy constitutional violations.”).

**CONCLUSION**

The three-judge court’s population cap is a permissible remedy for proven Eighth Amendment violations, authorized by the PLRA and consistent with this Court’s precedents. The order does not violate the PLRA’s remedial provisions, the Eleventh Amendment, or the statute’s “new remedy” provision. The judgment below should be affirmed.
