**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for polling is irrational and inconsistent with the Act.**

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of continued majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may rebut the presumption with “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status.” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board permits an employer to poll only when it possesses evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989).

This equivalence is irrational. Polling and withdrawal serve different ends. An employer meeting the reasonable-doubt threshold may lawfully withdraw recognition, yet the Board insists the same threshold must be satisfied before the employer may first verify employee sentiment through a poll conducted under *Struksnes* safeguards. The Board itself acknowledges that “there still remains an inherent uncertainty about whether the union has actually lost its majority support” even when reasonable doubt exists. *Id.* at 1063. Requiring an employer to forgo the very mechanism that would resolve that uncertainty before acting on the doubt it already possesses cannot be reconciled with the statutory policy favoring “stable collective-bargaining relationships.” *Id.* at 1062.

The three courts of appeals that rejected the Board’s standard recognized this anomaly. *See A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). Each held that an employer may poll upon “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support” even if that evidence would not yet justify withdrawal. The Board’s refusal to adopt a lower threshold for polling, while simultaneously preferring Board-conducted elections over employer polls, *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1061, produces the very result the Board purports to avoid: employers are driven either to withdraw recognition on marginal evidence or to forgo testing sentiment altogether.

**II. The Board’s finding that Allentown lacked reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence.**

The Board counted statements from only seven employees. It refused to credit statements from Ridgick and Marsh because they were made during job interviews after the company indicated it would be non-union. Board precedent holds such statements unreliable. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978). Yet the same precedent does not require an employer to ignore every expression of dissatisfaction voiced in that setting when the statements are uncontradicted and the employer has independent evidence of unit-wide disaffection.

Bloch reported that the entire night shift rejected the union; Mohr, the union steward, stated that a vote would defeat the union. The Board dismissed these reports as unverified. But the Act does not require an employer to obtain sworn affidavits from every employee before entertaining doubt. Where, as here, the statements are unrefuted and come from employees in a position to know unit sentiment, they constitute objective evidence the Board may not arbitrarily disregard.

The record therefore contains substantial evidence that Allentown possessed a good-faith reasonable doubt on the date it announced the poll. The Board’s contrary finding cannot stand.
