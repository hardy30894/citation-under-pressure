# ARGUMENT

Congress directed that "cases of discrimination" arising from actions appealable to the Board "shall be filed" in district court, not the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. §§ 7702(a)(1), 7703(b)(2). Whether a case qualifies as such a "mixed case" turns on what the employee alleged, not on how the Board later resolved a contested, fact-bound jurisdictional dispute. Settled principles of federal jurisdiction fix that classification at the pleading stage, and this Court has long refused to fracture a single controversy between separate courts merely because one component might, if resolved a certain way, support a different label.

## I. A "Mixed Case" Is Defined By The Employee's Allegations, Not By The Board's Ultimate Jurisdictional Ruling

This Court has long held that jurisdiction is tested by what a plaintiff alleges, not by whether those allegations ultimately prevail. In *Mottley*, the Court explained that whether a case "aris[es] under the Constitution or laws of the United States … must be determined from what necessarily appears in the plaintiff's statement of his own claim." *Louisville & Nashville R.R. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Applying that principle, *Bell v. Hood* held that a suit invoking a federal right is not defeated for want of jurisdiction simply because the court ultimately concludes the plaintiff cannot prevail; jurisdiction depends on whether "the right of the petitioners to recover under their complaint will be sustained if the Constitution and laws of the United States are given one construction and will be defeated if they are given another." *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678, 685 (1946) (quoting *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913)). A claim's character does not change simply because the tribunal ultimately rejects the premise on which its authority to hear that claim rested.

The same logic governs here. Perry alleged that he "ha[d] been affected by an action which [he] may appeal to" the Board, and that "a basis for the action was discrimination." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). That is precisely what a mixed case requires. The Board's later determination that Perry's retirement was voluntary, and thus beyond its jurisdiction, no more transforms his case into something other than a mixed case than a dismissal for failure to state a claim retroactively strips a case of the federal character it had at filing. *Bell*, 327 U.S. at 682. This reading also honors the statute's evident purpose. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 542–43 (1940) (statutory words must be read "in the light of" their purpose); *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (a statute's "spirit" controls over a literal construction Congress could not have intended). Congress could not have intended that an identical discrimination-plus-removal claim be routed to different appellate systems depending on how the Board characterizes its own dismissal.

## II. A Single Controversy Should Not Be Split Between Two Appellate Systems

This Court has repeatedly refused to dismember a unified controversy into separate proceedings merely because one part might independently justify a different forum. In *Hurn v. Oursler*, the Court distinguished "two distinct grounds in support of a single cause of action," which a court may resolve together, from "two separate and distinct causes of action," which it may not; where the grounds are unified, the court should "retain and dispose of the case" as a whole. *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238, 246 (1933). Likewise, *American Fire & Casualty Co. v. Finn* insisted that a "single wrongful invasion of a single primary right" not be split into artificially separate proceedings. *American Fire & Cas. Co. v. Finn*, 341 U.S. 6, 14 (1951).

Perry's claim is exactly such a unified controversy: discriminatory coercion allegedly rendered his retirement and suspension involuntary—one claim, one set of facts, supplying both the ground for Board jurisdiction and the substance of his discrimination case. Sending the voluntariness question to the Federal Circuit while reserving discrimination review generally for district court recreates the piecemeal litigation *Hurn* and *Finn* condemned, forcing a single record into two forums under two standards of review.

## III. Doubts Should Be Resolved In Favor Of Unified, Full Review

Where jurisdictional and merits questions are genuinely intertwined, this Court has favored full judicial scrutiny over truncated review confined to a specialized tribunal. See *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 54–56 (1932) (jurisdictional facts underlying agency authority warrant independent judicial determination). Exceptions to generally applicable review provisions, moreover, are "not to be lightly implied." *Stark v. Wickard*, 321 U.S. 288, 309 (1944). And a tribunal's power to determine its own jurisdiction does not wall that determination off from the review otherwise applicable to the underlying claim. See *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258, 291 (1947) (every court has "jurisdiction to determine its own jurisdiction").

Because Perry alleged a mixed case at the threshold, and because his jurisdictional and discrimination theories rest on identical facts, review belongs in district court. The judgment below should be reversed.
