The National Labor Relations Board's (NLRB) "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for polling employees on union support is inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA). The NLRB's standard is too strict, rendering employer polls useless, and is not supported by the NLRA. As the D.C. Circuit noted, the NLRB's approach "renders polling only marginally useful to employers." Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, 83 F.3d 1485, 1492 (D.C. Cir. 1996). 

The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have rejected the NLRB's standard, adopting a lower standard that allows employers to conduct polls if they have "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support." See, e.g., A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1144, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). This standard is more rational and consistent with the NLRA, as it allows employers to test employee sentiment while protecting the statutory goal of stable collective-bargaining relationships.

The NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Administrative Law Judge (ALJ) and the NLRB discounted the statements of several employees, including Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh, who expressed anti-union sentiments during job interviews. However, as the dissenting judge in the D.C. Circuit noted, the NLRB's precedent holding that an employer may not rely on an employee's anti-union sentiments expressed during a job interview is not applicable here, as the employer did not indicate that there would be no union. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, 83 F.3d at 1497 (Sentelle, J., dissenting).

Furthermore, the statements of Kermit Bloch and Ron Mohr, which were rejected by the ALJ and the NLRB, provide additional evidence of the union's loss of majority support. Bloch stated that the entire night shift did not want the union, and Mohr, the union steward, said that if a vote was taken, the union would lose. See Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, 83 F.3d at 1498 (Sentelle, J., dissenting). The NLRB's decision to discount these statements is arbitrary and capricious, as it is not supported by the record.

The Supreme Court has held that an employer's doubt about a union's majority status must be "reasonably grounded" and "based on objective considerations." See Broadway v. Bogan, 459 U.S. 994 (1982) (White, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari) (citing Soule Glass & Glazing Co. v. NLRB, 652 F.2d 1055, 1110 (CA1 1981); NLRB v. Windham Community Memorial Hospital, 577 F.2d 805, 811 (CA2 1978)). In this case, Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including the statements of several employees. The NLRB's determination that the company lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence and is therefore arbitrary and capricious.

In conclusion, the NLRB's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard is inconsistent with the NLRA, and the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. lacked such doubt is not supported by substantial evidence. The Court should reverse the NLRB's order and hold that Allentown Mack Sales, Inc. did not commit an unfair labor practice by conducting a poll and refusing to recognize the union based on the results.
