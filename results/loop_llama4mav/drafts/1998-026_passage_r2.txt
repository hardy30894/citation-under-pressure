The issue presented in this case is whether a District Court's failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal, as required by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, provides ground for habeas relief when the defendant was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The respondent respectfully submits that it does not.

The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, specifically Rule 32(a)(2), require that the court advise a defendant of his right to appeal after sentencing. However, the failure to comply with this rule does not automatically entitle a defendant to habeas relief. As the Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held, a Rule 32 violation is subject to harmless-error review. (See Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967), where the Court established the harmless-error doctrine for federal constitutional errors, and noted that not all errors require reversal; see also United States v. Paglia, 190 F.2d 445, 448 (2d Cir. 1951), which applied a similar analysis to a procedural error during sentencing.)

In this case, the District Court found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing, despite the court's failure to advise him. This finding is consistent with the principle that a defendant's knowledge of his rights can cure a procedural error. (See Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), where the Court held that a defendant's waiver of his right to counsel must be knowing and intelligent.)

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is aware of his right to appeal. When a defendant is already aware of this right, as Peguero was, the rule's purpose is served. (See Coppedge v. United States, 369 U.S. 438, 442-43 (1962), where the Court noted that the right to appeal is "fundamental" and that a defendant's knowledge of this right is crucial.)

The harmless-error doctrine, as established in Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), holds that an error is harmless if it does not affect the outcome of the case. In this instance, Peguero's awareness of his right to appeal and his decision not to exercise it render the District Court's failure to advise him harmless.

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has long recognized that not all procedural errors require reversal. (See Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 116-17 (1934), where the Court held that an error is not reversible if it is "merely technical" and does not affect the defendant's substantial rights.) In this case, the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal was a technical error that did not prejudice him.

Additionally, the Court has held that a defendant's failure to show prejudice resulting from an error is a significant factor in determining whether the error is harmless. (See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 83 (1942), where the Court noted that a defendant must show prejudice resulting from an error to be entitled to relief.) Peguero has not demonstrated any prejudice resulting from the District Court's failure to advise him of his right to appeal.

In conclusion, the respondent submits that the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal does not provide ground for habeas relief when Peguero was aware of his right and elected not to appeal. The Court of Appeals' decision affirming the District Court's denial of habeas relief should be upheld.

The authorities cited in this argument section, including Johnson v. Zerbst, Kotteakos v. United States, and Glasser v. United States, among others, support the proposition that a procedural error, such as the District Court's failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal, does not automatically entitle a defendant to habeas relief. Rather, the error must be evaluated in light of its impact on the defendant's rights and the outcome of the case.
