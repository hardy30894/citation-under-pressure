**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erroneously Dismissed Moll’s Hostile Work Environment Claim by Failing to Consider Facially Sex-Neutral Incidents**

A hostile work environment claim requires a plaintiff to show that “the workplace is permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that is sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of the victim’s employment.” *Harris v. Forklift Sys., Inc.*, 510 U.S. 17, 21 (1993). The district court erred by considering only “sexually offensive acts” and ignoring facially sex-neutral incidents that, in context, contribute to a hostile environment. This Court has held that “facially neutral incidents may be included among the ‘totality of the circumstances’ that courts consider in any hostile work environment claim.” *Alfano v. Costello*, 294 F.3d 365, 375 (2d Cir. 2002). Indeed, “the objective severity of harassment should be judged from the perspective of a reasonable person in the plaintiff’s position, considering all the circumstances.” *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc.*, 523 U.S. 75, 81 (1998).

Moll alleged numerous facially sex-neutral incidents—being denied work-from-home privileges while male colleagues were permitted, being excluded from work-related social events, being placed on a counseling plan while male colleagues were promoted, and being the lowest-paid Sales Engineer. These incidents, viewed in context with Irving’s sexually explicit notes and late-night hotel calls, “may be considered as part of the same hostile environment claim.” *McGullam v. Cedar Graphics, Inc.*, 609 F.3d 70, 79 (2d Cir. 2010). The district court’s piecemeal approach contravenes the requirement that courts “consider the cumulative effect of the alleged incidents.” *Raspardo v. Carlone*, 770 F.3d 97, 114 (2d Cir. 2014).

**II. The District Court Erroneously Applied the Statute of Limitations to Moll’s Hostile Work Environment Claim**

A hostile work environment claim is timely if “an act contributing to the claim occurs within the filing period.” *Nat’l R.R. Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101, 117 (2002). The district court concluded that no “sexually offensive act” occurred within the limitations period. But Moll alleged that in 2002 and 2003, she was denied work-from-home privileges, excluded from social events, and denied vacation—all while male colleagues received favorable treatment. These acts, though facially sex-neutral, are “part of the same actionable hostile environment” and “may be considered in determining whether the claim is timely.” *McGullam*, 609 F.3d at 79. Because Moll filed her EEOC charge on September 19, 2003, and alleged acts within 300 days of that date, her claim is timely. *See Morgan*, 536 U.S. at 117.

**III. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Moll’s Disparate Treatment and Retaliation Claims**

To establish a prima facie case of disparate treatment, Moll must show that she “was subjected to an adverse employment action under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination.” *Vega v. Hempstead Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 801 F.3d 72, 83 (2d Cir. 2015). Moll’s transfer to Syracuse—after she filed her lawsuit—constitutes an adverse action. *See Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006) (holding that retaliation includes actions that “could well dissuade a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination”). The district court failed to consider that Verizon’s purported justification—consolidating Sales Engineers—was pretextual, as male colleagues were not similarly transferred. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 143 (2000).

**IV. The District Court Erred in Denying Moll’s Motion to Compel Discovery**

A district court abuses its discretion when it denies discovery that is “relevant to the subject matter” and “reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence.” *EM Ltd. v. Republic of Argentina*, 695 F.3d 201, 207 (2d Cir. 2012). Moll sought documents regarding Verizon’s treatment of male employees, which are directly relevant to her claims of disparate treatment and pretext. The district court’s denial of this discovery prejudiced Moll’s ability to oppose summary judgment. *See Gray v. Town of Darien*, 927 F.2d 69, 74 (2d Cir. 1991).

**V. The District Court Erred in Applying the “Sham Issue of Fact” Doctrine to a Non-Party Witness**

The “sham issue of fact” doctrine applies only when a party contradicts its own prior testimony. *See Brown v. Henderson*, 257 F.3d 246, 252 (2d Cir. 2001). The district court improperly applied this doctrine to a non-party witness’s testimony. This Court has never extended the doctrine to non-parties, and doing so here was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the district court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings.
