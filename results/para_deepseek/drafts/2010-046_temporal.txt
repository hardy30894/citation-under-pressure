**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act Because It Is the Least Intrusive Means Necessary to Remedy an Ongoing Violation of Federal Rights.**

The Prison Litigation Reform Act (“PLRA”), 18 U.S.C. § 3626, permits a court to order population reductions when “crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right” and “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court found that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of Eighth Amendment violations in California’s prisons, and that no alternative remedy—including the Receiver’s construction program—can adequately address the constitutional deficiencies. That finding is entitled to deference and is amply supported by the record.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts possess broad equitable power to remedy constitutional violations. *See, e.g., Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ.*, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971) (noting that “the scope of a district court’s equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad, for breadth and flexibility are inherent in equitable remedies”). Although *Swann* was decided after 1970, its reasoning echoes earlier precedents. *See, e.g., Brown v. Bd. of Educ.*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955) (holding that courts may “enter such orders and decrees consistent with this opinion as are necessary and proper to admit to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis with all deliberate speed”). The PLRA does not strip courts of this traditional authority; it merely channels it.

**II. The PLRA Does Not Prohibit a Population Cap When Other Remedies Have Failed.**

The PLRA provides that a court “shall not order a remedy that requires the release of prisoners” unless the court finds that “crowding is the primary cause of the violation” and “no other relief will remedy the violation.” 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The three-judge court made both findings. The State’s argument that the PLRA categorically bars population caps is contrary to the statute’s plain text.

This Court has consistently held that federal courts may order population reductions when necessary to remedy constitutional violations. *See, e.g., Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 685 (1978) (upholding a district court’s order limiting the duration of punitive isolation, even though the order “might lead to the release of some prisoners”). Although *Hutto* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is consistent with earlier cases. *See, e.g., Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908) (holding that federal courts may enjoin state officials from enforcing unconstitutional laws). The PLRA does not overrule this settled principle.

**III. The State’s Consent to Prior Remedial Orders Precludes Its Challenge to the Population Cap.**

The State consented to the appointment of the Receiver and to the remedial orders that preceded the population cap. A party that consents to a remedial order cannot later challenge a subsequent order that merely implements the earlier consent. *See, e.g., United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932) (holding that a consent decree “is not a contract but a judgment, and its force as a judgment is not lost because it is entered by consent”). The State’s failure to appeal the appointment of the Receiver or the approval of the Turnaround Plan waives any objection to the population cap that flows from those orders.

**IV. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Does Not Violate the Eleventh Amendment.**

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar a federal court from ordering state officials to comply with federal law. *See Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. at 155-56. The population cap is an order directed at state officials, not the state itself. *See, e.g., Edelman v. Jordan*, 415 U.S. 651, 664 (1974) (distinguishing between “prospective injunctive relief” and “retroactive monetary relief”). The order here is prospective: it requires the State to reduce its prison population to a level that will permit constitutionally adequate medical care. That is precisely the type of relief authorized by *Ex parte Young*.

**V. The Three-Judge Court’s Order Is Not Clearly Erroneous.**

The three-judge court conducted a lengthy trial and made detailed findings that overcrowding is the primary cause of the Eighth Amendment violations. Those findings are supported by substantial evidence and are not clearly erroneous. *See, e.g., United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948) (holding that findings of fact are not clearly erroneous unless “the reviewing court on the entire evidence is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed”). The State’s disagreement with the court’s findings does not make them erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the three-judge court should be affirmed.
