The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), allows Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 372 (quoting J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, listing specific factors to consider when scheduling substances, including the substance's potential for abuse, scientific evidence of its pharmacological effect, and its history and current pattern of abuse. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). The Attorney General must also obtain a "scientific and medical" evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services, which is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b).

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is similarly guided by intelligible principles. The Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only to "avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety" and after considering specific factors related to the substance's abuse and risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). While Judge Becker's dissent expresses concern over the delegation's constitutionality, the majority opinion correctly notes that the Supreme Court has given Congress wide latitude in delegating its powers, upholding delegations even with negligible standards. Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 373.

The Attorney General's delegation of authority to the DEA is also authorized. The CSA explicitly allows the Attorney General to delegate his functions to other officials, 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), and the Attorney General has properly exercised this authority by subdelegating the temporary scheduling power to the DEA Administrator through regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b).

The Toubys' argument that a more stringent non-delegation analysis applies when the delegatee is empowered to create crimes is unpersuasive. While Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), invalidated delegations involving criminal penalties, the CSA's delegation is distinguishable due to its more specific guidelines and checks on the Attorney General's authority. The availability of judicial review, although limited by 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(6), does not preclude constitutional challenges, as held by the Third Circuit and other courts. See United States v. Emerson, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir.1988); United States v. Spain, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir.1987).

In conclusion, the CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General and the Attorney General's subdelegation to the DEA are constitutional. The statute provides sufficient guidance and checks on the delegated authority, and the courts have correctly upheld the delegation. The convictions of Daniel and Lyrissa Touby should be affirmed.
