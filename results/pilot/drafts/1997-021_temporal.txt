**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll of employees is not only irrational but fundamentally at odds with the statutory scheme of the National Labor Relations Act. The Act, enacted in 1935, was designed to promote industrial peace by ensuring that collective bargaining relationships remain stable and that employees’ rights to self-organization are protected. It does not, however, mandate that employers be deprived of any means to test the actual sentiment of their workforce when they have reason to believe that a union’s majority support has eroded.

The Board’s current standard renders polling an impractical and useless tool. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the purpose of the Act is to encourage the development of collective bargaining as a means of resolving labor disputes.” *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 37 (1937). Yet under the Board’s rule, an employer may only poll employees if it already possesses sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition—a standard so high that it effectively precludes polling altogether. This creates a paradox: the Board permits polling only when it is least needed, and forbids it when it would be most useful. Such a result is not only illogical but undermines the very purpose of the Act.

The Board’s justification—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—is not a valid basis for eliminating a lawful method of determining employee sentiment. The Act does not prohibit employers from seeking information about employee preferences; indeed, it implicitly recognizes that employers may have a legitimate interest in knowing whether a union continues to represent a majority. *NLRB v. Fansteel Metallurgical Corp.*, 306 U.S. 240, 245 (1939). The Board’s attempt to elevate procedural caution over factual clarity is not supported by the statute or by precedent.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with the principle that an employer may not be forced to bargain with a union that no longer enjoys majority support. *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 618 (1969). If an employer has reason to believe that a union has lost its majority, it must be allowed to test that belief through a fair and neutral process. The Act does not require employers to gamble on the outcome of a bargaining relationship with a minority union, which would itself constitute a violation of § 8(a)(2). *NLRB v. United Steelworkers of America*, 347 U.S. 461, 467 (1954). The Board’s rule, by making polling nearly impossible, forces employers into a Hobson’s choice: either bargain with a union they believe no longer represents a majority, or risk an unfair labor practice by refusing to bargain without any evidence.

The Board’s own precedents confirm that polling is a legitimate and lawful method of testing union support. In *Struksnes Construction Co.*, 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967), the Board acknowledged that “an employer may conduct a poll of employees to determine their union sentiment if there is other substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support.” The Board did not require that the employer already have “reasonable doubt” before polling; rather, it permitted polling as a means of *obtaining* such doubt. This is the only rational interpretation of the Act’s balance between employer rights and employee protections. The Board’s subsequent retreat from this position in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057 (1989), was not only inconsistent with its prior rulings but also contrary to the statutory framework.

The Court of Appeals erred in deferring to the Board’s revised standard. While deference to agency expertise is appropriate, *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 131 (1944), it does not extend to rules that are irrational or contrary to the Act’s purpose. The Board’s standard is not merely a matter of policy—it is a legal rule that governs the rights of employers and employees alike. When an agency’s interpretation of the law is so at odds with the statute and its own prior decisions, judicial review is not only warranted but required.

II. THE BOARD’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective indications” of a loss of union support is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Act requires that findings of fact be based on “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939). The Board’s decision fails this test.

The record shows that seven employees—Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, Randy Zoltack, Mike Ridgick, Dennis Marsh, Kermit Bloch, and Ron Mohr—expressed opposition to the union or indicated that it had lost support. While the Board excluded some of these statements, its reasoning was flawed. The ALJ correctly found that Ridgick’s statement during a job interview—“as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union”—was not “equivocal” but rather a clear expression of anti-union sentiment. Similarly, Marsh’s complaint that he was “paying $35 a month in union dues and not getting his money’s worth” was not merely dissatisfaction with representation; it was a direct repudiation of the union’s value. *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395, 400 (1952).
