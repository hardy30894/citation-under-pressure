The judgment below should be affirmed. The Court’s fee-shifting precedents do not create a categorical bar on enhancements for superior performance and exceptional results; rather, they make clear that the lodestar is presumed reasonable, but may be adjusted in the rare case where the record contains specific evidence that the fee award would otherwise undercompensate counsel’s extraordinary work. Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546, 565-68 (1986); Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 898-901 (1984); Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 434, 436-37 (1983).

Blum rejected the argument that an upward adjustment is “never appropriate” under 42 U.S.C. § 1988. 465 U.S. at 901. The Court explained that, where “a plaintiff has obtained excellent results,” counsel should recover a fully compensatory fee, and that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” Ibid. The Court’s concern was evidentiary and case-specific: it reversed the enhancement there because the district court had not identified “specific evidence” showing that the quality of representation was “superior” to what one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged. Id. at 898-99. That is not a prohibition; it is a standard.

Delaware Valley I confirms that point. There, the Court reaffirmed that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible” in “rare” and “exceptional” cases, supported by “specific evidence” and detailed findings. 478 U.S. at 565. Although the Court stated that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” it did not eliminate every possibility of enhancement. Id. at 566. To the contrary, the Court left open that “the overall quality of performance” ordinarily should not adjust the lodestar, but did not foreclose an extraordinary case. Id. at 566, 568.

Petitioner relies on language in later decisions describing the lodestar as the “most useful starting point” and emphasizing that the lodestar method “holds sway” in fee-shifting cases. Blanchard v. Bergeron, 489 U.S. 87, 94-95 (1989); Gisbrecht v. Barnhart, 535 U.S. 789, 801 (2002). But those cases did not overrule Blum or Delaware Valley I, and neither held that enhancements for extraordinary performance are forbidden. Instead, they reiterate that the fee inquiry begins with the lodestar, not that it necessarily ends there. Blanchard, 489 U.S. at 94-95; Gisbrecht, 535 U.S. at 802.

Nor does City of Burlington v. Dague, 505 U.S. 557 (1992), compel a different result. Dague rejected contingency enhancements because they would compensate for the risk of loss, not because they would never permit any enhancement for exceptional work or results. Id. at 562-67. The Court’s reasoning addressed the “contingent-fee model” and the undesirability of multiplying fees to reflect litigation risk. Ibid. This case does not involve a contingency multiplier; it involves the district court’s determination that the lodestar, standing alone, did not fully capture the extraordinary quality of the representation and the exceptional results obtained. That question remains governed by Blum and Delaware Valley I.

The district court here made precisely the type of findings the Court’s precedents contemplate. It concluded that counsel’s performance was “superb,” that the representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected” from the lodestar rates, and that the results were exceptional. Those findings were based on the court’s direct supervision of years of complex litigation and on the record as a whole. Hensley emphasizes that the district court has “superior understanding of the litigation” and broad discretion to evaluate the relationship between “the results obtained” and the fee awarded. 461 U.S. at 436-37. The Eleventh Circuit thus correctly held that the enhancement was not an abuse of discretion. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1236-42 (11th Cir. 2008).

At bottom, petitioner asks this Court to hold that performance and results can never support an enhancement under a federal fee-shifting statute. That would be inconsistent with Blum’s express rejection of a categorical rule, with Delaware Valley I’s recognition of “rare” and “exceptional” cases, and with Hensley’s instruction that reasonableness depends on the relationship between the fee and the results achieved. 465 U.S. at 901; 478 U.S. at 565; 461 U.S. at 436-37. The judgment should be affirmed.
