**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second attempt to prove a prior conviction allegation after the prosecution has failed to do so in the first proceeding. The Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” **North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969)**. Once a court has finally resolved an issue in the defendant’s favor on an evidentiary record, the State may not simply try again. **Burks v. United States, 437 U.S. 1, 11 (1978)**; **Greene v. Massey, 437 U.S. 19, 24-25 (1978)**.

Although sentencing has traditionally allowed broad judicial factfinding, this Court has recognized an important exception where the proceeding “bear[s] the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” **Bullington v. Missouri, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981)**. There, the Court held that because Missouri’s capital sentencing hearing required the State to prove aggravating facts in a trial-like proceeding, an unsuccessful attempt to secure a death sentence barred a second attempt after reversal. **Id. at 446**. The Court reaffirmed that principle in **Arizona v. Rumsey, 467 U.S. 203, 209-211 (1984)**, holding that when a sentencer resolves the factual predicate for an enhanced punishment in the defendant’s favor, double jeopardy prevents relitigation. And in **Poland v. Arizona, 476 U.S. 147, 154-157 (1986)**, the Court again treated the sentencing phase as subject to double jeopardy constraints when it operates as a factual adjudication of a greater punishment.

That reasoning applies here. California chose to give a defendant a separate adjudication of prior-conviction allegations under Penal Code section 1025. Under the scheme at issue, the prosecution must prove the allegation, the defendant may contest it, and the court must make a true finding before the enhanced punishment may be imposed. **People v. Tenner, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993)**; **People v. Reed, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224, 228 n.6 (1996)**; **In re Yurko, 10 Cal. 3d 857, 862-863 & n.5 (1974)**. California has thus created a structured, adversarial proceeding that goes well beyond ordinary discretionary sentencing. A proceeding with those features is materially different from the informal sentencing described in **Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241, 245-251 (1949)**, where the Court upheld a judge’s broad access to extra-record information at sentencing.

The State should not be permitted to benefit from that trial-like structure while avoiding the constitutional consequence that follows when the prosecution fails to prove its case. This Court has consistently recognized that once the prosecution has had a full and fair opportunity to prove a fact essential to punishment and fails, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars a second attempt. **Burks, 437 U.S. at 11; Greene, 437 U.S. at 24-25**. The prior-conviction allegation here was not an informal sentencing consideration; it was a litigated issue, separated from the guilt phase, submitted to the court, and resolved on the evidence. The Court of Appeal held the evidence insufficient. That sufficiency ruling is the equivalent of an acquittal on the enhancement allegation. **Burks, 437 U.S. at 18**.

The California Supreme Court erred in treating capital and noncapital cases as categorically distinct for double jeopardy purposes. The relevant question is not whether the sentence is capital, but whether the State has created a proceeding that functions like a trial to determine facts exposing the defendant to greater punishment. **Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439; Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 209-211**. Nothing in **Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 795-796 (1969)**, limits the Double Jeopardy Clause to capital cases. And while **Monge v. California, 524 U.S. 721, 728-729 (1998)** later rejected the application of double jeopardy to noncapital sentencing enhancements, the better reading of this Court’s precedents is that when the State elects to convert sentencing into a formal adjudication of historical facts, it must accept the constitutional consequences of failure. **Bullington, 451 U.S. at 439, 446; Rumsey, 467 U.S. at 211**.

The judgment below should be reversed.
