**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) adopts state law as surrogate federal law only when federal law leaves a “significant void or gap.” *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). The Ninth Circuit erred by discarding this gap-filling requirement and applying state law whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law. That interpretation conflicts with OCSLA’s text, purpose, and this Court’s precedent.

**I. OCSLA’s Text and Purpose Require a Gap in Federal Law Before State Law Applies**

OCSLA provides that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is a limiting condition. It means state law operates only where federal law does not already occupy the field. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365–66 (1969) (holding that state law fills the void left by the inapplicability of the Death on the High Seas Act). Congress enacted OCSLA because federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953). The Act thus borrows state law to fill gaps, not to supplement or duplicate federal law.

This Court in *Rodrigue* applied state law only after concluding that federal maritime law did not cover fixed-platform accidents. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court emphasized that “any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law” was removed because the federal remedy was unavailable. *Id.* The Fifth Circuit has consistently required a “significant void or gap” in federal law before adopting state law under OCSLA. *Cont’l Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036; *Union Texas Petroleum Corp. v. PLT Eng’g, Inc.*, 895 F.2d 1043, 1047 (5th Cir. 1990). That test respects Congress’s intent that federal law remain the primary rule of decision on the Outer Continental Shelf.

**II. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Wage and Hour Laws**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) establishes a comprehensive federal regime governing minimum wage, overtime, and meal periods. *See Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight Sys., Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 740 (1981) (noting FLSA’s “comprehensive” scope). The Department of Labor has issued detailed regulations implementing the FLSA, including rules on meal periods. 29 C.F.R. § 785.19. These regulations leave no “significant void or gap” for state law to fill. *Cont’l Oil*, 417 F.2d at 1036.

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), permits states to enact more protective wage and hour laws within their own territory. But that clause does not authorize state law to apply on the Outer Continental Shelf, which is an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction. *See Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 27 n.8 (1988) (OCSLA creates “a federal enclave out of the OCS”). The savings clause merely preserves state authority where it already exists; it does not create authority where Congress has otherwise occupied the field. Because the FLSA already governs wage and hour claims on the OCS, there is no gap for California law to fill.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Interpretation Undermines Uniformity and Congressional Intent**

The Ninth Circuit held that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law leaves a gap. That approach would allow state law to supplement federal law even where federal law is comprehensive. It would create a patchwork of state wage and hour regimes on the OCS, defeating Congress’s goal of uniform federal law. *See Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 365 (OCSLA “adopts” state law “as federal law” to ensure consistency). The Fifth Circuit’s gap-filling test preserves uniformity by limiting state law to areas where federal law is silent.

The Ninth Circuit also misread *Rodrigue* as rejecting a gap requirement. In *Rodrigue*, the Court applied state law only because the federal Death on the High Seas Act did not cover fixed-platform deaths. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court did not hold that state law could apply concurrently with federal law. To the contrary, it recognized that state law fills the void left by federal law’s inapplicability. *Id.*

**IV. The Savings Clause Does Not Alter the OCSLA Analysis**

Newton argues that the FLSA’s savings clause makes state law “not inconsistent” with federal law. But the savings clause addresses only the relationship between state and federal law within a state’s jurisdiction. It does not speak to whether state law may apply on the OCS. The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision is the exclusive mechanism for adopting state law on the OCS. *See* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). That provision requires a gap in federal law, not merely the absence of inconsistency. Because the FLSA leaves no gap, California law cannot apply.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
