**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE TEXT OF § 523(a)(2)(A) LIMITS NONDISCHARGEABILITY TO THE VALUE ACTUALLY OBTAINED BY FRAUD**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge "any debt ... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) (emphasis added). The phrase "to the extent obtained by" is not surplusage; it is a measure of quantity that Congress inserted to cabin the exception to the value the debtor actually acquired through the fraudulent conduct. This Court has repeatedly instructed that "the plain meaning of legislation should be conclusive" in construing the Bankruptcy Code. United States v. Ron Pair Enterprises, Inc., 489 U.S. 235, 242 (1989). Where Congress used narrowing language in one part of a statute, courts must give it effect rather than read the provision as though the limitation were absent. See Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983) ("Where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another ... it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.").

Congress could have written § 523(a)(2)(A) to except from discharge "any liability arising from" fraud, or "any debt for damages caused by" fraud. It did not. It chose instead to except a debt only "to the extent obtained by" fraud—language that ties nondischargeability to the amount of money, property, or services the debtor actually received through the misrepresentation. The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this textual limitation in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 198–99 (9th Cir. 1991), holding that a jury's punitive-damages award was dischargeable because it was not itself "obtained by" the debtor's fraud; only the compensatory portion tied to what the debtor actually acquired fell within the exception. The same logic governs here: the treble-damages multiplier, attorney's fees, and costs awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act were not money "obtained by" Cohen's conduct. They are statutory sanctions layered on top of the underlying overcharge, triggered by state law rather than by anything Cohen received from respondents.

**II. THE FRESH-START POLICY OF THE BANKRUPTCY CODE AND THE TRADITIONAL RULE OF NARROW CONSTRUCTION SUPPORT THIS READING**

This Court has long recognized that the central purpose of the bankruptcy laws is to give the debtor "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). Exceptions to that fresh start are not to be read expansively. As this Court held in Gleason v. Thaw, 236 U.S. 558, 562 (1915), exceptions to discharge "are to be confined to those plainly expressed" in the statute, for "the act should be liberally construed in favor of the debtor." That interpretive principle traces to Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877), where this Court cautioned that terms such as "fraud" in discharge statutes must be construed with restraint and applied only where the statutory language plainly requires it, lest the "fresh start" that bankruptcy promises be swallowed by expansive readings of narrow exceptions.

Consistent with these principles, this Court in Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991), reaffirmed that while creditors alleging fraud need prove their case only by a preponderance of the evidence, the "fresh start" policy remains available to "the honest but unfortunate debtor," id. at 286–87, and exceptions to that policy must not be stretched beyond their textual bounds. Grogan did not hold—and no decision of this Court has held—that once any fraud is shown, every dollar of a resulting state-law judgment, however punitive or multiplied, becomes automatically nondischargeable. To adopt that view would convert a narrow, quantitatively limited exception into an open-ended forfeiture of the fresh start whenever a state legislature happens to attach a damages multiplier to its consumer-protection statute. Nothing in the text Congress enacted commands that result.

**III. THE ELEMENTS-BASED FRAUD INQUIRY THIS COURT HAS ESTABLISHED CONFIRMS THAT THE EXCEPTION TRACKS WHAT THE DEBTOR RECEIVED, NOT COLLATERAL STATE-LAW PENALTIES**

In Field v. Mans, 516 U.S. 59, 69–70 (1995), this Court examined § 523(a)(2)(A) closely, explaining that Congress imported the common-law elements of fraud—including actual and justifiable reliance—into the exception because the provision targets debts traceable to the debtor's deceptive procurement of value from the creditor. That focus on what was procured, not on whatever remedial multiplier a state legislature later attaches to the wrong, confirms that the exception's boundary is measured by what the debtor "obtained," precisely as the statutory text states. Treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are punitive in function—they are designed to deter and punish, not to measure what Cohen received from respondents. See N.J. Stat. Ann. § 56:8-19. Such sanctions may be legitimate as a matter of state consumer-protection policy, but they are not debts "obtained by" fraud within the meaning of the federal exception, and Congress's chosen words should not be read to reach them.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, only the $31,382.50 in overcharged rent that Cohen actually obtained from respondents should be excepted from discharge under § 523(a)(2)(A); the treble-damages, fee, and cost components of the state-court judgment remain dischargeable, and the judgment below should be reversed in relevant part.
