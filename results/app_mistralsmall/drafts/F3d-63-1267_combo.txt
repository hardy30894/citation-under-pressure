**ARGUMENT**

**I. STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES UNDER THE ROBINSON-PATMAN ACT, THEREBY PRECLUDING RECOVERY OF TREBLE DAMAGES.**

To recover damages under the Clayton Act, a plaintiff must prove actual injury attributable to the antitrust violation. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). The Supreme Court has long held that "[t]he mere fact that a buyer has not paid the same price as others or that he has sustained a loss of business is not in itself sufficient" to establish damages under the Robinson-Patman Act. *Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, 485 (1977) (quoting *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, 395 U.S. 100, 123 (1969)). Instead, the plaintiff must demonstrate "antitrust injury," which is "injury of the type the antitrust laws were intended to prevent and that flows from that which makes defendants' acts unlawful." *Brunswick*, 429 U.S. at 489.

Here, Stelwagon failed to present sufficient evidence to establish actual damages resulting from Tarmac’s alleged price discrimination. The jury’s award was based on speculation and unsupported inferences rather than competent proof of lost profits or other compensable harm. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946) (holding that damages must be proven with reasonable certainty and cannot be based on mere conjecture). The record contains no competent evidence that Stelwagon lost sales, profits, or market share directly attributable to Tarmac’s pricing practices. *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931) (requiring proof of damages with reasonable certainty and rejecting awards based on speculation).

Moreover, Stelwagon’s damages model improperly assumed that all sales lost to competitors receiving preferential pricing were directly caused by the alleged discrimination. This approach ignores intervening market factors and the possibility that competitors may have gained business for reasons unrelated to price. *Eastman Kodak Co. v. Southern Photo Materials Co.*, 273 U.S. 359, 379 (1927) (holding that damages must be proximately caused by the defendant’s conduct). The Supreme Court has cautioned that antitrust plaintiffs cannot recover for losses that "might have occurred" absent the violation. *Perma Life Mufflers, Inc. v. International Parts Corp.*, 392 U.S. 134, 145 (1968) (quoting *Story Parchment*, 282 U.S. at 563).

Because Stelwagon failed to prove actual damages, it is precluded from recovering treble damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act. *Brunswick*, 429 U.S. at 489 (holding that plaintiffs must prove actual injury to recover damages under the antitrust laws). The district court’s judgment on the Robinson-Patman claim must therefore be vacated.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC’S MOTIONS FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ROBINSON-PATMAN CLAIM.**

A plaintiff asserting a Robinson-Patman claim must prove three elements: (1) the defendant discriminated in price between different purchasers of commodities of like grade and quality; (2) the discrimination occurred in interstate commerce; and (3) the discrimination caused or threatened to cause injury to competition. *FTC v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 363 U.S. 536, 543 (1960). Here, Stelwagon failed to prove the third element—competitive injury—because it did not demonstrate that Tarmac’s pricing practices harmed competition in the relevant market.

The Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff must show "a reasonable possibility" that the discrimination may substantially lessen competition or tend to create a monopoly. *Standard Oil Co. v. FTC*, 340 U.S. 231, 249 (1951). Stelwagon’s evidence fell far short of this standard. The record contains no proof that Tarmac’s pricing practices affected competition among distributors or that Stelwagon’s competitors gained an unfair advantage. *Moore v. Mead’s Fine Bread Co.*, 348 U.S. 115, 119 (1954) (holding that a Robinson-Patman plaintiff must prove competitive injury, not merely injury to itself).

Furthermore, Tarmac’s pricing practices were justified under the "meeting competition" defense of § 13(a) of the Robinson-Patman Act. *FTC v. A.E. Staley Mfg. Co.*, 324 U.S. 746, 759-60 (1945) (holding that a seller may lawfully meet a competitor’s lower price in good faith). Tarmac presented evidence that it reduced prices to Standard and Celotex in response to competitive pressures, and Stelwagon failed to rebut this defense. *Great Atlantic & Pacific Tea Co. v. FTC*, 340 U.S. 817, 824 (1951) (holding that the meeting competition defense is available if the seller acted in good faith).

Because Stelwagon failed to prove competitive injury and Tarmac’s pricing practices were justified under the meeting competition defense, the district court erred in denying Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the Robinson-Patman claim. *Anheuser-Busch*, 363 U.S. at 543.

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE BREACH OF CONTRACT CLAIM.**

The district court erred in enforcing an oral agreement that violated the Statute of Frauds. Under Pennsylvania law, contracts for the sale of goods for a price of $500 or more must be in writing. 13 Pa. Cons. Stat. Ann. § 2-201 (1959). Stelwagon’s alleged exclusive distributorship agreement with Tarmac was for an indefinite duration and involved substantial sales, thereby falling within the Statute of Frauds. *Criswell v. Western Union Tel. Co.*, 176 Pa. 280, 35 A. 143 (1896) (holding that oral agreements for the sale of goods must be in writing if they cannot be performed within one year).

Moreover, the district court erred in allowing the jury to infer the existence of an oral agreement from vague and contradictory testimony. *McCormick v. Philadelphia Transp. Co.*, 379 Pa. 359, 108 A.2d 787 (1954) (holding that oral agreements must be proven by clear and convincing evidence). The evidence presented at trial was insufficient to support the jury’s finding of a breach of contract. *Restatement (Second) of Contracts* § 139 (1959) (requiring proof of an enforceable contract by clear and convincing evidence).

Because Stelwagon failed to prove the existence of a valid, enforceable contract, the district court erred in denying Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the breach of contract claim.
