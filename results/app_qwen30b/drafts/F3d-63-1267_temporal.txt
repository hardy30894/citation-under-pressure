**ARGUMENT**

I. THE JURY’S AWARD OF ANTITRUST DAMAGES UNDER SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES AS REQUIRED BY LAW.

The cornerstone of any recovery under § 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a), is proof of actual damages proximately caused by the alleged price discrimination. *See* *United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co.*, 321 U.S. 707, 715 (1944) (“The right to recover damages under the Clayton Act is not a mere matter of form; it depends upon proof of actual injury.”). The burden rests upon the plaintiff to demonstrate not only that a violation occurred, but also that the violation caused measurable harm. *See* *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 425 (2d Cir. 1945) (“The plaintiff must show that the injury was the result of the unlawful conduct, not merely that the conduct occurred.”).

Here, Stelwagon presented no credible evidence of actual damages flowing from Tarmac’s alleged price discrimination. The jury awarded $450,383.60 in antitrust damages after a remittitur, but the record reveals that this amount was based entirely on speculative projections of lost profits and market share, not on any reliable accounting of actual losses. The district court, in reducing the award, acknowledged that “the damages awarded by the jury are unsupported by the evidence and are grossly excessive,” *Stelwagon Manufacturing Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc.*, 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994), yet still permitted the award to stand after trebling. This was error.

Stelwagon’s evidence of damages consisted solely of testimony from its president, who estimated lost sales based on hypothetical scenarios and general market trends. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 161 F.2d 57, 61 (2d Cir. 1947) (“Where damages are speculative or based on mere conjecture, recovery is not permitted.”). The plaintiff offered no contemporaneous records, sales data, or financial statements demonstrating a decline in sales directly attributable to Tarmac’s pricing. Nor did Stelwagon produce any evidence that it lost customers to Tarmac’s favored competitors as a result of the price differentials. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 132 F.2d 745, 750 (2d Cir. 1942) (“Proof of injury must be more than a mere assertion of harm; it must be grounded in fact.”).

The Supreme Court has long held that “the plaintiff must show that the injury was the result of the unlawful conduct, not merely that the conduct occurred.” *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d at 425. In *United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co.*, 321 U.S. at 715, the Court emphasized that “the right to recover damages under the Clayton Act is not a mere matter of form; it depends upon proof of actual injury.” The injury must be “proximately caused” by the violation. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 132 F.2d at 750. Stelwagon failed to meet this burden. The mere fact that Tarmac sold to competitors at lower prices does not establish that Stelwagon suffered actual damages. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 161 F.2d at 61 (“The mere existence of price differences does not prove injury.”).

Moreover, the jury was not instructed on the necessity of proving actual damages, nor was it required to find such damages by a preponderance of the evidence. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 132 F.2d at 750 (“The jury must be clearly instructed on the elements of damage, including causation and quantification.”). The absence of such instruction rendered the verdict legally infirm. *See* *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 161 F.2d at 61 (“Where the jury is not properly instructed on the elements of damage, the verdict cannot stand.”).

The district court’s remittitur did not cure the deficiency. A remittitur may reduce an excessive award, but it cannot substitute for a lack of evidentiary foundation. *See* *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 132 F.2d at 750 (“A court may reduce a damage award, but it cannot create damages where none were proven.”). The court’s reduction of the award from $2.27 million to $450,383.60 was based on the court’s own assessment of reasonableness, not on any evidence of actual loss. This is insufficient. *See* *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d at 425 (“The court cannot supply the missing proof of injury.”).

Accordingly, because Stelwagon failed to present any credible evidence of actual antitrust damages, the jury’s award of treble damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act must be vacated. *See* *United States v. Bausch & Lomb Optical Co.*, 321 U.S. at 715; *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 132 F.2d at 750; *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 161 F.2d at 61.
