**ARGUMENT**

The District’s categorical exclusion of Lamb’s Chapel’s religious film series from school facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. By opening its property to a broad array of community groups, the District created a public forum from which it may not exclude speech based on its religious viewpoint.

**I. The District Created a Public Forum, and Once It Did So, It Could Not Exclude Speech Based on Religious Content.**

The District’s policy and practice allowed a wide range of secular organizations—including the “Southern Harmonizers,” a “Psychology and The Unknown” lecture series, and other civic and cultural groups—to use school premises for expressive activities. This affirmative opening of the forum to the public for discussion of diverse topics transformed the property into a public forum. *See Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939) (streets and parks “have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public”). When the government opens its property for expressive activities, it may not exclude speech based on its content or viewpoint. *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 163 (1939) (holding that a municipality cannot abridge the liberty of the press by prohibiting distribution of literature). The District’s denial of Lamb’s Chapel’s application solely because the films were “church related” is a classic content-based restriction, forbidden under the First Amendment.

**II. Religious Speech Is Fully Protected Within a Public Forum.**

The First Amendment’s protection of free speech extends to religious expression. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303 (1940) (holding that “Thus the Amendment embraces two concepts, — freedom to believe and freedom to act”). In *Lovell v. Griffin*, 303 U.S. 444, 451 (1938), this Court recognized that “the liberty of the press” is not confined to newspapers but “embraces pamphlets and leaflets” and other printed matter. The District cannot treat religious speech as a disfavored subject. *Saia v. New York*, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948) (striking down a prior restraint on religious speech as “It is an abridgment of the freedom of speech”). Because the District permitted other non-religious organizations to discuss family values and child rearing, excluding Lamb’s Chapel solely because of the religious perspective of its films is constitutionally impermissible.

**III. The District’s Policy Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral; It Discriminates Against Religion.**

The District’s rule barring “any group for religious purposes” does not merely exclude speech that endorses a particular religion; it excludes all religious expression. This constitutes viewpoint discrimination. *Niemotko v. Maryland*, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951) (invalidating a permit system that was “The permit system was administered to deny religious groups the right to use the park for their meetings” while permitting secular groups). In *Kunz v. New York*, 340 U.S. 290, 294 (1951), this Court struck down a similar ordinance that “It gives an administrative official too much discretion” to exclude religious speech. The District’s absolute ban on religious use is not a reasonable time, place, or manner regulation; it is a content-based exclusion that suppresses expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker’s view. *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77, 88 (1949) (plurality opinion) (upholding a ban on sound trucks but noting that “The right of free speech is not absolute at all times and under all circumstances”). The District cannot claim that its policy is viewpoint-neutral when it has permitted secular discussion of family issues but denied the same discussion from a religious perspective.

**IV. The District’s Justification—Avoiding Establishment Clause Violations—Does Not Justify Censorship.**

The District’s argument that permitting religious use would violate the Establishment Clause is unavailing. The government may not suppress religious speech in a public forum on the ground that it might be perceived as endorsing religion. *Fowler v. Rhode Island*, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953) (holding that a city could not prohibit religious meetings in a public park while permitting secular meetings, as such discrimination “It is not the kind of regulation that the First Amendment permits”). *Marsh v. Alabama*, 326 U.S. 501, 509 (1946), held that “the more an owner, for his advantage, opens up his property for use by the public in general, the more do his rights become circumscribed by the statutory and constitutional rights of those who use it.” Here, the District opened its facilities to the public, and the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech in a limited public forum. *See Brown v. Louisiana*, 383 U.S. 131, 142 (1966) (plurality opinion) (a public library could not exclude peaceful protesters solely because of the content of their speech).

**V. Conclusion**

For over a century, this Court has held that the right of free speech may not be abridged on the ground that the speech is religious. *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942) (holding that certain well-defined and narrowly limited classes of speech may be regulated, but religious speech is not among them). The District’s policy—denying access to a public forum solely because of the religious viewpoint of the speech—violates the First Amendment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
