The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to plead guilty or waive counsel. The constitutional minimum is the standard articulated in Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam): whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” See also Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171 (1975); Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375, 387 (1966).

This Court has never imposed a more demanding test for the validity of a guilty plea or a waiver of counsel. In McMann v. Richardson, 397 U.S. 759, 766 (1970), the Court stated that a guilty plea is valid when it is “a voluntary and intelligent choice among the alternative courses of action open to the defendant.” The same inquiry governs waivers of counsel. See Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975) (waiver must be “knowing and intelligent”). Nothing in these decisions or in North Carolina v. Alford, 400 U.S. 25 (1970), or Henderson v. Morgan, 426 U.S. 637 (1976), suggests that a defendant must demonstrate a capacity for “reasoned choice” beyond the Dusky formulation.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule conflicts with the principle that a defendant who is competent to stand trial is also competent to decide whether to forgo trial. See Demosthenes v. Baal, 495 U.S. 731, 735-36 (1990) (per curiam) (upholding state-court finding of competency to waive further proceedings under the Dusky standard). Requiring a higher threshold would force trial courts to conduct duplicative inquiries and would undermine the finality of guilty pleas that satisfy constitutional minima. See Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) (recognizing the strong presumption of regularity attaching to counseled decisions).

The record here shows that Moran was examined by two psychiatrists who concluded he possessed the abilities required by Dusky. The state post-conviction court credited those findings. Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), those factual determinations are presumed correct. The Ninth Circuit’s imposition of an additional “reasoned choice” requirement was therefore erroneous as a matter of federal constitutional law.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
