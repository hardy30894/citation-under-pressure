The post-removal-period statute does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), is ambiguous and does not explicitly permit indefinite detention. To avoid serious constitutional concerns, it must be construed to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Constitution imposes limits on the government's power to detain individuals. In Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), the Court held that the government may not indefinitely detain aliens without a judicial trial. The Court stated that "it is not competent for Congress to arbitrarily deport an alien because he has committed a crime, without giving him an opportunity to be heard." Id. at 237.

Similarly, in Yamataya v. Fisher, 189 U.S. 86 (1903), the Court emphasized the importance of procedural due process in deportation proceedings. The Court held that an alien has the right to a fair hearing before being deported. Id. at 101.

In this case, the government's indefinite detention of Zadvydas raises similar constitutional concerns. The detention is not limited to a reasonable period necessary to effect removal, but rather is potentially indefinite. This is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in Wong Wing and Yamataya.

Furthermore, the government's authority to detain aliens is not unlimited. In United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod, 264 U.S. 131 (1924), the Court held that an alien's detention pending deportation must be reasonable and not excessive. The Court stated that "the detention of an alien ... is not to be prolonged indefinitely, but is subject to judicial review." Id. at 134.

In Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), although the Court upheld the detention of an excludable alien, it emphasized that the alien's status as an excludable alien was critical to its decision. The Court distinguished between excludable and deportable aliens, noting that the latter have a "right to remain and live" in the United States. Id. at 212 (quoting Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding, 344 U.S. 590, 596 (1953)).

Here, Zadvydas is a deportable alien who has been lawfully admitted to the United States. His detention is not justified by the same reasoning that applied in Mezei. Instead, the principles of Wong Wing, Yamataya, and Tisi suggest that his detention must be limited to a reasonable period.

Therefore, the post-removal-period statute should be construed to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, beyond which detention is not authorized. This interpretation is consistent with the Constitution and avoids the serious constitutional concerns that would arise from indefinite detention.
