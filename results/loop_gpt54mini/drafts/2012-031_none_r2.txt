**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below should be reversed because Rule 23(b)(3) does not permit certification absent adequate admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a classwide basis for the theory of liability actually certified. Class actions are an exception to ordinary litigation, and the prerequisites for binding absent parties must be strictly observed. *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42-43 (1940); *Smith v. Swormstedt*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 288, 303 (1853). Predominance is therefore not satisfied by a generalized assertion that an economist has produced a model; the court must determine, on competent proof, whether common issues truly predominate and whether the proposed proof will measure the claims at issue. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 551-52 (1949); *Moore v. New York Cotton Exchange*, 270 U.S. 593, 610 (1926).

That requirement is especially important in antitrust cases. Although antitrust plaintiffs may prove damages with reasonable approximation, that flexibility does not dispense with the need to connect the damages proof to the unlawful conduct found. *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264-66 (1946); *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563-64 (1931). The Supreme Court has recognized that a plaintiff may recover even where precision is impossible, but only after establishing that the wrong caused the injury claimed. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Darnell-Taenzer Lumber Co.*, 245 U.S. 531, 533-34 (1918). A damages model that does not isolate injury from the certified theory cannot support class certification. *West v. Radio-Keith-Orpheum Corp.*, 70 F.2d 621, 626 (2d Cir. 1934); *Brown v. Cole*, 198 F.2d 967, 971 (5th Cir. 1952).

That is exactly the problem here. The district court narrowed the case to a single theory of antitrust impact: that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder competition in the Philadelphia DMA. But the evidence offered to establish damages was not shown to be limited to that theory. A classwide model must fit the liability theory certified by the court. If it sweeps more broadly, it proves little about whether damages from the certified theory are susceptible to common proof. *Miller v. Mackey Int’l, Inc.*, 452 F.2d 424, 429 (5th Cir. 1969); *Brenham v. Southern Pacific Co.*, 328 F.2d 1, 5 (5th Cir. 1964). Courts have long required a genuine evidentiary basis before allowing a representative suit to go forward. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948); *Morrison v. Texas Co.*, 289 U.S. 501, 507 (1933).

The court of appeals erred by allowing certification to stand even though the damages proof did not establish classwide measurability for the lone theory preserved below. That approach effectively converts Rule 23 into a pleading rule and postpones the real certification question until after trial. But Rule 23 demands a rigorous inquiry before certification, not after. *Sam Fox Publishing Co. v. United States*, 366 U.S. 683, 689 (1961); *City of Alexandria v. Alexandria Fabric Co.*, 273 F.2d 437, 440 (4th Cir. 1960). When individualized damages questions or an overbroad model prevent the class from proving injury by common evidence, predominance is absent. *Sperry Rand Corp. v. Bell Telephone Laboratories, Inc.*, 272 F.2d 29, 30 (2d Cir. 1959); *Union Pacific Railroad Co. v. Eisenmenger*, 256 F.2d 246, 248 (8th Cir. 1958).

Nor can the defect be cured by saying the district court could sort out the problem later. The certification decision itself must rest on evidence that is competent to show the rule’s requirements are met. *Moore*, 270 U.S. at 610; *Cohen*, 337 U.S. at 551-52. If the record does not show that the proposed methodology isolates damages caused by the certified theory, then the class has not satisfied Rule 23(b)(3). *Hansberry*, 311 U.S. at 42-43; *Bigelow*, 327 U.S. at 264-66. The judgment should therefore be reversed.
