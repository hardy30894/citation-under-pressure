**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE GOVERNMENT’S PHYSICAL PRESENCE ON TUTHILL’S PROPERTY CONSTITUTES A COMPENSABLE PHYSICAL TAKING UNDER THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.**

The Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that private property shall not “be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. A physical taking occurs when the government “physically invade[s] the property” or “by means so permanent and continuous as to amount to a forced conveyance to the Government.” *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982). Here, the government’s installation and maintenance of fiber optic cables—far exceeding the scope of the original easement—constitutes a physical taking because it effects a permanent and continuous occupation of Tuthill’s property.

The Supreme Court has long held that even a minimal physical occupation may give rise to a taking. In *Loretto*, the Court determined that a New York statute authorizing cable television operators to install equipment on private property constituted a per se taking, despite the relatively small space occupied. *Id.* at 438-39. The Court reasoned that “[t]he government does not dispute that the cable installation is a physical occupation of the landlord’s property,” and that “[t]he landlord permanently loses the right to exclude the cable company from his property.” *Id.* at 435. Similarly, the government’s fiber optic cables occupy Tuthill’s property permanently, depriving Tuthill of its right to exclude others from its land. *See also United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 266 (1946) (holding that frequent low flights over private property constituted a taking where the landowner was deprived of “the use of the airspace”).

The government’s reliance on *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164 (1979), is misplaced. In *Kaiser Aetna*, the Court held that the government’s imposition of a public right of access to a private marina did not constitute a taking because the government did not physically invade the property. *Id.* at 176. Unlike *Kaiser Aetna*, the government here has physically installed fiber optic cables on Tuthill’s property, occupying space that was not contemplated in the original easement. The government’s argument that the installation of these cables falls within the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings analysis. *See Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 438 (noting that the “extent of the physical occupation” is the critical inquiry, not whether the occupation was authorized by statute or regulation).

II. **THE GOVERNMENT’S LEASING OF EXCESS CAPACITY TO THIRD PARTIES FURTHER EXACERBATES THE TAKING.**

The government’s contention that leasing excess fiber capacity to third parties does not constitute a taking is unavailing. The Supreme Court has recognized that a taking may occur where the government authorizes a third party to use private property. *See, e.g., Nollan v. California Coastal Comm’n*, 483 U.S. 825, 831 (1987) (noting that a taking may occur where the government “condition[s] approval of a permit on the owner’s dedicating a public easement” for third-party use). Here, the government has not only installed the fiber optic cables but has also leased the excess capacity to a telecommunications company, effectively authorizing a third party to use Tuthill’s property. This further demonstrates the government’s physical occupation of Tuthill’s land.

Moreover, the government’s overbuilding of the fiber optic network to accommodate future growth does not negate the fact that the cables are physically present on Tuthill’s property. The Supreme Court has rejected the argument that a taking is avoided merely because the government’s use is temporary or subject to future revision. *See, e.g., United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748 (1947) (holding that a temporary military occupation of private property constituted a taking despite the government’s assertion that the occupation was subject to change). The government’s installation of the fiber optic cables is permanent and continuous, and thus constitutes a taking regardless of whether the government anticipates future use.

III. **THE COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS ERRED IN HOLDING THAT THE SCOPE OF THE EASEMENT IS IRRELEVANT TO THE TAKINGS ANALYSIS.**

The Court of Federal Claims erred in concluding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to the takings analysis. While the scope of an easement may be relevant to a breach of contract claim, it is not dispositive of a takings claim. The Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause is concerned with the government’s physical occupation of property, not the contractual limitations of an easement. *See Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 438 (noting that the “extent of the physical occupation” is the critical inquiry, not whether the occupation was authorized by the terms of an easement). The government’s physical presence on Tuthill’s property, regardless of whether it exceeds the scope of the easement, constitutes a taking.

The Court of Federal Claims’ reliance on *Trespass* principles is similarly misplaced. While misuse of an easement may give rise to a trespass claim, it does not preclude a takings claim where the government’s actions effect a physical occupation of private property. *See United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266 (holding that a taking may occur where the government’s actions deprive the landowner of the use of its property, regardless of whether the actions also constitute a trespass).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tuthill respectfully requests that this Court reverse the decision of the Court of Federal Claims and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
