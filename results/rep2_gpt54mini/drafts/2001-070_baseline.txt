**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment does not permit the execution of persons with mental retardation. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court declined to announce a categorical bar at that time, but it did so because the record then did not demonstrate the “consensus” necessary to conclude that the practice violated contemporary standards of decency. *Id.* at 331-34. The Court expressly left open the possibility that “evolving standards of decency” could lead to a different result. *Id.* at 330 (quoting *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958)). The question now is whether that evolution has occurred. It has.

The Court’s Eighth Amendment cases require that punishment reflect society’s “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101. In determining those standards, the Court looks first to objective evidence, especially legislative enactments and actual sentencing practices. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 331; *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 406-10 (1986). That method points decisively toward a national consensus against executing persons with mental retardation.

Since *Penry*, the States have moved in one direction. A substantial and growing number of States have enacted laws barring the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and others have not authorized the practice in the first place. That legislative trend is significant because the Court has repeatedly treated state enactments as “the most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values.” *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 331; *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989). In *Ford*, the Court found a consensus against executing the insane even though the evidence was not uniform. 477 U.S. at 409-10. The trend here is stronger and more settled than in *Ford*.

The same conclusion follows from the reasons legislatures have given. Mental retardation reduces culpability in three ways that matter for the death penalty: it diminishes the offender’s ability to understand and process information, to engage in logical reasoning, and to control impulses; it also increases the risk of false confessions, poor assistance to counsel, and unreliable testimony. Those characteristics undermine the retributive and deterrent justifications for capital punishment. The Court recognized in *Penry* that evidence of mental retardation “is relevant to a defendant’s moral culpability.” 492 U.S. at 323-24. But *Penry* also accepted, in part because of then-existing law, that juries could consider such evidence in mitigation. *Id.* at 328-29. Experience since then has shown that individualized mitigation does not adequately protect this class of defendants from execution. The fundamental issue is categorical: some offenders are so diminished in culpability that death is disproportionate punishment under the Eighth Amendment. See *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977); *Enmund v. Florida*, 458 U.S. 782, 798-801 (1982).

The Court’s capital punishment jurisprudence also supports a categorical rule here. In *Ford*, the Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids executing the insane because such an execution serves little retributive purpose and “measurably contribute[s] nothing to the retributive goals of the criminal law.” 477 U.S. at 407-08. The same is true for persons with mental retardation, who, like the insane, are less able to understand the reasons for punishment, to assist counsel, and to comport themselves with the norms the law seeks to enforce. The Eighth Amendment does not require formal blindness to these realities.

Nor does the absence of a federal statute matter. The Constitution’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is not contingent on congressional action. The Court has repeatedly enforced it through constitutional adjudication when objective indicia show that a punishment offends contemporary standards. *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 100-01; *Ford*, 477 U.S. at 405-06. Here, the objective indicia, the purposes of capital punishment, and the diminished culpability of the affected offenders all converge on the same answer: executing persons with mental retardation is cruel and unusual.

Atkins therefore should be reversed. The Virginia Supreme Court relied on *Penry* as though it foreclosed Atkins’ claim. But *Penry* did not freeze the Eighth Amendment in time. It instructed that the Court must assess whether the understanding of decency has evolved. 492 U.S. at 330-31. It has. The Constitution now prohibits the execution of mentally retarded persons.
