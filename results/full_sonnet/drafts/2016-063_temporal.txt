**ARGUMENT**

The Civil Service Reform Act directs that "[c]ases of discrimination" arising from actions appealable to the Merit Systems Protection Board—so-called mixed cases—be reviewed by civil action in federal district court, not by petition in the Federal Circuit. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The Board's disposition of Mr. Perry's mixed case on jurisdictional grounds does not remove it from that statutory category. Review belongs in district court.

**I. The Statute's Purpose, Not a Technical Parsing of the Ground for Dismissal, Should Govern the Choice of Forum.**

Courts have sometimes parsed whether a Board dismissal rests on "the merits," "procedure," or "jurisdiction" to decide whether the mixed-case exception applies. But this Court has long cautioned against letting the literal words of a jurisdictional statute defeat its evident purpose. In *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States*, 143 U.S. 457 (1892), the Court explained that "a thing may be within the letter of the statute and yet not within the statute, because not within its spirit, nor within the intention of its makers." *Id.* at 459. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court reaffirmed that although "there is . . . no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes," when "that plain meaning has led to absurd or futile results," courts must look "beyond the words to the purpose of the act." *Id.* at 543.

Congress's purpose in channeling "[c]ases of discrimination" to district court was to guarantee federal employees plenary, de novo review of discrimination claims, unencumbered by the deferential, record-bound review the Federal Circuit affords ordinary civil-service rulings. That purpose applies whether the Board declines to reach the discrimination claim because it finds the underlying action outside its jurisdiction or because it finds the appeal untimely. In either circumstance the discrimination allegation has never been adjudicated, and the employee should be left exactly where the statute contemplated: in district court, where the claim can be tried anew.

**II. Splitting a Single Controversy Between Two Courts Based on the Board's Threshold Ruling Contravenes the Settled Preference for Unitary, Practical Review.**

This Court has repeatedly refused to construe jurisdictional and review statutes to fragment a single controversy into duplicative proceedings in different courts. In *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949), the Court instructed that such requirements be given "a practical rather than a technical construction," *id.* at 546, precisely so litigants are not forced into piecemeal, multi-forum litigation of what is, in substance, one dispute. The same concern governs here. If review of Board jurisdictional dismissals were carved out to the Federal Circuit while review of merits and procedural dismissals of the same type of claim went to district court, a single mixed-case appeal could be split apart depending on which of two closely related threshold rulings the Board happened to issue. Because voluntariness—the jurisdictional question in this case—frequently turns on the same allegations of discriminatory coercion that comprise the merits of the discrimination claim, a jurisdictional/merits divide invites relitigation of overlapping facts in two courts applying two different standards of review. Congress cannot be presumed to have intended so unworkable a scheme.

**III. The Remedial Character of Federal Employment-Discrimination Protections Counsels a Construction That Preserves District-Court Access.**

This Court has long read remedial legislation enacted to protect individuals from discrimination, or to supply a private remedy, so as to effectuate rather than frustrate its protective purpose. In *J.I. Case Co. v. Borak*, 377 U.S. 426 (1964), the Court adopted the construction "consistent with the general purpose" of the statute, treating the private remedy as "a necessary supplement" to agency enforcement. *Id.* at 432–33. And in *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co.*, 323 U.S. 192 (1944), the Court construed a federal statute to ensure that an aggrieved employee retained a judicial remedy against discriminatory treatment rather than being left without recourse. *Id.* at 207.

Section 7702(a)(1)'s "[c]ases of discrimination" provision is remedial in exactly this sense: it guarantees federal employees a forum—district court—competent to adjudicate discrimination claims on a full record, with discovery and trial, rather than confining them to the deferential review the Federal Circuit provides for ordinary personnel disputes. Reading the statute to strip away that guarantee whenever the Board resolves the antecedent voluntariness question against the employee would convert a remedial guarantee into a trap, turning access to a plenary forum on a ruling the employee never had a full opportunity to litigate outside the administrative record.

For these reasons, the Board's dismissal of Mr. Perry's mixed case for lack of jurisdiction remains, in substance and in law, a "[c]ase of discrimination" within the meaning of § 7702(a)(1), reviewable in district court under § 7703(b)(2).
