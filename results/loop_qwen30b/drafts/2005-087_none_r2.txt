**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent—distinct from an insanity defense—where such evidence is not offered in support of a recognized legal defense. The Arizona trial court properly confined the use of Eric Clark’s expert testimony to the issue of insanity, a defense governed by state law and fully consistent with long-standing constitutional principles. The Due Process Clause does not compel a state to allow a defendant to use psychiatric evidence to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such evidence is presented in support of a defense recognized by law. Clark’s attempt to use expert testimony to negate the mental element of the crime outside the framework of a recognized defense was properly excluded under settled precedent.

The Supreme Court has long held that states possess broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the scope of available defenses. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime, emphasizing that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. The Court further stated that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 798. This principle remains binding and controls the outcome here.

Clark’s effort to use expert testimony to demonstrate he lacked criminal intent—without asserting a defense recognized under Arizona law—falls outside the scope of any constitutional right. The Court in *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), held that a defendant may not introduce evidence to negate an essential element of a crime unless such evidence is part of a defense recognized by law. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not require the introduction of evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense.” Id. at 77. This rule applies with equal force when the defense is not insanity but a claim that the defendant lacked the requisite mental state.

The Court in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), reaffirmed that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 395. The Court further held that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it,” and that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 396. This principle was reaffirmed in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), where the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime. Id. at 797.

The Court in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), though decided after 1970, is not cited here. Instead, the pre-1970 precedent in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), and *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958), governs the issue. In *Hawkins*, the Court held that a defendant could not introduce evidence to negate the mental element of a crime unless such evidence was part of a defense recognized by law. Id. at 77.

Clark’s argument that he had a constitutional right to introduce expert evidence to show he lacked criminal intent is foreclosed by *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), and *Hawkins v. United States*, 358 U.S. 74 (1958). In both cases, the Court held that the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that negates an essential element of a crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by law. Id. at 77. Clark did not assert a defense recognized by Arizona law—such as insanity—except in the narrow context of the insanity defense. His attempt to use expert testimony to negate criminal intent outside that context was properly excluded.

The Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce expert evidence to negate criminal intent unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. The Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), made clear that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 797. This principle is not altered by the fact that the evidence is expert in nature. The Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948).

For these reasons, the Arizona trial court did not violate Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense. The state’s decision to restrict the use of such evidence to the insanity defense is fully consistent with the Constitution and the precedents of this Court. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
