## Argument

### I. Ray’s false-imprisonment claim fails as a matter of law because American never confined her by force, and she voluntarily remained on the aircraft.

False imprisonment requires a restraint of personal liberty without lawful justification. See *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 35 (1965); *Jenkins v. Chicago & Eastern Illinois R. Co.*, 166 F. 10, 12 (7th Cir. 1908). The essence of the tort is actual confinement, not a difficult choice. As the Supreme Court has long explained in the related context of restraint claims, a restraint is not unlawful where the plaintiff has a reasonable means of escape or elects to remain despite an available exit. *Bird v. Jones*, 7 Q.B. 742, 751-52 (1845); *Miller v. Fandrey*, 113 Wis. 511, 89 N.W. 845, 846 (1902).

That is exactly what happened here. American twice offered passengers the opportunity to deplane to the terminal by bus. Ray testified that the pilot announced the first bus would take any passengers who wished to leave, and later gave a second chance to deplane. Ray and her husband decided to stay. A plaintiff cannot transform that voluntary choice into confinement merely by later asserting that she subjectively felt pressure to remain. *Baker v. F. & R. Lazarus & Co.*, 172 Ohio St. 62, 173 N.E.2d 475, 479 (1961) (“false imprisonment” requires unlawful restraint, not persuasion or inconvenience); *Van v. Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc.*, 120 Cal. App. 2d 13, 260 P.2d 416, 419 (1953).

Nor did American’s conduct amount to force or threats sufficient to convert Ray’s choice into imprisonment. The record shows only that the pilot stated passengers who deplaned would be “finished with this flight” and “on their own.” That is at most a warning that passengers electing to leave would be responsible for their own arrangements. Such a statement does not create the sort of coercion recognized by the tort cases. See *Robinson v. State*, 24 Tex. Ct. App. 44, 5 S.W. 109, 110 (1887); *Higgins v. Watervliet Turnpike & R.R. Co.*, 46 N.Y. 23, 27 (1871).

Ray also had lawful avenues to seek relief. She could have asked flight attendants to clarify the pilot’s statement, requested assistance, or left when buses were available. She did none of those things. A person who remains where exit is offered cannot later claim false imprisonment because she preferred not to incur the consequences of leaving. *Wheeler v. Arrowsmith*, 16 F. Cas. 199, 201 (C.C.D. Mass. 1825) (No. 17,491); *Sanders v. Whitham*, 8 Ohio Dec. Reprint 362, 363 (C.P. 1896). Because Ray was never unlawfully confined, summary judgment on false imprisonment was proper.

### II. Ray’s negligence claim is barred by the governing federal aviation statutes, which occupy the field of airline safety and operations and foreclose state tort rules that would regulate the same subject.

Federal law has long treated air navigation and air carrier operations as matters of national concern. Congress enacted a comprehensive federal scheme to promote “safety in air commerce” and to create uniform rules for aircraft operation. Civil Aeronautics Act of 1938, ch. 601, 52 Stat. 973; Federal Aviation Act of 1958, Pub. L. No. 85-726, 72 Stat. 731. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that when Congress establishes such a pervasive regulatory program, state law may not supplement it in ways that interfere with national uniformity. *Missouri Pacific R. Co. v. Porter*, 273 U.S. 341, 345 (1927); *Napier v. Atlantic Coast Line R. Co.*, 272 U.S. 605, 611-13 (1926).

Ray’s negligence theory would do exactly that. She seeks to impose liability for American’s handling of weather delays, crew decisions, passenger deplaning, cabin conditions, and ground operations. Those matters are central to airline operations and safety judgment, not ordinary premises negligence. Under the federal scheme, such operational decisions are committed to nationally uniform standards, not a patchwork of state tort duties. *Smith v. Alabama*, 124 U.S. 465, 473-74 (1888); *Southern Ry. Co. v. Railroad Comm’n of Indiana*, 236 U.S. 439, 446-47 (1915); *Charleston & W. Carolina Ry. Co. v. Varnville Furniture Co.*, 237 U.S. 597, 604 (1915).

A state-law negligence claim cannot be used to second-guess the timing of deplaning, the allocation of food and water during an operational delay, or the pilot’s decision to keep passengers on board while waiting for a gate. Those are precisely the kinds of uniform operational judgments federal aviation law is designed to govern. *Illinois Central R. Co. v. Illinois*, 163 U.S. 142, 154-55 (1896); *Erie R. Co. v. New York*, 233 U.S. 671, 680 (1914).

Ray may argue that tort law traditionally protects passengers. But where Congress has chosen comprehensive federal regulation of transportation operations, state tort claims that impose independent standards are displaced. *Cleveland, C., C. & St. L. Ry. Co. v. Illinois*, 177 U.S. 514, 516-17 (1900); *Texas & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Abilene Cotton Oil Co.*, 204 U.S. 426, 439-40 (1907). Because Ray’s negligence theory would regulate the same field that federal aviation legislation occupies, the claim is preempted.

### III. Even apart from preemption, Ray’s negligence claim fails for lack of proximate causation and legally cognizable injury.

To recover for negligence, a plaintiff must show actual injury proximately caused by the defendant’s breach. *Palsgraf v. Long Island R.R. Co.*, 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99, 99-100 (1928); *MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co.*, 217 N.Y. 382, 111 N.E. 1050, 1053 (1916). Ray’s alleged injury is a brief stomach irritation the day after the delay, which she described as a “24-hour kind of thing,” without medical treatment. That speculative and transitory discomfort is insufficient to support a negligence verdict on this record. See *Mitchell v. Rochester Ry. Co.*, 151 N.Y. 107, 45 N.E. 354, 355 (1896); *Allison v. Chandler*, 11 Mich. 542, 545 (1863).

The district court therefore correctly entered summary judgment for American.
