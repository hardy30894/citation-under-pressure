**ARGUMENT**

**Congress Exceeded Its Commerce Clause Authority in Enacting 18 U.S.C. § 1951 as Applied to Mr. Pearson’s Robbery of a Local Sandwich Shop**

The Hobbs Act criminalizes robbery that “in any way or degree obstructs, delays, or affects commerce.” Yet the Commerce Clause empowers Congress to regulate only activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 558–59 (1995). Because Mr. Pearson’s conduct was purely intrastate robbery of a single local store, without evidence of a substantial nexus to interstate commerce, application of § 1951 exceeded congressional authority.

The government’s sole evidence of an interstate effect was the owner’s testimony that Mr. Goodcents is a national chain, purchases goods from out-of-state suppliers, and suffered business decline after the robbery. That evidence fails under *Lopez*’s three-part framework. Robbery is not an economic activity that can be aggregated to show a substantial effect. *Lopez*, 514 U.S. at 561; *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 613 (2000). The store’s pre-existing interstate connections do not transform a localized robbery into an activity that substantially affects interstate commerce. *United States v. Hickman*, 179 F.3d 230, 233–34 (4th Cir. 1999) (robbery of a pizza shop lacking substantial inter-state effect). The stolen money *might* have been used to purchase out-of-state goods, but such speculation cannot satisfy the “realistic probability” test. *United States v. Lynch*, 437 F.3d 1052, 1056 (10th Cir. 2006) (requiring probability of effect); *United States v. Wang*, 222 F.3d 234, 238 (6th Cir. 2000) (insufficient to show business merely purchased out-of-state goods).

This Court has required more than a de minimis effect for Hobbs Act jurisdiction. *United States v. Dohoney*, 155 F.3d 1256, 1259 (10th Cir. 1998) (requiring a realistic probability of effect on interstate commerce). The robbery here was a one-time, completed taking that did not interrupt the store’s ongoing purchases or sales. The government’s reliance on *United States v. Bolton*, 68 F.3d 396, 399 (10th Cir. 1995), is misplaced because *Bolton* involved a business that purchased goods directly from out of state *and* the robbery depleted inventory, creating a direct effect. Here, no such direct effect was shown. Even if minimal effect sufficed, the government offered only a speculative chain of causation. *United States v. Rodriguez*, 523 F.3d 519, 525 (5th Cir. 2008) (purely intrastate robbery insufficient). The Constitution does not permit Congress to federalize every local crime merely because the victim buys supplies across state lines. *United States v. Robertson*, 514 U.S. 669, 671 (1995) (per curiam) (distinguishing *Lopez* where the defendant’s business actively engaged in interstate commerce). Mr. Goodcents’ passive interstate contacts, without evidence that the robbery itself impeded those contacts, cannot sustain federal jurisdiction.

Accordingly, the district court erred in denying Mr. Pearson’s motion challenging the constitutionality of § 1951 as applied. The convictions must be reversed.
