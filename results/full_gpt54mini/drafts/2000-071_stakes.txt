Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club violated the First Amendment because the school created a limited public forum and then engaged in viewpoint discrimination. When the government opens its property for use by private speakers, it may impose subject-matter limits, but it may not “regulate speech based on its substantive content or the message it conveys.” Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the University of Virginia, 515 U.S. 819, 828 (1995). In a limited public forum, restrictions must be reasonable and viewpoint neutral. Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District, 508 U.S. 384, 392-93 (1993); Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

Milford’s policy allowed district residents to use school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” Once Milford opened its facilities to private groups discussing morals and character, it could not exclude the Club because the Club expressed its moral message from a religious perspective. That is exactly the kind of discrimination the Court rejected in Lamb’s Chapel, where a school district allowed use of school premises for social and civic purposes but denied access for a film series addressing child rearing from a Christian viewpoint. 508 U.S. at 387-88, 393-94. The Court held that the denial was viewpoint discrimination because the district had permitted discussion of the subject but disfavored one speaker’s perspective. Id. at 393-94. So too here: Milford permitted speakers to address youth development, character, and morality, but excluded the Good News Club because its teachings were religious. That is viewpoint discrimination, not permissible subject-matter exclusion. Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 831, 835-36.

The Second Circuit erred in treating the Club’s activities as “religious instruction” outside the forum. The Court has repeatedly recognized that religious speech is not excluded from First Amendment protection merely because it is religious. Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269-70 (1981). In Widmar, the Court rejected the notion that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are somehow unprotected by the Free Speech Clause simply because they are religious. Id. at 269 n.6. And in Rosenberger, the Court held that a university could not exclude a student publication that presented issues from a Christian perspective, explaining that “the exclusion of a religious viewpoint” is impermissible when private speech on the same subject is otherwise allowed. 515 U.S. at 831, 845-46. Milford’s distinction between “moral and character development” and “religious instruction” collapses under these precedents, because the Club sought to discuss morals and character from a religious viewpoint. See id. at 831; Lamb’s Chapel, 508 U.S. at 393-94.

Milford also contends that exclusion was necessary to avoid an Establishment Clause violation. That argument fails because the Court has already rejected the notion that the Establishment Clause compels viewpoint discrimination in a limited public forum. In Widmar, the Court held that a university’s compliance with a neutral access policy would not violate the Establishment Clause, explaining that an open forum does not have the “primary effect” of advancing religion where access is generally available on equal terms. 454 U.S. at 271-75. Likewise, in Lamb’s Chapel, the Court held that permitting religious access would not be perceived as government endorsement where the access was to a forum open to many private groups. 508 U.S. at 395. And in Rosenberger, the Court explained that the Establishment Clause does not require the State to discriminate against private religious speech; to the contrary, “there is no Establishment Clause violation in granting a generally available benefit to a broad class of speakers, including those who speak from a religious viewpoint.” 515 U.S. at 839.

Those principles control here. Milford’s facilities were available to community groups after school under neutral criteria. Allowing the Good News Club to meet alongside other private organizations would not amount to state sponsorship of religion; it would simply respect the school’s obligation to treat private speakers equally. See Widmar, 454 U.S. at 274; Lamb’s Chapel, 508 U.S. at 395; Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 846. The Establishment Clause does not authorize the government to suppress religious viewpoints in order to avoid the appearance of endorsement when the forum is already open to private speech. Rosenberger, 515 U.S. at 845-46.

The judgment should therefore be reversed.
