ARGUMENT

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to permit a defendant to use expert evidence of mental disease to negate the mens rea element of a crime when the State has chosen to treat such evidence solely as part of an affirmative insanity defense. Arizona’s evidentiary rule is a permissible exercise of its police power and violates no due process guarantee.

**I. Due Process Is a Flexible Standard That Affords States Wide Latitude in Defining Crimes and Defenses**

The Due Process Clause is “not a fixed code but a flexible standard.” *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 101 (1908). It does not require any particular form of procedure. *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 535 (1884). States may therefore define the elements of crimes and the availability of defenses as they see fit, subject only to the constraint that their rules not be “arbitrary or unreasonable.” *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 801 (1952). Arizona has chosen to treat mental disease solely as a basis for an insanity defense, not as a means to negate intent. That choice is a substantive policy determination within its traditional police power.

**II. The State May Allocate the Burden of Proof on Insanity to the Defendant**

In *Leland v. Oregon*, this Court upheld a state statute requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. 343 U.S. at 798–99. The Court expressly rejected the argument that due process compelled the State to follow the federal rule allowing evidence of mental condition to negate intent. *Id.* at 799. The Court observed that “the state is not bound by the federal rule” and that Oregon’s scheme was “not so arbitrary or unreasonable as to violate due process.” *Id.* at 798, 801. Arizona’s rule is even less restrictive: it merely channels mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense, while the defendant retains the burden of proving that defense by a preponderance of the evidence. *Leland* squarely forecloses petitioner’s claim.

**III. The State’s Evidentiary Rule Does Not Deprive the Defendant of a Fair Opportunity to Present a Defense**

Due process requires only that a defendant have a “reasonable opportunity” to present his case. *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Arizona affords that opportunity: Clark introduced extensive expert testimony on his mental state in support of his insanity plea. The State simply limited that evidence to the defense for which it is legally relevant. This is a reasonable classification, not a denial of a fair hearing.

**IV. The State Has Broad Authority to Prescribe Rules of Evidence**

“The State has the power to prescribe the rules of evidence in its own courts.” *West v. Louisiana*, 194 U.S. 258, 262 (1904). Arizona has determined that evidence of mental disease is irrelevant to the question of whether the defendant formed the specific intent required for the crime, because the State defines intent as a cognitive state unaffected by mental illness. That determination is a matter of substantive law, not a procedural due process violation. *See Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581, 602 (1900) (state may adopt different procedures from those used in federal courts).

**V. The Federal Rule Is Not Binding on the States**

Petitioner relies on *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 484 (1895), and *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 466 (1946), which held that in federal courts the government must prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt once the defendant introduces some evidence of insanity. But those decisions interpret federal common law, not the Fourteenth Amendment. As *Leland* made clear, “the state is not bound by the federal rule.” 343 U.S. at 798. The Constitution does not require uniformity between state and federal evidentiary schemes.

**VI. The State’s Rule Is Not Arbitrary and Serves Legitimate Interests**

Arizona’s rule avoids the confusion that would arise if juries were allowed to consider mental-disease evidence both to negate intent and to prove insanity under different standards. The State has a legitimate interest in ensuring that the insanity defense remains a coherent, unitary inquiry. *Leland* recognized that states may structure their insanity rules to prevent “confusion” and “misunderstanding.” 343 U.S. at 800. The rule also preserves the presumption of sanity, which this Court has long recognized as a permissible evidentiary device. *See Davis*, 160 U.S. at 486–87.

**VII. The Defendant’s Right to Present Evidence Is Not Absolute**

The right to present evidence is subject to reasonable restrictions. *West v. Louisiana*, 194 U.S. at 262. A state may exclude evidence that is irrelevant or that would mislead the jury. Arizona’s rule does not bar Clark from presenting expert testimony; it merely requires that such testimony be offered in the context of the insanity defense, where it is legally relevant. That is a far cry from the complete denial of a defense that would violate due process.

**VIII. Conclusion**

Arizona’s evidentiary rule is a permissible exercise of its sovereign authority to define crimes and defenses. The Due Process Clause does not require the State to adopt the federal rule or to allow mental-disease evidence to negate mens rea. The judgment of the Arizona courts should be affirmed.
