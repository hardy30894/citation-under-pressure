**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that documents relating to the effects of explosions at Naval Magazine Indian Island—specifically, Explosive Safety Quantity Distance (ESQD) arc maps—are exempt from disclosure under FOIA’s Exemption 2 because they are “predominantly” for internal use and pose a risk of circumventing agency regulation. That holding misapplies the narrow scope of Exemption 2, improperly expands its reach beyond Congress’s intent, and contravenes binding precedent establishing that such technical safety data is not “internal personnel rules and practices” within the meaning of § 552(b)(2). The Court must reverse.

### I. EXEMPTION 2 DOES NOT APPLY TO TECHNICAL SAFETY DATA USED FOR FACILITY DESIGN AND RISK MITIGATION

Exemption 2 protects only information that is “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). This language has been interpreted narrowly, consistent with the Supreme Court’s directive that FOIA exemptions “must be narrowly construed.” *Rose v. Dep’t of Air Force*, 425 U.S. 352, 361, 96 S.Ct. 1592, 48 L.Ed.2d 11 (1976); *Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. at 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841. The exemption does not extend to technical data used for operational planning or facility design, even if such data is generated by agency personnel.

In *Hardy v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 631 F.2d 653 (9th Cir.1980), this court held that an ATF manual on raids and searches was exempt under Exemption 2 because it contained “internal personnel practices” related to law enforcement operations. But the court emphasized that the exemption applied only because the material governed how agents conducted their duties—“the manner in which [agents] carry out their official responsibilities.” Id. at 656. The ESQD arc maps here do not regulate internal conduct; they are engineering tools used to determine safe storage distances and mitigate explosion risks. They are not “rules” governing employee behavior but rather scientific calculations informing infrastructure decisions. As the D.C. Circuit explained in *Schwaner v. Department of Air Force*, 898 F.2d 793, 795 (D.C. Cir.1990), Exemption 2 does not cover “technical data developed for operational purposes,” even if it is used internally.

Similarly, in *Maricopa Audubon Soc. v. United States Forest Service*, 108 F.3d 1082 (9th Cir.1997), we rejected Exemption 2 protection for goshawk nesting site data, noting that such information “does not relate ‘solely,’ or even predominantly, ‘to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.’” Id. at 1085. The data was used for environmental management, not internal personnel administration. Likewise, the ESQD maps serve a public safety function—preventing sympathetic detonations—not internal rulemaking. Their purpose is not to guide employee conduct but to ensure physical safety through engineering standards.

### II. THE “PREDOMINANTLY INTERNAL” STANDARD IS INCONSISTENT WITH CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND PRECEDENT

The Ninth Circuit’s adoption of the D.C. Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard—despite earlier precedent suggesting otherwise—creates a dangerous expansion of Exemption 2. In *Hardy*, we did not adopt a broad “predominantly internal” test; instead, we focused on whether the material was “law enforcement material” whose disclosure would risk circumvention. 631 F.2d at 656. The D.C. Circuit’s later decision in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir.1981) (en banc), introduced the “predominantly internal” framework, but that approach has never been adopted by this circuit as a general rule.

Indeed, in *Maricopa Audubon*, we explicitly rejected the idea that any internal document could be exempt simply because it is “internal.” We held that “mere internal use” is insufficient: “The key inquiry is whether the information is truly part of the agency’s internal personnel rules and practices.” 108 F.3d at 1085. The ESQD maps were created for the purpose of designing safe storage facilities, not for managing employees. They are not “rules” or “practices” governing personnel—they are technical specifications for construction and risk assessment.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against expanding exemptions beyond their statutory limits. In *U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts*, 492 U.S. 136, 151, 109 S.Ct. 2841, 106 L.Ed.2d 112 (1989), the Court stated that FOIA’s exemptions are “explicitly exclusive,” meaning agencies may not invoke them beyond the text Congress provided. Applying Exemption 2 to non-personnel technical data violates this principle.

### III. THE DOCUMENTS ARE NOT “COMPILED FOR LAW ENFORCEMENT PURPOSES” UNDER EXEMPTION 7(F)

The Navy also invoked Exemption 7(F), which protects records compiled for law enforcement purposes if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(F). But the ESQD maps were not compiled for law enforcement. Commander Whitbred confirmed that the maps are used “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities, and to organize ammunition operations for risk mitigation and enhanced safety.” This is not an “adjudicative or enforcement purpose.” *Church of Scientology v.
