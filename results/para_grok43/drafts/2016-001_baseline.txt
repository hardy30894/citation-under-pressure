**ARGUMENT**

The Federal Circuit erred in affirming an award of Samsung’s entire profits on the accused smartphones. Section 289 of the Patent Act provides that an infringer of a design patent “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit” on “any article of manufacture” to which the patented design “has been applied.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. When the patented design is applied only to a component, the statute limits recovery to profits on that component, not the entire product.

This Court has long recognized that patent damages must be confined to the inventive contribution. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 445 (1885), the Court held that a patentee “must show what part of his own damages” is attributable to the infringement, rejecting recovery of profits on an entire article when only a portion was covered by the patent. Although Congress responded to *Dobson* by enacting the predecessor to § 289, the statute did not abandon the principle of apportionment; it merely eliminated the need to prove the design’s effect on the article’s value when the design is applied to the whole article. *See Nike, Inc. v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 138 F.3d 1437, 1441 (Fed. Cir. 1998) (tracing the provision’s history).

Lower courts applying § 289 and its predecessor consistently required apportionment when the design covered only a component. In *Bush & Lane Piano Co. v. Becker Bros.*, 222 F. 902, 904 (2d Cir. 1915), the Second Circuit limited recovery to the profit on the infringing piano case, not the entire piano, because “the article of manufacture to which the patent was applied” was the case alone. The same rule was followed in *Young v. Grand Rapids Refrigerator Co.*, 268 F. 966, 974 (6th Cir. 1920), where damages were confined to the profit on the infringing refrigerator lid.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—that the entire smartphone is the “article of manufacture”—contradicts both the statutory text and these precedents. The smartphone is an assembled product containing numerous components; the patented designs were applied only to the front face, bezel, and grid of icons. Allowing recovery of profits on the entire device would revive the very overcompensation *Dobson* condemned and that § 289 was never intended to authorize.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for a determination of profits properly attributable to the components to which the patented designs were applied.
