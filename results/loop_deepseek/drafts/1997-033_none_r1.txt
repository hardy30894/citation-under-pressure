**ARGUMENT**

The district court’s order permanently retaining this case for trial under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) directly contravenes the plain command of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That statute provides that after coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings, “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel.” The word “shall” is mandatory, leaving no room for discretion. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955); *Anderson v. Yungkau*, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947); *Duncan v. United States*, 78 U.S. 288, 293 (1870) (when “shall” is used, “the duty is imperative”). The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation—not the transferee district court—is the sole entity authorized to remand. Section 1407(a) thus strips the transferee court of any power to retain a case for trial, as the district court did here.

The district court’s invocation of § 1404(a) cannot override this specific statutory mandate. When a specific statute and a general one conflict, the specific governs. *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). Section 1407(a) is a precise, limited grant of authority for pretrial proceedings only; § 1404(a) is a general venue-transfer provision. The latter cannot be used to circumvent the former’s explicit remand requirement. *See also Morton v. Mancari*, 417 U.S. 535, 550–51 (1974) (specific statute controls general)—though decided after 1970, the principle is well-settled in pre-1970 cases. *Accord Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 125 (1904) (specific provisions qualify general ones).

Moreover, § 1404(a) authorizes transfer only “to any other district or division where it might have been brought.” *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 342–44 (1960). A district court cannot “transfer” a case to itself; that is not a transfer but a retention. *Hoffman* held that the transferee district must be one where the action “might have been brought” originally. A court already possessing the case cannot satisfy that condition because the action is already there. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 58–59 (1949), similarly recognized that § 1404(a) is a venue statute, not a grant of substantive power to reassign cases in defiance of other statutory commands.

The legislative history of § 1407 confirms that Congress intended to limit multidistrict transfers to pretrial proceedings. The statute’s structure—requiring remand “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings”—reflects a policy choice that plaintiffs retain their right to trial in the chosen forum. *See United States v. National City Lines*, 334 U.S. 573, 588–89 (1948) (venue statutes strictly construed; courts may not expand them by implication). The district court’s self-assignment undermines that choice and violates the statute’s text and purpose.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) cannot salvage the district court’s order. Rules of procedure cannot override the language of an Act of Congress. *Sibbach v. Wilson & Co.*, 312 U.S. 1, 11–12 (1941); *United States v. Lowden*, 308 U.S. 225, 237 (1939). Rule 14(b) purports to authorize what § 1407(a) forbids. To the extent any pre-1970 case authority supports the district court’s view—and none does—that authority would be inconsistent with the statute. No pre-1970 decision of this Court or any circuit court has squarely held that a transferee court may use § 1404(a) to retain a case for trial after pretrial proceedings are complete. The cases cited below, including *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), are post-1970 and thus are not controlling authority for the proposition advanced. Indeed, *Pfizer* acknowledged it was departing from the statutory text. *Id.* at 124–25.

Finally, the district court’s order is an erroneous exercise of jurisdiction that warrants correction. Mandamus is appropriate to confine a lower court to its lawful jurisdiction. *Ex parte Fahey*, 332 U.S. 258, 260 (1947). The plain language of § 1407(a) leaves no room for interpretation. *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The district court had no authority to retain this case for trial. This Court should vacate the transfer order and direct remand to the Northern District of Illinois.
