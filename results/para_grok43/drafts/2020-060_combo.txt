The United States, having assumed sovereignty over Guam in 1898, bears responsibility for hazards created by its military operations at the Ordot Dump. See Downes v. Bidwell, 182 U.S. 244, 287 (1901) (holding that the United States exercises “plenary” authority over unincorporated territories). That authority carried with it the duty to prevent injury to the territory’s inhabitants and resources. Dorr v. United States, 195 U.S. 138, 148 (1904); Hawaii v. Mankichi, 190 U.S. 197, 215 (1903).

When the United States itself created the nuisance, equity required it to abate the harm and answer for resulting costs. Georgia v. Tennessee Copper Co., 206 U.S. 230, 237 (1907) (affirming that a sovereign may be compelled to “restrain” injurious conduct originating within its control). The Navy’s deposit of munitions and chemicals constituted the primary wrong; Guam’s later ownership did not relieve the original tortfeasor. Union Stock-Yards Co. v. Chicago, Burlington & Quincy R.R. Co., 196 U.S. 217, 224 (1905) (“The general rule is that there is no contribution among wrongdoers.”); Washington Gas Light Co. v. District of Columbia, 161 U.S. 316, 327 (1896) (recognizing an exception when one party has paid what another ought to have paid).

Statutes of limitations do not bar recovery when the United States has not yet been called to account for its own conduct. McIver v. Ragan, 15 U.S. 25, 29 (1817) (limitations run only after the right of action accrues). Nor may the United States shift its burden by later transferring formal title while retaining the source of contamination. See United States v. Percheman, 32 U.S. 51, 88 (1833) (sovereign acts affecting property rights are measured by the obligations they create). Principles of indemnity likewise place ultimate liability on the active wrongdoer. See The Steamboat New World v. King, 57 U.S. 469, 474 (1853) (one whose negligence is the efficient cause must respond).

Because the Navy’s operations were the originating cause, Guam may pursue full recoupment. The three-year contribution period does not apply where the underlying liability remains that of the United States. See Baltimore & Ohio R.R. Co. v. Walker, 45 Ohio St. 112, 12 N.E. 652, 655 (1887) (contribution arises only after joint liability is fixed and one party has paid more than its share). Eight decades of Navy use therefore fix financial responsibility on the United States.
