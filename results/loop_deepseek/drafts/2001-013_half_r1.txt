**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erroneously held that the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lacks jurisdiction over the working conditions aboard Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52, an uninspected oil and gas exploration barge. The plain text of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, coupled with the limited regulatory reach of the Coast Guard over uninspected vessels, compels the conclusion that OSHA’s citation was lawful.

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

The Act applies broadly to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). A drilling barge engaged in extracting oil from the seabed is not a “vessel in navigation” within the traditional maritime meaning. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 571 (1944) (noting that a floating workshop “was not used for the carriage of passengers or cargo, nor was it a vessel engaged in commerce or navigation”); *Evans v. United States*, 252 U.S. 569, 572 (1920) (a floating elevator moored to a wharf and used solely for loading grain was not a “vessel” for purposes of maritime jurisdiction). *Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 370, 373 (1957) (a dredge moored during operations may not be a “vessel in navigation” for Jones Act purposes). *Gahagan Construction Corp. v. Armao*, 350 U.S. 879 (1955) (per curiam) (same). Like those structures, Rig 52 was stationary while drilling—its sole purpose—and thus was not a vessel “engaged in commerce or navigation.” *The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17, 30 (1903). *Cf. Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187, 191 (1952) (ferry that actively navigated was a vessel). Consequently, Rig 52 is a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s authority.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). Mere jurisdiction is insufficient; the agency must *actually* regulate the same condition. This Court has long held that preemption requires an actual conflict, not speculative overlap. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (preemption necessary only when “the matter is so fully covered by federal law as to leave no room for state action”). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is minimal. 46 U.S.C. § 4102 grants it power over only fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar equipment. It has no authority to prescribe comprehensive safety standards for drilling operations. *The Robert W. Parsons*, 191 U.S. 17, 30 (1903) (inspection laws apply only to vessels “used in navigation of the waters of the United States”). The Coast Guard’s failure to regulate the working conditions that gave rise to this explosion—well control, blowout prevention, and general workplace safety—means it has not “exercise[d] statutory authority” over those conditions. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939) (exemptions from remedial statutes are construed narrowly).

**III. Workers on Uninspected Drilling Barges Are Not “Seamen” for OSHA Purposes**

The court below erroneously classified Rig 52’s employees as seamen. Workers on uninspected drilling barges perform industrial oil well work, not traditional maritime duties. *Norton*, 321 U.S. at 571 (jurisdiction of admiralty does not extend to structures like this barge). Their working conditions are analogous to those on a fixed platform—areas indisputably covered by OSHA. *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365 (1969) (workers on fixed platforms are not seamen for LHWCA purposes). Overlapping regulation is not the anomaly the lower court feared; it is the norm when maritime safety touches landside operations. *The Osceola*, 189 U.S. 158, 175 (1903) (seamen’s remedies limited to those specified in maritime law; no preemption of state safety statutes).

In sum, Rig 52 is a workplace, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over its working conditions, and OSHA’s citation is valid. The judgment below should be reversed.
