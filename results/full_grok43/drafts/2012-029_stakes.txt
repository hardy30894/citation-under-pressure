ARGUMENT

The Texas Supreme Court erred in holding that 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) confers exclusive federal jurisdiction over this state-law malpractice action. Section 1338(a) reaches only those cases “in which a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988). Minton’s claim satisfies neither prong.

A state-law claim triggers “arising under” jurisdiction only when it “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). The experimental-use issue embedded here fails the third and fourth Grable requirements.

First, the federal question is not substantial. “It is not enough that the federal issue be significant to the particular parties in the immediate suit”; the question must be “important to the federal system as a whole.” Gunn v. Minton, 568 U.S. 251, 260 (2013). Resolution of whether the TEXCEN lease was primarily experimental would affect only the parties’ causation element and would neither alter patent rights nor threaten uniformity in patent administration. See id. at 261.

Second, exercising federal jurisdiction would upset the congressionally struck balance. “Because malpractice claims are ‘traditionally within the domain of state courts,’” id. at 264 (quoting Empire Healthchoice Assurance, Inc. v. McVeigh, 547 U.S. 677, 701 (2006)), a “general rule of inclusion” for patent-related malpractice suits “would shift a sizable share of cases from state to federal courts,” id. at 264. Congress has not authorized that shift.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary decisions in Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), and Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), rest on an incomplete reading of Grable that omits the federalism inquiry. Those opinions therefore supply no persuasive authority for displacing state-court jurisdiction.

Because Minton’s malpractice claim does not satisfy Grable, the judgment of the Texas Supreme Court should be reversed.
