## ARGUMENT

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee award based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. Long before Congress enacted the Civil Rights Attorney’s Fees Awards Act of 1976, courts of equity and federal courts applying fee-shifting statutes consistently recognized that exceptional representation and extraordinary results may warrant a fee adjustment.

### I. The Common Law Tradition Recognized That Courts May Consider Results When Setting Fees

The common-fund doctrine, a venerable equitable principle, long empowered courts to award fees that reflected the value of the services rendered and the benefit conferred. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), this Court upheld a fee award calculated as a percentage of the fund created, recognizing that the chancellor “ought to allow just compensation” for “services performed” that produced a “benefit” to others. *Id.* at 536. That compensation necessarily turned on the quality and effect of the work. *See Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 124 (1885) (affirming enhancement where counsel’s “skill and diligence” achieved “great results”). In *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939), the Court reaffirmed that a court sitting in equity “has inherent power to award attorneys’ fees” and may “measure the amount” by “the benefit actually produced.” The discretion to enhance a fee for superior performance was thus embedded in the law before any modern fee-shifting statute.

Bankruptcy and receivership cases further illustrate this principle. In *In re Osofsky*, 50 F.2d 925, 927 (2d Cir. 1931), the court held that a bankruptcy referee could award a fee “larger than normal” where the attorney’s “unusual services” and “excellent results” surpassed what the base rate would capture. Similarly, in *Barber v. National Airlines*, 125 F.2d 884, 887 (2d Cir. 1942) (Hand, J.), the Second Circuit explained that when determining a reasonable fee, a court “must consider the result achieved” because “the value of the service varies directly with the benefit.” These authorities confirm that enhancement for exceptional performance is not a novel invention; it is a traditional exercise of judicial discretion.

### II. Statutory Fee-Shifting Provisions Do Not Prohibit Enhancement for Exceptional Results

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes, it did not jettison the traditional discretion courts had long exercised. *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717–18 (1967), reiterated the American Rule but acknowledged that “express statutory authority” creates an exception. And in *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968), this Court interpreted a fee-shifting provision under Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 to require that a prevailing plaintiff “should ordinarily recover an attorney’s fee.” The Court grounded this rule in the purpose of encouraging private enforcement, noting that fee awards must be “adequate to attract competent counsel.” *Id.* at 402 n.4. If the representation is of such exceptional quality that it produces extraordinary success, an enhancement may be necessary to ensure that counsel receive a fee commensurate with the value of the services—and to incentivize attorneys to take on such demanding cases in the future. Nothing in *Newman* suggests that Congress intended to forbid consideration of performance or results.

Moreover, in the many fee-shifting statutes that use the term “reasonable attorney’s fee”—such as the patent, trademark, and antitrust laws—courts before 1970 routinely examined results. For instance, in *Park v. Board of Education*, 259 U.S. 224, 229 (1922), the Court held that a “reasonable” fee under a state statute required “consideration of the results obtained.” And in *United States v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 283 U.S. 738, 744 (1931), the Court approved a fee award that “took into consideration the magnitude of the services and the benefit conferred.” The word “reasonable” itself has always invited a case-specific judgment that includes the quality of performance.

### III. The Respondent’s Award Rests on Established Precedent

The district court enhanced the lodestar only after finding that this case was “rare” and “exceptional”—criteria that echo the common-law approach. *Trustees*, 105 U.S. at 536; *Central Railroad*, 113 U.S. at 124. The court relied on its own observation of counsel’s “stellar performance” and the “comprehensiveness” of the injunction, as courts have done for generations without violating due process. *See Randolph v. Scruggs*, 190 U.S. 533, 539 (1903) (upholding fee award based on court’s “knowledge of the character of the services”). This is not a “blank check”; it is a circumscribed discretion that requires specific findings.

If the Court were to hold that enhancement for quality and results is *never* permissible, it would overrule a century of equitable practice and ignore the plain meaning of “reasonable.” It would also undermine the core purpose of fee-shifting statutes—to ensure that plaintiffs with meritorious claims can obtain skilled counsel. *Newman*, 390 U.S. at 402. The decision below should be affirmed.
