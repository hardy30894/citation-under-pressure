## ARGUMENT

### A. Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951

Mr. Pearson challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951, arguing that Congress exceeded its authority under the Commerce Clause when it enacted the statute. The Hobbs Act, codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1951, prohibits robbery or attempted robbery that affects interstate commerce. The district court rejected Mr. Pearson's challenge, and this Court reviews the issue de novo. United States v. Bolton, 68 F.3d 396, 398 (10th Cir. 1995).

The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3. The Supreme Court has held that Congress may regulate three categories of activity under the Commerce Clause: (1) the channels of interstate commerce, (2) the instrumentalities of interstate commerce, and (3) activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. United States v. Lopez, 514 U.S. 549, 558-59 (1995).

The Hobbs Act falls within the third category, as it regulates activities that substantially affect interstate commerce. The Supreme Court has upheld the constitutionality of the Hobbs Act, holding that it is a valid exercise of Congress's Commerce Clause power. United States v. Staszcuk, 502 F.2d 875, 888 (7th Cir. 1974) (citing Stirone v. United States, 361 U.S. 212 (1960)). In Stirone, the Court noted that the Hobbs Act "speaks in broad language, manifesting a purpose to use all the constitutional power Congress has to punish interference with interstate commerce by extortion, robbery or physical violence." Stirone, 361 U.S. at 215.

This Court has also addressed the constitutionality of the Hobbs Act in the context of a Commerce Clause challenge. In United States v. Bolton, this Court held that the Hobbs Act is constitutional as applied to a robbery that affects interstate commerce. 68 F.3d at 399. The Court noted that the government need only show a "de minimis" effect on interstate commerce to establish jurisdiction under the Hobbs Act. Id. (citing United States v. Zeigler, 19 F.3d 486, 491 (10th Cir. 1994)).

In this case, the government presented evidence that Mr. Goodcents purchases goods from out-of-state companies and that the robbery affected the store's business. Mr. Peterson testified that the stolen money would have been used to purchase goods produced outside of Kansas. This evidence is sufficient to establish a de minimis effect on interstate commerce, and thus, the Hobbs Act is constitutional as applied to Mr. Pearson. United States v. Nguyen, 155 F.3d 1219, 1224 (10th Cir. 1998).

Therefore, this Court should affirm the district court's ruling that 18 U.S.C. § 1951 is constitutional.
