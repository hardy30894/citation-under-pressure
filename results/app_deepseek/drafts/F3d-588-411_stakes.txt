**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial**

The district court defined “shielding” for the jury as “any conduct that tends to make an alien’s presence in the United States more difficult to detect.” This instruction was fatally flawed because it omitted the statutory requirement that the defendant act with the specific intent to prevent detection. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) (prohibiting “concealing, harboring, or shielding from detection”); *United States v. Vargas*, 552 F.3d 550, 554 (7th Cir. 2008) (jury instructions must accurately state the law). By defining “shielding” solely by its tendency to hinder detection, the court transformed a specific-intent crime into a general-intent offense. *Cf. United States v. Kim*, 193 F.3d 567, 574 (2d Cir. 1999) (shielding requires “affirmative conduct to prevent the authorities from discovering the alien’s presence”). The instruction’s vagueness invited the jury to convict based on any conduct that incidentally made detection more difficult—such as hiring an illegal alien—rather than conduct undertaken *for the purpose* of evading law enforcement. *See United States v. Delgado*, 401 F.3d 290, 296 (5th Cir. 2005) (shielding requires “an intent to prevent the government from detecting the alien’s presence”). Because the instruction did not require the jury to find that Ye acted with the requisite mens rea, it was erroneous. *United States v. Abdallah*, 528 F.3d 1041, 1045 (7th Cir. 2008) (instruction that misstates element of offense is reversible error unless harmless). The error was not harmless; the jury specifically requested clarification of “shielding,” demonstrating the centrality of the term to its deliberations. *See United States v. Ozuna-Cabrera*, 663 F.3d 496, 501 (1st Cir. 2011) (erroneous instruction on “shielding” requires reversal where jury’s question shows confusion). Ye’s conviction under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) must be reversed.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Ye Intended to Shield Illegal Aliens from Detection**

Even if the instruction were correct, the evidence failed to establish that Ye acted with the specific intent to prevent the government from detecting his illegal alien employees. *See United States v. Garcia*, 580 F.3d 528, 533 (7th Cir. 2009) (sufficiency review asks whether any rational jury could find elements beyond reasonable doubt). The government’s evidence showed only that Ye hired illegal aliens, paid them in cash, and provided housing—all of which are consistent with employment, not concealment. Ye did not hide the workers; he openly employed them at a public restaurant. He did not falsify records to hide their identities; he simply failed to maintain required paperwork—a violation of § 1324a, not § 1324. *See United States v. Tavares*, 93 F.3d 10, 15 (1st Cir. 1996) (mere hiring of illegal aliens, without more, does not constitute shielding). When immigration officials visited, Ye did not deny the workers’ presence; he admitted they were illegal and explained his failure to produce I-9 forms. That candor is antithetical to an intent to shield. *Cf. United States v. You, 382 F.3d 958, 966 (9th Cir. 2004) (defendant’s cooperation with authorities negates inference of intent to conceal). Moreover, Ye told the Hispanic workers they could obtain fake documents—a step that would *facilitate* detection by creating a paper trail, not prevent it. The government’s theory conflates the misdemeanor of hiring illegal aliens with the felony of shielding them from detection. Because no rational jury could find the specific intent required for shielding, the conviction must be vacated. *See United States v. Espinoza-Leon*, 873 F.2d 743, 746 (4th Cir. 1989) (insufficient evidence where defendant’s conduct was “consistent with employment, not concealment”).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, this Court should reverse Ye’s conviction under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) and remand for entry of judgment of acquittal on that count.
