Fernandez-Vargas respectfully submits that § 241(a)(5) of the Immigration and Nationality Act does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before the statute’s effective date, and that, in any event, applying it to him would be impermissibly retroactive.

The starting point is the familiar presumption against retroactivity. In United States v. Heth, 7 U.S. (3 Cranch) 399, 413 (1806), the Court stated that statutes are not to be given retroactive effect unless that intention is clear. The Court has repeated that principle for more than a century. See Shwab v. Doyle, 258 U.S. 529, 534-35 (1922); United States v. Magnolia Petroleum Co., 276 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1928); Greene v. United States, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964). Where Congress intends to alter the legal consequences of completed conduct, it must speak with unmistakable clarity. See United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co., 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926); Hassett v. Welch, 303 U.S. 303, 314 (1938).

Section 241(a)(5) contains no such clear statement as applied to aliens who had already returned before 1997. Fernandez-Vargas’s relevant conduct was completed when he illegally reentered in 1982 and remained here for years under then-existing immigration law. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a statute is retroactive when it imposes a new disability based on past acts. See Mathews v. Kidder, Peabody & Co., 161 U.S. 625, 629-30 (1896); Chase Securities Corp. v. Donaldson, 325 U.S. 304, 314-16 (1945). That is what § 241(a)(5) does here: it converts a past deportation and reentry into an automatic bar to relief and reinstates an old order without the procedural and substantive options that existed before IIRIRA.

The government will likely characterize the statute as addressing only Fernandez-Vargas’s continued unlawful presence after 1997. But the Court has repeatedly rejected formal labels that obscure a statute’s actual effect. In Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994), the Court explained that retroactivity turns on whether the new law “attaches new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment.” Earlier decisions say the same. See White v. United States, 191 U.S. 545, 554 (1903); Railroad Commission v. Northern Pacific Railway Co., 216 U.S. 579, 584-85 (1910). Here, the operative event triggering reinstatement is the pre-1997 illegal reentry. Congress did not merely regulate present conduct; it attached new, harsher consequences to a completed past act.

That point is critical because Fernandez-Vargas had, before IIRIRA, at least a legally cognizable opportunity to seek adjustment through marriage to a United States citizen. The later statute extinguished that opportunity. The Court has long guarded against legislation that destroys settled expectations or revives old liabilities without fair warning. See Ogden v. Blackledge, 2 Cranch 272, 275 (1805); Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler, 2 Gall. 105, 139-40 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.); Welch v. Henry, 305 U.S. 134, 147-48 (1938). A statute with such effect is retroactive in the constitutionally and interpretively relevant sense.

Nor does the government gain traction by emphasizing that Fernandez-Vargas remained unlawfully present after 1997. The Court has distinguished between a continuing condition and the past act that gives the new law its force. In Kaiser Aluminum & Chemical Corp. v. Bonjorno, 494 U.S. 827, 837-38 (1990), the Court refused to read a statute to reach conduct completed before enactment absent clear indication. Likewise, in Bowen v. Georgetown University Hospital, 488 U.S. 204, 208 (1988), the Court held that retroactivity is not favored and that administrative or statutory authority to reach back must be plainly expressed. Section 241(a)(5) supplies no such plain expression for pre-enactment reentrants.

The better reading is that Congress targeted future illegal reentries and post-enactment conduct, not aliens like Fernandez-Vargas who had already reentered and built their lives here before the new statute took effect. That construction respects the canon against retroactivity and avoids the serious unfairness of stripping relief from persons who could not have ordered their affairs in light of a law not yet enacted. See Greene, 376 U.S. at 160; Shwab, 258 U.S. at 534-35; Magnolia Petroleum, 276 U.S. at 162-63.

Because § 241(a)(5) should not be read to apply to pre-IIRIRA reentrants, the petition should be granted. And if the Court concludes the statute does reach Fernandez-Vargas, it should hold that such application is impermissibly retroactive under the settled principles of Heth, Shwab, Magnolia Petroleum, Greene, Landgraf, and their predecessors.
