**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt who collected the debt on its own behalf, was not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA. The statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent compel this conclusion.

### I. THE FDCPA REGULATES DEBT COLLECTORS, NOT CREDITORS, AND THE DISTINCTION IS DETERMINED BY THE NATURE OF THE COLLECTION ACTIVITY, NOT THE DEFAULT STATUS OF THE DEBT

The FDCPA’s core purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). It does not regulate creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995). The Act defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) as a person who (1) uses interstate commerce or the mails in a business whose principal purpose is debt collection, (2) regularly collects or attempts to collect debts owed to another, or (3) collects its own debts using a name other than its own. The definition is exclusive: a person is a “debt collector” only if it satisfies one of these three criteria.

The critical inquiry is not whether the debt was in default when acquired, but whether the entity is collecting debts *owed to another* at the time of collection. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (“Our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection.”). The phrase “debts owed or due another” refers to the status of the debt *at the time of collection*, not at the time of origination or purchase. *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another.’”).

Here, Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011. After that date, Santander was the sole holder of the debt and collected it on its own behalf. The complaint itself alleges that Santander “acquired” the debts “for a few cents on the dollar” and that it “began communicating with [the plaintiffs] ... in an attempt to collect on the alleged debts.” These allegations confirm that Santander was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector. It was not collecting on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto; it was collecting on its own account. The fact that the debt was in default when acquired is irrelevant to the statutory definition.

### II. THE PLAINTIFFS’ INTERPRETATION OF THE FDCPA WOULD UNDO THE ACT’S CLEAR STRUCTURE AND CREATE AN UNINTENDED EXPANSION OF REGULATION

The plaintiffs’ argument—that a purchaser of defaulted debt is automatically a “debt collector” because the debt was in default when acquired—rests on a flawed syllogism. They claim that because § 1692a(4) excludes from the definition of “creditor” any person who receives a defaulted debt “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection for another,” such a person must be a “debt collector.” But this logic ignores the statutory hierarchy: a person must first satisfy one of the definitions in § 1692a(6) before the exclusions apply. *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1314. If a person does not meet any of the three definitions, the exclusions do not save it from being a creditor.

Moreover, the plaintiffs’ interpretation would render the FDCPA’s exclusions meaningless. Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) explicitly excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was *not in default* when obtained. If the default status of the debt determined the classification, then the exclusion would be superfluous—every defaulted debt would automatically make the purchaser a debt collector, regardless of the nature of the collection activity. Congress did not intend such a result. *S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977)*, as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698, explains that the exclusion was meant to protect loan servicers who service non-defaulted debts, not to define the scope of the term “debt collector” based on default status.

### III. THE PLAINTIFFS’ CLAIM OF A “LOOPHOLE” IS BASED ON A MISUNDERSTANDING OF THE FDCPA’S SCOPE

The plaintiffs argue that Santander cannot “escape” FDCPA liability simply by purchasing a defaulted debt it previously serviced. But this misapprehends the Act’s design. A company may be a debt collector in one context and a creditor in another. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 293. Santander’s prior role as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto does not transform its subsequent collection of its own purchased debt into a “debt collection” activity under the FDCPA. The Act regulates conduct, not status. *Id.* at 294 (“The Act’s definition of the term ‘debt collector’ includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed to another.’” (emphasis added)). When Santander collected on its own debt, it was not acting “on behalf of another.” It was acting as a creditor.
