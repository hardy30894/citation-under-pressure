**ARGUMENT**

The Third Circuit’s objective “reasonable speaker” test for true threats under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) is irreconcilable with this Court’s decision in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003). Because the jury was instructed under that objective standard, Elonis’s conviction must be reversed.

**I. *Virginia v. Black* Requires Proof That the Defendant Subjectively Intended to Communicate a Threat**

In *Black*, this Court defined true threats as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” *Id.* at 359. The phrase “means to communicate” describes the speaker’s subjective purpose. The Court further explained that “[t]he speaker need not actually intend to carry out the threat.” *Id.* at 359-60. The required intent is therefore not an intent to inflict harm, but an intent to communicate a threat. That is a subjective standard.

*Black*’s holding confirms this reading. The Court struck down Virginia’s cross-burning statute because it “permits the Commonwealth to arrest, prosecute, and convict a person based solely on the fact of cross burning” without “any proof of intent to intimidate.” *Id.* at 362-63. If an objective standard were sufficient, the presumption would have been constitutional. It was not. The Court required the State to prove that the defendant “intend[ed] to intimidate.” *Id.* at 363.

**II. The Third Circuit’s Objective Standard Is Inconsistent with *Black***

The Third Circuit held that “a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991). That test asks what a reasonable person would foresee, not what the defendant intended. It is an objective negligence standard. Under that standard, a speaker who does not subjectively intend to threaten—but whose words a reasonable speaker might foresee as threatening—can be convicted. That is precisely what happened here. The district court instructed the jury that a statement is a true threat when “a defendant intentionally makes a statement in a context or under such circumstances wherein a reasonable person would foresee that the statement would be interpreted by a reasonable person as a threat.” That instruction allowed the jury to convict without finding that Elonis meant to communicate a threat.

This Court has long required that “what is a threat must be distinguished from what is constitutionally protected speech.” *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). The subjective intent requirement is essential to that distinction. Without it, a speaker could be punished for hyperbole, jest, or artistic expression that a reasonable listener might misperceive as a threat. The Court in *Black* recognized that true threats are unprotected only because the speaker “means to communicate” a serious expression of intent to commit violence. 538 U.S. at 359. A statement that the speaker does not intend as a threat is not a true threat, even if it causes fear.

The Third Circuit’s standard is an outlier. The majority of federal courts of appeals to consider the question after *Black* have held that the First Amendment requires proof of subjective intent to threaten. *See, e.g., United States v. Jeffries*, 692 F.3d 473, 478 (6th Cir. 2012) (holding that *Black* requires proof of subjective intent to threaten); *United States v. White*, 670 F.3d 498, 507 (4th Cir. 2012) (same). This Court should reject the Third Circuit’s contrary view.

**III. The Government’s Policy Concerns Do Not Justify an Objective Standard**

The government and the Third Circuit argued that a subjective intent standard would fail to protect individuals from the fear of violence. But *Black* already rejected that argument. The Court acknowledged that “a prohibition on true threats protects individuals from the fear of violence and from the disruption that fear engenders.” 538 U.S. at 359-60. Yet the Court still required that the speaker “mean[] to communicate” a threat. *Id.* at 359. The victim’s fear is relevant evidence of whether a statement is a true threat, but it is not a substitute for proof of the defendant’s intent. The subjective standard protects victims by punishing those who intentionally communicate threats, while preserving the First Amendment rights of those who do not.

Moreover, the presumption of mens rea in criminal statutes supports this reading. Section 875(c) does not specify a mental state, but this Court has held that “the existence of a mens rea is the rule of, rather than the exception to, the principles of Anglo-American criminal jurisprudence.” *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 69 (1994) (quoting *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 436 (1978)). The most natural reading of “any threat” is a communication that the defendant intends to be a threat. The Third Circuit’s objective standard would criminalize speech based on negligence, contrary to both the statute and the First Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
