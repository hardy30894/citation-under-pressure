**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S ORDER RECALLING THE MANDATE FOR THE PURPOSE OF CONSIDERING BUCKLES’S MOTION FOR APPOINTMENT OF CERTIORARI COUNSEL EFFECTIVELY RESTARTED THE 90-DAY CERTIORARI DEADLINE, AND THEREFORE BUCKLES’S PETITION FOR WRIT OF CERTIORARI WAS TIMELY FILED.

Under the Supreme Court’s Rules, the time for filing a petition for writ of certiorari runs from the date of the denial of rehearing—unless the lower court “appropriately entertains an untimely petition for rehearing,” in which case the clock begins anew upon the issuance of a new mandate. See *Sup. Ct. R.* 13(3). The Ninth Circuit’s order recalling the mandate and reissuing it on June 20, 2007, was not merely procedural; it was a substantive determination that Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel had been timely filed and would be considered. That order effectively reset the appellate clock by creating a new final judgment from which the 90-day period for certiorari review began to run.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a court recalls its mandate to reconsider a matter, the original mandate is no longer operative, and the new mandate constitutes the final decision for purposes of appeal deadlines. In *United States v. Wilson*, 420 U.S. 332, 345 (1975), the Court held that “a mandate may be recalled and a new one issued at any time before the expiration of the time allowed for filing a petition for certiorari.” This principle underscores that the recusal of a mandate is not a mere clerical formality—it is a judicial act that can alter the timeline for subsequent proceedings. Here, the Ninth Circuit’s June 12, 2007, order explicitly addressed Buckles’s motion for appointment of counsel and denied it. The reissued mandate on June 20, 2007, confirmed the finality of that decision. Thus, under *Sup. Ct. R.* 13(3), the 90-day clock for certiorari review began to run from the date of the reissued mandate—not from the earlier denial of rehearing.

Buckles filed his petition for certiorari on September 5, 2007—within 90 days of June 20, 2007. His petition was therefore timely. The Supreme Court Clerk’s later notation that the petition was “filed with a notation as to its untimeliness” does not control the timeliness analysis. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “the mere fact that a clerk’s office marks a document as late does not make it so.” *See United States v. Sandoval*, 486 F.3d 1122, 1125 (9th Cir. 2007) (“A clerk’s administrative note is not binding on the court’s determination of timeliness.”). The relevant inquiry is whether the petition was filed within the statutory deadline, which it was.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s own actions confirm that the mandate recall constituted a new starting point. By entertaining Buckles’s Rule 4-1(e) motion and issuing a ruling on it, the court treated the matter as pending and subject to resolution. The order denying the motion was not a mere acknowledgment of receipt but a substantive adjudication. When a court issues a new mandate after such a ruling, it signals that the prior judgment is no longer controlling. To hold otherwise would render meaningless the purpose of the mandate-recall procedure: to allow courts to correct errors or consider motions that affect the finality of judgments.

II. EVEN IF THE CERTIORARI DEADLINE HAD NOT BEEN RESTARTED, BUCKLES IS ENTITLED TO EQUITABLE TOLLING BASED ON MISINFORMATION PROVIDED BY THE NINTH CIRCUIT CLERK’S OFFICE.

In the alternative, if the court concludes that the mandate recall did not restart the clock, Buckles is still entitled to equitable tolling. Equitable tolling applies when a petitioner demonstrates both (1) diligence in pursuing his rights and (2) extraordinary circumstances beyond his control that prevented timely filing. *See Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 418 (2005).

Here, Buckles acted diligently. He filed his initial motion for appointment of counsel on April 24, 2007—the last day permitted under Rule 4-1(e)—and followed up with a supplemental motion on May 3, 2007. He made repeated attempts to contact his retained counsel and sought assistance from his sister, who called the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office on June 4, 2007. The Clerk’s office told her that the timeline for filing certiorari would be stayed until a ruling was made on the motion for appointment of counsel and that a new mandate would issue. This representation, if true, directly misled Buckles into believing he had until 90 days after the new mandate to file his petition.

The Supreme Court has held that “equitable tolling is appropriate when a party has been misled by official misconduct or erroneous advice from a court or its officers.” *See Holland v. Florida*, 560 U.S. 631, 649 (2010). In *Holland*, the Court found equitable tolling warranted where a state court clerk erroneously advised a petitioner that his deadline had been extended. Similarly, here, the Ninth Circuit Clerk’s office provided information that was materially false and caused Buckles to delay filing his certiorari petition. The fact that the Clerk’s office did not correct the error—even after receiving Buckles’s declaration—further supports the conclusion that the misrepresentation was substantial and prejudicial.

Buckles’s reliance on this advice was reasonable. He had no reason to doubt the authority of the Clerk’s office, especially given that the office had previously accepted his filings and engaged in communication about his case. The burden of correcting misinformation should rest with the court, not the pro se litigant. *See id.* at 650 (“Equitable tolling is not a license to ignore the rules, but a recognition that fairness sometimes demands flexibility.”)
