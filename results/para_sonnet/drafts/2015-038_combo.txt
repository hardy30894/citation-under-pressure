ARGUMENT

I. The Plain Text of ANILCA Preserves the Park Service’s Authority to Enforce Regulations of General Applicability Within Conservation System Units.

The starting point of statutory analysis is the text Congress enacted. “[W]here the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning[,] the duty of interpretation does not arise.” Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 103(c) of ANILCA withdraws only the special conservation-system-unit provisions of the Act from application to non-public lands; it does not purport to strip the Park Service of the general regulatory authority it exercises under its organic statutes throughout every unit it administers. The hovercraft ban is not a regulation that “applies solely to public lands within” a conservation system unit—it applies uniformly to all persons within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, without regard to who holds title to the land or water beneath their feet. Congress’s words, not Sturgeon’s preferred policy, must govern. As this Court explained in United States v. American Trucking Associations, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes.” Congress’s chosen words in § 103(c) address only lands, not the Park Service’s independent authority to regulate conduct that occurs within park boundaries.

II. Congress’s Property Clause Authority, and Its Corollary Power to Protect Federal Lands, Confirm the Park Service’s Continuing Regulatory Reach.

Congress’s power over federal lands is plenary. “The power over the public land thus entrusted to Congress is without limitations.” United States v. San Francisco, 310 U.S. 16, 29 (1940). That power necessarily includes authority to regulate conduct that would interfere with the use and enjoyment of adjoining or interspersed federal lands, even where the regulated conduct occurs on land or water that is not itself federally owned. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518 (1897), this Court sustained a federal statute forbidding fences that enclosed public land even though the fence in question was built entirely on privately owned sections checkerboarded with the public domain, holding that Congress may reach conduct on private land when necessary to protect the usefulness of the public land it surrounds. The same logic applies with even greater force here: the Nation River flows through, and its banks and gravel bars are surrounded by, the federally administered preserve, and unregulated hovercraft use on the river would directly affect the preserve’s wildlife, visitors, and resources that Congress placed under the Park Service’s care.

This authority is not diminished merely because Alaska may hold title to the submerged lands. As the Court held in Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 536 (1911), “the United States can prohibit absolutely or fix the terms on which its property may be used,” and the government may regulate activity affecting its lands regardless of competing claims of ownership or use asserted under state law. Likewise, in Utah Power & Light Co. v. United States, 243 U.S. 389 (1917), the Court confirmed that the United States’ control over the use and preservation of federal lands cannot be defeated by state law governing adjacent or underlying property interests. The same principle forecloses Sturgeon’s argument that Alaska’s ownership of the riverbed extinguishes federal regulatory authority over conduct occurring on the water above it, within a unit Congress placed under NPS administration.

III. The Federal Navigational Servitude Independently Supports the Park Service’s Authority Over the Nation River.

Even apart from the Property Clause, the federal government possesses a dominant navigational servitude over navigable waters that is not defeated by state ownership of the riverbed. This Court has long recognized that federal authority over navigable waters used for interstate commerce derives independently from the Commerce Clause and is not contingent on title to the submerged lands. See United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377 (1940); The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870). In United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222 (1956), the Court held that a State’s title to the bed of a navigable river is qualified by the federal government’s superior power to regulate the stream for purposes connected to commerce and navigation, and that a riparian owner’s state-law rights yield to that federal authority. Whatever interest Alaska holds in the bed of the Nation River, it does not oust the Park Service of authority to regulate hovercraft operation on the water above—authority Congress vested in the Secretary long before ANILCA and left undisturbed.

IV. Nothing in ANILCA Clearly and Manifestly Repeals the Park Service’s Pre-Existing Regulatory Authority.

Repeals of existing federal authority by implication are disfavored. “The intention of the legislature to repeal must be clear and manifest.” Posadas v. National City Bank, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936). Congress said as much expressly in ANILCA itself, providing that nothing in the Act shall be construed as superseding or modifying “existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions,” except as specifically set forth in the Act. 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Section 103(c) says nothing specific about regulations of general applicability such as the hovercraft rule. Under Posadas, that silence forecloses any claim that Congress meant to repeal the Park Service’s longstanding authority to protect the resources and visitors of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals affirming summary judgment for the Park Service should be affirmed.
