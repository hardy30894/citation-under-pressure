The Fourteenth Amendment affords no due process right to introduce expert mental-state evidence to rebut the prosecution’s proof of criminal intent when an insanity defense remains available. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952), the Court upheld a state’s assignment of the insanity burden to the defendant, recognizing the Clause’s deference to state procedural allocations. Arizona’s restriction follows directly from that principle.

States enjoy substantial latitude in defining offenses and defenses. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895), established the prosecution’s burden on elements while permitting states to channel mental-condition evidence to separate defenses. Similar deference appears in Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 420 (1902), and Matheson v. United States, 227 U.S. 540, 543 (1913).

Fundamental fairness under due process does not mandate admission of all relevant evidence. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934), requires only that proceedings not be fundamentally unfair. Exclusionary rules offend the Clause solely if they contradict “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Arizona’s rule satisfies this standard.

The Constitution imposes no obligation to accept every proffer of evidence. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), and Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 235 (1940), guard against sham proceedings but do not dictate specific evidentiary rules for expert testimony on mens rea. Accordingly, Arizona’s statute comports with the Fourteenth Amendment.
