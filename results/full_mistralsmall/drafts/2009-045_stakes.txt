**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Enhancing the Lodestar Based on Exceptional Performance and Results.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s award of attorneys’ fees, including a $4.5 million enhancement, because the court did not abuse its discretion in finding that this was a “rare” and “exceptional” case warranting an upward adjustment under the lodestar method. *See Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air (“Delaware Valley I”), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986)* (“upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts”); *Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984)* (enhancement may be justified where counsel offers “specific evidence” of “superior” performance and “exceptional” success).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the lodestar—calculated as the product of reasonable hours and a reasonable hourly rate—is presumptively sufficient to reflect a reasonable fee. *Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565; Blum, 465 U.S. at 897*. However, the Court has never foreclosed the possibility of enhancement in extraordinary cases. *See id.* at 566 (“the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar ... [but] the Court leaves open the possibility of an extraordinary case”). The Eleventh Circuit properly followed this guidance, holding that enhancements are permissible where supported by specific, detailed findings. *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008)*.

Here, the district court made extensive findings—based on its own observations and the record—demonstrating that plaintiffs’ counsel achieved exceptional results through superior performance. The court noted that counsel’s work “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected” and that the litigation produced “comprehensive systemic relief” benefiting thousands of foster children. *Kenny A. ex rel. Winn v. Perdue, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006)*. Such findings are precisely what the Supreme Court envisioned as justifying an enhancement. *See Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565; Blum, 465 U.S. at 899*.

**II. The District Court’s Findings Were Sufficiently Specific and Supported by the Record.**

Petitioners argue that the district court’s enhancement rested on impermissible factors, but this mischaracterizes the court’s analysis. The district court did not enhance fees based on vague impressions; it relied on concrete evidence, including:

- The complexity and duration of the litigation (nearly a decade of advocacy);
- The systemic reforms achieved (e.g., improved foster care standards, monitoring systems);
- The skill and dedication of counsel (e.g., “stellar performance” in negotiations and courtroom advocacy); and
- The market rates for comparable legal services in Atlanta.

*See id.* at 1286-90. These findings are consistent with the Supreme Court’s instruction that enhancements require “specific evidence” of superior performance and exceptional results. *Blum, 465 U.S. at 899; Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 565*.

Petitioners also contend that the district court’s reliance on its own observations violated due process, but this ignores that judges are uniquely positioned to evaluate counsel’s performance. *See Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983)* (“the district court has discretion in determining the reasonableness of a fee award”). The Eleventh Circuit correctly deferred to the district court’s firsthand assessment, as it is “in the best position to evaluate trial-level advocacy.” *Kenny A., 532 F.3d at 1242*.

**III. The Weight of Circuit Precedent Supports the Enhancement.**

Every circuit to consider this issue—including the Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh—has permitted enhancements in rare cases where counsel’s performance and results are exceptional. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999)*. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Kenny A.* aligns with this consensus, and no circuit has held that enhancements are categorically prohibited. *See id.* at 1242.

**IV. Petitioners’ Proposed Rule Would Undermine the Purpose of Fee-Shifting Statutes.**

Fee-shifting statutes like 42 U.S.C. § 1988 are designed to ensure that plaintiffs can obtain competent counsel to vindicate important rights. *See Blum, 465 U.S. at 891*. If enhancements are barred, attorneys may be discouraged from taking complex, high-stakes cases where exceptional skill is required to achieve systemic reform. The district court’s decision properly balanced these concerns by rewarding extraordinary advocacy that produced life-changing results for vulnerable children.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s enhancement was a proper exercise of its discretion, grounded in specific findings and supported by the record. The Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the award, and this Court should do likewise.
