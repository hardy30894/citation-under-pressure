The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining a defendant’s competency to plead guilty or waive counsel. A defendant who is competent to stand trial is competent to make those decisions.

This Court has long recognized that a guilty plea constitutes a waiver of multiple constitutional rights, including the privilege against self-incrimination, the right to confront witnesses, and the right to a jury trial. Arsenault v. Massachusetts, 393 U.S. 5, 5 (1968) (per curiam); Brookhart v. Janis, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966). Such waivers are valid when made “intelligently and knowingly.” Brookhart, 384 U.S. at 4 (quoting Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938)). The same standard governs waiver of counsel. Carter v. Illinois, 329 U.S. 173, 174-175 (1946).

Competency to stand trial has always been measured by whether the defendant possesses “the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi, 344 U.S. 561, 566 (1953). Nothing in the Due Process Clause demands a more demanding inquiry once a defendant seeks to forgo trial. Palmer v. Ashe, 342 U.S. 134, 135-136 (1951). The Constitution protects against conviction of an incompetent person; it does not impose an additional layer of scrutiny for the decision to plead guilty or proceed pro se.

This Court has rejected the notion that every waiver of rights requires a separate, heightened competency finding. In Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312, 314 (1966) (per curiam), the Court addressed a defendant’s attempt to withdraw a certiorari petition and forgo further proceedings, yet applied the ordinary competency standard. Similarly, McCarthy v. United States, 394 U.S. 459, 466 (1969), emphasized that a guilty plea must be voluntary and intelligent, without suggesting any departure from the trial-competency test.

The Ninth Circuit’s imposition of a “reasoned choice” requirement finds no support in these precedents. Due process is satisfied when the record demonstrates that the defendant understood the proceedings and the consequences of his choices. Hamilton v. California, 389 U.S. 921 (1967); Valentine v. Mercer, 201 U.S. 131, 136 (1906). Because the state courts applied the correct standard and found Moran competent, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
