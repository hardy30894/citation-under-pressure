**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” forbids executing a person whose disability renders him unable to understand the moral wrongness of his crime or to meaningfully participate in his own defense. *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986) (“The Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of the insane.”).  In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 339–40 (1989), this Court recognized that the same constitutional principle applies to persons with intellectual disability, because their impairments diminish culpability and heighten the risk of wrongful conviction.  The Virginia Supreme Court erred in relying on *Penry* to uphold Atkins’s death sentence, because subsequent developments in clinical science and evolving standards of decency now compel a categorical rule: executing a person with intellectual disability is cruel and unusual punishment.

A.  Evolving Standards of Decency

This Court has repeatedly held that the Eighth Amendment must be interpreted in light of “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958).  In *Atkins*, the Court recognized that a national consensus had emerged against executing persons with intellectual disability.  536 U.S. 304, 314–16 (2002).  Since then, the number of States prohibiting such executions has grown from twelve to nineteen, and the Federal Government has codified the prohibition.  *Id.* at 315 n.21; 18 U.S.C. § 4241(d).  The Court has also emphasized that “the clearest and most reliable objective evidence of contemporary values” is legislation. *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551, 564 (2005).  The expansion of legislative prohibitions since *Atkins* confirms that society’s standards have continued to evolve.

B.  Clinical Consensus on Intellectual Disability

Modern clinical definitions of intellectual disability—adopted by the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities and the American Psychiatric Association—require deficits in intellectual functioning (IQ below approximately 70) and adaptive behavior that manifest before age eighteen.  *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders* (5th ed. 2013) (“DSM-5”) at 37; AAIDD, *Intellectual Disability: Definition, Classification, and Systems of Supports* (11th ed. 2010) at 1.  These criteria reflect the medical consensus that intellectual disability is a permanent, neurodevelopmental condition that impairs reasoning, impulse control, and the ability to learn from experience.  *Hall v. Florida*, 572 U.S. 701, 720–21 (2014) (recognizing clinical consensus).

The clinical consensus also establishes that persons with intellectual disability are less culpable and more vulnerable to wrongful conviction.  *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318–19.  Their impairments diminish their capacity to understand legal proceedings, communicate with counsel, and control impulsive behavior.  *Id.* at 318.  Because they are more likely to confess falsely or to waive constitutional rights unknowingly, executing them risks punishing the innocent.  *Id.* at 319.

C.  Categorical Prohibition

This Court has applied a categorical rule when execution would serve no legitimate penological purpose.  *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407, 420 (2008) (execution for child rape is unconstitutional).  The same logic applies to intellectual disability.  Because persons with intellectual disability cannot comprehend the meaning of their punishment or why it is deserved, execution does not deter others or provide retributive justice.  *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 319–20.  The risk of wrongful execution is unacceptably high, and the diminished culpability of the offender makes the death penalty disproportionate.  *Id.* at 318–19.

D.  Application to Atkins

The record amply supports the conclusion that Atkins has intellectual disability.  A forensic psychologist testified that Atkins’s IQ is in the mildly retarded range and that his adaptive functioning is significantly impaired.  J.A. 123–25.  The State presented no contrary evidence.  The jury’s sentencing determination therefore rested on a constitutionally deficient assessment of Atkins’s culpability.  *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 339–40.  Because executing Atkins would violate the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment, the judgment below should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be reversed.
