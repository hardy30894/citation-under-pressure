**ARGUMENT**

The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages only where the plaintiff demonstrates that the employer “engaged in a discriminatory practice … with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This standard demands more than proof of intentional discrimination. It requires conduct that is “egregious” or “outrageous” in character, independent of the employer’s state of mind.

**I. The Statutory Text Imposes a Higher Threshold Than Mere Intentional Discrimination**

Congress deliberately chose the terms “malice” and “reckless indifference” to limit punitive damages to exceptional cases. In *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 46 (1983), this Court interpreted analogous language in 42 U.S.C. § 1983 to require “more than mere negligence” and to permit punitive damages only for conduct that is “motivated by evil motive or intent, or … involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” The same reasoning applies here. The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act confirms that § 1981a demands “an even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 29 (1991). Every circuit to address the issue has agreed that punitive damages under Title VII require a showing beyond intentional discrimination. *See, e.g., EEOC v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 156 F.3d 989, 992 (9th Cir. 1998) (requiring “egregious” misconduct); *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (punitive damages must surmount a “higher hurdle”).

**II. Legislative History Confirms Congress’s Intent to Limit Punitive Damages to Egregious Cases**

The interpretive memorandum of Senator Dole, a key sponsor, states that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991). This statement reflects the compromise that produced the 1991 Act: punitive damages were added to deter the most blameworthy conduct, not to punish every intentional violation. Representative Edwards’s contrary statement that punitive damages should be available “to the same extent … under 42 U.S.C. § 1981,” 137 Cong. Rec. H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991), is unavailing because § 1981 itself has been interpreted by many courts to require egregious conduct. *See, e.g., Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy-Province of Detroit, Inc.*, 816 F.2d 1104, 1109 (6th Cir. 1987) (punitive damages under § 1981 require “egregious” discrimination). The Dole memorandum thus harmonizes the legislative history and gives effect to Congress’s intent.

**III. The Common Law Tradition Supports Requiring Egregious Conduct**

At common law, punitive damages are reserved for conduct that is “outrageous” or “grossly wrong.” *Dayton v. Peck, Stow & Wilcox Co.*, 739 F.2d 690, 694 (1st Cir. 1984). The 1991 Act incorporated this tradition. The “malice or reckless indifference” standard is not satisfied by a showing that the employer acted intentionally; it requires proof that the employer knew its conduct was unlawful or acted with a “high probability” that it was violating federal rights. *See Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 527 U.S. 526, 535 (1999) (recognizing that “reckless indifference” requires knowledge that the conduct may violate Title VII). An employer who mistakenly but reasonably believes its actions are lawful cannot be said to act with malice or reckless indifference. *See id.* at 537. The egregiousness of the conduct—its severity, duration, or blatant disregard of law—is therefore an independent element of the punitive damages inquiry.

**IV. The Lower Court’s Approach Would Eviscerate the Statutory Standard**

The district court and the panel below erred by equating intentional discrimination with the “malice or reckless indifference” required for punitive damages. If every intentional Title VII violation automatically supported punitive damages, the statutory cap would become meaningless, and employers would face punitive exposure for routine discrimination. Congress did not intend that result. The 1991 Act was a careful compromise, and this Court should respect its limits. *See Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994) (recognizing the Act’s “significant expansion” of remedies but also its limitations).

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed, and punitive damages should be available only upon a showing that the employer’s conduct was egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind.
