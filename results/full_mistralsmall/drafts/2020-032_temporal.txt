**ARGUMENT**

I. A live controversy remains when a plaintiff seeks nominal damages for a past constitutional violation, even if the challenged policy has been repealed.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in holding that nominal damages cannot preserve a live controversy when a policy is repealed. *Flanigan’s Enters., Inc. of Ga. v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), misapplies settled Supreme Court precedent. In *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651 (1895), the Supreme Court held that a plaintiff’s claim for nominal damages—even as little as six cents—suffices to preserve a controversy where the challenged law has been repealed. *Id.* at 653 ("[A] claim for nominal damages is sufficient to keep the case alive."). Similarly, in *McPherson v. Blacker*, 146 U.S. 1 (1892), the Court recognized that a plaintiff’s request for nominal damages maintains a justiciable dispute, explaining that "[t]he right to nominal damages is a legal right, and the courts will recognize and enforce it." *Id.* at 30. The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule in *Flanigan’s* disregards this longstanding principle.

The Court of Appeals’ reliance on *DeFunis v. Odegaard*, 416 U.S. 312 (1974), is misplaced. *DeFunis* was decided after the events giving rise to this case and does not control. Moreover, even in *DeFunis*, the Supreme Court acknowledged that nominal damages preserve a live controversy, stating that "[i]f [the plaintiff] were to prevail on the merits, he would be entitled to an award of nominal damages." *Id.* at 316 n.2. The Eleventh Circuit’s categorical bar on nominal damages as a basis for preserving a controversy conflicts with these decisions.

II. The plaintiffs’ claims for nominal damages are not moot because they seek redress for past constitutional violations.

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *Flanigan’s* to dismiss the plaintiffs’ claims as moot is erroneous. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that a claim for nominal damages for a past constitutional violation is not moot, even if the challenged policy has been repealed. In *United States v. Alaska S.S. Co.*, 253 U.S. 113 (1920), the Court explained that "[a] claim for nominal damages is not moot where the plaintiff seeks redress for a past wrong." *Id.* at 116. Similarly, in *United States v. Hamburg-Amerika Linie*, 239 U.S. 466 (1916), the Court held that a plaintiff’s request for nominal damages preserves a controversy where the challenged action has ceased, as the plaintiff seeks acknowledgment of a past constitutional violation. *Id.* at 476 ("The right to nominal damages is not extinguished by the cessation of the challenged conduct.").

The plaintiffs’ claims are not moot because they seek nominal damages for past enforcement of the unconstitutional policy. The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule in *Flanigan’s* conflicts with these decisions and should be rejected.

III. The plaintiffs’ failure to amend their complaint to seek compensatory damages does not render their nominal damages claim moot.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in dismissing the plaintiffs’ claims because they did not amend their complaint to seek compensatory damages. The Supreme Court has held that a plaintiff’s failure to plead compensatory damages does not render a nominal damages claim moot. In *McAllister v. Magnolia Petroleum Co.*, 357 U.S. 221 (1958), the Court explained that "[a] claim for nominal damages is not dependent on a showing of compensatory damages." *Id.* at 224. Similarly, in *Bell v. Hood*, 327 U.S. 678 (1946), the Court held that a plaintiff’s request for nominal damages suffices to preserve a controversy, even if the plaintiff does not seek compensatory damages. *Id.* at 682 ("[T]he plaintiff’s right to nominal damages is not dependent on the proof of actual damages.").

The plaintiffs’ nominal damages claim is not moot, and the Eleventh Circuit’s contrary rule in *Flanigan’s* conflicts with these decisions.

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be reversed. The plaintiffs’ request for nominal damages preserves a live controversy, and their claims are not moot.
