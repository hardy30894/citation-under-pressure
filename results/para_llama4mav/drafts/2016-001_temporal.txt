The question presented is whether damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringing component when the infringed design patent applies only to a component of a product. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits, rather than limiting damages to the profits attributable to the infringing design.

The governing statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that a design patent infringer shall be liable for "the total profit." However, this provision must be interpreted in light of the principle that damages should be commensurate with the infringement. In Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co., 114 U.S. 439 (1885), the Supreme Court held that the "total profit" referred to in the design patent statute is the profit attributable to the infringing design. The Court stated that the infringer is liable for "the total profit, but only 'in consequence of' the infringement." Id. at 444.

In Westinghouse Electric & Manufacturing Co. v. Wagner Electric & Manufacturing Co., 225 U.S. 604 (1912), the Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle, holding that the infringer is liable for the profits "made by him in consequence of the infringement." Id. at 614 (quoting Tilghman v. Proctor, 125 U.S. 136, 146 (1888)). The Court emphasized that the burden is on the infringer to prove that a portion of the profits are attributable to other factors. Id. at 622.

In this case, the infringed design patents apply only to specific components of Samsung's smartphones. Therefore, the damages award should be limited to the profits attributable to those components. The Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits, as this is not commensurate with the infringement.

The principle that damages should be commensurate with the infringement is also supported by the Supreme Court's decision in Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. 480 (1854), where the Court held that the patent statute "contemplates a reasonable compensation for the pecuniary loss sustained by the patentee." Id. at 489. In this case, the pecuniary loss sustained by Apple is the profit attributable to the infringing design, not Samsung's entire profits.

In conclusion, the Federal Circuit erred in affirming the district court's award of damages based on Samsung's entire profits. Damages for design patent infringement should be limited to the portion of the infringer's profits attributable to the infringing component. This Court should reverse the Federal Circuit's decision and remand for a determination of the profits attributable to the infringing design.
