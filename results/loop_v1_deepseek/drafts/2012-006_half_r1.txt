The government bears the burden of proving every element of a charged conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, including that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period. That principle flows from the presumption of innocence, which is "an instrument of proof created by the law in favor of one accused, whereby the jury is instructed to presume the accused innocent until the contrary is established beyond a reasonable doubt." Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432, 459 (1895). Thus, the government must prove every element of the crime charged by proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895); see also id. at 487 (presumption of innocence is "a substantial part of the law").

A conspiracy is a continuing offense. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). "The conspiracy continues until the object of the conspiracy is attained." Id. at 608. Because the offense is continuing, the statute of limitations does not run while the conspiracy remains active. Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211, 216 (1946) (conspiracy to violate immigration laws remained a continuing offense until its objective was accomplished). It follows that, to convict a defendant, the government must prove not only that he entered into the conspiracy but also that he remained a member of it during the period charged in the indictment and within the limitations period. The government's burden of persuasion therefore encompasses membership during the relevant period.

Evidence that the defendant withdrew from the conspiracy before the limitations period does not shift that burden. In Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347 (1912), this Court observed that "[t]he burden of proof, however, is on the accused to show that he withdrew from the conspiracy." Id. at 369. But that statement presupposes that the government has otherwise proved the defendant's membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period. Hyde does not relieve the government of its initial burden to prove membership within that period. Where the defendant introduces evidence that he withdrew before the limitations period, the government must still persuade the jury beyond a reasonable doubt that, notwithstanding that evidence, the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the period. The lower court's contrary approach effectively shifted to petitioners the burden of disproving membership during the limitations period, contrary to the settled rule that the government must prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged.

The courts of appeals have uniformly recognized this rule. "The burden is on the Government to prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy within the period of the statute of limitations." United States v. Mennuti, 239 F.2d 747, 750 (2d Cir. 1957). The government must likewise "prove that the conspiracy continued into the period of limitations and that the defendant was then a party to it." United States v. Liss, 105 F.2d 144, 146 (2d Cir. 1939). These cases make plain that evidence of withdrawal—even if insufficient to establish withdrawal as a complete defense standing alone—may be considered by the jury in determining whether the government has carried its burden of persuasion. Alexander v. United States, 138 F.2d 359, 362 (2d Cir. 1943) (prosecution must establish that the conspiracy extended into the limitations period and that the defendant was a member during that period); see also Pizzarello v. United States, 408 F.2d 579, 585 (2d Cir. 1969) (same).

Here, the government's evidence showed that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the statute of limitations period began. Petitioners moved for a new trial, arguing that the jury lacked sufficient evidence to convict them of participation in a single conspiracy continuing into the limitations period. The trial court denied the motion, and the court of appeals affirmed. That was error. Once petitioners presented evidence that the conspiracy, if any, had ended before the limitations period, the government was required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners remained members during the period. The jury should have been instructed that the government bore the burden of persuasion on that element. Without such an instruction, the jury could have convicted based on a misapprehension that petitioners were obliged to prove the conspiracy's termination. Because the government's evidence of continued membership was contested, and the existence of a single ongoing conspiracy was central to the trial, the error cannot be deemed harmless. See Mennuti, 239 F.2d at 750; Alexander, 138 F.2d at 362.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the cause remanded for a new trial with proper instructions placing on the government the burden of persuasion to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that petitioners were members of the conspiracy during the statute-of-limitations period.
