**ARGUMENT**

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This predominance inquiry “tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation.” Amchem Prods., Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997). A district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” that may “entail some overlap with the merits of the plaintiff’s underlying claim.” Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 564 U.S. 338, 351 (2011). That analysis is no less demanding when the issue is whether damages are susceptible of measurement on a class-wide basis. See In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig., 552 F.3d 305, 318 (3d Cir. 2008) (holding that “the court must resolve” factual disputes bearing on certification requirements).

The District Court failed to perform this analysis. Plaintiffs’ damages expert offered a single regression model that attributed all supra-competitive prices in the Philadelphia DMA to Comcast’s entire course of conduct. The court, however, limited the class to a single theory: that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilders. App. 00032. Nothing in the record demonstrated that the model could isolate damages traceable solely to that theory. The expert conceded that the model did not attempt to do so. Because the model therefore measured damages that the class could not recover, it supplied no evidence that damages were measurable on a class-wide basis with common proof.

This defect is fatal under the predominance requirement. “Common questions” cannot predominate when each class member’s damages still depend on individualized proof of which portion of the overcharge, if any, resulted from the sole remaining theory of liability. See Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc., 259 F.3d 154, 187 (3d Cir. 2001) (affirming denial of certification where damages calculations required “individualized inquiries”). The District Court’s acceptance of an undifferentiated model therefore rested on a clearly erroneous factual finding and an erroneous legal standard.

The court of appeals compounded the error by deferring to the District Court’s conclusion without examining whether the model actually matched the certified theory. That approach is inconsistent with the obligation to ensure that Rule 23’s requirements are met by a preponderance of admissible evidence. Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 320. Because the record contains no such evidence that damages can be measured class-wide, the certification order cannot stand.
