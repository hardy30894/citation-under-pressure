**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over state-based legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law, even when the patent issue is not the primary issue in the case—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal patent law creates a unique and exclusive jurisdictional regime, and that claims arising from the handling of patent litigation are properly within the exclusive cognizance of federal courts. This is not a matter of judicial preference or policy, but of statutory construction and constitutional principle. The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over any case that “arises under” federal patent law, and the present case unquestionably falls within that category.

The jurisdictional foundation for this conclusion is found in *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.*, 305 U.S. 250 (1938), where the Court held that “the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under the patent laws.” Id. at 254. This principle applies with particular force to patent law, which is a federal creation and governed by a comprehensive statutory scheme. As the Court explained in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “the patent laws are a part of the general law of the United States, and the courts of the United States have exclusive jurisdiction of all cases arising under them.” Id. This exclusive jurisdiction is not limited to claims that directly assert patent infringement; it extends to any case in which the resolution of a patent issue is necessary to the outcome.

In *United States v. L. A. Tucker Truck Lines, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 33, 37 (1952), the Court stated: “The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over matters arising under the patent laws because of the need for uniformity in the interpretation and enforcement of federal patent rights.” Id. This concern for uniformity and national consistency is the very reason Congress granted exclusive federal jurisdiction over patent-related matters. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “a suit which necessarily depends upon the construction of a federal statute is one arising under the Constitution or laws of the United States.” Id. The same principle applies to federal patent law. The malpractice claim cannot be resolved without determining whether the experimental use exception applies—a question of federal patent law.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that state courts may adjudicate claims that turn on the interpretation of federal patent law. In *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.*, 305 U.S. 250, 254 (1938), the Court reiterated: “The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over all cases arising under the patent laws, whether the claim is for infringement or for damages.” Id. These cases make clear that the jurisdictional reach of federal patent law is not confined to the initial infringement action; it extends to any claim that depends on the resolution of a patent law issue.

The argument that the patent issue is not the “primary” issue in the malpractice suit is irrelevant. As the Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), “the character of the suit is determined not by the form of the pleading, but by the substance of the claim.” Id. The malpractice claim is not a state tort claim in disguise; it is a claim that depends on the validity of a federal patent right. The outcome of the malpractice suit turns entirely on whether the experimental use exception applies—a question of federal patent law. The Court in *United States v. American Linen Supply Co.* made this clear: “The jurisdiction of the federal courts is not limited to cases in which the patent is the subject of the litigation, but extends to all cases arising under the patent laws.” 305 U.S. at 254.

Finally, the concern for federalism raised by the dissent is misplaced. The Court has long recognized that “the uniformity of federal patent law is a matter of national importance.” *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939). Allowing state courts to interpret federal patent law would undermine that uniformity and create the very “conflict in the administration of the patent laws” that the Supreme Court has sought to prevent. As the Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “the federal courts are the proper forum for the adjudication of federal rights.” Id.

For these reasons, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case. The claim arises under federal patent law, the patent issue is substantial and actually disputed, and the resolution of that issue is necessary to the outcome. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas must be reversed, and the case remanded for adjudication in the federal courts.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner prays that this Court reverse the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas and remand the case for proceedings consistent with this brief.
