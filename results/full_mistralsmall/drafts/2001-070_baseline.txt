**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT PROHIBIT THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS.**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” must be interpreted by reference to “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). In assessing such standards, this Court accords substantial deference to legislative judgments and does not impose its own “ subjective conceptions of justice.” *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977). Where there is no national consensus against a particular punishment, and where the punishment furthers legitimate penological objectives, it does not offend the Eighth Amendment.

A. There is no national consensus against executing mentally retarded offenders.

This Court has repeatedly held that “the Eighth Amendment draws its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 311 (2002) (quoting *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101). In determining whether such standards exist, the Court examines “objective indicia of society’s standards, as expressed in legislative enactments and state practice.” *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551, 563 (2005) (quoting *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 312).

At the time of *Atkins*, 18 States prohibited the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 315. By contrast, 20 States permitted such executions, and the Federal Government authorized the death penalty for mentally retarded offenders under the Federal Death Penalty Act. *Id.* Since *Atkins*, the number of States prohibiting such executions has increased to 20, while 29 States and the Federal Government continue to permit it. *See Hall v. Florida*, 572 U.S. 701, 710 (2014). This Court has repeatedly cautioned that “a small number of States” cannot alone establish a national consensus. *Roper*, 543 U.S. at 565 (quoting *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 315). The current division among the States confirms that there is no “consistent trend” toward abolition. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 315.

B. The execution of mentally retarded offenders serves legitimate penological objectives.

This Court has recognized that the death penalty serves two principal penological goals: retribution and deterrence. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 183 (1976) (plurality opinion). The execution of mentally retarded offenders furthers both objectives.

First, retribution is “a primary justification for the death penalty.” *Id.* at 183. Mentally retarded offenders, though less culpable than their non-disabled counterparts, are still capable of forming criminal intent and committing heinous crimes. *See Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 335 (1989) (plurality opinion) (“[T]he fact that a person is mentally retarded does not mean that he can demonstrate no moral culpability or that society has no interest in punishing him.”). The death penalty thus serves the legitimate retributive purpose of holding such offenders accountable for their crimes.

Second, the death penalty may deter future crimes. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 186. While this Court has acknowledged that empirical evidence on deterrence is “inconclusive,” *id.*, it has nonetheless deferred to legislative judgments that the death penalty serves a deterrent function. *See id.* at 186 n.30. The same deference applies here. The execution of mentally retarded offenders may deter others—including those who might exploit such individuals to commit crimes—from engaging in similar conduct.

C. The execution of mentally retarded offenders does not violate the Eighth Amendment’s proportionality principle.

This Court has held that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are “grossly disproportionate” to the offense. *Ewing v. California*, 538 U.S. 11, 20 (2003) (plurality opinion). However, the Court has also recognized that “death is different” and that the Eighth Amendment allows for a “narrow category” of punishments for the most serious crimes. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 187. The execution of mentally retarded offenders does not fall within the category of grossly disproportionate punishments.

In *Atkins*, this Court concluded that mentally retarded offenders possess “diminished capacities to understand and process information, to communicate, to abstract from mistakes and learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, to control impulses, and to understand the reactions of others.” 536 U.S. at 318. While these characteristics may diminish moral culpability, they do not render the offender categorically ineligible for the death penalty. *See id.* at 319 (“[M]entally retarded defendants who commit murder in especially heinous circumstances may be guilty of behavior which society cannot tolerate and still retain the spirit of moral proportionality that characterizes the Eighth Amendment.”).

**II. THE VIRGINIA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY APPLIED PENRY AND DID NOT ERR IN AFFIRMING PETITIONER’S DEATH SENTENCE.**

The Virginia Supreme Court relied on *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), in rejecting petitioner’s claim that his mental retardation barred his execution. The court did not err in doing so.

In *Penry*, this Court declined to recognize a categorical bar on the execution of mentally retarded offenders, holding instead that the Eighth Amendment permitted such executions where the jury was allowed to consider the defendant’s retardation as a mitigating factor. *Id.* at 340. The Court emphasized that “the Constitution does not prohibit the execution of mentally retarded individuals who are competent to stand trial and whose crimes demonstrate sufficient culpability to warrant the death penalty.” *Id.* at 335.

The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied *Penry* in affirming petitioner’s death sentence. The jury was instructed to consider petitioner’s mental retardation as a mitigating factor, and it did so before unanimously recommending the death penalty. The court’s reliance on *Penry* was consistent with this Court’s precedent and did not violate the Eighth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Virginia Supreme Court should be affirmed.
