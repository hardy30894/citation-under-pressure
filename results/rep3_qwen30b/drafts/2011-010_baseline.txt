**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore Act coverage only to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), which provides workers’ compensation benefits for “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding, which rejects any geographic limitation on the situs of injury, misreads the statute, disregards controlling precedent, and undermines the foundational principle that statutory coverage is determined by *situs*, not merely by *status* or *nexus*. The Court should reverse.

### I. THE STATUTE’S LANGUAGE AND STRUCTURE DEMAND A SITUS-OF-INJURY REQUIREMENT

The plain language of § 1333(b) does not extend coverage to injuries occurring anywhere in the world simply because they are “the result of” offshore operations. Rather, it conditions coverage on injuries that “occur” as a result of operations on the outer Continental Shelf. The word *occurring* is a temporal and spatial term, not a causal one. It refers to the *location* and *timing* of the injury, not merely its cause. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (“The word ‘occurrence’ in the statute is used in its ordinary sense, meaning the happening of an event at a particular time and place.”). The statute does not say “resulting from,” which would suggest a purely causal test; it says “occurring as the result of,” which implies that the injury must take place *in the context of* or *at the site of* the operation.

This interpretation is reinforced by the structure of OCSLA. Section 1333(a)(2)(A) explicitly applies state law to the “subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Court in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986), emphasized that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual.” The Court’s footnote in *Tallentire*—which noted that § 1333(b) “makes compensation . . . payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act” only for injuries “as the result of operations on the Outer Continental Shelf”—is not dictum in the sense of being irrelevant; it is *contextual* and *consistent* with the Court’s broader holding that OCSLA’s reach is defined by *situs*. See *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. The Court did not need to resolve the issue before it, but it did not contradict its own jurisprudence. To the contrary, it reaffirmed that the outer Continental Shelf is the *locus* of coverage.

### II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S “SUBSTANTIAL NEXUS” TEST IS CONTRARY TO PRECEDENT AND THE STATUTE

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test—under which an injury on land may qualify if it is “substantially connected” to offshore operations—creates a regime of limitless liability and undermines the geographic boundaries that Congress established. The Court has long held that the LHWCA’s maritime situs requirement is not a mere formality but a “critical jurisdictional element.” *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). The Court has rejected attempts to expand the situs beyond the “adjoining area” used for loading or unloading vessels. See *Motoviloff v. Dir., Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs*, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982). The Ninth Circuit’s approach, which would allow coverage for injuries on land even when the facility is not used for maritime commerce, contradicts this settled law.

The Court has also rejected the notion that a “but for” causal connection is sufficient. In *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray (Herb’s Welding I)*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985), the Court held that a worker injured on a platform in state waters was not entitled to LHWCA benefits because the platform was not on the outer Continental Shelf. The Court emphasized that “the inconsistent coverage here results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” Id. at 422. The same logic applies here: Congress limited coverage to operations *on* the outer Continental Shelf, not to operations *conducted from* the outer Continental Shelf.

### III. THE FIFTH CIRCUIT’S SITUS-OF-INJURY TEST IS THE CORRECT INTERPRETATION

The Fifth Circuit’s adoption of a situs-of-injury requirement in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989), and its repeated reaffirmation in *Becker v. Tidewater, Inc.*, 586 F.3d 358, 366–67 (5th Cir. 2009), is consistent with the statutory text, structure, and precedent. The Fifth Circuit correctly recognized that the outer Continental Shelf is a *geographic zone* with defined boundaries, and that OCSLA’s benefits are triggered only when an injury occurs *within* that zone. This is not a rigid rule; it is a principled application of statutory construction. The Court should not adopt a test that would allow coverage for injuries on land, even when the facility is not used for maritime purposes, simply because the employee worked on an offshore platform.
