ARGUMENT

I. A CREDIBLE CLAIM OF ACTUAL INNOCENCE, STANDING ALONE, WARRANTS EQUITABLE TOLLING OF AEDPA'S LIMITATIONS PERIOD UNDER THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT.

This Court has already answered the question presented. In Souter v. Jones, 395 F.3d 577 (6th Cir. 2005), this Court held that AEDPA's one-year limitations period is subject to equitable tolling where a petitioner makes "a credible claim of actual innocence." Id. at 588, 599-602. Souter did not condition that relief on a separate showing of diligence in discovering or presenting the exculpatory evidence; the actual-innocence showing itself carried the tolling analysis. Nothing in Pace v. DiGuglielmo, 544 U.S. 408 (2005), overrules that holding. Pace addressed a generic equitable-tolling claim premised on confusion about state filing requirements, not a claim resting on the fundamental-miscarriage-of-justice exception recognized in Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), and Schlup v. Delo, 513 U.S. 298 (1995). Pace's two-part test—that a petitioner show "(1) that he has been pursuing his rights diligently, and (2) that some extraordinary circumstance stood in his way," 544 U.S. at 418—was announced to police the ordinary run of tolling requests, not to displace the separate, substantive innocence gateway this Court and the Supreme Court have long recognized.

II. THE ACTUAL-INNOCENCE GATEWAY IS A DISTINCT DOCTRINE ROOTED IN THE RISK OF CONVICTING THE INNOCENT, NOT IN THE PETITIONER'S DILIGENCE.

The actual-innocence exception exists because "in an extraordinary case, where a constitutional violation has probably resulted in the conviction of one who is actually innocent, a federal habeas court may grant the writ even in the absence of a showing of cause for the procedural default." Murray v. Carrier, 477 U.S. at 496. That concern is not diminished because a petitioner was slow to act. The Supreme Court's fundamental-miscarriage-of-justice cases have never treated diligence as a threshold requirement; they treat the reliability of the conviction itself as the touchstone. See Sawyer v. Whitley, 505 U.S. 333, 339 (1992) (the exception is concerned with "actual" as compared to "legal" innocence). Similarly, in Herrera v. Collins, 506 U.S. 390 (1993), the Supreme Court recognized that "a truly persuasive demonstration of 'actual innocence'" implicates constitutional concerns independent of any procedural default or delay. Id. at 417. The logic of these decisions is that society's interest in not executing or imprisoning the innocent outweighs its interest in procedural regularity—an interest that does not evaporate because a habeas petitioner filed late.

Requiring diligence as a precondition to even reaching the merits of an innocence claim inverts this doctrine. It would allow courts to procedurally bar consideration of new evidence establishing that "no reasonable juror" would have convicted the petitioner, Schlup, 513 U.S. at 327, based solely on the timing of the petition, without ever examining whether the conviction is reliable. The Schlup standard is already, in the Supreme Court's words, "demanding and permits review only in the 'extraordinary' case." House v. Bell, 547 U.S. 518, 538 (2006) (quoting Schlup, 513 U.S. at 327). That demanding threshold—not an independent diligence requirement layered on top of it—is the safeguard against abuse of the writ. Grafting a diligence requirement onto an already rigorous evidentiary standard is unnecessary and defeats the purpose of the exception: ensuring that federal courts do not turn away demonstrably innocent prisoners on account of a procedural technicality.

III. SISTER CIRCUITS HAVE REACHED THE SAME CONCLUSION, AND THIS COURT SHOULD REAFFIRM SOUTER.

The weight of authority confirms that Pace's diligence requirement does not extend to actual-innocence gateway claims. In Doe v. Menefee, 391 F.3d 147 (2d Cir. 2004), the Second Circuit held that a credible showing of actual innocence permits review of an untimely petition without regard to the reasons for the delay, explaining that the equitable inquiry must focus on the reliability of the new evidence rather than on the petitioner's timeliness. Likewise, in Lee v. Lampert, 653 F.3d 929 (9th Cir. 2011) (en banc), the Ninth Circuit, sitting en banc, held that "a credible claim of actual innocence constitutes an equitable exception to AEDPA's limitations period," without imposing an independent diligence prerequisite. Id. at 932, 937. This consensus reflects the same principle articulated in Holland v. Florida, 560 U.S. 631 (2010), where the Supreme Court cautioned against applying equitable-tolling rules mechanically and instead directed courts to consider the "particular circumstances" of each case. Id. at 649-50.

IV. PERKINS'S SHOWING SATISFIES THE ACTUAL-INNOCENCE STANDARD, AND THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN IMPOSING A DILIGENCE REQUIREMENT.

The district court alternatively held that even a successful innocence showing must yield to a diligence requirement drawn from Pace. That was error for the reasons above. Because the certificate of appealability was granted solely on the diligence question, this Court need not reach the sufficiency of Perkins's affidavits in the first instance; it need only hold, consistent with Souter, Schlup, Murray, and the decisions of the Second and Ninth Circuits, that reasonable diligence is not a freestanding precondition to invoking actual innocence as a basis for equitable tolling. The case should be remanded for the district court to evaluate Perkins's evidence under Schlup's substantive standard alone.
