**ARGUMENT**

**The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act Incorporates State Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law, Not Whenever State Law Is Merely Not Inconsistent.**

The text, structure, and purpose of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA), 43 U.S.C. § 1331 *et seq.*, compel the conclusion that state law applies on the Outer Continental Shelf only when necessary to fill a significant void or gap in federal law. The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that state law may apply whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law already covers the field.

**I. The Plain Language of OCSLA’s Choice-of-Law Provision Requires a Gap-Filling Function.**

OCSLA provides that federal law extends to the Outer Continental Shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law is then adopted as surrogate federal law only “[t]o the extent that [state laws] are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “[t]o the extent that” is a limiting qualifier. It does not authorize the wholesale importation of state law whenever it is not preempted. Rather, it permits state law to fill the interstices of federal law—i.e., to operate only where federal law is absent or incomplete.

This Court has recognized that OCSLA’s purpose was “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures such as [drilling platforms] on the outer Continental Shelf.” *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969). Congress understood that federal law alone might be inadequate to govern the broad range of activities on the Shelf. *See* 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953) (federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself”). The solution was not to superimpose state law on top of federal law, but to borrow state law to fill gaps where federal law was silent. *Rodrigue* itself applied state wrongful-death law only because the Death on the High Seas Act did not cover accidents on fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. Where federal law provides a comprehensive remedy, no gap exists, and state law cannot apply.

**II. The Fair Labor Standards Act Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Wage-and-Hour Law.**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, establishes a comprehensive federal regime governing minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping. The FLSA contains its own savings clause, which preserves state wage-and-hour laws that provide greater protections. 29 U.S.C. § 218(a). But that savings clause operates only within the states’ territorial jurisdiction. On the Outer Continental Shelf—a federal enclave—the FLSA itself is the exclusive source of wage-and-hour obligations. The FLSA’s savings clause does not incorporate state law onto the Shelf; it merely confirms that states may enact more protective laws within their own borders.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading would render the FLSA’s savings clause a vehicle for importing state law onto federal enclaves, a result Congress never intended. The FLSA’s comprehensive regulations—including Department of Labor regulations defining worktime, meal periods, and compensable hours—leave no void for state law to fill. *See* 29 C.F.R. §§ 785.1–785.50. Where federal law is complete, OCSLA does not permit state law to supplement it.

**III. The Fifth Circuit’s “Significant Void or Gap” Test Is the Correct Standard.**

The Fifth Circuit has consistently held that OCSLA incorporates state law only when necessary “to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969). This test respects OCSLA’s text and purpose. It ensures that federal law remains the primary governing law on the Shelf, with state law serving only as a gap-filler. The Ninth Circuit’s “applicable and not inconsistent” standard, by contrast, would allow state law to override or duplicate federal law whenever it is not expressly preempted. That approach conflicts with OCSLA’s structure, which makes federal law supreme and state law subsidiary.

**IV. Applying the Gap-Filling Test, California’s Wage-and-Hour Laws Do Not Apply.**

The FLSA and its implementing regulations comprehensively address wages, hours, and meal periods. There is no gap. California’s meal-period requirements, overtime rules, and penalty provisions are not needed to fill a void; they would merely add a second layer of regulation atop an existing federal scheme. Because the FLSA leaves no significant void, OCSLA does not authorize the incorporation of California law.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
