**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer, consistent with decades of Supreme Court precedent and the uniform view of every circuit to address the issue, is yes—but only in the “rare” and “exceptional” case, supported by specific evidence on the record and detailed findings by the district court.

**I. Supreme Court Precedent Permits Enhancement for Superior Performance and Exceptional Results in Rare Cases**

The lodestar method—multiplying hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—is the presumptively reasonable fee. *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984). However, the Court has never held that the lodestar is irrebuttable. To the contrary, *Blum* expressly left the door open: “In some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* at 901 (quoting *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)). Although the *Blum* Court reversed the enhancement before it because the applicant failed to offer “specific evidence” of exceptional results beyond that already reflected in the lodestar, the Court explicitly stated that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988” was a position it “reject[ed].” *Id.* at 901.

Two years later, in *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) (“*Delaware Valley I*”), the Court reaffirmed that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible . . . in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” *Id.* at 565 (citing *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Critically, the Court did not foreclose enhancements based on the quality of representation and results obtained. Instead, it held only that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar.” *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). The qualifier “ordinarily” preserves the possibility of an extraordinary case—precisely what the district court found here.

**II. The Lower Courts Did Not Err in Concluding That This Is the Rare and Exceptional Case**

The district court meticulously explained why this case qualifies. After observing plaintiffs’ counsel’s performance throughout the lengthy litigation, the court found that “the superb quality of counsel’s representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court cited specific, record-supported reasons: counsel’s extraordinary commitment, dedication, and professionalism; the comprehensive injunction obtained; and the exceptional nature of the results in a complex, high-stakes class action involving the welfare of foster children. *Id.* at 1289-90. The court also relied on its own “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to evaluate the prevailing market rates and the quality of performance. *Id.* at 1290.

The Eleventh Circuit’s affirmance, *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209 (11th Cir. 2008), is consistent with every other circuit to consider the issue. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits all agree that a district court may grant a lodestar enhancement for exceptional performance and results in rare cases. *See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). No circuit holds that such enhancements are categorically prohibited.

**III. Petitioners’ Arguments Misread Precedent and Would Undermine the Purpose of Fee-Shifting Statutes**

Petitioners contend that *Delaware Valley I* and *Blum* foreclose enhancements for quality of performance and results obtained. That reading is erroneous. In *Delaware Valley I*, the Court did not overrule *Blum*; it cited *Blum* with approval and reiterated that enhancements remain possible in rare cases. *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-66. The Court’s statement that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors” does not eliminate the possibility of an enhancement; it merely explains why enhancements are not routine. *Id.* at 566.

Petitioners’ suggestion that the district court improperly “testified” or acted *ex parte* is a mischaracterization. The court permissibly relied on its own observations—a practice explicitly approved in *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*, which require only “specific evidence on the record.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565. The district court’s detailed findings, grounded in the record and its own expertise, satisfy that standard.

**IV. Affirming the Enhancement Serves the Core Purpose of Fee-Shifting Statutes**

Fee-shifting statutes like § 1988 exist to ensure that plaintiffs with meritorious claims can secure legal representation. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 429. A rigid rule barring enhancements for exceptional performance would undermine that purpose by failing to compensate counsel for truly extraordinary results that confer exceptional benefits on the public. The district court’s discretion to award an enhancement in rare cases preserves the flexibility necessary to achieve the statutory goal.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
