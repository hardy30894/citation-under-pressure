**ARGUMENT**

**I. A Procedural Default Attributable to Counsel's Failure, Rather Than the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice, May Serve as Cause to Excuse a Related Default**

This Court has consistently held that federal habeas relief may be withheld only where the petitioner himself has forfeited his state remedies through a deliberate choice. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court explained that a habeas court may deny relief to a petitioner "who has deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts and in so doing has forfeited his state court remedies." *Id.* at 438. That rule presupposes a personal, considered decision by the petitioner—not an omission by counsel of which the petitioner was unaware. Carpenter did not decide to abandon his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim; his appellate counsel simply failed to raise it. Under *Fay*, that failure cannot be equated with Carpenter's own deliberate bypass.

This distinction reflects the settled principle, articulated in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. Attributing counsel's oversight to Carpenter as though he had knowingly relinquished his claim would collapse the careful distinction *Zerbst* draws between the client's own waiver and counsel's unilateral neglect. If a claim of constitutional magnitude cannot be waived by counsel alone, then a default rooted in that same neglect should not permanently foreclose the petitioner's ability to invoke it as cause for a related default.

**II. Because the Underlying Claim Concerns the Fundamental Right to Effective Appellate Counsel, It Retains Force Even Though It, Too, Was Defaulted**

The right at stake here—effective assistance of appellate counsel—is not a technical formality but a fundamental guarantee. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), this Court held that "any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him." *Id.* at 344. That same Term, in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), the Court extended this guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that "where the merits of the one and only appeal an indigent has as of right are decided without benefit of counsel, we think an unconstitutional line has been drawn between rich and poor." *Id.* at 357. Carpenter's ineffective-assistance claim asserts exactly the kind of deprivation these decisions were designed to redress. A rule that strips such a claim of any excusing force simply because it, too, went unexhausted would punish Carpenter twice for the same constitutional failure—once by losing his sufficiency claim, and again by losing the very claim that explains why.

**III. The Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require Independent Exhaustion of a Cause Claim Where the State Had a Fair Opportunity to Correct the Error**

Exhaustion exists to preserve the State's opportunity to correct its own errors, not to erect an additional procedural trap. In *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), the Court explained that habeas relief is available only after "all state remedies available . . . have been exhausted," precisely so that the State may first "pass upon and correct alleged violations" of federal rights. *Id.* at 118. Similarly, *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), grounded the exhaustion requirement in considerations of comity between the state and federal judicial systems, not in a desire to bar otherwise meritorious claims from consideration. *Id.* at 204. Ohio had that opportunity here: Carpenter presented his ineffective-assistance claim to the Ohio courts by moving to reopen his direct appeal. The state court's rejection of that motion as untimely does not diminish the fact that the State was given, and declined, the chance envisioned by *Hawk* and *Darr* to correct the underlying error.

**IV. This Court's Habeas Jurisprudence Counsels Against Mechanical Forfeiture Rules That Sacrifice Liberty to Procedural Formalism**

The writ of habeas corpus has always demanded flexibility where fundamental rights are at risk. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), Justice Holmes made clear that federal courts retain the duty to look through the "machinery" of state proceedings to ensure that constitutional guarantees are not defeated by procedural default alone. *Id.* at 91. And in *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), the Court cautioned that "conventional notions of finality of litigation have no place where life or liberty is at stake." *Id.* at 8. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), likewise confirmed that federal courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims raised in habeas petitions, rather than treating state-law procedural rulings as automatically dispositive of the federal question. *Id.* at 458.

Taken together, *Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Brown v. Allen* demonstrate that this Court has never treated procedural default as an inflexible, self-compounding bar—particularly where, as here, the default flows from counsel's own constitutional failing rather than the petitioner's deliberate choice. The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient force to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
