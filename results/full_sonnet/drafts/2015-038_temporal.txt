# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) LEAVES UNDISTURBED THE PARK SERVICE'S AUTHORITY TO ENFORCE REGULATIONS OF GENERAL APPLICABILITY WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS.

Statutory interpretation begins, and here ends, with the text Congress enacted. "It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Section 103(c) provides that only public lands—not entire conservation system units—are deemed part of the National Park System, and that state, Native, and private lands within such units are "not subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units." That proviso, by its terms, reaches only regulations that apply *solely* to federal public land. The hovercraft ban, 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e), applies uniformly to every unit of the National Park System nationwide, without regard to who holds title to the submerged land beneath a particular stretch of river. It is not a regulation "applicable solely to public lands," and § 103(c) therefore does not exempt Sturgeon from it.

This reading also honors the settled canon that provisos carving out exceptions to an existing regulatory regime are to be construed no more broadly than their language requires. Congress does not lightly disturb the pre-existing authority of federal agencies, and "repeals by implication are not favored." Posadas v. National City Bank, 296 U.S. 497, 503 (1936). Nothing in § 103(c)'s text withdraws the Secretary's general regulatory authority over activities occurring within unit boundaries; it withdraws only land-specific regulation of the non-federal inholdings themselves. Reading § 103(c) as Sturgeon urges—to bar enforcement of any regulation whenever it happens to touch non-federal land inside a park—would require this Court to insert words Congress did not write. "This Court has no power to alter [a statute] but only the power to enforce it in accord with the fair import of its terms." United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940).

## II. ANILCA'S STRUCTURE AND PURPOSE CONFIRM THAT CONGRESS PRESERVED, RATHER THAN CURTAILED, EXISTING FEDERAL REGULATORY AUTHORITY.

A statute's words "must be read in their context," Church of the Holy Trinity Church v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892) (construing text against the whole statute and its evident object), and ANILCA's context confirms respondent's reading. Congress enacted ANILCA to reconcile competing interests in Alaska lands, not to create a patchwork of navigable rivers within park boundaries immune from the uniform safety, resource-protection, and wildlife regulations that apply everywhere else in the National Park System. Had Congress intended so radical a result—stripping the Secretary of authority to prevent motorized incursions into a wildlife preserve merely because the streambed beneath the water is state-owned—it would have said so in unmistakable terms, not through an ownership-classification proviso buried in a definitional section.

Where, as here, an agency's interpretation of an ambiguous provision it administers is reasonable, that interpretation is entitled to substantial weight. "When faced with a problem of statutory construction, this Court shows great deference to the interpretation given the statute by the officers or agency charged with its administration." Udall v. Tallman, 380 U.S. 1, 16 (1965). The Park Service's construction of § 103(c)—that it exempts non-federal inholdings from land-specific regulation but not from regulations of park-wide application such as the hovercraft ban—is eminently reasonable and consistent with the statute's evident design.

## III. THE FEDERAL NAVIGATIONAL SERVITUDE AND THE PROPERTY CLAUSE INDEPENDENTLY SUPPORT THE PARK SERVICE'S AUTHORITY OVER THE NATION RIVER.

Even if the submerged lands beneath the Nation River passed to Alaska upon statehood, the United States retains a dominant servitude over the river as a navigable water of the United States. "The power of the Government to control and regulate navigable waters in the interest of commerce is a broad one," and title to the streambed does not diminish it. United States v. Twin City Power Co., 350 U.S. 222, 224–25 (1956). This servitude "is a right of the public which no State can abolish, and no State can compel the United States to pay for," and it extends to the entire bed of a navigable stream below the ordinary high-water mark. United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967); see also United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P. R. Co., 312 U.S. 592, 596–97 (1941).

Independently, the Property Clause empowers Congress to protect federal lands from activities on adjacent or intermingled non-federal land where necessary to accomplish the purposes for which the federal land is held. In Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525–26 (1897), the Court upheld federal authority to regulate fencing on private land checkerboarded with public land because the power to protect the public domain "is a settled principle" that does not depend on ownership of every parcel affected. The same logic sustains NPS's authority to ban hovercraft use on the Nation River to protect the wildlife and wilderness values of the surrounding Preserve, regardless of who owns the streambed.

## CONCLUSION

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 103(c)'s narrow ownership-based exemption does not immunize Sturgeon from a regulation of general park-wide applicability. The judgment below should be affirmed.
