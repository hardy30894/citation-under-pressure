**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Highly Prejudicial Opinion Testimony from Government Witnesses**

The admission of opinion testimony from Assistant District Attorneys (“ADAs”) and a police lieutenant violated fundamental evidentiary rules that preclude witnesses from vouching for credibility, offering legal conclusions, or interpreting evidence for the jury. *See generally* **United States v. Grunewald**, 233 F.2d 556, 562 (2d Cir. 1956) (holding that witnesses may not express opinions on the credibility of other witnesses); **United States v. Walker**, 197 F.2d 277, 280 (2d Cir. 1952) (prohibiting witnesses from testifying as to whether another witness is telling the truth); **United States v. Dennis**, 183 F.2d 201, 227 (2d Cir. 1950) (stating that witnesses may not offer legal conclusions or opinions on ultimate issues).

The ADAs’ testimony that probable cause existed and that Ramos and Rivera were credible invaded the jury’s province. *See United States v. Indiviglio**, 352 F.2d 276, 280 (2d Cir. 1965) (holding that witnesses may not testify as to the legal sufficiency of evidence or the credibility of witnesses). Similarly, the police lieutenant’s opinion that the officers’ conduct was justified improperly bolstered their credibility and misstated the legal standard for probable cause. *See United States v. Lipscomb**, 117 F.2d 879, 883 (2d Cir. 1941) (prohibiting witnesses from expressing opinions on the legal adequacy of evidence).

These errors were not harmless. The testimony directly addressed the two central disputes in the case—whether Ramos and Rivera had probable cause to arrest and whether their version of events was credible. *See Kotteakos v. United States**, 328 U.S. 750, 764 (1946) (holding that errors affecting the jury’s deliberations on key issues are not harmless). The admission of this testimony deprived Appellants of a fair trial and requires reversal.

**II. The District Court Correctly Denied Appellants’ Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law**

Appellants argue that security camera photographs “blatantly contradict” the officers’ account, entitling them to judgment as a matter of law. However, the photographs do not definitively resolve the factual dispute. *See Tennant v. Peoria & P.U. Ry. Co.**, 132 F.2d 85, 87 (7th Cir. 1942) (holding that a motion for judgment as a matter of law should be denied where evidence is susceptible to conflicting inferences). The jury was entitled to credit the officers’ version of events despite the photographs, and the District Court properly submitted the case to the jury.

**III. The District Court Erred in Failing to Instruct the Jury on the Lawful Arrest Requirement for Obstructing Governmental Administration**

Under New York law, obstructing governmental administration requires that the obstructed officers be engaged in a lawful arrest. *See People v. Case**, 220 N.Y. 315, 318 (1917) (holding that obstruction must occur during a lawful arrest). The District Court failed to instruct the jury on this essential element, depriving Appellants of a fair determination of Higgenbottom’s claim. *See Cardozo, J., in People v. Defore**, 242 N.Y. 13, 21 (1926) (stating that jury instructions must accurately reflect the law).

**IV. The District Court Erred in Failing to Instruct the Jury on Punitive Damages**

Appellants’ version of events, if credited, would support an inference of malice, entitling them to a punitive damages instruction. *See Day v. Woodworth**, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1852) (holding that punitive damages may be awarded where malice is shown). The District Court’s refusal to give such an instruction was error, particularly given the highly contested nature of the officers’ conduct.

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with this opinion.
