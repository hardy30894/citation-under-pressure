**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that the ESQD documents are exempt under 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). That provision exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The court’s “predominantly internal” standard, derived from Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981) (en banc), finds no support in the statutory text or this Court’s decisions.

FOIA mandates “a general philosophy of full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” Dep’t of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 U.S. 352, 360-61 (1976) (quoting S. Rep. No. 813-89, at 3 (1965)). The nine exemptions “are explicitly exclusive” and “are to be interpreted narrowly.” U.S. Dep’t of Justice v. Tax Analysts, 492 U.S. 136, 151 (1989) (quoting Adm’r FAA v. Robertson, 422 U.S. 255, 262 (1975)); see also Rose, 425 U.S. at 361. Exemption 2 therefore reaches only materials that relate solely to “internal personnel rules and practices.”

Rose itself distinguished “personnel” matters—such as “employee use of parking facilities” or “sick leave”—from “more substantial matters” that “the public might have a great interest in knowing.” 425 U.S. at 363, 369. The Court rejected any reading that would shield “all matters of some routine significance” or permit withholding based solely on a risk of circumvention. Id. at 369-70. The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” test expands Exemption 2 far beyond these limits, allowing agencies to withhold any document that is mostly used inside the agency and whose disclosure might aid circumvention of regulation. That construction cannot be reconciled with Rose’s insistence on narrow interpretation or with the statutory requirement that the matter relate “solely” to personnel rules and practices.

The ESQD documents do not satisfy even the Ninth Circuit’s erroneous standard. They consist of mathematical calculations and arc maps showing the effects of hypothetical explosions at ammunition storage sites. As the Navy’s own declarations establish, these materials are “designed to be a long term planning tool” used “to design, array, and construct ammunition storage facilities.” They are technical specifications governing facility layout, not rules governing the conduct of agency personnel. The Ninth Circuit’s holding that such documents are “predominantly internal” because they are not released to the public would exempt virtually every internal agency record, rendering the statutory language meaningless.

Because the documents fall outside the plain terms of Exemption 2, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
