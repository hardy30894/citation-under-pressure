### Argument

The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 disability claim is a “final decision” subject to judicial review. The Railroad Retirement Act incorporates the judicial-review provision of the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act, which authorizes review of “any final decision of the Board.” *Railroad Retirement Act of 1974*, 45 U.S.C. §§ 231g, 355(f). A denial of reopening is final in the ordinary and practical sense: it ends the agency proceeding, fixes the claimant’s rights, and leaves nothing further for the Board to do. Courts have long treated agency action as final when it marks “the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process” and determines rights or obligations with legal consequences. *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. 154, 177–78 (1997). That settled understanding of finality fits especially well here, where the Board’s refusal to reopen definitively foreclosed reconsideration of Salinas’s 2006 application.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that reopening decisions are reviewable when they effectively resolve a claimant’s entitlement. In *Mills v. Duryee*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 481, 484 (1813), the Court explained that final judgments are those that conclude the controversy and are not merely interlocutory. Likewise, in *The Palmyra*, 25 U.S. (12 Wheat.) 1, 10 (1827), the Court held that finality turns on whether the tribunal has done “all that remains to be done” in the cause. The Board’s denial of reopening satisfies that standard: it concluded the agency’s authority to revisit the 2006 claim and left Salinas with no further administrative avenue on that claim.

The government’s contrary position cannot be squared with the text authorizing review of final Board decisions. The ordinary meaning of “final” is not limited to the first merits determination; it includes later agency action that finally disposes of a request. *Bennett v. Spear*, 520 U.S. at 177–78. Here, the Board’s refusal to reopen was not a tentative procedural ruling. It was the Board’s last word on whether the 2006 denial could be revisited, and thus it was final for purposes of judicial review.

Nor does the agency’s characterization of reopening as discretionary defeat jurisdiction. Courts look to the effect of the agency action, not the label attached to it. In *Chicago, M. & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Minnesota*, 134 U.S. 418, 458 (1890), the Court emphasized that judicial review turns on whether an order has operative legal effect. The denial here had such effect: it foreclosed any change to the 2006 decision. That is enough to make it final.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule should not control this Court’s interpretation of the statute. This Court has repeatedly held that statutory review provisions are construed to permit review of definitive agency action unless Congress clearly says otherwise. *Rusk v. Cort*, 369 U.S. 367, 379–80 (1962). Nothing in the Railroad Retirement Act or the incorporated review provision excludes denials of reopening from review. Had Congress intended such a carveout, it knew how to say so. See *United States v. Oregon*, 366 U.S. 643, 648 (1961).

The policy behind finality confirms the result. Judicial review exists to prevent agencies from insulating dispositive decisions from oversight by merely styling them as refusals to reopen. That concern was central to the Court’s insistence that finality be judged by practical consequence, not formalism. *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). Salinas seeks review not of a stale procedural step, but of the Board’s last and binding refusal to reconsider his 2006 claim. That is precisely the sort of final agency action the statute makes reviewable.

For these reasons, the Court should hold that the Board’s denial of reopening is a final decision subject to judicial review and reverse the dismissal of Salinas’s petition.
