The Board’s poll rule cannot stand because it is untethered from the Act and internally incoherent. The Act itself does not regulate employer polling of employees about incumbent-union support. Allentown Mack Sales and Service, Inc. v. National Labor Relations Board, 522 U.S. 359, 377 (1998). When an incumbent union enjoys the presumption of majority status, an employer may rebut that presumption with objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about majority support. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987); NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board’s insistence that polling is permissible only when the employer already has enough evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition collapses polling into withdrawal and makes the poll standard functionally useless. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 377-79. The Board may choose among reasonable implementations of the Act, but it may not adopt a standard that “render[s] polling only marginally useful to employers” and then refuse to explain why that choice is compelled by the statute. Id. at 379.

The Court should therefore reject the Board’s argument that its standard is necessary to protect stable bargaining relationships. The Board itself has recognized that polling can serve a legitimate function when an employer has a reasonable doubt and wishes to test that doubt before withdrawing recognition. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-63 (1989), remanded as modified, 923 F.2d 398 (5th Cir. 1991). The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits correctly recognized that an employer need not already possess proof sufficient to support unilateral withdrawal before it may seek the additional information a poll provides. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867-68 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). As those courts explained, the proper question is whether the employer has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of support even if that evidence does not yet justify withdrawal. Thompson, 651 F.2d at 1145; Mingtree Restaurant, 736 F.2d at 1299; Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 867. The Board’s contrary rule is not rational because it makes polling available only after the employer has effectively already won the case. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 377-79.

Nor is the Board’s factual finding sustainable. The Act permits courts to set aside agency conclusions unsupported by substantial evidence. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 487-88 (1951). Here, the Board discounted multiple employee statements and then concluded that only seven of thirty-two employees indicated antiunion sentiment. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 384-89. That conclusion cannot survive review because the Board applied a standard in a manner that did not fairly account for the record evidence it accepted elsewhere. Id. at 379-90. The Board rejected statements by employees who said the union was no longer wanted, including reports that the “entire night shift” opposed the union and that the union steward believed the union would lose. Id. at 386-89. The Board also discounted direct statements of dissatisfaction with union representation, despite precedent recognizing that dissatisfaction with representation may evidence opposition to continued representation. Destileria Serralles, Inc. v. NLRB, 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989); Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995).

Most importantly, the Board’s own treatment of the evidence demonstrates irrationality. It discounted employee statements made after the new employer made its nonunion position known, yet relied on a rigid January 25 cutoff that had no basis in the Act and no relation to whether the company had a reasonable doubt when it polled. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 385-87. The Board may not convert a flexible “reasonable doubt” inquiry into a game of evidentiary formalism. Id. at 389-90. On this record, substantial evidence does not support the conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked an objective basis for doubt about Local 724’s majority status. Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 487-88; Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 868. At minimum, the Board’s contrary conclusion is arbitrary in light of the unrefuted objective evidence the company gathered before conducting a carefully safeguarded poll. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 383-90.

The order should be reversed.
