## ARGUMENT

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed. The district court’s failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal, in violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2), does not warrant habeas relief because petitioner undisputedly knew of that right and elected not to appeal. The error was harmless, and habeas corpus is not available for non-jurisdictional procedural violations that cause no prejudice.

### I. The Right to Appeal Is Statutory, Not Constitutional, and Rule 32 Violations Are Subject to Harmless-Error Review

The Constitution does not guarantee a right to appeal. *McKane v. Durston*, 153 U.S. 684, 687 (1894) (“[A]n appeal from a judgment of conviction is not a matter of absolute right, independently of constitutional or statutory provisions allowing such appeal.”). The right to appeal in federal criminal cases exists solely by statute. *United States v. Hark*, 320 U.S. 531, 534 (1944). Rule 32(a)(2) implements that statutory right by requiring the district court to advise a defendant of his appeal rights. But a violation of a procedural rule does not automatically entitle a defendant to relief. *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (holding that failure to comply with Rule 32(a)’s allocution requirement was not “an error of the character or magnitude cognizable under a writ of habeas corpus” because it did not prejudice the defendant).

Non-constitutional errors are subject to harmless-error review. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946) (holding that errors not affecting substantial rights are harmless). The Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure themselves codify this standard. Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a) (“Any error, defect, irregularity or variance which does not affect substantial rights shall be disregarded.”). Because Rule 32(a)(2) is a procedural requirement, not a constitutional mandate, its violation is harmless if the defendant already knew of his right to appeal and suffered no prejudice.

### II. The Error Was Harmless Because Petitioner Knew of His Right to Appeal and Chose Not to Appeal

The district court found, after an evidentiary hearing, that petitioner knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is undisputed. The sole purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that a defendant is informed of his appeal rights. When the defendant already possesses that knowledge, the rule’s purpose is fully served. The omission of a formal advisement causes no injury. *Cf. Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428 (no habeas relief where defendant was not prejudiced by failure to ask if he wished to speak before sentencing). Petitioner cannot claim that he was deprived of the opportunity to appeal; he knew of that opportunity and deliberately declined it. A defendant who knowingly waives a right cannot later complain that he was not reminded of it. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is the “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right”).

### III. Habeas Corpus Does Not Lie for Non-Jurisdictional Errors That Cause No Prejudice

Habeas relief is reserved for jurisdictional defects or errors that fundamentally undermine the legality of a conviction or sentence. *Hill*, 368 U.S. at 428; *Ex parte United States*, 242 U.S. 27, 32 (1916) (habeas corpus “cannot be used as a writ of error”). The failure to advise a defendant who already knows his appeal rights is not a jurisdictional error. It does not render the sentence void or violate due process. Petitioner received a fair sentencing hearing, was represented by counsel, and understood his appellate options. The district court’s oversight, while regrettable, did not affect any substantial right. *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764-65. Accordingly, habeas relief is unwarranted.

The judgment below should be affirmed.
