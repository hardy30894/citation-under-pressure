# ARGUMENT

## I. THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR DEBTS ARISING FROM FRAUD REACHES THE ENTIRE DEBT CREATED BY THE FRAUD, NOT MERELY THE MONEY OR PROPERTY ORIGINALLY OBTAINED.

Since the first federal Bankruptcy Act, Congress has withheld discharge from debts born of the debtor's own dishonesty. This Court has consistently read that exception according to its evident purpose: to prevent a wrongdoer from using bankruptcy to escape the full consequences of his fraud, not merely the initial sum extracted from his victim.

In Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, 177 U.S. 177 (1900), a bankrupt sought to discharge the portion of a state-court judgment representing interest that had accrued on goods he had fraudulently obtained, arguing that only the value of the goods themselves fell within the fraud exception. This Court refused to sever the judgment into a dischargeable remainder and a nondischargeable core. The debt excepted from discharge was not limited to the bare price of the goods obtained; it extended to the liability the fraud had produced, including the judgment as a whole.

This Court reinforced that principle in Friend v. Talcott, 228 U.S. 27 (1913), treating provability and dischargeability as analytically distinct questions, so that a debt may be fully provable in the bankruptcy proceeding and yet remain wholly outside the discharge because of the fraud that produced it.

The same logic controls when damages beyond simple restitution attach to the underlying wrong. In Tinker v. Colwell, 193 U.S. 473 (1904), this Court held that a judgment resting on a willful and intentional injury remained outside the discharge even though the award included elements of damage beyond the plaintiff's bare pecuniary loss. The debtor could not divide the judgment into a compensable, dischargeable portion and an exemplary, nondischargeable portion; the whole debt traced to the same wrong and shared its character. See also Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. 704, 709 (1877) (the fraud that defeats discharge is "positive fraud, or fraud in fact, involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong," and a debt so tainted is treated as a unit, not parsed into severable components).

Petitioner's position—that only the $31,382.50 in rent overcharges, and not the treble damages, fees, and costs New Jersey law attached to that same fraud, falls within the exception—cannot be squared with Forsyth and Tinker. Cohen's fraud is the source of the entire $94,147.50 debt. The treble damages are not a separate liability arising from an independent wrong; they are the very measure state law assigns to the fraud Cohen committed against these tenants. Just as the interest in Forsyth and the exemplary damages in Tinker did not sever from the underlying nondischargeable debt, the multiplied damages here likewise remain part of the single debt "obtained by" fraud.

## II. THE DISCHARGE IS RESERVED FOR THE HONEST DEBTOR, NOT THE DEFRAUDER.

This Court has long recognized that the discharge exists to give the debtor "a new opportunity in life and a clear field for future effort, unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt." Local Loan Co. v. Hunt, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934). But Local Loan Co. presupposed a debtor who, whatever his misfortune, dealt honestly with his creditors. Cohen did not.

Cohen set rents that exceeded the limits fixed by the Hoboken rent-control ordinance, and by his silence represented to these tenants that the rents he charged were lawful. He never consulted counsel about the ordinance, never obtained a copy of it, and never inquired of the Rent Leveling Board—though he took care to inquire about permissible tax and water surcharges when it benefited him. When the Board found he had overcharged these tenants, he did not reimburse a cent. This is not the case of an unfortunate debtor undone by circumstance beyond his control; it is the case of a landlord who profited from tenants disadvantaged by language and education from detecting his overreaching. Such conduct is precisely the "positive fraud... involving moral turpitude or intentional wrong" that Neal v. Clark, 95 U.S. at 709, placed outside the reach of discharge.

Denying discharge of the full debt—principal, treble damages, fees, and costs—does not offend the fresh-start policy, because that policy was never designed to shield a defrauder from the entire measure of liability his fraud created. To hold otherwise would let a debtor commit fraud secure in the knowledge that state remedies designed to deter and punish that very fraud could later be discharged in bankruptcy, leaving his victims with only a fraction of their due. Nothing in this Court's fraud-exception precedents, from Neal v. Clark through Tinker v. Colwell, Forsyth v. Vehmeyer, and Friend v. Talcott, countenances that result.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
