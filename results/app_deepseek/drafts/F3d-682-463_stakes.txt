**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Holding That Wasek Failed to Show the Harassment Occurred Because of His Gender.**

Title VII prohibits an employer from discriminating “against any individual with respect to his compensation, terms, conditions, or privileges of employment, because of such individual’s race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1). Michigan’s Elliot-Larsen Civil Rights Act (“EL-CRA”) similarly forbids discrimination “because of . . . sex.” Mich. Comp. Laws § 37.2202(1)(a). A plaintiff may establish a hostile work environment claim under both statutes by showing that (1) he was subjected to unwelcome harassment; (2) the harassment was based on his sex; (3) the harassment was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of employment and create an abusive working environment; and (4) the employer is vicariously liable. *Williams v. CSX Transp. Co.*, 643 F.3d 502, 511 (6th Cir. 2011). The district court concluded that Wasek could not satisfy the second element—that the harassment occurred “because of” his sex. That conclusion was error.

**A. Same-Sex Harassment Is Actionable Under Title VII and EL-CRA.**

The Supreme Court has squarely held that same-sex sexual harassment is actionable under Title VII. *Oncale v. Sundowner Offshore Servs., Inc.*, 523 U.S. 75, 79 (1998). The critical inquiry is whether the harassment was “because of” the victim’s sex, not whether the harasser and victim share the same gender. *Id.* at 80. The Court identified three ways a plaintiff can prove same-sex harassment based on sex: (1) by showing that the harasser made explicit or implicit proposals of sexual activity, which themselves raise an inference of discrimination because of sex; (2) by offering credible evidence that the harasser was motivated by general hostility to the presence of one gender in the workplace; or (3) by offering direct comparative evidence about how the harasser treated members of both sexes in a mixed-gender workplace. *Id.* at 80–81. The first method applies squarely here.

**B. Ottobre’s Conduct Was Overtly Sexual and Thus “Because of” Wasek’s Sex.**

Ottobre repeatedly touched Wasek in a sexual manner—grabbing his buttocks, poking him in the rear with a hammer handle and a sucker rod. He made sexually explicit comments: “you’ve got a pretty mouth,” “boy you have pretty lips,” and “you know you like it sweetheart.” He told sexually explicit jokes, stories, and fantasies. This conduct is “explicit or implicit proposals of sexual activity” that, under *Oncale*, “themselves raise an inference of discrimination because of sex.” *Id.* at 80. The Sixth Circuit has applied *Oncale* to hold that “sexually explicit conduct” directed at a male employee by a male coworker is sufficient to establish the “because of sex” element. *See, e.g., Vickers v. Fairfield Med. Ctr.*, 453 F.3d 757, 764 (6th Cir. 2006) (recognizing that “sexually explicit conduct” can support an inference of sex-based discrimination). Indeed, the very nature of sexual touching and comments is inherently gender-based. *See Williams*, 643 F.3d at 511 (noting that “harassment that is sexual in nature is almost always considered to be because of sex”).

**C. The Harassment Also Reflected Gender Stereotyping.**

Even if the overtly sexual nature of the conduct were insufficient, the harassment was also based on Wasek’s failure to conform to male gender stereotypes. Ottobre called Wasek “sweetheart” and mocked him for not being “tough” enough—for “whining” and complaining. Gender stereotyping is a form of sex discrimination. *Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins*, 490 U.S. 228, 250–51 (1989) (plurality opinion). The Sixth Circuit has held that “an employer’s discrimination on the basis of an employee’s failure to conform to gender stereotypes” is actionable under Title VII. *Smith v. City of Salem*, 378 F.3d 566, 575 (6th Cir. 2004). Here, Ottobre’s taunts and physical assaults were designed to humiliate Wasek for not acting like a “man”—for being perceived as weak or effeminate. That is precisely the kind of stereotyping that Title VII prohibits.

**D. The Employer’s Response Was Inadequate, Establishing Vicarious Liability.**

When a supervisor’s harassment results in a tangible employment action—such as constructive discharge—the employer is strictly liable. *Burlington Indus., Inc. v. Ellerth*, 524 U.S. 742, 765 (1998); *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775, 807 (1998). Wasek’s rig boss, Pat Tripp, was his supervisor. Tripp observed the harassment, laughed, and told Wasek not to report it or he would be put on a “starvation schedule.” Tripp’s threats and failure to intervene constitute a tangible employment action—Wasek was forced to quit because the employer condoned the abuse. *See Pennsylvania State Police v. Suders*, 542 U.S. 129, 143 (2004) (constructive discharge is a tangible employment action). Even if Tripp were not a supervisor, Arrow Energy had actual knowledge through higher management—Dick Smillie and Bill Strong—who told Wasek to “kick [Ottobre’s] ass” or “duke it out.” An employer that knows of harassment and does nothing is liable. *See Vance v. Ball State Univ.*, 570 U.S. 421, 424 (2013) (employer liable if it fails to take prompt remedial action after notice).

**II. Conclusion**

Wasek presented ample evidence that Ottobre’s harassment was sexual in nature and based on his gender. The district court’s contrary conclusion was erroneous. This Court should reverse and remand for trial.
