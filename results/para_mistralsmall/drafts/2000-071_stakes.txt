**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S DENIAL OF ACCESS TO THE GOOD NEWS CLUB WAS CONSTITUTIONALLY PERMISSIBLE BECAUSE IT WAS BASED ON SUBJECT, NOT VIEWPOINT, DISCRIMINATION.**

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s application was constitutional subject discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. *See Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (explaining that a restriction is viewpoint-neutral if it is “based on the subject matter of the speech” rather than the speaker’s “particular position on an issue”); *Rosenberger v. Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (holding that a restriction is viewpoint-based only if it “discriminate[s] on the basis of the speaker’s viewpoint”).

Milford’s Community Use Policy expressly excludes “religious purposes,” including “religious instruction and prayer.” The policy is facially neutral and applies to all applicants equally, regardless of their religious affiliation or the content of their speech. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (noting that a policy excluding “use for religious purposes” is viewpoint-neutral). The Club’s proposed activities—singing hymns, hearing Bible lessons, memorizing scripture, and praying—fall squarely within the prohibited category of religious worship. *See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 512 (2d Cir. 2000) (characterizing the Club’s activities as “quintessentially religious” and thus outside the scope of permissible use).

The Club’s reliance on *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981), is misplaced. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university’s open forum for student groups could not exclude a religious group solely because of its religious content. *Id.* at 267. But the Court emphasized that the university’s policy was “neutral” and applied to all student groups equally. *Id.* at 277. Here, by contrast, Milford’s policy explicitly excludes religious worship, making it a subject-based restriction. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 829 (distinguishing between viewpoint-based and subject-based restrictions).

**II. MILFORD’S POLICY IS JUSTIFIED BY THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE BECAUSE IT PROHIBITS RELIGIOUS WORSHIP IN A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM.**

Even if the denial were deemed viewpoint discrimination, Milford’s policy is justified by the Establishment Clause. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395 (holding that a school’s exclusion of religious worship in a limited public forum does not violate the Free Speech Clause when justified by the Establishment Clause). The Supreme Court has recognized that a government entity may “close its facilities to religious groups” when their activities would “convey a message of government endorsement of religion.” *Id.* at 394 (citing *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 271-72).

Milford’s policy is narrowly tailored to avoid the appearance of state endorsement of religion. *See Agostini v. Felton*, 521 U.S. 203, 234 (1997) (explaining that the Establishment Clause prohibits government actions that “endorse or disapprove of religion”). The Club’s proposed activities—prayer, scripture memorization, and evangelical instruction—are indistinguishable from religious worship. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. Permitting such activities in a public school would risk impermissible endorsement, particularly when the audience includes young, impressionable children. *See Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (holding that school-sponsored religious activities violate the Establishment Clause when they “place[] public pressure, as well as peer pressure, on attending students to participate in the religious exercise”).

The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is unavailing. In *Rosenberger*, the Court held that a university’s refusal to fund a religious student publication violated the Free Speech Clause because the policy was viewpoint-based. 515 U.S. at 836. But the Court expressly distinguished policies that exclude “worship or proselytizing,” noting that such restrictions are permissible under the Establishment Clause. *Id.* at 839 n.16. Milford’s policy fits squarely within this exception.

**III. THE SECOND CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REJECTED THE CLUB’S EQUAL PROTECTION CLAIM.**

The Club’s equal protection argument fails because Milford’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of religion. *See Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye, Inc. v. City of Hialeah*, 508 U.S. 520, 542 (1993) (holding that a law is not facially discriminatory if it “applies to all persons or entities within its jurisdiction” without regard to religion). The policy applies equally to all religious and secular groups, prohibiting only religious worship. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is again misplaced, as that case involved viewpoint discrimination, not subject-based exclusion. 515 U.S. at 829.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s denial of the Good News Club’s application was constitutional subject discrimination, not unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination, and was further justified by the Establishment Clause. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
