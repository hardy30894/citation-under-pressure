The Supreme Court's decision in Johnson v. United States, 559 U.S. 133 (2010), does not directly control the outcome of this case, but the subsequent decision in Johnson v. United States, --- U.S. ---, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), does. In the latter Johnson decision, the Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) was unconstitutionally vague. The Court's reasoning in this decision controls the outcome of Welch's case.

The Eleventh Circuit's determination that Welch's 1996 Florida conviction for strong arm robbery was a predicate offense under the ACCA was based, in part, on the residual clause. The court held that the conviction involved force that was "capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person." However, in light of the Supreme Court's decision in Johnson, the residual clause can no longer be relied upon to support this determination.

The question presented is whether the rule announced in Johnson applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, such as Welch's. The Supreme Court's decision in Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), provides the framework for determining whether a new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively. Under Teague, a new rule applies retroactively if it is a substantive rule or a "watershed rule of criminal procedure." Id. at 311.

The rule announced in Johnson is substantive because it narrows the scope of the ACCA's residual clause, which is a substantive provision that defines the conduct that is punishable under the statute. See Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004) (holding that a rule is substantive if it "alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes"). By holding that the residual clause is unconstitutionally vague, the Court in Johnson effectively narrowed the range of conduct that is punishable under the ACCA.

Therefore, the rule announced in Johnson applies retroactively to cases on collateral review, including Welch's. As a result, Welch's sentence under the ACCA should be vacated, and his case should be remanded for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.

The Eleventh Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Welch's case should be remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Supreme Court's decision in Johnson.
