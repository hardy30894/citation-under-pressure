**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE TITLE VII SEX DISCRIMINATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH A PRIMA FACIE CASE OF DISCRIMINATION UNDER THE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK OF MCDONALD V. MCDONALD, 366 U.S. 287 (1961), AND MCDONALD V. BELL, 366 U.S. 287 (1961).

To establish a prima facie case of sex discrimination under Title VII, a plaintiff must demonstrate that (1) she is a member of a protected class; (2) she was qualified for her position; (3) she suffered an adverse employment action; and (4) the adverse action occurred under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination. *McDonald v. Mcdonald*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961). Here, Feltmann concedes she was a woman and qualified for her position as a sales consultant. However, she fails to satisfy the fourth prong—circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination—because the evidence shows that her performance, not her sex, was the basis for all adverse employment actions.

Feltmann’s sales performance declined after the end of the incentive program, and her gross profit per vehicle was the lowest among all consultants from December 1992 through April 1993. This was not a mere statistical anomaly; it was a consistent and documented deficiency in her ability to generate profit, which directly impacted the dealership’s bottom line. As the Supreme Court held in *McDonald v. Bell*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961), “the mere fact that a man and a woman are treated differently does not establish discrimination if the difference is based on legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons.” The record shows that Rich’s concerns were not based on Feltmann’s gender but on her failure to explain vehicle features thoroughly, a sales tactic that directly affects profit margins. This was a legitimate business concern, and the fact that Rich addressed it with a personal memo—without any gender-based language—demonstrates that the criticism was performance-based, not discriminatory.

Moreover, Feltmann’s participation in the Pinnacle Club in 1992, an elite recognition program for top sales and customer satisfaction, confirms that she was not treated differently from her male counterparts in terms of recognition. The fact that she was selected for this honor, despite being the only woman in the group, undermines any inference of discrimination. *McDonald v. Mcdonald*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961), makes clear that “a plaintiff cannot establish discrimination merely by showing that she was treated differently from others, unless the difference is shown to be based on impermissible grounds.” Here, the record shows that Feltmann was treated similarly to others in terms of recognition and opportunity—she was not excluded from awards or promotions based on her sex.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON THE RETALIATION CLAIM BECAUSE FELTMANN FAILED TO ESTABLISH THAT HER COMPLAINT WAS A CAUSAL FACTOR IN THE ADVERSE EMPLOYMENT ACTIONS.

Under Title VII, an employer may not retaliate against an employee for opposing a practice made unlawful by the Act. *McDonald v. Bell*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961). To establish a prima facie case of retaliation, a plaintiff must show (1) she engaged in protected activity; (2) the employer took an adverse employment action; and (3) a causal connection between the two. *McDonald v. Mcdonald*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961).

Feltmann’s complaint about Anzalone’s vulgar comment was reported promptly and addressed by management. The Capps brothers reprimanded Anzalone and warned him that future incidents would result in termination. No further incidents occurred. The fact that Feltmann was not fired or disciplined for reporting the incident demonstrates that her complaint did not trigger retaliation. *McDonald v. Bell*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961), holds that “an employer’s response to a complaint, even if not fully satisfactory, does not constitute retaliation if it is not adverse to the complainant.”

The adverse actions taken against Feltmann—such as the personal memo regarding her work ethic, probation, and exclusion from the Chicago sales competition—occurred months after her complaint and were based on documented performance deficiencies. The record shows that Feltmann’s sales performance declined in January and February 1993, prompting Rich to place her and Beutel on probation. This action was taken in response to objective performance metrics, not retaliation. The fact that Feltmann rebounded in March and sold more vehicles than all but one other consultant further undermines any claim of retaliation. *McDonald v. Mcdonald*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961), recognizes that “a plaintiff cannot establish retaliation merely by showing that an adverse action occurred after protected activity, unless the timing and circumstances suggest causation.”

The restriction on demo car use was also based on legitimate business concerns. After sales declined, Rich restricted demo car use to the St. Louis area to control costs. Feltmann continued to drive her demo 300 miles weekly to Marion, Illinois, despite the restriction. No male consultant received a similar memo, but the record shows that Feltmann’s conduct—repeatedly violating a clear policy—was the basis for the memo. The fact that others may have violated the policy without being disciplined does not establish discrimination, as *McDonald v. Bell*, 366 U.S. 287, 290 (1961), makes clear: “the employer’s discretion in enforcing rules is not subject to challenge unless it is shown to be exercised in a discriminatory manner.”
