The Fourteenth Amendment does not require Arizona to admit expert evidence of mental condition to negate mens rea when the defendant may assert an insanity defense. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952), sustained a state rule assigning the burden of proving insanity to the accused and confirmed that the Due Process Clause leaves states free to allocate that burden. The same authority permits Arizona to limit the use of such evidence to the insanity defense alone.

States retain broad discretion to define criminal elements and the defenses available. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895), required the prosecution to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt yet left states free to decide whether evidence of mental unsoundness bears on those elements or only on a separate defense. Hotema v. United States, 186 U.S. 413, 420 (1902), and Matheson v. United States, 227 U.S. 540, 543 (1913), treated the admissibility of mental-condition evidence as a matter of local procedural regulation.

Due process demands only that a criminal proceeding be fundamentally fair. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105 (1934). Rules excluding particular evidence violate the Clause only when they offend “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319, 325 (1937). Arizona’s limitation channels mental-condition evidence into the insanity defense without denying any historically guaranteed opportunity to contest the prosecution’s case. Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78, 106 (1908), and Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581, 592 (1900), likewise upheld state procedural choices that did not transgress settled notions of fairness.

The Clause does not compel admission of every item of relevant evidence. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), and Chambers v. Florida, 309 U.S. 227, 235 (1940), protect against procedures that render a trial a mere sham, but neither requires states to adopt any particular rule governing expert testimony on mens rea. Arizona’s statute therefore satisfies the Fourteenth Amendment.
