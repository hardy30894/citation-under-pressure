**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the exercise of specific personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company comports with the Due Process Clause. The question presented assumes that personal jurisdiction requires a causal link between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s claims. The Due Process Clause imposes no such requirement. Instead, jurisdiction is proper when the defendant’s contacts with the forum “make it reasonable, in the context of our federal system of government, to require the corporation to defend the particular suit which is brought there.” *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 317 (1945). The claims here “relate to” Ford’s extensive activities in Montana, even if those activities did not directly cause the accident.

**I. Ford Purposefully Availed Itself of the Privilege of Conducting Activities in Montana.**

Ford has “purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws.” *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). Ford advertises its vehicles in Montana, operates a network of authorized dealerships, sells replacement parts, and provides warranty services to Montana residents. These contacts are not “random, isolated, or fortuitous.” *Id.* at 253. Rather, Ford “systematically and continuously” solicits business in Montana, establishing a “substantial connection” with the State. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 320; *Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643, 648 (1950) (holding that mail-order solicitation of business in a forum constitutes purposeful availment).

Ford’s contacts are precisely the type that “make it reasonable to require the corporation to defend the suit in that state.” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 318. When a corporation “deliberately engages in business activities within a state,” it “has the benefits and protection of the laws of that state” and “must submit to the burdens of litigation in that forum as well.” *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957).

**II. The Claims “Relate to” Ford’s Forum Contacts.**

The Due Process Clause requires only that the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum contacts. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. The phrase “relate to” is broader than “cause.” A claim relates to a defendant’s forum contacts when there is a “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue” that makes jurisdiction fair. *Id.* at 319. Here, the connection is manifest.

The claims against Ford—design defect and failure to warn—concern a Ford Explorer that was driven in Montana, sold in Montana, and maintained using Ford parts. Ford’s in-state activities (advertising the Explorer, selling parts, and servicing vehicles) are directly related to the very vehicle and the very design choices at issue. Ford’s nationwide marketing and distribution of the Explorer created a foreseeable risk that the vehicle would be driven in Montana and cause injury there. That is precisely the “relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation” that supports specific jurisdiction. *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253 (quoting *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319).

This Court has never required a causal nexus between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s injury. In *McGee*, the Court upheld jurisdiction over an insurance company whose only contact with California was a single policy mailed to a resident. 355 U.S. at 223. The claim arose from that policy—a direct causal link. But the Court’s reasoning focused on the overall fairness of requiring the company to defend suit in the state where it “solicited” the business. *Id.* at 223. Similarly, in *Travelers Health*, the Court upheld jurisdiction over a mail-order company that solicited members in Virginia, even though the claims did not arise from any specific Virginia solicitation but from the company’s general course of business. 339 U.S. at 648–50. The Court held that the “continuous course of business” in the forum made it “reasonable” to require the company to defend. *Id.* at 648.

Here, Ford’s continuous course of business in Montana—advertising, selling parts, and servicing vehicles—makes it reasonable to require Ford to defend claims that a Ford vehicle caused injury in Montana. The claims “relate to” Ford’s forum contacts because the same design, warnings, and marketing are at issue. The fact that the specific vehicle entered Montana through a chain of private resales does not sever the connection. Ford’s “purposeful availment” of the Montana market for its vehicles “makes it reasonable to require the corporation to defend the suit in that state.” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 318.

**III. The Exercise of Jurisdiction Satisfies Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice.**

Ford’s contacts with Montana are “such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.’” *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316 (quoting *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940)). Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents injured by defective products. Ford has “the benefits and protection of the laws of that state” through its business activities, and it “must submit to the burdens of litigation in that forum as well.” *McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223.

Ford cannot plausibly claim surprise or unfairness. It “deliberately” engaged in “a continuous and systematic course of business” in Montana, *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 320, and could foresee that its vehicles would be driven in Montana and that it might be sued there for defects. The “burden of defending a suit in a foreign jurisdiction” is “not unreasonable” when the defendant “has actively sought and obtained the benefits of that jurisdiction’s laws.” *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253.

**CONCLUSION**

The Due Process Clause does not require a causal link between the defendant’s forum contacts and the plaintiff’s claims. The claims here “relate to” Ford’s extensive, purposeful activities in Montana. The Montana Supreme Court’s decision should be affirmed.
