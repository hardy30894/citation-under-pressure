**Argument**

I. **Congress did not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power in the Controlled Substances Act.**

The Constitution permits Congress to seek assistance from another branch so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of delegated authority. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). The Court has “almost unfailingly upheld” delegations where Congress identifies the goal, the factors to be considered, and the limits on the delegated power. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372, 379 (1989).

The Controlled Substances Act easily satisfies that standard. For permanent scheduling, Congress specified the relevant factors the Attorney General must consider, including a substance’s abuse potential, scientific evidence, medical knowledge, history and pattern of abuse, risk to public health, dependence liability, and whether it is an immediate precursor. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). Congress also required consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose scientific and medical evaluation is binding if the Secretary recommends against scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). And Congress required notice-and-comment procedures under the Administrative Procedure Act. 21 U.S.C. § 811(a). Those limits are far more concrete than the standards upheld in *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 374-79, and *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 426-27 (1944).

The temporary scheduling provision is likewise constitutional. Congress authorized the Attorney General to act only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” and only after considering the statutory factors tied to abuse and risk. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). It limited the order to one year, with a possible six-month extension, and required notice to the Secretary of HHS. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(2), (4). Congress thus “sufficiently mark[ed] the field within which [the Attorney General] is to act.” *United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911). The delegation is narrower than those sustained in *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-79, and *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 425-27.

Petitioners’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved statutes that lacked any meaningful policy standard or limiting principle. *Panama Refining*, 293 U.S. at 415; *Schechter Poultry*, 295 U.S. at 541-42. By contrast, Congress here identified the emergency objective, the criteria for action, the procedural safeguards, and a short duration. This Court has repeatedly upheld delegations in the criminal context where Congress defined the offense by reference to agency action authorized by intelligible standards. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-73; *Touby v. United States*, 500 U.S. 160, 165-67 (1991).

II. **The Attorney General was authorized to delegate temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator.**

The Act expressly authorizes the Attorney General to delegate his functions. Section 871(a) provides that “the Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Supreme Court has held that when Congress authorizes delegation in this manner, an agency head may assign statutory functions to subordinate officers absent a statutory restriction. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 514-15 (1974); *United States v. Morton Salt Co.*, 382 U.S. 263, 272-73 (1965). The Attorney General’s delegation of temporary scheduling authority to the DEA Administrator therefore falls squarely within the statute.

Petitioners suggest that the 1984 temporary scheduling authority was not included within § 871(a). But Congress amended the Act against the backdrop of § 871(a)’s broad delegation language, and nothing in § 811(h) withdraws that authority. Where Congress wishes to limit subdelegation, it does so expressly. *Giordano*, 416 U.S. at 514-17. It did not do so here.

Nor is there any force to petitioners’ claim that the DEA Administrator lacked authority because temporary scheduling is “important” or “criminal.” The Court has long recognized that administrative implementation of criminal statutes may be carried out by subordinate officials when Congress has authorized the delegation. *Grimaud*, 220 U.S. at 518-19; *Morton Salt*, 382 U.S. at 273. Congress did exactly that in § 871(a).

III. **The Third Circuit correctly sustained the convictions.**

Because the statutory delegation contains an intelligible principle and adequate procedural safeguards, and because the Attorney General validly subdelegated to the DEA Administrator, petitioners’ constitutional challenge fails. The judgment should be affirmed. *Touby v. United States*, 500 U.S. 160, 165-67 (1991).
