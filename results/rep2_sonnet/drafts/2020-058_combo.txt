# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Commands That a TPS Recipient Be Treated as Having Been Admitted, and That Command Must Be Given Full Effect.

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that once TPS is granted, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment under § 1255. Congress did not choose this phrase idly. Because nonimmigrant status under the INA cannot exist independent of an admission—every category of nonimmigrant enumerated in the statute presupposes lawful entry after inspection—the direction that a TPS recipient be "considered as being in" that status necessarily incorporates the predicate act of admission. To read § 1254a(f)(4) as conferring only a free-floating "status" untethered from any admission strips the words "as a nonimmigrant" of their operative content.

This Court has long insisted that every clause of a statute be given meaning. In *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528 (1955), the Court explained: "It is our duty to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute." 348 U.S. at 538–39. The court of appeals' reading violates that duty. By treating "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" as wholly severable from admission, the Third Circuit rendered the phrase "as a nonimmigrant" surplusage—Congress could as easily have written "lawful status" alone. This Court should decline an interpretation that empties statutory language of independent force.

## II. Any Ambiguity Must Be Resolved in Petitioner's Favor Under the Settled Canon Governing Immigration Statutes.

Even if the interaction between §§ 1254a and 1255 were genuinely ambiguous, that ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the alien. This Court has repeatedly applied that canon where, as here, the consequence of the government's reading is to foreclose an avenue to lawful permanent residence. In *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), the Court held that "we resolve the doubts in favor of that construction because deportation is a drastic measure and at times the equivalent of banishment or exile." 333 U.S. at 10. The same solicitude applies with equal force to adjustment-of-status provisions that determine whether a family may remain together in this country. *See Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388 (1947) (construing immigration statute against the harsher reading where the stakes to the individual are severe); *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637 (1954) (same); *Bridges v. Wixon*, 326 U.S. 135 (1945) (immigration statutes affecting an alien's continued presence in the United States are not to be construed expansively against the individual).

This Court reaffirmed the principle in the context of relief from removal in *Costello v. INS*, 376 U.S. 120 (1964), and again in *Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214 (1966), where the Court favored the construction of the INA that preserved the alien's eligibility for relief rather than the one urged by the agency. The same approach governs here. Congress created TPS as a humanitarian safeguard; it should not be presumed, absent clear language, to have left TPS recipients permanently barred from the ordinary path to lawful permanent residence that Congress made generally available to nonimmigrants under § 1255.

## III. The Agency's Contrary Interpretation Is Entitled to No Weight.

The government's position rests substantially on a 1991 Immigration and Naturalization Service General Counsel opinion issued shortly after Congress created TPS. That opinion is not owed deference. Under *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134 (1944), the weight due an agency's interpretation "will depend upon the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements, and all those factors which give it power to persuade, if lacking power to control." 323 U.S. at 140. The 1991 opinion offers no sustained textual analysis of § 1254a(f)(4)'s command that a TPS recipient be "considered as being in" nonimmigrant status; it simply asserts the conclusion the government now defends. An unreasoned agency pronouncement of this kind cannot overcome the statutory text.

## IV. The Court of Appeals' Reading Produces an Absurd Result That Defeats the Remedial Purpose of the TPS Program.

Finally, the government's construction should be rejected because it frustrates the evident purpose of the statute. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), this Court cautioned that although "there is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes," 310 U.S. at 543, a literal reading that produces a result plainly at odds with that purpose cannot control. Congress designed TPS to allow nationals of countries devastated by war or disaster to remain lawfully in the United States. Reading § 1254a(f)(4) to withhold the benefit of admission from precisely the aliens whom Congress placed in lawful nonimmigrant status—while affording that benefit to nonimmigrants who happened to enter through a port of entry—produces an arbitrary two-tiered system Congress never intended. Petitioner's construction avoids that anomaly by giving full, harmonious effect to both provisions.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
