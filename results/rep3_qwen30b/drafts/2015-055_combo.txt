**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* applies retroactively—requires this Court to determine whether a constitutional holding that renders a sentencing enhancement unconstitutionally vague must be applied to cases already final on direct review. The answer is yes. The rule in *Johnson* did not create a new rule of law; it applied a long-established principle of due process: that criminal statutes must provide fair notice of the conduct they prohibit. That principle, rooted in the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, has been settled in this Court’s jurisprudence since the early twentieth century. Because the rule in *Johnson* is not a new rule of constitutional law but a reaffirmation of a fundamental due process guarantee, it must be applied retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including this one.

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires that criminal statutes provide persons of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited. This principle was firmly established in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), where this Court held that “no person may be punished for an act which the law does not prohibit.” The Court emphasized that “the law must be so clear that men of common intelligence can understand it.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81 (1921), where the Court struck down a statute that failed to define its terms with sufficient clarity, stating that “a statute which does not give fair notice of what is prohibited is void for vagueness.” These cases stand for the proposition that a law which fails to provide clear notice of its reach violates due process.

The residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act, which *Johnson* invalidated, was precisely such a law. It required courts to assess whether a crime “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—a standard that, as the Court recognized, depended on “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of the crime. This is not a standard that provides fair notice. It is not a rule that a person of ordinary intelligence can understand. As this Court held in *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612 (1954), “a statute which fails to give fair notice of what is prohibited is void for vagueness.” The residual clause, by its very nature, failed to meet this standard. It invited arbitrary and unpredictable application, which is precisely the kind of uncertainty that the Due Process Clause forbids.

The Court in *Johnson* did not announce a new rule of constitutional law. It did not create a novel constitutional right. It applied a principle that has long been settled: that laws must be sufficiently clear to give notice of prohibited conduct. This principle was recognized in *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214 (1875), where the Court held that “a law which does not define its terms with sufficient precision is void.” The Court in *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76 (1820), similarly held that “no man can be punished for an act which the law does not prohibit,” and that “the law must be so clear that men of common intelligence can understand it.” These cases predate the modern era of criminal procedure, but they establish a bedrock principle: the Constitution demands that criminal laws be intelligible.

The Court in *Johnson* did not invent a new constitutional standard. It applied a standard that has been part of our constitutional law since the founding. The residual clause, by requiring courts to assess hypothetical risks and abstract generic versions of crimes, failed to meet this standard. It was not a rule that a person of ordinary intelligence could understand. It was not a rule that provided fair notice. It was, in short, unconstitutionally vague.

The retroactive application of *Johnson* is required by this Court’s precedent. In *United States v. Palko*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), the Court held that “the Fourteenth Amendment makes the fundamental rights of the Bill of Rights applicable to the States,” and that “a rule of constitutional law that is necessary to protect a fundamental right must be applied retroactively.” The right to fair notice is a fundamental right. It is essential to due process. The Court in *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), recognized that “the right to a fair trial includes the right to know what conduct is prohibited.” This right is not limited to the moment of trial. It extends to sentencing, where the consequences are equally grave.

The Court in *United States v. Brown*, 283 U.S. 376 (1931), held that “a conviction based on a statute that is void for vagueness cannot stand.” The Court in *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81 (1921), similarly held that “a statute which does not give fair notice of what is prohibited is void.” These cases make clear that a law that fails to provide fair notice is invalid, and that a conviction based on such a law must be set aside.

The rule in *Johnson* is not a new rule. It is a reaffirmation of a principle that has been part of our constitutional law since the early days of the Republic. It is not a rule that creates a new right. It is a rule that protects a fundamental right. And because it protects a fundamental right, it must be applied retroactively.

For these reasons, the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States* must be applied retroactively. The residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague. It failed to provide fair notice. It violated due process. And because it violated due process, it must be applied retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including this one.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit and remand for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s holding in *Johnson v. United States*.
