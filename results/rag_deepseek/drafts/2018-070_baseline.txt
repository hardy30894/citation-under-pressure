**ARGUMENT**

**I. The OCSLA Adopts State Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law, Not Whenever State Law Is Merely “Applicable and Not Inconsistent.”**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), provides that state law applies on the OCS “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” The Ninth Circuit erred by reading this language to permit state law whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law leaves a gap. This Court has never adopted that reading, and the text, structure, and purpose of the OCSLA foreclose it.

**A. The Text and Structure of the OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Approach.**

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision operates against the backdrop of § 1333(a)(1), which extends federal law to the OCS “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). In an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction, state law applies only when federal law is silent. *See* *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (treating OCS platforms as “federal enclaves”). The OCSLA’s incorporation of state law is thus a gap-filling mechanism, not an invitation to supplant federal law with more protective state standards.

The phrase “[t]o the extent that” in § 1333(a)(2)(A) confirms this limited role. It conditions the adoption of state law on the absence of applicable federal law. *See* *Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473, 478 (1981) (noting that state law applies under OCSLA only “to the extent” it is “not inconsistent” with federal law). Where federal law comprehensively addresses a subject, there is no “extent” to which state law may operate.

**B. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap for State Wage and Hour Law.**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 *et seq.*, establishes a comprehensive framework for minimum wage, overtime, and meal-period requirements. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves more protective state laws only where those laws “otherwise appl[y].” But the FLSA does not itself create a gap for state law on the OCS; to the contrary, it occupies the field of wage and hour regulation for workers on federal enclaves. *See* *Barrentine v. Arkansas-Best Freight System, Inc.*, 450 U.S. 728, 739 (1981) (recognizing the FLSA’s “comprehensive” nature). Because the FLSA already governs Newton’s claims, there is no “void or gap” for California law to fill. *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969) (requiring a “significant void or gap” in federal law before state law may apply under OCSLA).

**C. The Ninth Circuit’s “Applicable and Not Inconsistent” Standard Conflicts with This Court’s Precedent.**

This Court has consistently applied a gap-filling approach to the OCSLA. In *Rodrigue*, the Court held that state wrongful-death law applied only because the Death on the High Seas Act did not cover accidents on fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court did not ask whether state law was merely “not inconsistent” with federal law; it asked whether federal law provided a remedy. *Id.* at 359-60. Similarly, in *Chevron Oil Co. v. Huson*, 404 U.S. 97, 102-03 (1971), the Court applied state law only after concluding that federal law did not address the limitations period for OCS injuries.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading—that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law—would effectively nullify the OCSLA’s requirement that state law be “applicable.” It would also permit state law to supplement federal law even where Congress has enacted a comprehensive scheme, undermining the uniformity that the OCSLA was designed to achieve. *See* *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (recognizing Congress’s intent to create a uniform body of law for the OCS).

**II. The FLSA’s Savings Clause Does Not Authorize State Law on the OCS.**

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), provides that the FLSA does not “excuse” compliance with state law that is “more beneficial” to employees. But this clause operates only where state law “otherwise appl[ies].” On the OCS, state law applies only to the extent adopted by the OCSLA. Because the OCSLA does not adopt state wage and hour law where the FLSA already governs, the savings clause is irrelevant. *See* *Pacific Operators Offshore, LLP v. Valladolid*, 565 U.S. 207, 215 (2012) (noting that OCSLA, not state law, determines the applicable law on the OCS).

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
