**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Tribal Court Denied Mr. Selam’s Right to Compulsory Process Under the Indian Civil Rights Act.**

The Indian Civil Rights Act (“ICRA”) guarantees that “[n]o Indian tribe in exercising powers of self-government shall . . . deny to any person in a criminal proceeding the right . . . to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This right mirrors the Sixth Amendment’s Compulsory Process Clause, which the Supreme Court has held entitles a defendant to “a fair opportunity to secure the attendance of witnesses in his behalf.” *United States v. Valenzuela-Bernal*, 458 U.S. 858, 867 (1982). The tribal court’s failure to ensure that Mr. Selam could present defense witnesses—despite his apparent lack of cooperation—violated ICRA.

Mr. Selam provided his appointed spokesperson, Patricia Leno-Baker, with a list of five potential character witnesses. Leno-Baker located three on her own, but two admitted their testimony would be damaging. The tribal judge and Leno-Baker advised Mr. Selam of his right to compel witnesses, but Mr. Selam—a layperson unfamiliar with legal procedures—did not ask the judge to issue subpoenas. No defense witnesses were presented, and Mr. Selam was convicted based solely on prosecution testimony, including hearsay from the five-year-old victim.

The right to compulsory process is not contingent on a defendant’s independent ability to navigate the legal system. ICRA, like the Sixth Amendment, imposes an affirmative duty on the tribal court to ensure that the defendant can exercise that right. *See Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967) (the Compulsory Process Clause “is in plain terms the right to present a defense”). Here, the tribal court knew of Mr. Selam’s desire to call witnesses, yet it did not take any action to secure their attendance. This failure rendered the trial fundamentally unfair. *See Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284, 302 (1973) (the right to present witnesses is “a fundamental element of due process of law”).

**II. The District Court Erred in Holding That Mr. Selam Waived His Confrontation Clause Claim by Failing to Exhaust Tribal Remedies.**

**A. Exhaustion of tribal remedies is not a jurisdictional prerequisite for federal habeas review under ICRA.**

The district court treated Mr. Selam’s failure to raise his Confrontation Clause claim in the tribal appellate court as a waiver. But the exhaustion requirement for ICRA habeas claims is a prudential, not jurisdictional, doctrine. *See Jeffredo v. Macarro*, 599 F.3d 913, 919 (9th Cir. 2009) (“[E]xhaustion of tribal remedies is a matter of comity, not jurisdiction.”). Courts may waive the exhaustion requirement when “exhaustion would be futile, or where the tribal court lacks jurisdiction.” *See id.* at 919-20. Here, the tribal court of appeals dismissed Mr. Selam’s entire appeal without a hearing, signaling that it would not entertain any challenge to the fairness of the proceedings. Requiring exhaustion under these circumstances would be futile.

**B. Mr. Selam’s Confrontation Clause claim is not subject to waiver because it arises from the same core factual basis as his compulsory process claim.**

The Confrontation Clause of ICRA provides that “[i]n all criminal proceedings, the accused shall have the right . . . to be confronted with the witnesses against him.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). Mr. Selam contends that the tribal court’s admission of testimonial hearsay from the five-year-old victim through four witnesses—including a doctor, parents, and an investigator—deprived him of his right to face-to-face confrontation. *See Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 59 (2004) (testimonial statements of a declarant who does not testify at trial are inadmissible absent prior opportunity for cross-examination). Although Mr. Selam did not separately label this claim in his tribal appeal, the substance of the claim—that the trial was unfair due to the lack of live witness testimony—was necessarily encompassed in his argument that he was denied compulsory process. This Court should not exalt form over substance. *See Zepeda v. INS*, 753 F.2d 719, 724 (9th Cir. 1985) (waiver is not lightly inferred; a claim is preserved if the “substance of the argument” was presented to the lower court).

Moreover, the tribal court’s failure to provide Mr. Selam with any opportunity to cross-examine the five-year-old victim—or any of the witnesses who repeated her statements—was a structural error that cannot be waived. *See Delaware v. Van Arsdall*, 475 U.S. 673, 681 (1986) (the Confrontation Clause “guarantees an opportunity for effective cross-examination”). Because the error goes to the very framework of the trial, it is not subject to ordinary forfeiture principles. *Id.*

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of habeas relief and remand with instructions to grant the writ unless the Tribe provides Mr. Selam a new trial consistent with ICRA’s guarantees of compulsory process and confrontation.
