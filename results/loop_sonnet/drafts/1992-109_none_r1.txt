**ARGUMENT**

The Ninth Circuit held that due process requires a heightened "reasoned choice" standard—more demanding than the standard for competency to stand trial—before a court may accept a waiver of counsel or a guilty plea. That holding conflicts with this Court's settled competency doctrine, misreads this Court's waiver jurisprudence, and finds no support in *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), the very decision on which it purports to rest. The judgment below should be reversed.

**I. Due Process Requires Only the Competency Standard of *Dusky v. United States*, Not a Heightened "Reasoned Choice" Test.**

This Court has defined the constitutional floor of competency in a single, uniform way, applicable whenever a defendant's mental capacity to participate in criminal proceedings is at issue. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. *Dusky* announced the constitutional measure of competency itself; it did not purport to establish a floor applicable only to the decision to stand trial while leaving other, more consequential decisions to a stricter test. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirmed that due process forbids subjecting a defendant to proceedings when he lacks the capacity described in *Dusky*, explaining that the inquiry concerns his "capacity ... to understand the nature of the proceedings against him" and to participate rationally. *Id.* at 384–85.

Nothing in this framework changes when a defendant, rather than proceeding to trial, elects to plead guilty or to represent himself. The Ninth Circuit's demand for a distinct "reasoned choice" standard rests on the premise that the *decision* to waive rights requires greater rationality than the *capacity* to stand trial. But the competency inquiry has always asked whether a defendant possesses sufficient mental capacity to understand and participate in the proceedings—not whether the particular choice he makes is the wisest one, or the choice that competent counsel would advise. As this Court explained in *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), competency doctrine protects a defendant from being subjected to proceedings he cannot comprehend; it does not authorize courts to second-guess the soundness of decisions made by a defendant who does comprehend them. *Id.* at 108. Once a defendant satisfies *Dusky*, due process asks not whether his choice was strategically sound, but whether it was voluntarily and intelligently made.

**II. This Court's Waiver Decisions Confirm That the Relevant Inquiry Is Voluntariness and Knowledge, Not an Elevated Capacity Threshold.**

The decisions of this Court addressing waiver of counsel and guilty pleas confirm that the constitutional inquiry concerns the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not some heightened capacity beyond *Dusky*. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court described waiver of counsel as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and instructed courts to "indulge every reasonable presumption against waiver," *id.* at 464. That standard asks whether the defendant knew and intended to relinquish a right—a question about the character of the waiver, not the defendant's baseline mental capacity. *Von Moltke v. Gillies*, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), likewise required that a waiver of counsel be made "with eyes open," *id.* at 724, focusing on the defendant's awareness of the risks of proceeding without counsel, again a voluntariness-and-knowledge inquiry rather than a separate competency threshold.

*Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173 (1946), confirms that the touchstone is whether the record shows an informed choice: absence of counsel at arraignment "does not vitiate ... proceedings" where the record shows the plea was "entered understandingly." *Id.* at 174–75. And in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court recognized that "the Constitution does not force a lawyer upon a defendant," *id.* at 279, because a defendant may competently elect self-representation—an election this Court analyzed as a matter of intelligent waiver, not as one requiring superior mental capacity. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), is to the same effect: because a guilty plea "involves the waiver of several constitutional rights," *id.* at 243, the record must affirmatively show the plea was voluntary and intelligent—but *Boykin* nowhere suggests that a defendant must meet a heightened standard of rationality beyond that required to stand trial.

**III. *Westbrook v. Arizona* Does Not Support a Heightened Competency Standard.**

The Ninth Circuit traced its "reasoned choice" language to *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), but that decision cannot bear the weight placed on it. *Westbrook* remanded because the record failed to show that the trial court had determined "whether petitioner had the capacity to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel," *id.* at 150. The Court did not articulate a standard distinct from the one governing competency generally, nor did it suggest that waiver of counsel demands a quantum of rationality beyond *Dusky*'s "reasonable degree of rational understanding." 362 U.S. at 402. *Westbrook* establishes only that a court must separately determine whether a waiver occurred at all; it does not create a new, elevated mental-capacity threshold applicable only to waiver decisions.

**CONCLUSION**

The competency standard governing whether a defendant may stand trial—articulated in *Dusky* and *Pate*—likewise governs whether he may plead guilty or waive counsel. The additional protections required by *Johnson*, *Von Moltke*, *Carter*, *Adams*, and *Boykin* concern the voluntariness and intelligence of the waiver, not a heightened competency threshold, and *Westbrook* provides no basis for one. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
