The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court erred in failing to apply this standard when Richard Moran waived his right to counsel and pleaded guilty to three counts of capital murder.

The competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is distinct from the standard for standing trial. As this Court has held, a defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a "rational and factual understanding of the proceedings" and can "assist in preparing his defense." Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). In contrast, a defendant is competent to waive counsel or plead guilty only if they have the capacity for "reasoned choice" among the alternatives available to them. See Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973).

The state court's failure to apply the correct "reasoned choice" standard when evaluating Moran's competency to waive counsel and plead guilty was not harmless error. Moran's mental state at the time of the plea hearing raised significant concerns about his ability to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of his constitutional rights. Moran had attempted suicide just a few months prior, was taking multiple medications, and responded monosyllabically to the court's questions. Under these circumstances, due process required the trial court to hold a hearing to evaluate Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights.

The post-conviction hearing held three years later did not cure the state court's error. The state court applied the wrong legal standard, relying on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran's competency to stand trial, not his competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. As this Court has held, a state court's factual findings are presumed correct only if they are made after a hearing on the merits and are "evidenced by a written finding, written opinion, or other reliable and adequate indicia." 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The state court's failure to apply the correct legal standard renders its findings unreliable.

The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record does not support a finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights. The psychiatric evaluations on which the state court relied did not assess Moran's capacity for "reasoned choice" and did not consider the effects of his medication. Under these circumstances, the state court's failure to hold a competency hearing at the time of the plea hearing was a clear due process violation.

In sum, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard when determining a defendant's competency to waive counsel or plead guilty. The state court's failure to do so in this case was not harmless error, and the Ninth Circuit correctly reversed the district court's denial of Moran's habeas petition. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's judgment.
