ARGUMENT

**I. The Release Does Not Bar Bancorp’s Claims Because Its Plain Language Does Not Reach Them.**

The release executed by Bancorp releases the FDIC only from claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the OCC to close the Bank.” Bancorp’s claims—unjust enrichment for post-insolvency interest, breach of fiduciary duty in selling assets below value, waste of receivership assets, and inadequate accounting—do not arise from the *decision* to close the Bank. They arise from the FDIC’s *post-closing conduct* as receiver. A release must be construed according to the plain meaning of its terms. *United States v. Lennox Metal Mfg. Co.*, 225 F.2d 302, 306 (2d Cir. 1955). “The language of a release is not to be extended beyond its plain meaning.” *Schine v. Schine*, 250 F.2d 590, 594 (2d Cir. 1957). Because the release explicitly limits itself to the closure decision, it cannot bar claims that challenge entirely separate acts—such as the FDIC’s self-payment of interest years later or its sale of assets at a fraction of their value. Any ambiguity in the release must be construed against the FDIC, its drafter. *M. W. Kellogg Co. v. Standard Oil Co.*, 239 F.2d 618, 620 (10th Cir. 1956); *67 Wall St. Assocs. v. Schram*, 7 N.Y.2d 347, 353 (1960).

**II. The Statute of Limitations Did Not Bar Bancorp’s Claims Because They Accrued Only Upon Termination of the Receivership.**

The district court erred in applying New York’s six-year statute of limitations from the dates of the FDIC’s alleged wrongful acts. A cause of action does not accrue until the plaintiff has a right to maintain suit. *Bailey v. Glover*, 88 U.S. 342, 346 (1874). Where a fiduciary relationship exists, the statute does not run until the fiduciary repudiates the trust or the relationship ends. *Wood v. Carpenter*, 101 U.S. 135, 139 (1879). The FDIC acted as receiver—a fiduciary for the benefit of the Bank’s shareholders, including Bancorp. *Porter v. Sabin*, 149 U.S. 473, 479 (1893). A receiver owes a duty of loyalty and full accounting to all interested parties. *Pepper v. Litton*, 308 U.S. 295, 307 (1939). Until the receivership terminated on November 1, 1995, Bancorp could not bring an independent action for an accounting or for breach of fiduciary duty because the FDIC remained in control of the estate and had not rendered a final account. *King v. Richardson*, 136 F.2d 849, 853 (4th Cir. 1943) (statute of limitations on claim for accounting from fiduciary does not run until fiduciary relationship ends). Bancorp’s claims for unjust enrichment and waste are similarly dependent on the final accounting. *Keystone Driller Co. v. General Excavator Co.*, 290 U.S. 240, 244 (1933) (unjust enrichment claim accrues when the benefit is retained under circumstances that make retention inequitable—here, only after the FDIC’s final self-payment and closure). Because Bancorp filed this action on October 31, 1995—one day before the receivership ended—its claims were timely.

**III. The FDIC’s Post-Closing Conduct Constitutes Independent Wrongs Not Released.**

Even if the release were read broadly, it cannot shield the FDIC from liability for separate breaches of fiduciary duty and waste committed after the closure decision. *Hawes v. Oakland*, 104 U.S. 450, 458 (1881) (shareholders may sue for waste of corporate assets). The FDIC’s payment of post-insolvency interest to itself, its sale of assets below true franchise value, and its charging of legal and office expenses to the receivership estate are not “decision[s] . . . to close the Bank.” They are independent torts. The release does not mention such conduct, and a release “does not extend to matters which the parties could not have contemplated at the time of its execution.” *O’Conner v. O’Conner*, 88 N.Y. 622, 624 (1882). The FDIC’s later self-dealing was not within the scope of the 1988 release.

**Conclusion**

The district court’s summary judgment should be reversed. The release does not cover Bancorp’s claims, and the statute of limitations did not begin to run until the receivership ended on November 1, 1995.
