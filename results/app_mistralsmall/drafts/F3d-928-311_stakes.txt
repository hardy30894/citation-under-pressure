**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Designating Furlow as an Armed Career Criminal Under the ACCA Because His Prior Arson Conviction Is Not a “Violent Felony”**

The ACCA mandates a fifteen-year mandatory minimum sentence for a § 922(g)(1) conviction if the defendant has three prior convictions for either a “violent felony” or a “serious drug offense.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). The ACCA defines a “violent felony” to include any offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” or “is burglary, arson, or extortion, [or] involves the use of explosives.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). Furlow’s prior conviction for first-degree arson in Georgia does not qualify as a “violent felony” under this definition.

The Supreme Court has held that the “elements clause” of the ACCA’s “violent felony” definition requires a categorical approach, examining the elements of the prior offense rather than the facts underlying the conviction. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). Under this categorical approach, arson is not categorically a “violent felony” because it does not necessarily involve the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against another person. *See id.* at 598 (explaining that the enumerated offenses in § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii) are “limited by the requirement that they be violent, forceful, and aggressive”). The Court has further clarified that the “use of explosives” clause does not encompass arson, as arson does not involve the “active employment” of force against a person. *United States v. Castleman*, 572 U.S. 157, 167 (2014).

The government’s reliance on *United States v. McNeal*, 818 F.3d 141 (4th Cir. 2016), is misplaced. In *McNeal*, the Fourth Circuit held that South Carolina’s first-degree arson statute qualified as a “violent felony” under the ACCA because it required proof of “malicious burning” of a dwelling, which the court deemed inherently dangerous to human life. *Id.* at 147. However, *McNeal* did not address whether arson is categorically a “violent felony” under the enumerated offenses clause of § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). The Fourth Circuit’s decision in *McNeal* is therefore distinguishable and does not control this case.

Because Furlow’s prior arson conviction does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA, the district court erred in designating him as an armed career criminal. The government has not alleged three qualifying predicate convictions, and the ACCA enhancement must be vacated.

**II. The District Court Erred in Designating Furlow as a Career Offender Under the Guidelines Because His Prior Drug Conviction Is Not a “Controlled Substance Offense”**

The career offender provision, USSG § 4B1.1, enhances a defendant’s advisory Guidelines range if (1) the instant offense is a “crime of violence” or “controlled substance offense,” and (2) the defendant has at least two prior convictions for such offenses. USSG § 4B1.1(a). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” as an offense under federal or state law, punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, that prohibits the manufacture, import, export, distribution, or dispensing of a controlled substance, or the possession of a controlled substance with intent to manufacture, import, export, distribute, or dispense. USSG § 4B1.2(b).

Furlow’s prior conviction in South Carolina for distribution of crack cocaine does not qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under this definition. The South Carolina statute under which Furlow was convicted, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-370(a)(1), criminalizes the distribution of cocaine, including crack cocaine. However, the statute does not require proof that the defendant intended to distribute a controlled substance; it criminalizes mere possession with intent to distribute. *See State v. Tucker*, 324 S.C. 110, 113 (1996) (interpreting § 44-53-370(a)(1) as requiring proof of possession with intent to distribute). The Guidelines’ definition of “controlled substance offense” requires an offense that prohibits the *distribution* of a controlled substance, not mere possession with intent to distribute. USSG § 4B1.2(b) (emphasis added). Because Furlow’s prior conviction did not require proof of an act of distribution, it does not qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. McVey*, 752 F.3d 330 (4th Cir. 2014), is unavailing. In *McVey*, the Fourth Circuit held that a prior conviction for possession with intent to distribute cocaine under Maryland law qualified as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines because the Maryland statute criminalized the *distribution* of cocaine. *Id.* at 335. However, the South Carolina statute under which Furlow was convicted criminalizes mere possession with intent to distribute, not distribution itself. The Fourth Circuit’s decision in *McVey* is therefore distinguishable and does not control this case.

Because Furlow’s prior drug conviction does not qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines, the district court erred in designating him as a career offender. The career offender enhancement must be vacated.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Furlow respectfully requests that this Court vacate his ACCA and career offender designations and remand for resentencing.
