**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Designating Furlow as an Armed Career Criminal Under the ACCA**

A. Furlow’s prior conviction for first-degree arson in Georgia does not qualify as a “violent felony” under the ACCA.

The ACCA defines a “violent felony” to include any offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” or “is burglary, arson, or extortion.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). However, the Supreme Court has clarified that the “elements clause” of the ACCA’s violent felony definition requires a categorical approach, under which courts look only to the statutory elements of the prior offense, not the facts underlying the conviction. *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990). The Court has further held that the “residual clause” of the ACCA—though now invalidated—required a similar categorical approach. *Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133, 137 (2010).

Here, the district court erred by relying on the underlying facts of Furlow’s arson conviction rather than the statutory elements of Georgia’s first-degree arson statute. Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, O.C.G.A. § 16-7-60(a), criminalizes the burning of any dwelling house “of another,” without requiring proof of any risk to human life. *See* *United States v. McNeal*, 55 F.3d 105, 107 (4th Cir. 1995) (holding that Georgia’s first-degree arson statute does not require proof of danger to human life and thus does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA). Because the statute does not require the use or threatened use of physical force against another person, it does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA. *Taylor*, 495 U.S. at 600.

B. Furlow’s prior conviction for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina does not qualify as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA.

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as an offense under state law involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute a controlled substance, where the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by law is ten years or more. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Fourth Circuit has held that a state drug offense qualifies as a serious drug offense only if the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by the state statute is at least ten years. *United States v. Harcum*, 587 F.3d 544, 550 (4th Cir. 2009).

South Carolina’s statute criminalizing distribution of crack cocaine, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-370(a)(1), prescribes a maximum term of imprisonment of five years. *See* *State v. Robinson*, 342 S.C. 140, 144 (2000). Because the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by South Carolina law is less than ten years, Furlow’s prior conviction does not qualify as a serious drug offense under the ACCA. *Harcum*, 587 F.3d at 550.

**II. The District Court Erred in Designating Furlow as a Career Offender Under the Sentencing Guidelines**

A. Furlow’s prior conviction for first-degree arson in Georgia does not qualify as a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines.

The Guidelines define a “crime of violence” to include any offense that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” or “is burglary, arson, or extortion.” USSG § 4B1.2(a)(1)-(2). The Fourth Circuit has held that the Guidelines’ “crime of violence” definition is identical to the ACCA’s “violent felony” definition for purposes of the categorical approach. *United States v. McNeal*, 55 F.3d at 107.

As discussed above, Georgia’s first-degree arson statute does not require proof of any risk to human life and thus does not qualify as a crime of violence under the Guidelines. *Id.* Therefore, the district court erred in designating Furlow as a career offender based on his arson conviction.

B. Furlow’s prior conviction for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina does not qualify as a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.

The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” as an offense under federal or state law, punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, that prohibits the manufacture, import, export, distribution, or dispensing of a controlled substance, or the possession of a controlled substance with intent to manufacture, import, export, distribute, or dispense. USSG § 4B1.2(b). The Fourth Circuit has held that a state drug offense qualifies as a controlled substance offense only if the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by the state statute is at least five years. *United States v. Harcum*, 587 F.3d at 550.

As discussed above, South Carolina’s statute criminalizing distribution of crack cocaine prescribes a maximum term of imprisonment of five years. *State v. Robinson*, 342 S.C. at 144. Because the maximum term of imprisonment prescribed by South Carolina law is not greater than five years, Furlow’s prior conviction does not qualify as a controlled substance offense under the Guidelines. *Harcum*, 587 F.3d at 550.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Furlow respectfully requests that this Court vacate his designation as an armed career criminal under the ACCA and as a career offender under the Sentencing Guidelines, and remand for resentencing.
