### ARGUMENT

#### I. The TCPA does not impose liability for every call that may later play a prerecorded message; liability turns on whether the call was itself made “using” an artificial or prerecorded voice within the meaning of 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii).

The TCPA makes it unlawful to “make any call” to a cellular telephone “using any automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice” absent consent. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii). The statute is not violated merely because a caller employs equipment that can, in some circumstances, play a recorded message. Rather, the call itself must be made using the prohibited technology or voice. See *Mims v. Arrow Financial Services, LLC*, 565 U.S. 368, 373 (2012) (describing the TCPA’s restrictions on certain calls to cell phones); *Van Patten v. Vertical Fitness Group, LLC*, 847 F.3d 1037, 1043-44 (9th Cir. 2017) (TCPA liability depends on whether a call falls within the statute’s prohibited categories).

Here, DISH does not challenge liability as to the three calls the district court found actionable and DISH has conceded. But the undisputed record does not support summary judgment on the four remaining 8047 calls. The Cisco Dialer’s “positive voice” feature did not automatically make every outbound call a prohibited prerecorded-voice call. As the record reflects, the dialer played a prerecorded message only when the called number was met by a “positive voice,” which could be either an actual human voice or a voicemail greeting. That factual distinction matters. A call is not transformed into a TCPA violation simply because the dialer detects a voice and then begins playing a message; the statute reaches calls “using” a prerecorded voice, not every call from equipment capable of responding to a voice. *47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A)(iii)*.

The district court’s ruling collapsed that distinction. It reasoned that DISH “admitted to using a prerecorded voice in calls from the 8047 number,” and therefore summary judgment was warranted without deciding whether the Cisco Dialer was an ATDS. But the record shows no such categorical admission as to all seven calls. The call logs identify only three calls that resulted in a “Positive Voice” and three that resulted in “Answering Machine”; one call contained a “Data Error-No Value.” Summary judgment may be granted only when there is no genuine dispute of material fact. *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248-49 (1986). On this record, a reasonable factfinder could conclude that at least four calls did not involve the use of a prerecorded voice prohibited by the TCPA.

#### II. Summary judgment was improper on the four disputed calls because the evidence does not establish, as a matter of law, that those calls used a prerecorded voice.

The party seeking summary judgment must show the absence of a genuine dispute as to each material fact. *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986). Where the moving party’s own evidence permits competing inferences, summary judgment must be denied. *Anderson*, 477 U.S. at 255. That principle is dispositive here.

DISH’s calls from the 8047 number were made through a system that could play a prerecorded message only after detecting a voice. The district court treated the mere fact that the system ultimately played a message as enough. But the record does not establish, for the four disputed calls, that the calls were made “using” a prerecorded voice within the meaning of the TCPA. The call log itself creates uncertainty: two calls were flagged as “Answering Machine,” one was “Data Error-No Value,” and only three were flagged as “Positive Voice.” The district court resolved those uncertainties against DISH on summary judgment, which is impermissible. *Tolan v. Cotton*, 572 U.S. 650, 656-57 (2014).

At minimum, the “Answering Machine” entries do not show a call to a cell phone answered by a person or by a prerecorded voice within the statute’s reach. Nor does the “Data Error-No Value” entry establish any TCPA violation at all. The summary-judgment record therefore does not support a ruling that all four calls were unlawful. Because liability under the TCPA must be tied to the facts of each call, the district court erred in entering judgment for Ybarra on these four calls.

#### III. The district court’s damages award should be reduced accordingly.

The district court awarded statutory damages of $500 per call based on seven calls. Because summary judgment should not have been entered on four of those calls, the damages award cannot stand in full. See *47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(3)* (authorizing statutory damages per violation). DISH concedes liability as to the remaining three calls; any judgment should be limited to those calls only. See *Foman v. Davis*, 371 U.S. 178, 181-82 (1962) (courts should ensure judgments reflect the proper legal basis for recovery).

For these reasons, the Court should reverse the partial summary judgment as to the four disputed calls and remand for entry of judgment limited to the three calls on which DISH has conceded liability.
