# ARGUMENT

## THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

This Court's precedent, the settled methodology for Eighth Amendment claims, and the continued absence of a genuine national consensus all compel affirmance of the judgment below.

### I. Penry v. Lynaugh Controls and Forecloses Petitioner's Claim

Just thirteen years ago, this Court squarely held that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically bar the execution of mentally retarded offenders. *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989). The Court there found that "there was insufficient evidence of a national consensus" against such executions, noting that only Georgia and Maryland had enacted statutes prohibiting the practice at that time. *Id.* at 334. The Court further explained that mental retardation is a factor properly weighed by sentencing juries as a mitigating circumstance rather than as an absolute bar to the death penalty, reasoning that "mental retardation is a factor that may well lessen a defendant's culpability for a capital offense," but concluding that individualized consideration—not categorical exemption—was the constitutionally required response. *Id.* at 328.

Petitioner offers no principled basis for this Court to abandon *Penry*'s core holding. The Virginia Supreme Court correctly applied binding precedent when it rejected Atkins's constitutional challenge. *Atkins v. Commonwealth*, 260 Va. 375 (2000). Stare decisis "promotes the evenhanded, predictable, and consistent development of legal principles," *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991), and petitioner has identified no changed circumstance sufficient to overcome that presumption.

### II. No National Consensus Has Emerged Against Executing the Mentally Retarded

This Court's Eighth Amendment jurisprudence requires that "evolving standards of decency" be measured against objective indicia, primarily legislative enactments. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958); *Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370 (1989). In *Stanford*, the Court rejected reliance on "the subjective views of individual Justices" and instead demanded "a clear and convincing" showing of consensus reflected in the actual practice of the States. *Id.* at 378.

Although some additional States have enacted legislation since *Penry*, a bare majority of death-penalty jurisdictions still permit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. This falls well short of the consensus required to establish a new categorical rule. In *Stanford*, the Court declined to find a national consensus against executing 16- and 17-year-old offenders even though a comparable or greater number of States prohibited that practice. *Id.* at 371. The same analytical restraint should govern here.

Moreover, even where legislatures have acted, the definitional and procedural variance among state statutes—differing IQ thresholds, differing burdens of proof, differing timing of the determination—demonstrates the absence of the settled societal judgment this Court has required before constitutionalizing a categorical exemption. Legislative activity alone, without uniformity of practice, cannot substitute for the "clearest proof" this Court has demanded before overturning presumptively valid statutes. *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144, 154 (1938); see also *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 175 (1976) (emphasizing that legislative judgments about proportionality "should not be disturbed unless a clear and convincing case is made out").

### III. Individualized Sentencing Adequately Protects Mentally Retarded Defendants

The Eighth Amendment's core requirement in capital cases is not the categorical exclusion of classes of offenders, but individualized sentencing that permits full consideration of mitigating evidence. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion) (requiring that the sentencer not be precluded from considering "any aspect of a defendant's character or record ... as a mitigating factor"); *Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 110 (1982). This constitutional command was fully satisfied in petitioner's case.

At resentencing, petitioner's own forensic psychologist testified extensively regarding petitioner's mild mental retardation, and the jury weighed that evidence alongside the State's rebuttal testimony. The jury—having heard both sides—concluded that the aggravating circumstances of the abduction, armed robbery, and capital murder outweighed whatever mitigating force petitioner's intellectual limitations carried. This is precisely the individualized moral judgment *Penry* contemplated: "the sentencer must be given adequate guidance to consider" the mitigating evidence, but the ultimate weighing remains within the jury's province. 492 U.S. at 328. Nothing in the Eighth Amendment required the jury to reach a different conclusion, nor does petitioner's disagreement with the jury's weighing transform this case into one presenting a categorical constitutional violation.

### IV. A Categorical Rule Would Usurp the Legislative Function and Create Unworkable Line-Drawing Problems

Mental retardation, unlike the bright-line chronological threshold at issue in juvenile-offender cases, exists on a spectrum and is subject to competing clinical definitions, fluctuating IQ measurements, and contested etiologies. Adopting a categorical constitutional bar would require this Court to resolve definitional questions that are quintessentially legislative in character—precisely the concern animating this Court's admonition in *Stanford* that courts must not substitute their own assessment of proportionality for that reflected in the considered judgments of the elected branches. 492 U.S. at 378-79. Virginia's capital sentencing scheme already permits juries to consider mental retardation as mitigation while preserving legislative primacy over the ultimate line-drawing question.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
