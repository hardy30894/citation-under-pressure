**ARGUMENT**

**I. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE SIXTH AMENDMENT’S JURY TRIAL GUARANTEE.**

The Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, does not require that a jury unanimously find every fact necessary to impose the death penalty. *McGautha v. California*, 402 U.S. 183, 208 (1971) ("[T]he Constitution does not require that the procedures used in determining whether to impose the death penalty be the same as those used in deciding guilt or innocence."). In *McGautha*, the Court upheld California’s death penalty scheme, which permitted the sentencer to impose death based on any aggravating factor found, without requiring jury unanimity or even a written verdict. *Id.* at 199-200. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment does not mandate jury unanimity in sentencing proceedings, as such proceedings are not part of the "core" jury trial right. *Id.* at 208-09.

Florida’s scheme is consistent with *McGautha*. Under Florida law, the jury’s role is advisory; the trial court makes the ultimate sentencing determination after considering the jury’s recommendation. Fla. Stat. § 921.141(3) (1974). The jury’s recommendation need not be unanimous, and the trial court is not bound by it. *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam) (upholding Florida’s scheme where the jury’s advisory verdict was not unanimous). The Sixth Amendment does not require more. *See alsoSpaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 464 (1984) ("The Sixth Amendment does not require that the sentencing function be performed by the jury.").

**II. FLORIDA’S DEATH SENTENCING SCHEME DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT’S PROHIBITION AGAINST CRUEL AND UNUSUAL PUNISHMENT.**

The Eighth Amendment does not prohibit Florida’s death sentencing scheme, which permits the trial court to impose death based on aggravating factors found by a preponderance of the evidence and a non-unanimous jury recommendation. *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242, 252 (1976) (plurality opinion) ("[T]he concerns that prompted our decisions in Furman and its companion cases are not implicated here."). In *Proffitt*, the Court upheld Florida’s scheme, which, like the one at issue here, allowed the trial court to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors and impose death without a unanimous jury recommendation. *Id.* at 258. The Court emphasized that Florida’s scheme provided sufficient safeguards against arbitrary and capricious imposition of the death penalty, including guided discretion, appellate review, and the opportunity for the defendant to present mitigating evidence. *Id.* at 250-52.

Florida’s scheme also comports with *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), which upheld Georgia’s death penalty statute as constitutional. Like Florida’s scheme, Georgia’s required the jury to find at least one statutory aggravating factor before imposing death, but it did not require jury unanimity in the finding of aggravating factors or a unanimous recommendation of death. *Id.* at 192-93. The Court in *Gregg* rejected the argument that the Eighth Amendment requires jury unanimity in capital sentencing, holding that the Constitution permits the States flexibility in designing their death penalty schemes. *Id.* at 195.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989) (plurality opinion) ("The Constitution does not prohibit the execution of a capital offender who is mildly or even moderately mentally retarded."). In *Penry*, the Court upheld Texas’s death penalty scheme, which, like Florida’s, permitted the sentencer to consider mental retardation as a mitigating factor but did not require the jury to make an explicit finding on the issue. *Id.* at 318-19. The Court reasoned that the Eighth Amendment does not mandate specific procedures for the consideration of mitigating evidence. *Id.* at 328.

Florida’s scheme is also consistent with *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238 (1972) (per curiam), which invalidated death penalty schemes that permitted unguided jury discretion. Unlike the schemes invalidated in *Furman*, Florida’s scheme provides clear statutory aggravating factors and requires the trial court to weigh them against mitigating factors. *Proffitt*, 428 U.S. at 250-52. The Court in *Proffitt* distinguished *Furman* on this basis, holding that Florida’s scheme sufficiently channels the sentencer’s discretion to avoid the Eighth Amendment concerns raised in *Furman*. *Id.* at 253.

Finally, the Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury unanimously recommend death. *Spaziano*, 468 U.S. at 464 ("The Constitution does not require that the sentencing function be performed by the jury."). In *Spaziano*, the Court upheld Florida’s scheme, which permitted the trial court to impose death based on a non-unanimous jury recommendation. *Id.* at 460-61. The Court reasoned that the Sixth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in sentencing, and the Eighth Amendment does not impose additional requirements. *Id.* at 464.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Florida’s death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
