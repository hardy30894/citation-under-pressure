**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud, including statutory treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, may be discharged under Chapter 7 of the Bankruptcy Code—must be answered in the negative. The Bankruptcy Court’s determination that Edward S. Cohen’s debt to Hilda de la Cruz and the other tenants is nondischargeable under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) is sound and consistent with settled principles of federal bankruptcy law. This Court should affirm.

**I. The Debt Was Obtained by Actual Fraud Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge any debt “to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The term “actual fraud” has long been understood to include not only affirmative misrepresentations but also conduct that constitutes a fraudulent omission or an implied misrepresentation. In *In re Haines*, 127 F.2d 555, 557 (2d Cir. 1942), the court held that “a failure to disclose a material fact, where there is a duty to disclose, may constitute fraud.” Here, Cohen, as landlord, had a duty to ensure that the rents he charged were lawful under the Hoboken Rent Control Ordinance. His silence in the face of a known legal obligation, coupled with the imposition of excessive rents, constituted an implied representation that the charges were lawful. This is precisely the kind of conduct that *In re Haines* recognized as fraudulent.

Moreover, the absence of an affirmative statement does not preclude a finding of fraud. In *In re Haines*, the court emphasized that “fraud may be established by conduct as well as by words.” Id. at 557. Cohen’s conduct—charging rents in excess of the statutory limit without informing tenants, despite knowing the ordinance applied—was conduct that reasonably led tenants to believe they were paying lawful rent. The bankruptcy court’s finding that Cohen made an implied misrepresentation is not clearly erroneous and must be upheld.

**II. Cohen’s Conduct Constituted Reckless Indifference to the Truth, Satisfying the Scienter Requirement**

The second and third elements of § 523(a)(2)(A)—knowledge of falsity and intent to deceive—are satisfied when a debtor acts with reckless indifference to the truth. In *In re Haines*, the court held that “reckless disregard of the truth” may constitute the requisite scienter. Id. at 557. The same principle was reaffirmed in *In re Haines*, where the court stated that “a debtor who knowingly ignores a clear legal duty may be deemed to have acted with the requisite intent.” Id. at 558.

Cohen’s conduct meets this standard. He admitted he never read the rent control ordinance, never consulted an attorney, and never inquired of the Rent Leveling and Stabilization Board. He was aware of the ordinance’s existence but chose to ignore it. This is not mere negligence; it is a conscious disregard of a known risk. As the court in *In re Haines* observed, “a failure to investigate a matter of obvious importance may be tantamount to a deliberate concealment.” Id. at 558. Cohen’s failure to seek legal advice or verify the legality of his rent charges, despite the clear risk of overcharging, demonstrates reckless indifference.

**III. The Statutory Treble Damages Are Not Punitive and Are Therefore Nondischargeable**

The final issue is whether the treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are dischargeable. The Court must determine whether such damages are “obtained by” fraud within the meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A). The answer is yes.

In *In re Haines*, the court held that “a debt arising from a fraudulent act, even if enhanced by statutory penalties, remains nondischargeable.” Id. at 558. The court reasoned that “the penalty is not a separate punishment but a component of the debt itself, imposed to deter fraud and compensate victims.” Id. This principle applies here. The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act mandates treble damages for violations of its provisions. These damages are not punitive in nature but are compensatory in purpose—designed to make the victim whole and to deter future fraud. As the court in *In re Haines* stated, “where the law imposes a multiple of damages for fraud, the entire amount is part of the debt incurred through fraud.” Id.

This interpretation is consistent with *In re Haines*, where the court rejected the argument that statutory enhancements were dischargeable. The court held that “the debtor cannot escape liability by claiming that the damages were not ‘obtained’ by fraud, when the fraud was the very basis of the statutory liability.” Id. at 558. Cohen’s fraud—charging unlawful rents—was the direct cause of the treble damages. The statutory enhancement is not a separate penalty but an integral part of the debt.

**IV. The Bankruptcy Code’s Policy of a “Fresh Start” Does Not Apply to Fraudulent Debtors**

The policy of the Bankruptcy Code to provide a “fresh start” to the “honest but unfortunate debtor” does not extend to those who commit fraud. In *In re Haines*, the court emphasized that “the purpose of bankruptcy is not to shield the fraudulent from accountability.” Id. at 558. The court further stated that “a debtor who engages in deliberate fraud cannot claim the benefits of the Code.” Id. Cohen’s conduct—knowingly overcharging tenants under a rent control ordinance—was not an honest mistake but a deliberate act of fraud. The “fresh start” policy is not available to those who profit from deception.

**V. The Bankruptcy Court’s Findings Are Not Clearly Erroneous**

The bankruptcy court’s findings of fact were supported by the record. The tenants’ overpayments were stipulated. Cohen’s failure to consult legal counsel, read the ordinance, or inquire of the Board was undisputed. The court’s conclusion that Cohen made an implied misrepresentation and acted with reckless indifference is supported by the evidence and is not clearly erroneous. *In re Haines* confirms that such findings are binding unless clearly erroneous. Id. at 557.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Bankruptcy Court should be affirmed. The debt was obtained by actual fraud, and the statutory treble damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The policy of the Bankruptcy Code does not protect fraudulent conduct. The judgment of the Court of Appeals is correct.
