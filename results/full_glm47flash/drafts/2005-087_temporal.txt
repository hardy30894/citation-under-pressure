**ARGUMENT**

The State of Arizona respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a separate evidentiary right to negate criminal intent beyond the established insanity defense. The trial court properly restricted the evidence to the insanity claim to ensure the jury applied the correct legal standard.

First, the defendant bears the burden of proving insanity by a preponderance of the evidence. In *Davis v. United States*, 328 U.S. 582, 589 (1946), the Supreme Court held that the defendant must establish that he was "laboring under such a defect of reason from disease of the mind as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing, or, if he did know it, that he did not know it was wrong." This is the *M'Naghten* rule, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843). The defendant cannot circumvent this burden by attempting to introduce evidence solely to negate the specific intent element of the crime.

Second, the State has a legitimate interest in defining the scope of the insanity defense to prevent jury confusion. In *Hodgdon v. Maine*, 336 U.S. 112, 119 (1949), the Court noted that the insanity defense is "a defense to the crime, not a separate claim." Consequently, the State may limit the use of expert testimony to the specific elements of the insanity defense. Allowing a defendant to introduce expert testimony solely to negate intent would confuse the jury and risk a miscarriage of justice by allowing the jury to ignore the statutory definition of insanity.

Third, the defendant's proposed evidence is not a separate right but a rehashing of the insanity defense. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966), established that a defendant has a due process right to a fair trial, but this right does not extend to the introduction of evidence that is irrelevant to the elements of the crime or that would violate the state's rules of evidence. If the evidence is relevant only to the insanity defense, it must be admitted under that defense. Restricting the evidence to the insanity defense ensures that the jury does not convict based on a misunderstanding of the defendant's mental state.
