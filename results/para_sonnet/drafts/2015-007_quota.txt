**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida's Capital Sentencing Scheme Satisfies the Sixth Amendment.**

Petitioner asks this Court to extend *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), to invalidate Florida's advisory-jury procedure. But this Court has twice sustained Florida's scheme against precisely this challenge, and nothing in *Ring* disturbed those holdings. In *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447 (1984), the Court held that "the Eighth Amendment does not require that the jury's recommendation be binding," and that a State may commit the ultimate sentencing decision to the trial judge. *Id.* at 464–65. In *Hildwin v. Florida*, 490 U.S. 638 (1989), the Court squarely held that "the Sixth Amendment does not require that the specific findings authorizing the imposition of the sentence of death be made by the jury." *Id.* at 640–41.

*Ring* did not overrule *Hildwin*. *Ring* addressed Arizona's scheme, in which the trial judge alone—without any jury participation whatsoever—found the aggravating circumstance that rendered the defendant eligible for death. 536 U.S. at 588–89. The Court held that "[b]ecause Arizona's enumerated aggravating factors operate as 'the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense,'" the Sixth Amendment "requires that they be found by a jury." *Id.* at 609 (quoting *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 494 n.19 (2000)). Florida's scheme is materially different: a Florida jury unanimously convicts the defendant of the predicate felony beyond a reasonable doubt in the guilt phase, and that verdict itself establishes the statutory aggravating circumstance—commission during a robbery—that exposes the defendant to a death sentence. Here, the jury unanimously found Hurst guilty of first-degree murder committed in the course of a robbery. That verdict necessarily and unanimously found, beyond a reasonable doubt, the very fact—contemporaneous felony—that increased the maximum punishment to death. *Apprendi*'s core command, reiterated in *Ring*, is that "the jury must find the existence of any particular fact that the law makes essential to the punishment." *Apprendi*, 530 U.S. at 483 n.10. That command was satisfied.

The remaining task—weighing the aggravating circumstance against mitigation—is not a factual finding subject to *Apprendi*/*Ring* but a moral judgment about the appropriateness of the sentence. This Court has long recognized that once eligibility is established, "the manner in which a State may weigh" aggravating and mitigating evidence is a matter of legislative discretion, and a sentencer's ultimate selection decision does not require the same procedural safeguards as the eligibility determination. *Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 647–49 (1990); *see also Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004) (describing *Ring* as concerning only "which factfinder" determines the "existence of aggravating factors," not the subsequent weighing). Because the eligibility fact was found unanimously by the jury in the guilt phase, no Sixth Amendment violation occurred in the penalty phase's advisory weighing procedure.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Unanimity or Jury Fact-Finding on Mental Retardation.**

Petitioner's Eighth Amendment claims fare no better. This Court held in *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), that the Eighth Amendment forbids executing the mentally retarded, but expressly left "to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction." *Id.* at 317. That delegation followed the same approach taken in *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399 (1986), where the Court likewise left "the procedures for determining sanity" to the States, requiring only that they be adequate to the task. *Id.* at 416–17. Florida did precisely what *Atkins* invited: it adopted a three-part statutory test—subaverage intellectual functioning, concurrent adaptive deficits, and onset before age eighteen—and afforded Hurst a full evidentiary hearing on that issue in his initial postconviction proceeding, at which competing experts testified and the factfinder resolved the question against him. Nothing in *Atkins* requires that a *jury*, rather than a court, make that determination, and Petitioner already received the individualized process *Atkins* demands.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment require jury unanimity in a death recommendation. In *Harris v. Alabama*, 513 U.S. 504 (1995), this Court upheld a scheme in which the jury's recommendation was merely advisory and could be overridden by the judge, explaining that "the Constitution permits the trial judge, acting alone, to impose a capital sentence." *Id.* at 515. If the Eighth Amendment tolerates a judge overriding a unanimous jury recommendation of life, it necessarily tolerates a judge accepting a non-unanimous recommendation of death. This Court's decision in *Proffitt v. Florida*, 428 U.S. 242 (1976), likewise upheld Florida's capital sentencing structure—including its advisory jury procedure—as satisfying the Eighth Amendment's demand for guided, reliable capital sentencing. *Id.* at 252–53. And *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), confirms that the Eighth Amendment is satisfied where a State channels sentencing discretion through statutory aggravating and mitigating factors, as Florida's scheme does, regardless of the precise voting mechanism used by the advisory jury.

For these reasons, Florida's capital sentencing scheme, applied to Petitioner Hurst, violates neither the Sixth nor the Eighth Amendment. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
