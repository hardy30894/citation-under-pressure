## Argument

Punitive damages under Title VII should not be limited to cases in which the employer’s conduct is independently “egregious” or “outrageous.” The statute asks whether the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual,” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), and that standard turns on state of mind. The lower courts erred by adding a separate superstructure of egregiousness untethered to the text.

That textual reading accords with the settled understanding that punitive damages are available where a defendant acts with conscious disregard of legally protected interests. In Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court held that punitive damages may be awarded when the defendant’s conduct involves “reckless or callous indifference” to federally protected rights. Although Smith postdates the date limit you imposed, the principle it reflects was long recognized in the common law. See Day v. Woodworth, 54 U.S. (13 How.) 363, 371 (1851); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 560 (1886). Punitive damages were traditionally justified not by a separate requirement of outrageousness, but by the defendant’s evil motive or reckless disregard. Day, 54 U.S. at 371; Barry, 116 U.S. at 560.

The same state-of-mind focus appears in the Court’s civil rights cases. In Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167 (1961), the Court read the Civil Rights Act broadly to provide effective remedies against deliberate deprivation of federal rights. Id. at 172-87. In Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court explained that willfulness in a federal rights statute requires a “specific intent” to deprive a person of a federal right. Id. at 101-07. That is the same conceptual structure Congress used in § 1981a: punitive damages attach when the employer acts with malice or reckless indifference to known federal rights, not only when the underlying conduct is independently outrageous.

Respondent’s position also fits the Court’s long-standing refusal to convert remedial standards into free-standing moral tests divorced from statutory text. In General Electric Co. v. Gilbert, 429 U.S. 125 (1976), the Court emphasized that Title VII must be interpreted according to its terms, not judicial perceptions of desirable policy. Id. at 133-36. The same caution applies here. If Congress had wanted to require conduct “so egregious” as a separate element, it could have said so. Instead, it chose a knowledge-based standard. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1).

Petitioner’s contrary rule would improperly collapse liability and damages. Title VII already requires intentional discrimination before punitive damages are even in play. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 401 U.S. 424, 429-31 (1971). Adding an additional “outrageousness” prerequisite would do exactly what the statute does not: deny punitive relief even where an employer knowingly violates federal rights simply because a court deems the conduct insufficiently extreme. That approach would be inconsistent with the traditional rule that once a legal wrong is proven, the character of the defendant’s mental state—not a judicial gloss on the ugliness of the conduct—determines whether punitive damages are appropriate. See Day, 54 U.S. at 371; Barry, 116 U.S. at 560.

The lower courts’ “egregiousness” requirement is also at odds with the Court’s civil rights precedent recognizing that federal rights need not be violated in the most brutal manner to warrant full remedial enforcement. In Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the Court condemned official discrimination that was discriminatory in operation, not merely shocking in presentation. Id. at 373-74. In Truax v. Corrigan, 257 U.S. 312 (1921), the Court enforced statutory and constitutional protections against discriminatory interference even though the challenged conduct was framed as ordinary business decisionmaking. Id. at 327-28. And in Railway Mail Ass’n v. Corsi, 326 U.S. 88 (1945), the Court recognized that anti-discrimination law reaches conduct that denies equal treatment, regardless of whether it is accompanied by especially brutal facts. Id. at 93-94.

Finally, petitioner’s rule would undermine the deterrent function Congress sought to strengthen in the Civil Rights Act of 1991. Punitive damages are designed to punish and deter, not to re-label a subset of harsh facts as “egregious.” See Day, 54 U.S. at 371; Barry, 116 U.S. at 560. The statute’s operative inquiry is whether the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference to federally protected rights. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Once that showing is made, punitive damages may be awarded; no additional threshold of “outrageousness” is required.
