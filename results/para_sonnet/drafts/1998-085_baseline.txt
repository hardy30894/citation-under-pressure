# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text and Structure of § 1981a Confirm That Congress Reserved Punitive Damages for Egregious Misconduct.

Congress enacted the Civil Rights Act of 1991 to provide "additional remedies under Federal law . . . needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a note (Congressional Findings). But Congress did not make punitive damages coextensive with liability. Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where a plaintiff "demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). Every Title VII plaintiff who prevails on the merits has already proven intentional discrimination. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). If "malice" and "reckless indifference" meant nothing more than the intent already required for liability, § 1981a(b)(1) would be a nullity—every successful plaintiff would automatically qualify for punitive damages. This Court does not construe statutes to render entire clauses superfluous. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994) (recognizing that the 1991 Act "significantly expanding the monetary relief potentially available" nonetheless imposed distinct, heightened thresholds for each new remedy). The only way to give § 1981a(b)(1) independent meaning is to require proof that the employer's conduct was of a qualitatively different, more blameworthy character than the garden-variety discrimination that satisfies liability alone—conduct that is, in short, egregious.

## II. The Legislative History Confirms That Congress Intended an Egregiousness Threshold.

The floor debates leading to passage of the 1991 Act repeatedly signal that Congress meant to limit punitive damages to the worst cases of discrimination. Senator Dole's interpretive memorandum, joined by other sponsors, explained that plaintiffs could recover punitive damages only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Mem. of Sen. Dole et al.). The House Report likewise demanded of plaintiffs seeking punitive damages "an even higher standard" than that required to establish simple intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 40(I), 102d Cong., 1st Sess. 12 (1991). Even the House's own bill, which Respondent recognizes was not the version ultimately enacted, confirms the underlying premise that Congress viewed punitive damages as reserved for outrageous conduct, since that bill would have permitted such damages only for discrimination undertaken "with malice, or with reckless or callous indifference" to federally protected rights—language chosen to track the common-law standard for outrageous torts. See House Report at 12. Nothing in this history suggests Congress meant simply to duplicate the liability standard under a new label. To the contrary, Congress capped punitive damages according to employer size, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), a structural choice that makes sense only if punitive damages were meant to be the exception, not the rule, following every jury verdict of discrimination.

## III. Traditional Punitive-Damages Principles and This Court's Precedents Confirm That Egregiousness Is the Appropriate Threshold.

This Court's punitive-damages jurisprudence has long recognized that such awards serve retribution and deterrence functions reserved for conduct beyond ordinary wrongdoing. In BMW of North America, Inc. v. Gore, 517 U.S. 559 (1996), the Court explained that "the most important indicium of the reasonableness of a punitive damages award is the degree of reprehensibility of the conduct." Id. at 575. That principle reflects a broader common-law tradition, embodied in the Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979), under which punitive damages are available "for conduct that is outrageous, because of the defendant's evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others"—a formulation that ties the *state of mind* language to conduct already understood as *outrageous*, not to routine violations. Even this Court's decision in Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), which permitted punitive damages under § 1983 upon a showing of "reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others," id. at 56, arose against a backdrop of conduct—physical abuse of a prisoner—that was independently egregious; the state-of-mind standard there did not operate in a vacuum divorced from the severity of the underlying conduct.

Respondent recognizes that some courts of appeals, construing the analogous remedy under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, have held that punitive damages may follow from proof of the requisite state of mind alone. See, e.g., Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co., 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987). But that approach cannot be squared with Congress's evident purpose in enacting § 1981a to create capped, carefully bounded remedies distinct from the uncapped damages available under § 1981—a distinction this Court should honor rather than erase. Divorcing the availability of punitive damages from the character of the underlying conduct would convert every Title VII verdict into a potential punitive-damages case, contrary to Congress's design and to the settled understanding that punitive damages are an "extraordinary remedy," reserved, in the words of the Act's own sponsors, for "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S15473.

## CONCLUSION

For these reasons, this Court should hold that an employer's conduct must be egregious, independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII, and should affirm the judgment below.
