**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from its after-hours facilities—despite the district’s own policy of opening those facilities to non-religious community organizations—violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses. The Second Circuit erred in concluding that the school district’s facilities constituted a “limited public forum” that had not been opened to religious use by policy or practice. In truth, the district’s selective access policy created a limited public forum, and its exclusion of religious speech based on content and viewpoint is unconstitutional under binding Supreme Court precedent. The district’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a religiously themed film series on family values constitutes a content-based restriction on speech that fails even the most lenient scrutiny.

**I. The School District’s Facilities Are a Limited Public Forum Open to Non-Religious Groups, and the District’s Exclusion of Religious Speech Is a Content-Based Discrimination Violative of the First Amendment**

The First Amendment protects the right to speak, assemble, and petition government, even when the speech is religious in nature. When a government creates a limited public forum—by opening its property to certain types of expressive activity—it may not exclude speech based on its religious content or viewpoint. *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983); *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the First Amendment prohibits the government from denying access to a public forum on the basis of content.” *Regan v. Taxation With Representation of Wash.*, 461 U.S. 540, 547 (1983).

Here, the Center Moriches School District has opened its facilities to non-religious community groups for after-hours use. The district has permitted the Southern Harmonizers to perform music, the Center Moriches Free Public Library to sponsor a lecture series on parapsychology, and other civic and cultural groups to use the auditorium and gymnasium. These uses were not incidental or isolated; they were part of a consistent policy of allowing non-religious, non-curricular groups to use school facilities during non-school hours. This policy created a limited public forum. *See Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981) (finding that a university’s policy of allowing student groups to meet on campus created a limited public forum).

The district’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a film series on family values—despite the fact that the film was nonsectarian in presentation and the series was open to the public—constitutes a content-based exclusion. The film, *Turn Your Heart Toward Home*, was described as a “practical” and “non-religious” presentation on parenting, and Dr. James Dobson, its featured speaker, is a well-known family psychologist. The district’s own staff acknowledged that the film “does appear to be church related,” but this determination was based on the speaker’s religious affiliation and the film’s Christian perspective—not on any actual religious content or ritual. *See Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 770 F. Supp. 91, 98 (E.D.N.Y. 1991). The district’s refusal was thus not neutral but based on the religious *content* of the speech.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that content-based exclusions in a limited public forum are unconstitutional unless justified by a compelling state interest and narrowly tailored. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 45. The district offers no such justification. It cannot claim a compelling interest in excluding religious speech when it allows non-religious groups to use the same facilities for similar purposes. *See *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 247 (holding that a school’s refusal to allow a religious student group to meet, when non-religious groups are permitted, violates the Equal Access Act and the First Amendment). The district’s policy is not viewpoint-neutral; it discriminates against religious speech while allowing secular speech on the same subject matter.

**II. The District’s Categorical Ban on Religious Use Violates the Free Exercise Clause and the Principle of Equal Treatment**

The Free Exercise Clause protects the right to engage in religious expression, even in public forums. *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872, 878 (1990), does not override the principle that the government may not deny access to a public forum based on religious content when it allows access to others. *See *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993) (plurality opinion). The Court in *Lamb’s Chapel* held that “a government that opens its forum to private speech may not exclude speech based on religious content.” Id. at 395.

The district’s policy of banning all religious use—regardless of content, context, or purpose—violates this principle. The district allows secular groups to use the facilities for discussions on family, parenting, and community values. Yet it denies access to a religious group seeking to discuss the same topics. This is not neutrality; it is discrimination. *See *Good News Club v. Milford Central Sch.*, 533 U.S. 98, 107 (2001) (holding that a school’s refusal to allow a religious group to meet, when secular groups are permitted, violates the Free Speech Clause).

Moreover, the district’s policy is not even content-neutral. It treats religious speech differently from secular speech on the same subject. The district’s own rules prohibit “religious purposes,” but allow “family-oriented” and “community” programming. This distinction is impermissible. *See *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273 (holding that a university could not exclude religious student groups from a forum open to other student groups). The district’s policy is not a general rule against religious activity; it is a content-based exclusion.
