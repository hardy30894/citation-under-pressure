**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment does not compel a public school to provide access to its facilities for religious groups seeking to conduct sectarian worship or religious instruction. The exclusion of the Good News Club from Milford Central School’s after-hours use of school premises did not violate the Club’s free speech rights under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The school’s policy—prohibiting religious use of its facilities—was a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral regulation of a limited public forum, and the Club’s activities were properly characterized as religious instruction and worship, not mere moral or character development from a religious perspective. Moreover, the school’s decision was justified by a compelling interest in avoiding Establishment Clause violations.

**I. The School’s Policy Is a Content-Neutral, Viewpoint-Neutral Regulation of a Limited Public Forum**

Milford Central School operates a limited public forum under New York’s Community Use Policy, which permits district residents to use school facilities for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” The policy explicitly excludes religious purposes, requiring applicants to certify that their use complies with this restriction. This is not a blanket ban on religious speech; it is a content-based exclusion of religious *use*—a distinction that is constitutionally significant.

In *Regents of the University of California v. Bakke*, 438 U.S. 265, 302 (1978), the Court recognized that “a State may exclude certain categories of speech from a public forum without violating the First Amendment.” Similarly, in *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School District*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993), the Court held that a school district may exclude religious groups from a limited public forum if the exclusion is “justified by a legitimate, content-neutral reason.” Here, the school’s policy is content-neutral: it applies equally to all religious and non-religious uses. It does not target the Good News Club’s viewpoint; it prohibits all religious use, regardless of the speaker’s intent or message.

The Club’s activities—prayer, Bible lessons, memorization of scripture, and invitations to accept Jesus as Savior—are not “moral and character development” in the secular sense. They are religious exercises. As the Court stated in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), “there is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’” The Club’s activities are not merely expressive of a religious viewpoint on a secular topic; they are themselves acts of religious worship and instruction.

**II. The Good News Club’s Activities Are Religious Instruction and Worship, Not Secular Moral Education**

The Club’s own materials and lesson plans demonstrate that its purpose is not to teach values in a secular context, but to convert children to Christianity. The lesson titled “Israel Demands a King” explicitly distinguishes between “saved” and “unsaved” children, and invites the latter to “believe on Jesus” and “be saved today.” The “Challenge and Invitation” segment is a direct call to personal conversion, culminating in a prayer to “receive Jesus as [a child’s] personal Savior.” This is not moral instruction—it is evangelism.

The Court has long recognized that “religious instruction” includes teaching that “involves the study of religious texts, the recitation of prayers, and the dissemination of religious doctrine.” *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395. The Club’s activities fall squarely within that category. The fact that the Club frames its message as “moral development” does not transform its religious content into secular speech. As the Court held in *Good News Club v. Milford Central School*, 533 U.S. 98, 108 (2001), “the fact that a religious group’s message is couched in terms of moral or character development does not render it nonreligious.” The government may not be required to treat religious worship as secular speech simply because the speaker labels it otherwise.

**III. The School’s Exclusion Is Justified by a Compelling Interest in Avoiding Establishment Clause Violations**

The school’s decision to exclude the Club was not based on hostility to religion, but on a compelling interest in avoiding the appearance and reality of government endorsement of religion. The Establishment Clause prohibits government from “advancing or inhibiting religion.” *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872, 879 (1990). When a public school provides access to its facilities for religious groups to conduct worship, it risks creating the impression that the government is sponsoring religious activity.

In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court acknowledged that “a school district may have a legitimate interest in avoiding the appearance of endorsing religion.” 508 U.S. at 396. The school’s policy—prohibiting religious use of its facilities—was a reasonable means of preventing such an appearance. The Club’s activities, including prayer and proselytizing, are inherently religious and would be perceived as government-sanctioned religious exercises if conducted on school premises.

The Club’s argument that the school must allow its use because other groups (e.g., Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts) are permitted is misplaced. Those groups are not engaged in religious worship or instruction. The Boy Scouts, for example, have a secular program of character development and leadership training, even if they incorporate a “duty to God” in their oath. *See *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 397. The Good News Club, by contrast, is not teaching values in a secular framework; it is teaching Christian doctrine and inviting children to accept Christ as their Savior. The school’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint; it treats all religious uses the same.

**IV. The Club’s Free Speech Rights Were Not Violated**

The First Amendment does not require a public school to provide a forum for religious worship. *See *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 601 (1972) (“The First Amendment does not require the government to provide a forum for speech.”). The school’s limited public forum is not a traditional public forum, and the government may impose reasonable, content-neutral restrictions. *See *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S.
