# ARGUMENT

**I. Due Process Requires Only That the Suit "Arise Out of or Be Connected With" the Defendant's Forum Activities, Not That Those Activities Be the Proximate Cause of the Injury.**

Since *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), the touchstone of specific jurisdiction has been whether the exercise of jurisdiction offends "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *Id.* at 316. This Court explained that a corporation's presence in a forum, "if only occasional," may create obligations, and that "so far as those obligations arise out of or are connected with the activities within the state, a procedure which requires the corporation to respond to a suit brought to enforce them can, in most instances, hardly be said to be undue." *Id.* at 319. That formulation—"arise out of or are connected with"—is disjunctive and relational; it does not require the rigid, mechanical causal link Ford now demands. The inquiry asks whether the *relationship* among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation makes it fair to require a defense there, not whether a particular contact is the "but-for" cause of the plaintiff's injury.

**II. Ford's Continuous, Purposeful Activities Marketing, Selling, and Servicing Explorers in Montana Relate Directly to Gullett's Claims.**

Ford is not a stranger to Montana. It has, for decades, advertised, distributed, sold, and serviced the very model at issue—the Explorer—throughout the State, deriving substantial revenue from Montana consumers who purchase and drive Ford vehicles on Montana roads. Under *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235 (1958), the critical question is whether the defendant "purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." *Id.* at 253. Ford has done exactly that with respect to the Explorer line, cultivating a Montana market for the product that killed Markkaya Gullett. That the specific vehicle first entered the stream of commerce through Washington and Oregon does not sever the relationship between Ford's Montana activities and this suit; Ford's nationwide marketing of the Explorer—including in Montana—created the very demand and market presence that resulted in Explorers, including this one, being driven on Montana highways.

This Court's decision in *Travelers Health Assn v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643 (1950), confirms that a corporation which solicits business within a State subjects itself to that State's jurisdiction for matters connected with that solicited business, because the State has a legitimate interest in "regulat[ing] the conduct" of those "who solicit business" there and cause harm to its residents. *Id.* at 649. Ford solicited, and profited from, the Montana market for Explorers. Its claim that it should be immune from suit there merely because the tortious tire happened to be manufactured or first sold elsewhere elevates fortuity over the functional relationship Due Process protects.

**III. Montana's Manifest Interest in Providing Its Residents a Forum for Redress Supports Jurisdiction.**

In *McGee v. International Life Insurance Co.*, 355 U.S. 220 (1957), this Court upheld jurisdiction over a foreign insurer whose only contact with California was a single reinsurance certificate mailed into the State, emphasizing that California had a "manifest interest in providing effective means of redress for its residents when their insurers refuse to pay claims." *Id.* at 223. The same logic applies with even greater force here. Montana has a manifest interest in providing redress to its own citizen, killed on a Montana highway, from a manufacturer that has systematically cultivated a Montana market for the very product line responsible for her death. As this Court recognized in *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457 (1940), the State "which accords . . . privileges and gives protection" to those who avail themselves of activity within its borders "has an interest in extending" its judicial power to redress wrongs connected to that activity. *Id.* at 463. Ford has enjoyed the benefits of the Montana market for years; it cannot now disclaim the burdens that accompany those benefits.

**IV. Requiring Ford to Defend This Suit in Montana Comports With Fair Play and Substantial Justice.**

Ford is a large, sophisticated manufacturer with continuous, systematic activity in Montana of the kind this Court found sufficient even for *general* jurisdiction in *Perkins v. Benguet Consolidated Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437 (1952). Ford's regular business in the forum, its purposeful cultivation of a Montana market for Explorers, and Montana's strong interest in affording redress to its own injured citizen together satisfy the flexible "fair play and substantial justice" standard of *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. There is nothing unfair, unjust, or unforeseeable about requiring Ford—an entity that markets and sells Explorers throughout Montana—to answer in Montana courts for a design defect in that same vehicle line that caused a fatal accident on a Montana road.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
