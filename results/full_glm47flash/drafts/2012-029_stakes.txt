**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT’S “AIR MEASUREMENT” TEST IS INCONSISTENT WITH SUPREME COURT PRECEDENT AND THE “WELL-PLEADED COMPLAINT” RULE.**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts are not a general forum for resolving state law claims that merely involve federal issues. In *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, the Court explained that for a case to arise under federal patent law under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), a plaintiff’s right to relief must “necessarily depend on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” 486 U.S. at 808-09. This “well-pleaded complaint” rule prevents a plaintiff from “open[ing] the ‘arising under’ door” by simply pleading a federal issue. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. at 117-18.

The Federal Circuit’s test, articulated in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), is incompatible with this framework. *Air Measurement* holds that federal jurisdiction exists when “establishing patent infringement is a necessary element of a [state] malpractice claim.” Id. at 1273. This test conflates the specific patent statute with the general federal question statute, ignoring the Supreme Court’s instruction that federal jurisdiction must be limited to cases where the federal issue is the source of the cause of action. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808-09. Here, Minton’s malpractice claim is a state law claim for negligence. The patent issue is merely a potential defense to the underlying claim, not the source of the cause of action. Therefore, the “well-pleaded complaint” rule bars exclusive federal jurisdiction.

**II. THE GRABLE FACTORS ARE NOT MET, AND FEDERAL JURISDICTION WOULD DISTURB THE BALANCE OF FEDERAL AND STATE JUDICIAL RESPONSIBILITIES.**

Even under the *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.* framework, which allows state law claims to be heard in federal court when they implicate significant federal issues, exclusive jurisdiction is not proper here. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. The Supreme Court has emphasized that federal courts may not entertain such claims if doing so would “disturb any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.” Id. The Federal Circuit’s approach in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), fails to account for this federalism concern.

The first *Grable* factor—that the federal issue be “necessary” to the state law claim—is not satisfied. The patent issue is not necessary to the malpractice claim; it is merely one potential defense. As the Supreme Court has noted, a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily one arising under those laws unless it “really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. at 569. Here, the dispute is about attorney negligence, not the validity of the patent itself. The state court is fully capable of applying the experimental use exception without encroaching on federal patent rights. See *Roof Technical Servs., Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F.Supp.2d 749, 753-54 (N.D.Tex. 2010) (holding that federal courts do not have jurisdiction over malpractice claims because adjudicating them would not threaten uniformity in patent law). Because the patent issue is not necessary and federal jurisdiction would disturb the balance of judicial responsibilities, the Texas Supreme Court erred in finding exclusive federal jurisdiction.
