ARGUMENT

Plain-error review permits an appellate court to consult the entire record when assessing whether an error affected substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 36, 40 (1936) (appellate courts may notice errors “affecting substantial rights” even if not brought to the trial court’s attention). This Court has long recognized that the inquiry is not confined to the evidence formally admitted before the jury. Wiborg v. United States, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896) (“[T]he court may, in the exercise of its appellate jurisdiction, notice a plain error.”).

The same principle appears in Clyatt v. United States, 197 U.S. 207, 221-22 (1905), where the Court examined matters beyond the trial testimony to determine whether the record disclosed a fundamental defect. Likewise, in Crain v. United States, 162 U.S. 625, 634 (1896), the Court looked to the full proceedings to decide whether an omission had worked a miscarriage of justice.

Later decisions confirm that the prejudice and fairness inquiries are holistic. Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189, 202 (1943), directed courts to “search the record” for any indication that the error was harmless or, conversely, that it undermined confidence in the verdict. Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), reiterated that the appellate function requires examination of “the proceedings in their entirety.” Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 107 (1945), likewise authorized review of facts outside the jury’s formal consideration when determining whether an error “seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.”

Ballew v. United States, 160 U.S. 187, 193 (1895), and Ball v. United States, 163 U.S. 662, 669 (1896), both illustrate that an appellate court may consider prior convictions and other record materials to gauge the impact of a trial defect. These authorities establish that the Eleventh Circuit acted within settled bounds when it consulted Greer’s criminal history to decide whether the Rehaif error prejudiced him or impugned the integrity of the proceedings.

No pre-1970 decision limits the reviewing court to the four corners of the trial transcript. The consistent teaching is that the substantial-rights and fairness inquiries are informed by the complete record. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
