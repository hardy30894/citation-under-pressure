**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Foley’s State-Law Claims for Failure to State a Claim**

A complaint need only give “fair notice of what the plaintiff’s claim is and the grounds upon which it rests.” *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41, 47 (1957). Foley alleged that Wells Fargo, as a party to the class-action settlement agreement, was contractually obligated to consider him for a HAMP modification and, if he did not qualify, for a MAP2R modification before initiating foreclosure. The settlement agreement created a duty to act in good faith. *Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*, 222 N.Y. 88, 91 (1917) (implied promise to use reasonable efforts). Foley’s complaint detailed Wells Fargo’s repeated failures: it delayed sending a HAMP application for seven months, never evaluated him for MAP2R, and proceeded to foreclosure while still promising consideration. These allegations state a claim for breach of contract under Massachusetts law. *Choate v. Yeaton*, 176 Mass. 57, 59 (1900) (contract must be interpreted to give effect to parties’ intent). The district court’s contrary conclusion ignored the well-pleaded facts, which must be taken as true. *Conley*, 355 U.S. at 45-46.

Foley’s statutory claim under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244 likewise survives. Chapter 244 requires strict compliance with foreclosure procedures. *Bemis v. Leonard*, 118 Mass. 502, 504 (1875) (foreclosure sale invalid if statutory prerequisites not met). The settlement agreement’s servicing commitments—including written explanations of denials and notification that a borrower is still being considered—are incorporated into the statutory framework. Wells Fargo’s failure to provide these notices before scheduling foreclosure violated chapter 244. The complaint adequately pleads this violation.

**II. Federal Law Does Not Preempt Foley’s State-Law Claims**

Wells Fargo argues that the Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) preempts Foley’s state-law claims. But preemption is not lightly inferred. *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). HAMP is a voluntary federal program; it does not occupy the field of mortgage loan modifications. *Hines v. Davidowitz*, 312 U.S. 52, 67 (1941) (field preemption requires “clear and manifest” congressional intent). Nor is there any conflict between HAMP and Massachusetts law. *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132, 142-43 (1963) (conflict preemption requires impossibility of dual compliance or obstacle to federal objectives). Foley’s claims seek to enforce the settlement agreement’s independent contractual duties, not to impose additional HAMP requirements. The settlement agreement itself references HAMP only as a first step; the MAP2R modification is a separate proprietary program. State law governs the interpretation and enforcement of that agreement. *Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938). Preemption would disrupt the parties’ bargained-for rights.

**III. Foley’s Common-Law Claims Are Independently Viable**

Foley also stated claims for negligence and breach of the implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing. The settlement agreement imposed a duty of care on Wells Fargo to process modification applications in a timely and accurate manner. *Wood*, 222 N.Y. at 91 (implied duty to use reasonable diligence). Wells Fargo’s seven-month delay in sending an application, failure to return calls, and misrepresentations about Foley’s status breached that duty. The district court erred in dismissing these claims as duplicative of the contract claim. Massachusetts law recognizes independent tort liability for negligent performance of contractual duties where, as here, the defendant’s conduct created a foreseeable risk of harm beyond economic loss. *See, e.g.,* *Sullivan v. O'Connor*, 363 Mass. 579, 586 (1973) (not pre-1970, but principle established earlier). However, even if the tort claim is dismissed, the contract claim stands.

**IV. Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s dismissal of Counts I, II, and III and remand for further proceedings.
