**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Plain Language and Structure of § 1231(a)(6) Do Not Authorize Indefinite Detention**

Section 1231(a)(6) provides that an alien ordered removed “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The statute does not specify any temporal limit. Yet Congress elsewhere demonstrated its ability to authorize indefinite detention when it wished, as in the detention of inadmissible aliens pending removal proceedings. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1226(c). The absence of such explicit authorization for the post-removal period indicates that Congress did not intend to permit perpetual confinement. *See United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 202 (1957) (construing immigration detention statute narrowly to avoid “serious constitutional doubts”). The provision’s companion section, § 1231(a)(3), mandates supervision for aliens not removed during the removal period, reinforcing that release is the norm. A statute purporting to authorize indefinite detention would require far clearer language. *Cf. Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 541–42 (1952) (sustaining detention pending deportation because statutory scheme provided for periodic review and release upon conditions).

**II. Indefinite Detention of a Resident Alien Violates Substantive Due Process**

Resident aliens are “persons” within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment and enjoy substantial constitutional protections. *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896) (immigration detention limited; criminal punishment requires judicial process). Indefinite civil detention must serve a legitimate nonpunitive purpose and be limited in duration and scope. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 746–47 (1987). For resident aliens subject to removal, detention is permissible only to ensure appearance at removal and to protect the community during the removal period. *Carlson*, 342 U.S. at 538. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, those purposes evaporate. Detention beyond that point becomes punitive in effect, yet it is imposed without the procedural protections required for criminal punishment. *Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (holding that indefinite civil confinement based solely on dangerousness violates due process). Here, Zadvydas has been in custody for years with no prospect of removal; continued detention serves no immigration purpose and is irreconcilable with the Constitution.

**III. The Statute Must Be Construed to Avoid Serious Constitutional Doubts**

When a statute is susceptible to two constructions, one of which raises grave constitutional questions, courts must adopt the interpretation that avoids those problems. *Kent v. Dulles*, 357 U.S. 116, 130 (1958); *Crandon v. United States*, 494 U.S. 152, 168 (1990). Section 1231(a)(6) can fairly be read to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal. That interpretation avoids the constitutional conflict presented by indefinite confinement. The alternative—reading the statute to permit limitless detention—would transform an immigration measure into an indefinite civil detention scheme, raising due process concerns similar to those in *Foucha*. This Court’s decision in *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is inapposite because it involved an excludable alien at the border, not a resident alien who has entered the country and established constitutional ties. *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 215–16. As the Court recognized, the power to detain a resident alien is not the same as the power to exclude an alien at the border. *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 305–06 (2001) (noting that resident aliens have stronger constitutional claims than inadmissible aliens).

**IV. The Reasonable-Time Limitation Is Consistent With the Statute’s Purpose**

Congress’s objective in § 1231(a)(6) was to “assur[e] the alien’s presence at the moment of removal.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 699 (2001). That purpose is satisfied by detention for a period reasonably necessary to effect removal, not by indefinite confinement. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, the justification for detention disappears. The statutory scheme also provides for supervised release under § 1231(a)(3), which adequately protects the government’s interests pending removal. The Attorney General may impose stringent conditions of release, and the alien remains subject to criminal penalties for violating those conditions. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1253. Thus, a reasonable-time construction does not leave the government without tools to manage removable aliens who are not actually removed.

**Conclusion**

The Fifth Circuit’s earlier decision—upholding Zadvydas’s detention as long as “good faith efforts” to remove him continue—demands an impossible showing that removal will never occur. That standard is inconsistent with the statute’s implicit reasonable-time limitation and with the constitutional imperative against indefinite detention. The judgment of the district court granting the writ should be affirmed.
