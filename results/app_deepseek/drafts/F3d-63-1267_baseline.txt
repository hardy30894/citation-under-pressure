**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Denying Tarmac’s Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law Because Stelwagon Failed to Prove Actual Antitrust Damages with Reasonable Certainty.**

A plaintiff seeking treble damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act for a violation of § 2(a) of the Robinson-Patman Act must prove not only the fact of price discrimination but also that the discrimination caused actual injury to its business or property. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the mere occurrence of price discrimination does not automatically give rise to damages.” *Id.* at 561. Rather, the plaintiff must demonstrate that it suffered a “competitive injury” – typically lost profits or lost sales – that is attributable to the discriminatory pricing. *Id.* at 562–63. This proof must be “based upon a just and reasonable estimate” and cannot be “speculative or conjectural.” *Bigelow v. RKO Radio Pictures, Inc.*, 327 U.S. 251, 264 (1946).

Stelwagon’s damages case rested entirely on the testimony of its expert, who calculated lost profits by assuming that every sale Tarmac made to Standard and Celotex at lower prices would have been a sale to Stelwagon at higher prices. That assumption is pure speculation. The record contains no evidence that Stelwagon would have captured those sales absent the discrimination. Stelwagon did not show that its customers would have purchased from it rather than from Standard or Celotex, nor did it account for market factors such as customer preference, product availability, or the independent competitive efforts of those rivals. This Court has repeatedly held that such “but-for” assumptions, unsupported by market data or customer testimony, are insufficient to establish antitrust damages. *See, e.g., In re Lower Lake Erie Iron Ore Antitrust Litig.*, 998 F.2d 1144, 1165 (3d Cir. 1993) (rejecting damages model that “assumed without proof that all sales lost to competitors would have been made by plaintiffs”).

The district court itself recognized the deficiency. In granting remittitur, it found that the jury’s original $2,272,000 award was “unsupported by the evidence and grossly excessive.” *Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc.*, 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994). Yet the court then reduced the antitrust damages to $450,383.60 – a figure that still lacked any evidentiary foundation. The reduced award was simply a fraction of the original, chosen arbitrarily without reference to any proven loss. A remittitur cannot salvage a verdict that rests on no legally sufficient evidence. *See Spence v. Bd. of Educ.*, 513 F.2d 862, 865 (3d Cir. 1975) (remittitur proper only when damages are “clearly excessive” but still supported by some evidence; here, no evidence supported any amount).

Moreover, Stelwagon failed to prove that the price discrimination caused any competitive injury at all. The Robinson-Patman Act requires a showing that the discrimination “may substantially lessen competition” or “injure, destroy, or prevent competition.” 15 U.S.C. § 13(a). In a secondary-line case, the plaintiff must demonstrate that the favored purchasers gained a competitive advantage that harmed the disfavored purchaser. *Falls City Indus., Inc. v. Vanco Beverage, Inc.*, 460 U.S. 428, 435 (1983). Stelwagon offered no evidence that Standard or Celotex undercut its prices or took away its customers. To the contrary, Stelwagon’s own sales of Tarmac MAPs increased during the relevant period. Without proof of actual lost sales or diverted customers, there is no causal link between the discrimination and any claimed damages. *See J. Truett Payne*, 451 U.S. at 565 (reversing damages award where plaintiff “failed to prove that it lost any sales or profits as a result of the discrimination”).

Because Stelwagon’s damages evidence was speculative and failed to establish causation, the district court should have granted Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the antitrust claim. The treble damages award must be vacated.
