**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE ONLY TO INJURIES OCCURRING ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF ITSELF.

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that § 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to injuries occurring on land. The plain text of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test contravenes the statute’s clear geographic limitation.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory coverage under the OCSLA is determined by locale. In *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Supreme Court explained that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Id.* at 219. The Court further noted that § 1333(b) “makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act,” but explicitly declined to extend this provision beyond its geographic scope. *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court’s dictum in *Tallentire* thus supports a situs-of-injury requirement.

The Fifth Circuit has likewise adopted a situs-of-injury test, holding that an injury must occur on the outer Continental Shelf or the waters above it to qualify for OCSLA benefits. *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). The court reasoned that § 1333(b) “incorporates the LHWCA’s coverage provisions, which are themselves limited to injuries occurring on navigable waters or adjoining areas.” *Id.* at 361. This interpretation aligns with the statute’s text and the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Tallentire*.

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is unpersuasive. The *Curtis* court applied a “but for” test, holding that an injury occurring en route to an offshore platform was compensable under OCSLA. *Id.* at 811. However, this approach ignores the statute’s explicit geographic limitation. As the Fifth Circuit noted in *Mills*, the “but for” test “would extend coverage to virtually any injury that could be traced to OCS operations, regardless of where the injury occurred.” 877 F.2d at 361.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is similarly flawed. It reads the phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” to mean that the injury must merely be related to OCS operations, rather than occurring on the OCS itself. This interpretation stretches the statute beyond its plain meaning. The Supreme Court has cautioned against such expansive readings, stating that “general expressions in every opinion are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used.” *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935) (quoting *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821)).

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s test conflicts with the LHWCA’s situs requirement. The LHWCA provides compensation only for injuries occurring “upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The OCSLA incorporates the LHWCA’s coverage provisions, *see* 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b), and thus inherits its geographic limitations. The Ninth Circuit’s decision effectively nullifies this requirement by allowing coverage for injuries occurring on land.

II. THE DECEDENT’S INJURY DID NOT OCCUR ON A MARITIME SITUS UNDER THE LHWCA.

Even if the Court were to reject the situs-of-injury requirement for OCSLA claims, the decedent’s injury did not occur on a maritime situs under the LHWCA. The LHWCA extends coverage only to injuries occurring on “navigable waters” or “adjoining areas customarily used by an employer in loading, unloading, repairing, or building a vessel.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a).

The Ninth Circuit has consistently applied a “functional relationship” test to determine whether a facility qualifies as an “adjoining area.” *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). Under this test, courts consider factors such as the facility’s proximity to navigable waters, its suitability for maritime uses, and whether it is used as an integral part of longshoring operations. *Id.*

The decedent was killed at La Conchita, an onshore oil-processing facility located 250-300 feet from the coast. The facility is separated from the ocean by a highway and railroad tracks and has no direct access to any pier, dock, or loading facility. The Ninth Circuit has held that such a facility is not an adjoining area under § 903(a). *Motoviloff v. Director, Office of Workers’ Comp. Programs*, 692 F.2d 87, 89 (9th Cir. 1982).

The decedent’s work at La Conchita—consolidating scrap metal for eventual removal by third-party dealers—did not transform the facility into a maritime situs. The Supreme Court has held that warehousemen handling cargo within a port are engaged in maritime employment only if they are involved in “intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation.” *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 82-83 (1979). Here, the scrap metal was unloaded at a pier three miles away, loaded into trucks, and driven to La Conchita, where it was stored for up to two years before removal. This process did not involve the “direct” movement of cargo between ship and land transportation. *Id.*

The decedent’s injury thus did not occur on a maritime situs, and the Ninth Circuit erred in reversing the Benefits Review Board’s denial of LHWCA benefits.
