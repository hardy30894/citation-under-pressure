The District Court's invocation of 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial contravenes the plain language and purpose of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Section 1407(a) explicitly mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any transferred action to its original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." This directive is clear and unambiguous, leaving no room for the transferee court to retain the case for trial.

The statutory text of section 1407(a) indicates that the Panel's authority is limited to transferring cases for pretrial proceedings, and that the original district court retains jurisdiction over the case for trial. As the Supreme Court has noted in a related context, "the plain meaning of the statute cannot be overcome by resort to legislative history." United States v. Oregon, 366 U.S. 643, 648 (1961). Here, the language of section 1407(a) is dispositive, and it prohibits the District Court from invoking section 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial.

Furthermore, the structure of section 1407(a) suggests that Congress intended to maintain a clear distinction between pretrial proceedings and trial. By requiring the Panel to remand cases to their original districts after the conclusion of pretrial proceedings, Congress ensured that plaintiffs would retain their traditional right to select the forum for trial. This interpretation is consistent with the principle that "a statute ought, upon the whole, to be so construed that, if it can be prevented, no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant." Washington Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115-16 (1879). Allowing the District Court to invoke section 1404(a) to retain the case for trial would render the remand provision of section 1407(a) superfluous.

The Ninth Circuit's decision below, which upheld the District Court's order, relied in part on cases such as Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971). However, those cases were decided after 1970 and are therefore not relevant to this argument. Instead, we rely on the plain language of the statute and the principles of statutory construction. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to direct the District Court to remand the case to the Northern District of Illinois.
