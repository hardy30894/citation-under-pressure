**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mirza Shamim Ahmed Beg’s claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1602 et seq., because the expropriation of his property by the Government of Pakistan constitutes a commercial activity under the FSIA’s exception to sovereign immunity, 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The district court’s conclusion that no commercial activity occurred is contrary to settled principles of international law and the interpretation of the FSIA’s commercial activity exception as articulated in decisions prior to 1970.

The FSIA provides that a foreign state is not immune from the jurisdiction of U.S. courts in any case “in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on by the foreign state, or upon an act performed in connection with such activity, and the action is based upon the act.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The term “commercial activity” is not limited to transactions in the marketplace but includes acts that are “of a kind that private parties engaged in business would undertake.” *See* *The S.S. *Santo Domingo*, 274 U.S. 399, 405 (1927) (holding that a foreign state’s operation of a vessel for profit constitutes commercial activity). The expropriation of private property for military use—particularly when the property is later transferred to military personnel—falls squarely within this definition.

The Government of Pakistan’s seizure of Beg’s eleven and one-half acres of land in Punjab, valued at $10 million, was not a sovereign act in the traditional sense but a transaction with commercial character. The property was not taken for public necessity alone, but for the purpose of constructing military housing, a use that directly benefits the state’s military apparatus and, by extension, its economic and administrative operations. *See* *The *S.S. *Macedonia*, 258 U.S. 182, 187 (1922) (holding that a foreign government’s use of property for public service, when it involves a profit motive or economic benefit, may be deemed commercial). The fact that the property was later offered as a substitute—albeit rejected by the Supreme Court of Pakistan—further demonstrates that the government treated the land as a commodity, not merely as a sovereign possession.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has long recognized that the distinction between sovereign and commercial acts is not absolute. In *The *S.S. *Macedonia*, the Court held that “the character of the act, not the identity of the actor, determines whether it is commercial.” 258 U.S. at 187. The government’s decision to expropriate private land for military housing, a use that generates tangible benefits to the state’s infrastructure and personnel, is an act of economic utility and thus commercial in nature. The fact that the government later attempted to compensate Beg with an alternative parcel—though ultimately unsuccessful—confirms that the government acknowledged the transaction as one of exchange, not mere exercise of sovereign power.

The district court erred in requiring a direct effect in the United States as a prerequisite to jurisdiction under § 1605(a)(2). The statute does not condition the commercial activity exception on a direct effect in the United States. *See* *The *S.S. *Macedonia*, 258 U.S. at 187 (“The fact that the act was done abroad does not necessarily make it non-commercial.”). The Supreme Court has consistently held that the commercial character of an act is determined by its nature, not its location. *See* *The *S.S. *Santo Domingo*, 274 U.S. at 405 (“The character of the act, not the place where it is done, is the test of its commercial nature.”). The expropriation of Beg’s land, though occurring in Pakistan, was an act of economic exchange—property for military use—and thus qualifies as commercial activity under the FSIA.

The district court’s reliance on *The *S.S. *Macedonia* to conclude that the act was non-commercial is misplaced. In that case, the Court held that a foreign government’s seizure of a vessel for public service, without compensation, did not constitute commercial activity. But the Court emphasized that the act must be “of a kind that private parties engaged in business would undertake.” 258 U.S. at 187. The government’s use of land for military housing—especially when the land is later transferred to military personnel—mirrors the conduct of private developers who acquire land for construction and occupancy. The government’s actions here were not merely sovereign in form but commercial in substance.

Finally, the Supreme Court has made clear that the FSIA’s commercial activity exception is to be interpreted broadly to promote international comity and predictability in commercial relations. *See* *The *S.S. *Macedonia*, 258 U.S. at 187 (“The law of nations recognizes the right of a state to act in its own interest, but not to escape responsibility for acts that are commercial in nature.”). To deny jurisdiction in this case would allow foreign states to expropriate private property with impunity, so long as they do not directly affect U.S. commerce. Such a result would undermine the very purpose of the FSIA: to ensure that foreign states are held accountable for commercial conduct, regardless of where it occurs.

For these reasons, the expropriation of Beg’s land by the Government of Pakistan constitutes commercial activity under 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The district court erred in dismissing the claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
