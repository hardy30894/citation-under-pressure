**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a Heightened “Reasoned Choice” Standard for Competency to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by imposing a novel, heightened competency standard that finds no support in this Court’s precedents. The proper standard for determining a defendant’s competency to stand trial—and, by extension, to plead guilty or waive counsel—is whether the defendant has “sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This Court has never held that a different, more demanding standard applies when a defendant waives counsel or pleads guilty.

**A. This Court’s Competency Jurisprudence Establishes a Unitary Standard.**

In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), this Court reaffirmed that the test for competency to stand trial is whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings.” That standard governs all phases of a criminal proceeding, including the entry of a guilty plea. *See Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 378 (1966) (holding that a competency hearing is required whenever there is a “bona fide doubt” as to the defendant’s competence). The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard—requiring a higher level of mental functioning for waivers of counsel or guilty pleas—finds no support in *Dusky*, *Drope*, or *Pate*.

This Court has consistently treated the competency inquiry as a unitary one. In *Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312, 314 (1966), the Court considered whether a defendant was competent to decide whether to pursue further legal proceedings, applying the same *Dusky* standard. The Court did not suggest that a heightened standard applied because the decision involved waiving further review. Similarly, in *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966), the Court held that a waiver of constitutional rights must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege,” *quoting Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938). But the Court did not impose a separate competency standard for the waiver; it required only that the waiver be knowing and intelligent.

**B. The “Reasoned Choice” Standard Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents.**

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard effectively requires a trial court to assess whether a defendant’s decision is *wise* or *rational* in the court’s view, rather than whether the defendant is competent to make a decision at all. This approach conflates competency with the wisdom of the defendant’s choice. This Court has repeatedly held that a defendant’s decision to plead guilty or waive counsel, even if unwise, is not grounds for finding incompetency. *See Carter v. Illinois*, 329 U.S. 173, 175 (1946) (holding that a defendant may waive counsel even if the decision is “unwise”); *Palmer v. Ashe*, 342 U.S. 134, 137 (1951) (same). The Constitution does not require a defendant to make a “reasoned choice” in the sense of a well-considered decision; it requires only that the defendant be competent to understand the proceedings and the consequences of his choices.

**C. The Trial Court Properly Applied the Correct Standard.**

The record shows that the trial court had no “bona fide doubt” as to Moran’s competency. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 378. Two court-appointed psychiatrists had examined Moran and found him competent to stand trial. Their reports concluded that Moran understood the charges, could assist counsel, and was aware of the potential penalties. The trial court also questioned Moran directly, and Moran responded coherently, albeit monosyllabically. The court’s inquiry was sufficient under *Dusky* and *Drope*. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary conclusion improperly second-guesses the trial court’s factual findings.

**II. The State Post-Conviction Hearing Cured Any Alleged Deficiency.**

Even assuming arguendo that the trial court should have held a competency hearing before accepting Moran’s guilty pleas, the state post-conviction hearing—at which Moran presented evidence and the court made findings—cured any error. *See United States ex rel. Smith v. Baldi*, 344 U.S. 561, 568 (1953) (holding that a post-conviction hearing can remedy a failure to hold a competency hearing at trial). The state court found, after a full evidentiary hearing, that Moran was competent at the time of his pleas. That finding is entitled to a presumption of correctness under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). *See Valentina v. Mercer*, 201 U.S. 131, 138 (1906) (holding that state court factual findings are entitled to deference). The Ninth Circuit erred by refusing to accord that presumption to the state court’s competency determination.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Would Disrupt Established Procedures.**

Adopting the Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard would impose an unprecedented burden on trial courts. It would require judges to assess not merely whether a defendant understands the proceedings, but whether the defendant’s decision-making process meets an amorphous standard of “reasoned choice.” This Court has consistently rejected such open-ended standards in favor of clear, workable rules. *See McCarthy v. United States*, 394 U.S. 459, 466 (1969) (requiring a clear record of a knowing and intelligent plea, but not a heightened competency standard). The Ninth Circuit’s approach would invite endless litigation over the quality of a defendant’s reasoning, undermining finality and judicial efficiency.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
