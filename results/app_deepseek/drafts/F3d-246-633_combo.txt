**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment on Smoot’s RLA Conspiracy Claim.**

Summary judgment is a drastic remedy, appropriate only when no genuine issue of material fact exists. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System*, 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962). The evidence must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). Smoot presented direct evidence—the tape of the executive session—that UTU’s representative and CSX’s representative coordinated to deny his stock claim. That evidence creates a genuine dispute requiring trial.

The Railway Labor Act imposes on a union a duty of fair representation. *Steele v. Louisville & Nashville R.R.*, 323 U.S. 192, 202 (1944); *Tunstall v. Brotherhood of Locomotive Firemen*, 323 U.S. 210, 213 (1944). A conspiracy may be inferred from concerted action and surrounding circumstances. *Interstate Circuit, Inc. v. United States*, 306 U.S. 208, 226–27 (1939). Here, the tape reveals Earley and Emerick discussing Smoot’s claim in a manner that suggests a prearranged plan to defeat it. The district court impermissibly weighed the credibility of the tape and the PLB members’ explanations. Summary judgment was therefore improper.

**II. The District Court Erred in Awarding Damages Under the Wiretap Act.**

The Wiretap Act is a penal statute and must be strictly construed. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820); *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 485 (1917). The district court’s finding of a violation rests on its conclusion that the PLB members had a reasonable expectation of privacy in the executive session. But the Supreme Court has held that the Fourth Amendment protects only objectively reasonable expectations of privacy. *Katz v. United States*, 389 U.S. 347, 360 (1967) (Harlan, J., concurring). The executive session was held in a room where Smoot, a party to the proceeding, had left his tape recorders in plain view. The PLB members knew Smoot had brought recorders to the hearing. Any expectation of privacy was therefore not objectively reasonable. Moreover, a participant in a conversation may record it without violating the Act. *Lopez v. United States*, 373 U.S. 427, 439 (1963). Smoot was a participant in the PLB hearing, and the executive session was part of that hearing.

Even if a violation occurred, the damage award is erroneous. Punitive damages are disfavored and require a showing of actual malice or wrongful intent. *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Ry. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893). The district court awarded $100,000 in punitive damages without any finding of malice. The $250,000 statutory award, combined with punitive damages, constitutes an impermissible double recovery. Finally, attorney’s fees are recoverable only as authorized by statute and must be reasonable. *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717 (1967). The $86,308.74 fee award is unsupported by the record and should be vacated.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded.
