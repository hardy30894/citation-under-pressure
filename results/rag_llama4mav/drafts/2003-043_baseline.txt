The government's argument that it can collect a partnership's tax debt from individual partners without assessing the partners or obtaining a judgment against them is unpersuasive. The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess a tax against a "taxpayer" within three years after the filing of the taxpayer's return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). The Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax," and a "person" includes "an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). As the Ninth Circuit correctly held, both the partnership and the individual partners are separate "taxpayers" under the Code. Galletti v. United States (In re G. Galletti), 298 F.3d 1107, 1111 (9th Cir.2002). Therefore, the IRS's assessment against the partnership did not extend the statute of limitations for collecting the tax debt from the individual partners.

The government's reliance on Young v. Riddell is misplaced. In Young, the district court held that a partner was not entitled to a refund of taxes paid because the partner was liable for the partnership's tax debt under state law. 60-1 U.S. Tax Cas. (CCH) ¶ 9831, at 76,054 (S.D.Cal.1959). However, the court also acknowledged that the IRS would have had to either assess the tax against the partner or bring an action to collect the tax in order to collect the debt. Id. at 76,050. The Ninth Circuit's affirmance of the district court's decision in Young did not reject this interpretation. Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir.1960).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in United States v. Coson further supports the respondent's position. In Coson, the court held that the IRS's failure to assess a tax against an individual partner rendered its attempt to collect the tax from the partner improper. 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir.1961). Although the government attempts to distinguish Coson on the grounds that it involved a challenge to a tax lien, the validity of the lien depended on an underlying assessment against the partner. Id.

Under California law, a creditor may not collect a partnership debt from an individual partner without first obtaining a judgment against the partner. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The IRS did not obtain a judgment against the individual partners, and the statute of limitations for doing so has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a). Therefore, the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS's claims against the individual partners.

The government's argument that it can collect the partnership's tax debt from the individual partners under state law is unavailing. While state law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, it also requires a creditor to obtain a judgment against the partner before collecting from the partner's assets. Cal. Corp.Code § 16307(c). The IRS failed to obtain such a judgment, and the statute of limitations has expired.

In conclusion, the IRS's failure to assess the individual partners or obtain a judgment against them renders its claims against the partners unenforceable. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS's claims, and the Ninth Circuit's decision should be affirmed.
