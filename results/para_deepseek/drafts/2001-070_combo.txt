**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Death Penalty Is Not Cruel and Unusual Punishment Within the Original Meaning of the Eighth Amendment.**

The Cruel and Unusual Punishments Clause is directed against "atrocious" and "inhuman" punishments, such as "burning at the stake, crucifixion, breaking on the wheel, or the like." *Wilkerson v. Utah*, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878). In *In re Kemmler*, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890), this Court explained that "the punishment of death is not cruel within the meaning of that word as used in the constitution." The Clause "implies there something inhuman and barbarous, something more than the mere extinguishment of life." *Id.* A lawful sentence of death, imposed after a full trial, does not fall within that description. *See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) (rejecting an Eighth Amendment challenge to a second execution attempt because the "cruelty against which the Constitution protects a convicted man is cruelty inherent in the method of punishment").

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Exempt Classes of Offenders from a Lawful Sentence.**

The Amendment's guarantee is one of proportionality between punishment and offense. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910) ("It is a precept of justice that punishment for crime should be graduated and proportioned to offense."). The crime here is capital murder—the most serious offense in the Commonwealth's code—and the sentence is death, a penalty this Court has never held unconstitutional. *Wilkerson*, 99 U.S. at 135–36. The Clause requires proportionality to the *offense*, not to the *offender*. Nothing in the text or history of the Amendment suggests that a particular class of defendants must be categorically immune from a punishment otherwise lawfully imposed.

**III. Mental Retardation Is an Issue of Fact for the Jury, Not a Constitutional Prohibition on Punishment.**

At common law, a defendant's mental capacity was a question of fact for the jury, not a constitutional bar to sentence. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 486 (1895) (holding that the question of sanity is "one of fact" for the jury). Likewise, in capital cases, juries have long been permitted to consider "the character of the crime and the circumstances of the offender" in determining whether mercy is appropriate. *Winston v. United States*, 172 U.S. 303, 313 (1899). Here, the jury heard Atkins's own psychologist testify that he was mentally retarded, and it heard the Commonwealth's rebuttal. The jury was free to weigh that evidence and nonetheless impose death. The Eighth Amendment does not substitute this Court's judgment for the jury's factfinding.

**IV. The Sentence Is Not "Unusual" Within the Meaning of the Clause.**

A punishment is "unusual" only if it departs from the established modes of punishment under the common law. *O'Neil v. Vermont*, 144 U.S. 323, 332 (1892) (Field, J., dissenting) (describing the Clause as prohibiting punishments "not warranted by law" or "contrary to the common law"). Capital punishment was a well-established punishment at common law and remained so under the laws of the states at the time of the Amendment's adoption. *Wilkerson*, 99 U.S. at 137. The Virginia statute under which Atkins was sentenced is no novelty; it is the traditional punishment for premeditated murder.

**V. "Evolving Standards of Decency" Do Not Compel a New Constitutional Rule.**

This Court has observed that the Eighth Amendment draws its meaning from "the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). But *Trop* involved denationalization as a punishment for wartime desertion, not capital punishment, and the Court explicitly reaffirmed that "the death penalty" is a constitutionally permissible punishment. *Id.* at 99. The determination of contemporary standards belongs to the legislature, which may revise the law if it concludes that the death penalty should not apply to mentally retarded offenders. The Virginia General Assembly has not made that choice, and nothing in the Constitution requires this Court to make it for the legislature.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
