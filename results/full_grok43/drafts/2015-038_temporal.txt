The Property Clause vests Congress with power to make all needful rules respecting federal property. U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2; Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897) (“the power vested in the United States to protect its property”). That authority extends to regulation of activities on or affecting lands within federal reservations. Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 536-537 (1911); United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 516-517 (1911).

Congress likewise possesses plenary control over navigable waters under the Commerce Clause. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 189-190 (1824). This power reaches “every species of commercial intercourse” and “comprehends navigation within the limits of every State.” The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). Federal authority over such waters is not diminished by state ownership of the bed. United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co., 229 U.S. 53, 62 (1913).

ANILCA’s text does not withdraw these established powers. Where a statute is silent on displacement of preexisting federal authority, the ordinary presumption is that Congress did not intend such displacement. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 426 (1819). The statute’s reference to “public lands” therefore leaves untouched the independent sources of federal regulatory jurisdiction over navigable waters and over conduct that affects federal reservations. See Minnesota Rate Cases, 230 U.S. 352, 399 (1913) (federal power over navigation “is not to be destroyed or impaired” by state claims).

Because the hovercraft prohibition rests on these longstanding constitutional foundations rather than on any ANILCA grant, the statute does not prohibit its application within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve.
