**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when evaluating a defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights by pleading guilty or discharging counsel.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that competency to waive fundamental constitutional rights demands more than the minimal showing required for competency to stand trial. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987), cert. denied, 488 U.S. 871 (1988); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981). This Court has long recognized that the Constitution protects a defendant’s right to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of counsel and to plead guilty. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 242–43 (1969). Because such waivers implicate the most basic protections of the adversarial system, due process requires a heightened inquiry into the defendant’s capacity to exercise reasoned choice among available alternatives. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986).

The competency standard for waiving rights is not satisfied by a mere showing that the defendant can understand the nature of the proceedings or assist counsel. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (defining competency to stand trial). Rather, the defendant must possess the “mental capacity to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him.” *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (quoting *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1983)); *Chavez*, 656 F.2d at 518. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the waiver of constitutional rights is not valid unless it is the product of a competent decision. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399–400 (1993) (citing *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806, 835 (1975)).

II. The trial court erred by failing to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte when substantial evidence raised a good-faith doubt about Moran’s ability to make a reasoned choice.

Due process requires a trial court to initiate a competency hearing whenever it has or should have a good-faith doubt about the defendant’s competency to waive constitutional rights. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d at 1480; *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973). A good-faith doubt arises when there is substantial evidence of incompetence. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856 (citing *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217, 1223 (9th Cir. 1982), cert. denied, 456 U.S. 946 (1982)).

In this case, the record contained substantial evidence triggering such a doubt. Moran had recently attempted suicide and was taking multiple medications, including sedatives and antiepileptics, at the time of his plea. *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 267–68 (9th Cir. 1992). His responses at the plea hearing were monosyllabic, and he expressed a desire to prevent the presentation of mitigating evidence—an objective inconsistent with reasoned decision-making. *Id.* at 267. Despite these red flags, the trial court made no inquiry into Moran’s capacity to understand the consequences of his decisions or to make a reasoned choice. *Id.*

This Court has recognized that psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine competency to stand trial do not address the higher standard of competency to waive constitutional rights. *Godinez*, 509 U.S. at 399–400. The Nevada courts compounded the error by relying on such evaluations to assess Moran’s competency to waive counsel and plead guilty. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 271–72. The state court’s post-conviction ruling applied the wrong legal standard and failed to consider whether Moran possessed the capacity for reasoned choice. *Id.* at 270–72.

III. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the state court’s failure to hold a competency hearing violated due process and that Moran is entitled to federal habeas relief.

Under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), a state court’s factual findings are presumed correct only if they are supported by a written opinion or other reliable indicia. The Nevada Supreme Court’s post-conviction order did not apply the correct legal standard and thus does not warrant deference. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963); *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 270–71. Moreover, this Court has held that the voluntariness of a waiver is a mixed question of law and fact subject to independent review. *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 116–17 (1985). The Ninth Circuit properly conducted de novo review and determined that the record did not support a finding that Moran was competent to make a reasoned choice. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 266, 272–73.

The state’s failure to hold a competency hearing when substantial evidence raised a good-faith doubt violated due process. *Harding*, 834 F.2d at 856; *Sieling*, 478 F.2d at 214. The Ninth Circuit’s judgment should be affirmed.
