**Argument**

A state prisoner may not rely on a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was fairly presented to the state courts, or the petitioner separately shows cause and prejudice for that default. That rule is the necessary application of this Court’s exhaustion and procedural-default precedents, which require respect for state process and give the state courts the first opportunity to address federal constitutional claims. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 447-48, 485-86 (1953); *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950); *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944); *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87-91 (1977).

The exhaustion doctrine is foundational. A petitioner must “fairly present” the federal claim to the state courts before seeking federal habeas relief. *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971); *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447-48 (1965). The reason is comity: state courts must have the first chance to correct alleged constitutional errors. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 447-48. If a petitioner could invoke a defaulted ineffectiveness claim as cause, he would evade exhaustion by indirection and nullify the state’s opportunity to adjudicate the attorney-error claim in the first instance.

That is exactly what *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), forecloses. The Court held that attorney error can constitute cause only when it amounts to constitutionally ineffective assistance under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). But the Court also stated that “the exhaustion doctrine” requires an ineffective-assistance claim to be presented to the state courts “as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause.” *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 489. That is the controlling principle here. A defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse another default unless the petitioner first complied with state procedures for the ineffective-assistance claim or otherwise established cause and prejudice for its default.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would collapse the distinction between cause and default. Under that theory, a prisoner could default an underlying constitutional claim, default the ineffectiveness claim said to explain that default, and still obtain federal review by layering one forfeiture on top of another. This Court has repeatedly rejected such an approach. *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 87-91; *Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 493-94 (1976); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947). Habeas review is not a device for bypassing state procedural rules whenever a petitioner can repackage a missed claim as attorney error.

The older right-to-counsel cases do not support petitioner either. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938), concerned waiver of the constitutional right to counsel. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516-17 (1962), applied the waiver principle where the record did not show a valid relinquishment. Neither case holds that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim may automatically excuse another default. The controlling framework came later in *Strickland* and *Carrier*: ineffective assistance is a constitutional claim judged by its own standards, 466 U.S. at 687, 694, and, before it may serve as cause, it must itself be properly presented to state court, 477 U.S. at 489.

Finality and regularity reinforce the same result. Procedural default rules protect the States’ interest in orderly adjudication and prevent habeas from becoming a substitute for direct review. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 485-86; *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 88-91. If a defaulted ineffectiveness claim could always serve as cause, state timeliness and presentation rules would be optional in practice. A prisoner would need only assert after the fact that counsel should have raised the omitted issue, regardless of whether that ineffectiveness theory was ever fairly presented below. That would undercut *Picard*, *Darr*, *Hawk*, and *Carrier* alike.

The rule is therefore straightforward. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the ineffectiveness claim itself was exhausted and preserved, or the petitioner independently establishes cause and prejudice for that default. Because the contrary rule would defeat exhaustion, comity, and procedural-default doctrine, the judgment below should be affirmed.
