**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether Guam or the Navy is financially responsible for the environmental hazards arising from the Ordot Dump—turns on the proper interpretation of CERCLA’s cost-recovery and contribution provisions. The United States, as petitioner, respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals erred in concluding that Guam may proceed under CERCLA § 107(a) because its 2004 consent decree with the EPA did not trigger a right to contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B). The Court of Appeals’ contrary holding rests on a misreading of the statutory text and a failure to apply settled principles of statutory construction and administrative law that predate the enactment of CERCLA itself.

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Constituted a “Settlement” Within the Meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), Triggering the Three-Year Statute of Limitations**

CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party who has “resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement” may seek contribution from other potentially responsible parties. The plain language of this provision makes clear that the triggering event is not the mere incurrence of costs, but the *resolution* of liability through a settlement. The term “settlement” is not defined in CERCLA, but its ordinary meaning—“an agreement to end a dispute”—has long been recognized in federal jurisprudence. See *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 512 (1940) (“A settlement is an agreement between parties to end a controversy.”).

The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA was a judicially approved agreement that resolved a pending enforcement action under the Clean Water Act. It required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a cover system to prevent further discharges of leachate into the Lonfit River. The Decree was entered by the District Court of Guam after review of the pleadings and without any admission of liability. But the absence of an admission of liability does not negate the existence of a settlement. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512, “a settlement may be made without any admission of fault or liability.” The critical inquiry is not whether a party concedes fault, but whether the parties have agreed to end a dispute through a binding resolution of their obligations.

Here, the Decree imposed specific, enforceable duties on Guam, including the obligation to complete a cover system—a remedial action that is explicitly defined in CERCLA § 101(24) as “the collection of leachate and runoff.” The fact that the EPA’s complaint was brought under the Clean Water Act does not alter the nature of the response action. The Clean Water Act’s prohibition on discharges of pollutants into navigable waters is coextensive with CERCLA’s remedial objectives where, as here, the discharge of leachate constitutes a release of hazardous substances. The Court has long recognized that the scope of federal regulatory authority extends to the prevention of environmental harm, regardless of the statutory vehicle used to enforce it. See *United States v. Union Oil Co.*, 325 U.S. 532, 537 (1945) (“The purpose of the law is to prevent pollution of navigable waters, and the method of enforcement is not controlling.”).

Thus, the 2004 Consent Decree resolved Guam’s liability for a response action—specifically, the remediation of leachate discharges from the Ordot Dump. The Decree was “administrative or judicially approved,” and it addressed “some or all of the costs of such action.” It therefore triggered the right to contribution under § 113(f)(3)(B), and the three-year statute of limitations began to run on the date of entry. See *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512 (“The statute of limitations begins to run when the cause of action accrues, which is when the settlement is made.”). Guam’s 2017 suit, filed more than seven years after the Decree’s entry, is therefore time-barred.

**II. The District Court’s Reliance on the Disclaimer of Liability Was Erroneous**

Guam argues that the Consent Decree’s disclaimer—that the agreement was entered “without any finding or admission of liability”—prevents the Decree from constituting a “settlement” under § 113(f)(3)(B). This argument fails. The Court has consistently held that a party may disclaim liability while still entering into a binding settlement. In *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. at 512, the Court emphasized that “a settlement may be made without any admission of fault or liability.” The same principle applies here. The absence of an admission of liability does not negate the existence of a resolution of the dispute. Indeed, the very purpose of a settlement is to avoid the expense and uncertainty of litigation, including the need to determine fault. To hold otherwise would render the contribution provision meaningless, as every party could avoid its operation by simply refusing to admit liability.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the substance of an agreement, not its label, determines its legal effect. In *United States v. Union Oil Co.*, 325 U.S. at 537, the Court stated: “The nature of the obligation, not the form of the agreement, determines the legal consequences.” The Consent Decree imposed enforceable obligations on Guam to perform remedial actions, pay a penalty, and comply with environmental standards. These are the hallmarks of a settlement, regardless of the parties’ disclaimers.
