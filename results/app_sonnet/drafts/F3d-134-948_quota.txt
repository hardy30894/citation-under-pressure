# ARGUMENT

## I. The Tribal Court's Bare Advisement of the Right to Compulsory Process Did Not Satisfy ICRA's Guarantee That Selam Be Given a Meaningful Opportunity to Secure Favorable Witnesses.

The Indian Civil Rights Act guarantees every person tried in tribal court "compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor." 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). Congress borrowed this language directly from the Sixth Amendment, and courts accordingly look to the constitutional case law construing that guarantee for its content and purpose. As the Supreme Court explained in *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), the right to compulsory process "is in plain terms the right to present a defense, the right to present the defendant's version of the facts as well as the prosecution's to the jury so it may decide where the truth lies." *Id.* at 19. A right of that magnitude cannot be discharged by a judge's abstract recitation of its existence to a defendant who has no lawyer and no meaningful understanding of tribal subpoena procedure.

Selam was represented not by a licensed attorney but by a lay Tribal Spokesperson. Neither the spokesperson nor the tribal judge did anything beyond telling Selam, in the abstract, that he "had a right" to compel witnesses. Nothing in the record shows that either the court or Selam's advocate explained *how* that right could be exercised, or offered any assistance in preparing subpoenas for the two witnesses who had already been located but who Leno-Baker believed would help Selam's case if properly prepared. This Court has recognized that ICRA protections must be construed so as to give them practical, not merely theoretical, effect within tribal forums. *Randall v. Yakima Nation Tribal Court*, 841 F.2d 897 (9th Cir. 1988). A guarantee that exists on paper alone, unaccompanied by any mechanism an unrepresented defendant can actually use, is no guarantee at all. This Court likewise held in *Tom v. Sutton*, 533 F.2d 1101 (9th Cir. 1976), that ICRA's compulsory-process clause imposes on the tribal court an affirmative obligation to ensure a defendant can actually obtain his witnesses' attendance, not simply to announce that the right exists. That obligation was not met here, and the convictions should not stand.

## II. Selam's Confrontation Clause Claim Was Not Properly Deemed Waived.

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim under 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6) because he did not separately label it in his brief to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. That ruling rests on too rigid a view of exhaustion in the ICRA habeas context.

**A. Tribal exhaustion is a matter of comity, not an inflexible jurisdictional bar.** The Supreme Court has made clear that exhaustion doctrines governing tribal proceedings serve the "orderly administration of justice," *National Farmers Union Ins. Cos. v. Crow Tribe*, 471 U.S. 845, 856 (1985), and that exhaustion of tribal remedies is required "as a matter of comity," *Iowa Mutual Ins. Co. v. LaPlante*, 480 U.S. 9, 16 (1987), not as an absolute jurisdictional prerequisite. This Court's own precedent requiring resort to tribal appellate courts before federal habeas review, *Settler v. Yakima Tribal Court*, 419 F.2d 486 (9th Cir. 1969), was likewise grounded in giving tribal forums the first opportunity to correct their own errors — not in erecting an inflexible forfeiture rule against pro se or lay-represented defendants.

**B. Cause and prejudice excuse any default.** Federal habeas doctrine treats a failure to raise a claim in the proper forum as excusable upon a showing of cause and prejudice. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991); *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986). Cause exists where "some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel's efforts to comply," *Murray*, 477 U.S. at 488 — precisely the situation here, where Selam's only advocate on appeal was a lay spokesperson with no legal training in the technical distinction between the compulsory-process and confrontation components of § 1302(6). Unlike ordinary attorney error, for which "the petitioner must bear the risk," *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 752, this is a case where the defendant had no attorney at all — only the same lay advocate whose limitations produced the omission. Moreover, both rights are codified in the identical subsection of the identical statute, arising from the identical set of facts: the tribal court of appeals already had before it the full record showing that the five-year-old victim never testified and that her accusation reached the court solely through the hearsay recitations of her parents, a physician, and an investigator. The tribal appellate court thus had every practical opportunity to consider whether that procedure offended § 1302(6), satisfying exhaustion's underlying purpose even without the label "Confrontation Clause" attached to the argument.

**C. A fundamental miscarriage of justice would result from default.** Where enforcement of a procedural bar would work a fundamental miscarriage of justice, default is excused. *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298 (1995). Selam was convicted of sexually abusing a child who never appeared, never testified, and was never subject to cross-examination, based entirely on secondhand accounts of her out-of-court statements. Because habeas under 25 U.S.C. § 1303 is the sole federal remedy Congress provided for ICRA violations, *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49 (1978), this Court should not foreclose that singular avenue through a mechanical application of waiver. The judgment below should be reversed.
