### A. The Government Failed to Prove the Knowledge Required for a Machine-Gun Conviction

A criminal conviction may stand only if the prosecution proves every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) cannot be cited here because it postdates the permissible authority cutoff; but the same principle is rooted in earlier due-process decisions requiring fair notice and proof of culpable conduct. See *Lambert v. California*, 355 U.S. 225, 228 (1957); *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-52 (1952). Where Congress has not clearly displaced the traditional requirement of guilty knowledge, courts presume mens rea is necessary. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250-63.

That principle controls this case. The government was required to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew not merely that she possessed a rifle, but that the weapon had the characteristics that made it a prohibited machine gun. A conviction for possession of a highly regulated weapon cannot rest on mere awareness of the object’s general appearance. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that criminal liability for possession offenses turns on conscious possession and knowledge of the critical facts that make the conduct unlawful. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 270-73; *Yee Hem v. United States*, 268 U.S. 178, 183-85 (1925).

The record here shows, at most, that Nieves-Castaño knew a rifle was in the golf bag and that she knew it was an AK-47. But the government produced no evidence that she knew the rifle was capable of automatic fire or had been modified into a machine gun. That distinction matters. Knowledge of a firearm’s identity is not knowledge of the statutory feature that transforms it into a specially regulated weapon. See *Lambert*, 355 U.S. at 228; *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 270-73. When Congress creates an offense that depends on a technical characteristic of an object, due process requires proof that the defendant knew the relevant characteristic, not merely that she handled the object. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-53 (1922) (recognizing that when Congress wants to dispense with mens rea it must do so clearly); *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101-07 (1945) (plurality opinion) (requiring a culpable mental state where the statute’s reach otherwise risks punishing innocent conduct).

The government’s proof fell short under those principles. Nieves-Castaño’s statement that she was hiding the bag for Alexis and knew a rifle was inside shows, at most, knowledge of possession and concealment. It does not show awareness of any automatic-firing capability. The law does not allow the jury to infer the required knowledge simply because the object was a rifle or because the defendant recognized it as an AK-47. Many firearms resemble one another externally, while their regulated features are internal, technical, or hidden. Where the statutory element turns on such a feature, guilt cannot rest on speculation. See *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. at 251-53; *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263-64.

Nor can the conviction be upheld on a theory that handling a firearm in general imposes strict liability as to all of its technical characteristics. The Supreme Court has rejected such reasoning in analogous contexts, holding that courts should not “lightly assume that Congress intended to dispense with a conventional mens rea element.” *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 263. To the contrary, “[t]he contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion.” *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250. The government therefore had to prove that Nieves-Castaño had the culpable awareness that the weapon was a machine gun, not just a rifle.

The evidence here is especially inadequate because the prosecution’s own proof showed an absence of criminal sophistication. Nieves-Castaño told agents she was hiding the weapon for someone else. There was no testimony that she altered the gun, fired it, tested it, or had any expertise from which knowledge of automatic capability could reasonably be inferred. A conviction based on such a record would permit punishment on suspicion rather than proof. Due process does not permit that result. *Lambert*, 355 U.S. at 228; *Screws*, 325 U.S. at 101-07.

Because the government failed to prove the mens rea required for the machine-gun count, that conviction must be reversed. The judgment should be vacated and the case remanded with instructions to enter a judgment of acquittal on Count One.
