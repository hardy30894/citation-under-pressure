# ARGUMENT

## ARIZONA'S RESTRICTION ON THE USE OF MENTAL-DISEASE EVIDENCE TO NEGATE CRIMINAL INTENT DOES NOT VIOLATE DUE PROCESS

### A. States Possess Broad Authority to Define the Elements of Crimes and Structure the Presentation of Defenses

This Court has long recognized that "the definition of the elements of a criminal offense is entrusted to the legislature, particularly in the case of federal crimes, which are solely creatures of statute." Liparota v. United States, 471 U.S. 419, 424 (1985). This principle applies with equal, if not greater, force to state criminal law, where "preventing and dealing with crime is much more the business of the States than it is of the Federal Government." Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201 (1977). In Patterson, this Court upheld New York's allocation of the burden of proving an affirmative defense to the defendant, explaining that due process does not require States "to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact, the existence or nonexistence of which it is willing to recognize as an exculpatory or mitigating circumstance." Id. at 207.

Arizona's channeling rule—permitting mental-disease evidence to be considered on the insanity defense but not on the mens rea element itself—is a permissible exercise of this same legislative authority to structure how such evidence bears on criminal responsibility. As this Court held in Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), a state may exclude evidence of a defendant's mental condition (there, voluntary intoxication) from consideration on the mens rea element without offending due process, because "the doctrine of stare decisis is not rigid enough to require us to place all the weight of foundation on this historical footnote" and because "the *mens rea* requirement" is itself a matter of "state prerogative." Id. at 56 (plurality opinion). The plurality in Egelhoff recognized that the exclusion of exculpatory evidence for one purpose does not offend due process merely because that evidence might otherwise have raised a reasonable doubt as to intent.

### B. Arizona's Rule Reflects a Longstanding and Widely Accepted Distinction Between Insanity and Mens Rea

Arizona's approach is consistent with this Court's historical treatment of the insanity defense as conceptually and procedurally distinct from the prosecution's mens rea burden. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), this Court upheld a state rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process required a different allocation. The Court explained that "the science of psychiatry has made tremendous strides since that time, but the problem of insanity and criminal responsibility has remained exceedingly difficult for the psychiatrist, and no less so for the lawyer." Id. at 800-01. This recognition of the law's legitimate skepticism toward psychiatric testimony on ultimate questions of responsibility supports Arizona's decision to cabin such evidence to the insanity inquiry, where the legislature has determined it is most reliably weighed under a structured burden-shifting framework.

The Court reaffirmed this principle in Clark v. Arizona's companion reasoning in Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), where the plurality declined to constitutionalize a particular understanding of mental disease and its relationship to criminal responsibility, noting that "the doctrine of *mens rea*, the insanity defense, [and] the whole history of the evolution of the collateral defenses" reflect an area where "this Court has never expressed the view" that due process fixes a single constitutional approach. Id. at 535-36. Arizona's line-drawing is precisely the sort of policy judgment about causation, responsibility, and the reliability of psychiatric evidence that Powell left to state legislatures.

### C. The Arizona Rule Serves Legitimate State Interests in Reliable Fact-Finding

Arizona's channeling rule serves the substantial interest identified in Egelhoff—avoiding jury confusion between distinct legal concepts and preventing the mens rea inquiry from becoming a de facto insanity determination without the corresponding burden of proof. As the Egelhoff plurality observed, a state may reasonably conclude that "outside the criminal justice system, [] intoxication is not a defense to civil liability" and analogous reasoning applies to organize how mental-disease evidence functions procedurally. 518 U.S. at 49-50. Arizona's Mott rule, prohibiting use of mental-disease testimony to directly negate mens rea while permitting observation testimony about the defendant's specific behavior, reflects precisely this kind of considered channeling, not an arbitrary exclusion of exculpatory evidence.

This Court's decision in Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), confirms the appropriate standard: state procedural rules governing the insanity defense violate due process only if they "offend some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." Id. at 445 (internal quotation marks omitted). Given the divergent approaches among the States to the relationship between mental illness and mens rea—some following Arizona's model, others treating mental-disease evidence as freely admissible on intent—no such fundamental, historically rooted right exists. As Medina recognized, "the analogy to the shifting of the burden of proof… is not apt" where, as here, the State merely channels rather than eliminates the defendant's opportunity to present his evidence. Id. at 449.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Arizona's restriction on the use of mental-disease evidence does not violate the Due Process Clause, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
