**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Holding, as a Matter of Law, that Erdman Failed to Accumulate 1,250 Hours of Service.**

An employee is eligible for FMLA leave only if she "has been employed for at least 1,250 hours of service with such employer during the previous 12-month period." 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). Whether an employee has met that threshold is ordinarily a question of fact, and summary judgment is improper where the record permits conflicting inferences. See McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009) (summary judgment appropriate only where no genuine issue of material fact exists).

The District Court treated Nationwide's official time records as conclusive, but those records do not capture the full extent of Erdman's labor. For years, Erdman's direct supervisor, Patty Sarno, authorized payment for Erdman's extra hours or permitted her to bank them as "comp" time. That practice did not cease the moment Erdman moved to a four-day schedule; rather, the record shows continued ambiguity about whether "comp" time remained available, culminating in Erdman's September 2002 e-mail asking her new supervisor, Stella Getgen, for clarification—a request that went unanswered. Getgen did not expressly revoke the practice until February 10, 2003, well within the relevant twelve-month look-back period. A reasonable jury could conclude that Erdman continued to perform, and Nationwide continued to permit, compensable work beyond what appears in the company's formal ledgers.

This matters because the 1,250-hour inquiry is not confined to an employer's internal bookkeeping. Time an employee is "suffered or permitted" to work is compensable, and an employer cannot shield itself from FMLA liability by failing to record hours it knew, or should have known, an employee was performing. Erdman's evidence—Sarno's testimony regarding regular extra hours, Getgen's own admission that she did not object to Erdman's "comp" time until January 2003, and the ambiguity surrounding the September 2002 e-mail—creates a genuine dispute as to whether Erdman crossed the statutory threshold. The District Court's decision to resolve that dispute on summary judgment, rather than submit it to a jury, was error. See McTernan, 564 F.3d at 646 (courts must view the facts in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party).

**II. Even If a Shortfall in Hours Existed, Nationwide Is Equitably Estopped from Asserting Erdman's Ineligibility.**

Even accepting, arguendo, that Erdman fell short of 1,250 hours, Nationwide should be barred from relying on that shortfall because it affirmatively represented to Erdman that she qualified for FMLA leave, and Erdman detrimentally relied on that representation.

This Court has not yet addressed whether equitable estoppel may be invoked against an employer who misrepresents an employee's FMLA eligibility, but other courts of appeals confronting the question have concluded that it may. The Second Circuit, for example, has held that where an employer's own conduct leads an employee reasonably to believe she is entitled to FMLA leave, the employer may be estopped from later asserting the 1,250-hour requirement as a defense. Kosakow v. New Rochelle Radiology Assocs., P.C., 274 F.3d 706, 725 (2d Cir. 2001). The Fifth Circuit has likewise recognized that equitable estoppel can apply to FMLA eligibility determinations where an employer's representations induce reasonable and detrimental reliance. Minard v. ITC Deltacom Commc'ns, Inc., 447 F.3d 352, 359 (5th Cir. 2006).

The facts here fit comfortably within that framework. When Erdman submitted her FMLA paperwork on April 21, 2003, a Nationwide human resources employee reviewed the request and told her, "as far as the FMLA, I probably don't see any problems with this." Erdman had every reason to rely on that representation: it came from the department charged with administering FMLA compliance, at the very moment Nationwide was processing her leave request. Erdman took no steps to seek alternative arrangements—such as insisting on her previously approved August vacation—because she believed her FMLA leave request already resolved the conflict. Instead, she proceeded under the reasonable belief that her job was protected, only to be terminated two-and-a-half weeks later, before her leave period even began.

Nationwide cannot now disown the very assurance its own human resources office gave to induce Erdman's reliance. To permit an employer to represent eligibility, watch an employee forgo alternative remedies in reliance on that representation, and then assert ineligibility as a shield against liability would undermine the FMLA's remedial purpose and reward exactly the kind of inconsistent conduct equitable estoppel exists to prevent. See Kosakow, 274 F.3d at 725; Minard, 447 F.3d at 359.

At minimum, whether the elements of estoppel—misrepresentation, reasonable reliance, and resulting prejudice—are satisfied on this record presents a factual question inappropriate for summary disposition. The District Court never reached this issue because it treated the 1,250-hour requirement as an absolute bar. That was error.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the District Court's grant of summary judgment on Erdman's FMLA claims and remand for trial, including consideration of whether Nationwide is equitably estopped from denying Erdman's eligibility.
