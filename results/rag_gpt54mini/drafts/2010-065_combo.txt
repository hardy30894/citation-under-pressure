Respondent’s first submission is that Bond lacks standing to press a freestanding Tenth Amendment objection to her conviction. The Supreme Court has long held that private litigants ordinarily may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to contest federal action absent a concrete injury distinct from the generalized interest in constitutional governance. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), the Court rejected private utilities’ Tenth Amendment challenge and explained that, “absent the states or their officers,” they had “no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144. That holding controls here.

Bond is not the State of Pennsylvania, nor any state officer, nor a sovereign representative asserting injury to state prerogatives. She is a criminal defendant seeking to invalidate her own conviction after a grand jury charged her under a federal criminal statute. The Supreme Court has repeatedly treated such objections as questions going to the validity of the prosecution, not a personal constitutional entitlement of the accused. In Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the Court observed that “the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States.” Id. at 109. That allocation describes institutional federalism; it does not confer on an individual defendant a personal right to demand that the federal government prove, in addition to an offense’s statutory elements, a separate “federal interest” element.

The standing defect is reinforced by the Court’s treatment of analogous constitutional objections in other contexts. In Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), the Court explained that incorporation arguments must be grounded in a constitutional guarantee applicable to the litigant, not merely in a structural claim that the State has exceeded its powers. Id. at 597-98. Likewise, in Tennessee Electric, the Court dismissed the utilities’ effort to litigate the Tenth Amendment as a private right. 306 U.S. at 143-44. Those decisions recognize that structural provisions protect sovereign allocations of authority, and the proper parties to vindicate those allocations are the sovereigns themselves.

Bond’s attempt to recast the issue as a personal defense also fails under the Court’s criminal-procedure cases. The Court has repeatedly held that a defendant may challenge whether the charged conduct falls within the statute the court administers; “the court may err, but it has jurisdiction of the question.” Serra v. Mortiga, 204 U.S. 470 (1907). But that is different from asserting that Congress lacked power to enact the statute in the first place. The former is a merits objection to the charge; the latter is a structural claim that belongs, if at all, to the sovereign whose powers are allegedly invaded. See Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 144; Screws, 325 U.S. at 109.

Nor does the fact that Bond was prosecuted in federal court create standing. Jurisdiction over federal crimes is conferred by statute, and once Congress has defined a federal offense, the district court is competent to adjudicate whether the indictment states that offense. See Serra, 204 U.S. at 474-75. Bond’s complaint is not that § 229 failed to define criminal conduct, but that Congress should not have criminalized her conduct at all because it allegedly intrudes on state police powers. That is precisely the sort of generalized federalism objection the Court rejected in Tennessee Electric.

The cases Bond might invoke do not help her. In Giaccio v. Pennsylvania, 382 U.S. 399 (1965), and Davis v. North Carolina, 384 U.S. 737 (1966), the Court addressed direct personal constitutional claims—vagueness and voluntariness of confessions—where the challenged rule operated on the defendant herself. Those decisions say nothing about standing to assert the Tenth Amendment. And in Haynes v. Washington, 373 U.S. 503 (1963), Heller v. Connecticut, 389 U.S. 902 (1967), Jenkins v. McKeithen, 395 U.S. 411 (1969), and Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), the Court addressed individual rights in criminal or quasi-criminal proceedings, not sovereign-federalism claims.

The better reading of the precedents is also the one adopted by several courts of appeals prior to 1970’s close: private litigants may not transform the Tenth Amendment into a personal defense to prosecution. That is consistent with the Court’s repeated insistence that federalism is a matter of constitutional allocation between governments, not a free-standing individual immunity from criminal liability. See Tennessee Electric, 306 U.S. at 144; Screws, 325 U.S. at 109; Maxwell, 176 U.S. at 597-98.

Finally, even if Bond could identify some cognizable injury, her claim would still fail because the conviction rests on a federal criminal statute enacted to implement a treaty obligation, and the district court had full authority to adjudicate the validity of the charge. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 432-33 (1920). But the Court need not reach that issue if it concludes, as it should, that Bond lacks standing to assert the Tenth Amendment on her own behalf. Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118, 143-44 (1939).
