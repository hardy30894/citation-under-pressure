**ARGUMENT**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the State acquired title to the riverbeds at issue if, and only if, the relevant stretches of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time Montana entered the Union in 1889. That is the settled constitutional test for title under the equal footing doctrine. See *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75-77 (1931); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935); *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 34-35 (1997). The trial court therefore applied the correct legal standard.

1. **Title to riverbeds turns on navigability at statehood.**  
Under the equal footing doctrine, a new State enters the Union with the same sovereignty as the original States, including title to the beds of waters that were navigable in fact when statehood was achieved. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229-30 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57-58 (1894). The Court has repeatedly stated that the navigability inquiry for title is “whether the river was navigable at the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75; see also *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14-15; *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 404-05 (1940). That rule exists because title vests at statehood; later changes in use, commerce, or engineering do not retroactively alter ownership. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 408; *United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. at 34-35.

2. **The trial court properly focused on the historical condition of the rivers in 1889.**  
PPL’s challenge misunderstands the nature of the title inquiry. The Court has distinguished the title test from other navigability doctrines used for regulatory or admiralty purposes. For title, the question is historical; for other doctrines, later conditions may matter. *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 565 U.S. 576, 593-95 (2012); *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). The Montana court therefore acted consistently with federal law when it examined evidence bearing on whether the relevant river segments were used, or capable of being used, as highways of commerce at the time of statehood. See *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82; *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 407-08.

3. **Navigability is assessed segment by segment, not by reference to an entire river in the abstract.**  
The federal test requires attention to the particular reach claimed by the State. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 596-97; *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87-88. A river may be navigable in one stretch and nonnavigable in another, depending on natural conditions such as rapids, falls, and portages. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 406-07; *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 87-88. The Montana Supreme Court followed that rule by examining the river segments underlying PPL’s dams rather than treating the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers as uniformly navigable or nonnavigable. That approach accords with the Court’s instruction that title depends on the “particular segments” of water at issue. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 596.

4. **PPL’s contrary theory would sever title from statehood and conflict with settled precedent.**  
If navigability could be determined by conditions long after admission, ownership of submerged lands would fluctuate with later developments, contrary to the equal footing doctrine’s premise that title is fixed at statehood. *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. at 57-58; *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-77. The Court has rejected efforts to redefine the title inquiry in light of later improvements or changed commercial realities. *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. at 407-08; *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 592-93. Montana’s trial court did exactly what federal law requires: it asked whether the relevant river sections were navigable in 1889, and on that basis concluded that title vested in the State.

5. **Because the correct legal standard was applied, the State’s title and resulting damages award stand.**  
Once the court correctly determined that the State held title to the streambeds, Montana law permitted recovery for PPL’s use of those public lands. The Constitution leaves States free to assert ordinary incidents of ownership in lands they acquired at statehood, so long as federal law is not displaced. *United States v. Cherokee Nation of Okla.*, 480 U.S. 700, 704-05 (1987); *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 591-92. No such displacement occurred here. The judgment should therefore be affirmed.
