**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Lacked Federal Question Jurisdiction Because the Pressls’ Complaint States Only a Claim Under Virginia Property Law.**

Federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 must be apparent from the face of a well-pleaded complaint. *Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley*, 211 U.S. 149 (1908). In *Mottley*, the Supreme Court held that “a suggestion of one party, that the other will or may set up a claim under the Constitution or laws of the United States, does not make the suit one arising under that Constitution or those laws.” *Id.* at 152. The Pressls’ complaint asks a Virginia court to construe an instrument executed in 1960 between private parties and to declare their rights “in any manner not inconsistent with” the easement’s terms. That is a claim sounding entirely in Virginia property and contract law. Nothing on the face of the complaint invokes the Federal Power Act, FERC’s license, or any other federal enactment as a source of the Pressls’ right to relief.

The presence of a federal license somewhere in the factual background does not convert a state-law property dispute into one “arising under” federal law. As the Court explained in *Gully v. First National Bank*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), “to bring a case within the statute, a right or immunity created by the Constitution or laws of the United States must be an element, and an essential one, of the plaintiff’s cause of action.” *Id.* at 112. The Pressls’ cause of action requires no reference to the Federal Power Act; it requires only interpretation of the granting language of the easement under ordinary Virginia rules of deed construction. That a federal license might bear on APCO’s asserted defense—that its operation of a FERC-licensed project justifies removal of the dock—does not matter. “A suit to establish a right does not arise under the laws of the United States merely because it may be … determined by … reference to those laws.” *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Federal issues that arise only as potential defenses cannot support removal. *Mottley*, 211 U.S. at 152.

Nor does the declaratory character of this action change the analysis. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the Declaratory Judgment Act does not expand federal jurisdiction beyond what the well-pleaded complaint rule allows; a court must ask what claim the plaintiff could have brought coercively, and whether that claim would itself present a federal question. *Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co.*, 339 U.S. 667, 672 (1950) (“Congress enlarged the range of remedies available in the federal courts but did not extend their jurisdiction.”). Here, the coercive analogue to the Pressls’ declaratory claim would be a state-law suit to quiet title or to enjoin APCO from interfering with use of the property consistent with the easement—an action that would arise exclusively under Virginia law. Moreover, “the party who brings a suit is master to decide what law he will rely upon,” and may frame his complaint to rest solely on state law even where a federal claim might also be available. *The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913). The Pressls chose to seek only a declaration of their rights under the easement instrument itself, not under the Federal Power Act.

**II. The District Court Also Lacked Jurisdiction Under 16 U.S.C. § 825p Because the Complaint Does Not Allege a Violation of the Federal Power Act.**

Section 825p confers jurisdiction over suits to enforce liabilities or duties created by the Federal Power Act or by FERC’s orders and regulations. The Pressls’ complaint alleges no such violation. It does not claim that APCO breached the terms of its FERC license or violated any FERC order; it claims that APCO misconstrued a privately negotiated easement under state law. The Federal Power Act’s licensing scheme addresses the relationship between FERC and its licensees in the operation of hydroelectric projects, not the private property rights of landowners whose deeds happen to lie within a project boundary. *See First Iowa Hydro-Elec. Coop. v. FPC*, 328 U.S. 152 (1946) (describing the Act’s licensing scheme as governing the relationship between the licensee and the Commission). Section 825p cannot be stretched to cover every private dispute that touches upon land within a licensed project’s boundary.

**III. Any Doubt About Jurisdiction Must Be Resolved in Favor of Remand.**

APCO, as the removing party, bore the burden of establishing federal jurisdiction. *Chicago, Rock Island & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Martin*, 178 U.S. 245, 248 (1900); *Metcalf v. City of Watertown*, 128 U.S. 586, 589 (1888). Because removal implicates significant federalism concerns, the removal statutes must be strictly construed against removal jurisdiction, with all doubts resolved in favor of remand. *Shamrock Oil & Gas Corp. v. Sheets*, 313 U.S. 100, 108–09 (1941) (explaining that the policy of federal jurisdictional statutes “call[s] for the strict construction of such legislation”). APCO cannot satisfy that burden here, where the complaint states nothing more than a state-law dispute over the meaning of a 1960 easement.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the district court lacked subject matter jurisdiction. The judgment should be vacated and the case remanded with instructions to return it to the Circuit Court of Franklin County, Virginia.
