**Argument**

I. **The Controlled Substances Act supplies an intelligible principle and therefore does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power.**

Article I permits Congress to seek assistance from another branch so long as it “lay[s] down by legislative act an intelligible principle” to guide the exercise of the delegated authority. *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928); *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372–73 (1989). The modern doctrine is highly deferential: Congress may delegate power through broad general directives when the subject is complex and technical. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372–73; *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 425–27 (1944). Since 1935, the Court has invalidated congressional delegations only twice. *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935); *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935). Outside those extraordinary cases, the Court has consistently upheld delegations that include standards, procedures, and limitations. *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 373–79; *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947).

The Controlled Substances Act easily satisfies that standard. Congress identified the policy: control substances that pose a public-health danger. 21 U.S.C. §§ 801, 811(c). It specified the universe of permissible schedules and the consequences of placement. 21 U.S.C. § 812. It required the Attorney General to consider eight enumerated factors, including abuse potential, scientific evidence, current knowledge, history and pattern of abuse, public-health risk, dependence liability, and precursor status. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). It further required consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, whose scientific and medical evaluation binds the Attorney General when the Secretary recommends against control. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b). And it imposed notice-and-comment procedures under the Administrative Procedure Act before permanent scheduling. 21 U.S.C. § 811(a).

Those restrictions are more than sufficient under *Mistretta*. There, the Court upheld a delegation that directed the Sentencing Commission to pursue specified purposes and consider listed factors. 488 U.S. at 374–79. The CSA is at least as constrained, and in some respects more so, because it cabins the Attorney General’s discretion with scientific criteria and mandatory interbranch consultation. See *Touby v. United States*, 500 U.S. 160, 165–67 (1991) (upholding temporary scheduling delegation).

Nor does the criminal context change the analysis. Congress may delegate authority that affects criminal liability when it defines the policy and limiting standards. In *Yakus*, the Court sustained wartime price-setting authority even though violations carried criminal penalties. 321 U.S. at 425–27. And in *Touby*, the Court rejected the claim that criminalization demands a stricter nondelegation rule, explaining that “our nondelegation jurisprudence has been driven by a practical understanding” that Congress may delegate in complex areas. 500 U.S. at 165–66. Here, Congress itself made the policy judgment that dangerous substances should be controlled, and it supplied objective standards to guide implementation.

II. **Congress authorized the Attorney General to subdelegate temporary scheduling authority to the DEA.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA is likewise valid. Congress expressly authorized the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice.” 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That broad delegation includes the functions assigned under the CSA unless Congress says otherwise. *United States v. Giordano*, 416 U.S. 505, 513–17 (1974), and *United States v. Kahn*, 415 U.S. 143, 152–54 (1974), teach that when Congress specifies who may exercise a power, courts must enforce that limitation; but here Congress specified the Attorney General and then separately authorized him to delegate his CSA functions within the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a).

Temporary scheduling under § 811(h) falls comfortably within that authorization. The temporary scheduling provision is part of the CSA, and the Attorney General’s duties under it are “functions under this subchapter” within § 871(a)’s plain text. See *Touby*, 500 U.S. at 167–68. The DEA Administrator is an officer within the Department of Justice for purposes of that delegation structure, and the Attorney General validly reassigned those functions by regulation. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). The Third Circuit correctly held that the Attorney General’s subdelegation was authorized. *United States v. Touby*, 909 F.2d 759, 768–71 (3d Cir. 1990).

III. **Temporary scheduling procedures provide added constitutional assurance.**

Section 811(h) is especially secure because Congress imposed emergency-specific constraints: the Attorney General may act only to “avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” must consider specified abuse and risk factors, must publish notice in the Federal Register, must transmit notice to HHS, and may maintain the order only for a limited period. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (2), (3), (4). These procedural safeguards reinforce the conclusion that Congress supplied an intelligible principle and did not transfer legislative power wholesale. See *Touby*, 500 U.S. at 168–69.

**Conclusion**

The Controlled Substances Act does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power, and Congress authorized the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA. The judgment should be affirmed.
