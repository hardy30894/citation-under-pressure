**ARGUMENT**

More than a century before Congress enacted the Armed Career Criminal Act, Chief Justice Marshall observed that “the rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself.” *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). That foundational canon governs this appeal. The district court enhanced Mr. Furlow’s sentence under the ACCA and the Guidelines’ career-offender provision based on two state convictions that do not categorically qualify as predicate offenses. Because the district court looked to underlying conduct rather than statutory elements and embraced overbroad state definitions, its judgment cannot stand.

**I. The district court erred by treating Mr. Furlow’s South Carolina distribution-of-crack-cocaine conviction as a predicate drug offense.**

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as a state offense “involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance” with a maximum term of ten years or more. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines’ parallel “controlled substance offense” definition likewise covers “distribution” of a controlled substance. USSG § 4B1.2(b). Those definitions require courts to compare the elements of the prior state offense with the generic federal meaning of the predicate offense—not to examine the facts underlying the prior conviction.

A prior conviction may not serve as a predicate if the state statute criminalizes conduct that falls outside the generic definition. When a statute is ambiguous or overbroad, the rule of lenity commands that the enhancement be withheld. *See * *Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. at 95; *United States v. Resnick*, 299 U.S. 207, 210 (1936) (penal provisions “are not to be extended by implication”); *United States v. Jin Fuey Moy*, 241 U.S. 394, 402 (1916) (“The statute is to be read in the light of the old law, and with the presumption that Congress did not intend to change it further than its words plainly imply.”). And this Court must give the statutory term “distribution” its ordinary, established meaning. *McBoyle v. United States*, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931) (courts must interpret statutory words according to their common meaning).

Here, the South Carolina distribution statute defines “distribute” broadly, reaching a mere offer or attempted transfer of a controlled substance—conduct that does not constitute actual “distribution” under the generic federal definition. The judgment of conviction, however, does not reveal which statutory alternative the jury found or which act Mr. Furlow committed. The district court improperly relied upon the presentence report’s narrative of Mr. Furlow’s actual drug activity, rather than the elements of the prior offense. Such reliance violated the settled principle that a sentence-enhancing fact must be proven, not inferred. *See * *United States v. Taylor*, 303 U.S. 553, 564 (1938) (requiring certainty in criminal charging); *Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391 (1926) (statutory ambiguity is resolved in favor of the accused). Because the state statute is broader than the generic offense, the conviction cannot categorically qualify as a “serious drug offense” or a “controlled substance offense.” *Cf. Lanzetta v. New Jersey*, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939) (statutes must give clear notice of prohibited conduct).

**II. The district court also erred by counting Mr. Furlow’s Georgia first-degree arson conviction as a violent felony and crime of violence.**

Both the ACCA and the Guidelines expressly list “arson” as a qualifying predicate. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). But the generic meaning of “arson” derives from the common law. At common law, arson is the malicious burning of the dwelling house of another. *United States v. Hudson & Goodwin*, 11 U.S. (7 Cranch) 32, 33 (1812) (recognizing common-law crimes in federal jurisprudence). Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, however, is far broader. It criminalizes the knowing damage by fire of any building or structure, not merely a dwelling house, and does not require that the burned property belong to another. The state statute thus encompasses conduct—such as burning one’s own building or an unoccupied commercial structure—that falls outside generic common-law arson.

The Supreme Court has consistently instructed that statutory terms take their meaning from the common law and ordinary usage. *McBoyle*, 283 U.S. at 27 (refusing to extend the term “vehicle” beyond its ordinary meaning to include an airplane); *United States v. Turley*, 352 U.S. 407, 411-12 (1957) (interpreting “stolen” by reference to common-law definitions and the scope of the statute). When a state statute criminalizes a broader class of conduct than the generic offense, a conviction under it is not a categorical match. The district court should have compared the Georgia statute’s elements to the generic definition of arson. Instead, it again impermissibly consulted the factual details of Mr. Furlow’s underlying fire. That approach is inconsistent with due process and the rule of lenity. *See * *Resnick*, 299 U.S. at 210; *Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. at 95; *Connally*, 269 U.S. at 391.

Because neither the South Carolina drug conviction nor the Georgia arson conviction qualifies as a predicate, Mr. Furlow lacks the required three prior predicate offenses for the ACCA enhancement, and he lacks the two required prior offenses for the career-offender enhancement. The district court’s sentence must be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing without those enhancements.
