The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment's jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment's prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The Supreme Court's decision in Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), does not require a jury to determine whether a capital defendant is mentally retarded or to unanimously sentence a defendant to death.

First, regarding the determination of mental retardation, the Supreme Court in Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), left it to the states to determine the manner in which the constitutional restriction on execution of mentally retarded defendants would be enforced. Florida law sets forth a three-pronged test to determine mental retardation as a bar to the death penalty, which is to be determined by the trial court. See Nixon v. State, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla.2009); § 921.137(1), Fla. Stat. (2012). The trial court's determination of mental retardation is a factual finding that is not required to be made by a jury under the Sixth Amendment. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 597 n.4 (distinguishing between the "enumerated aggravating factors" that increase the maximum penalty and other factual findings).

Second, regarding the requirement of a unanimous jury verdict, Florida's capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment. While Ring held that the Sixth Amendment requires a jury to find aggravating factors that increase the maximum penalty, it did not require a unanimous jury verdict. See Ring, 536 U.S. at 609. Florida's scheme, which allows a jury to recommend death by a majority vote, is consistent with the Sixth Amendment.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. The Supreme Court has emphasized the importance of "heightened reliability" in capital sentencing, see Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987), but it has not held that a unanimous jury verdict is required. While Justice Pariente's dissent below noted that Florida is an "outlier" among states that impose the death penalty, this alone does not render the scheme unconstitutional. The fact that other states have abolished the death penalty or require a unanimous jury verdict does not establish a constitutional requirement.

In conclusion, the Florida death sentencing scheme is constitutional under both the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. The trial court's determination of mental retardation and the jury's non-unanimous recommendation of death are consistent with the Supreme Court's decisions in Atkins and Ring.
