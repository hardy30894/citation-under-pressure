The installation of fiber optic cables exceeding the BPA’s transmission needs, together with the subsequent leasing of excess capacity to third parties, effects a physical taking. The easement conveyed only “a perpetual easement and right to enter and erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol one or more electrical power transmission lines and appurtenant signal lines, poles, towers, wires, cables, and appliances necessary in connection therewith.” Any additional physical occupation lies outside that grant.

A permanent physical occupation of private property by the United States constitutes a taking requiring just compensation. United States v. Causby, 328 U.S. 256, 261 (1946). The same rule applies when government action produces a direct and substantial invasion of the surface estate. Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States, 260 U.S. 327, 329 (1922); United States v. Cress, 243 U.S. 316, 327-28 (1917). Here the excess fibers occupy space on Tuthill’s land that the easement never authorized. That occupation is physical, not merely regulatory. Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon, 260 U.S. 393, 415 (1922).

When the United States exceeds the scope of an existing easement, the additional burden is compensable. Chicago, B. & Q. R. Co. v. Chicago, 166 U.S. 226, 241 (1897). The Fifth Amendment secures the full measure of the owner’s property interest; any material enlargement of the servitude therefore requires payment. Monongahela Navigation Co. v. United States, 148 U.S. 312, 325 (1893). The government’s decision to install thirty-six fibers when only a fraction are required for its own operations, and to lease the remainder for commercial gain, enlarges the servitude beyond the “necessary” signal lines permitted by the 1953 deed.

Compensation is measured by the value of the property interest taken at the time of the taking. Boom Co. v. Patterson, 98 U.S. 403, 406 (1878). The permanent presence of the excess cables and the third-party leases that utilize them fix the date of taking. United States v. Dickinson, 331 U.S. 745, 748 (1947). Because the occupation is both physical and permanent, it is per se compensable without regard to the government’s asserted future needs or the absence of additional surface disturbance.

The Court of Federal Claims therefore erred in holding that the scope of the easement is irrelevant to a physical-taking claim. The undisputed facts establish an unauthorized physical occupation for which just compensation is due.
