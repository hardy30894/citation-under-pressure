# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ASSESSMENT AGAINST THE PARTNERSHIP DID NOT EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS AS TO THE INDIVIDUAL PARTNERS

### A. A Partnership Is a Distinct Entity from Its Individual Partners for Purposes of Assessment and Collection

This Court has long recognized that a partnership is treated as an entity separate and distinct from its individual partners under federal law. In *Forsyth v. Woods*, 78 U.S. 484 (1871), this Court explained that "[t]he partnership is a distinct thing from the partners themselves," and that "debts of the firm are different in character from other joint debts of the partners." That fundamental distinction—between the partnership as an entity and the individuals who compose it—is precisely what the Ninth Circuit recognized below, and it forecloses the government's position.

This entity/aggregate distinction has particular resonance in the bankruptcy context. In *Francis v. McNeal*, 228 U.S. 695 (1913), this Court held that the Bankruptcy Act's structure "assumption ... was that the partnership and individual estates both were to be administered," and that the partnership and its individual partners are treated as separate for purposes of bankruptcy proceedings absent an explicit statutory mechanism joining them. The Court there rejected the notion that adjudication against a partnership automatically extends to its partners, reasoning that "it would be an anomaly to allow proceedings in bankruptcy against joint debtors from some of whom, at any time before, pending, or after the proceeding, the debt could be collected in full." *Id.* The same anomaly would result here if an assessment against the Partnership were treated as an assessment against Debtors, allowing the ten-year collection period to extend automatically to persons never separately identified as taxpayers.

This Court reinforced the entity-based separation of partnership and partner liability in *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 268 U.S. 426 (1925). There the Court held that a bankruptcy petition filed in the name of a partnership could not automatically bind the individual partners, explaining that a petition filed by one partner "purporting to act in its behalf" could not be sustained "without an affirmative showing" of individual consent or process. *Id.* The Court's insistence on individualized process before individual liability attaches directly supports Debtors' position that the IRS's assessment against the Partnership, standing alone, cannot substitute for individualized assessment or judgment against Debtors themselves.

### B. Partnership-Level Tax Assessments Do Not Automatically Bind Individual Partners Absent Separate Process

The statutory assessment scheme requires identification of "the taxpayer," and this Court's precedents confirm that a partnership and its partners are not interchangeable for purposes of that identification requirement. In *Heiner v. Mellon*, 304 U.S. 271 (1938), this Court carefully distinguished between the tax obligations of a partnership and the tax obligations of the individual partners with respect to partnership profits, treating each partner's liability as a matter requiring separate determination based on that partner's distributive share. The Court's approach in *Heiner* confirms that a partner's personal tax liability is not simply coextensive with, or automatically established by, action taken against the partnership entity.

Similarly, in *Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 303 U.S. 493 (1938), this Court examined a deceased partner's individual tax liability for his distributive share of partnership income, treating the determination of the partner's personal liability as a distinct inquiry from the partnership's own fiscal accounting. The Court's separate treatment of partner-level tax consequences from partnership-level determinations undermines the government's contention that assessment against the entity automatically operates as assessment against each partner.

This Court's family-partnership cases likewise proceed from the premise that individual partner status and liability must be independently established, not presumed from the existence of the partnership itself. In *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280 (1946), the Court scrutinized whether a purported partner truly held an independent stake in the partnership, holding that where the alleged partner's "economic relation" to partnership income remained functionally unchanged, the partnership arrangement did not establish independent individual tax consequences. And in *Commissioner v. Culbertson*, 337 U.S. 733 (1949), this Court reaffirmed that a partner's individual tax status depends on whether that individual "either invests capital originating with h[er] or substantially contributes to the control and management of the business, or otherwise performs vital additional services" — an individualized inquiry incompatible with the government's theory that partnership-level assessment automatically fixes each partner's personal liability. *Culbertson*, 337 U.S. at 739 (quoting *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280, 290 (1946)).

*Lusthaus v. Commissioner*, 327 U.S. 293 (1946), decided the same day as *Tower*, likewise treated the individual partner's tax position as requiring separate analysis from the partnership's structure, notwithstanding that a state certificate had formally authorized the parties "to carry on the business as a partnership." These cases collectively establish that this Court has never treated partnership-level action as self-executing against individual partners; rather, individual liability must be independently and separately established.

### C. Equitable Marshaling Principles Confirm That Partnership and Individual Estates Remain Distinct

Finally, in *Liberty National Bank of Roanoke, Va. v. Bear*, 276 U.S. 215 (1928), this Court described the statutory scheme under which "[t]he net proceeds of the partnership property shall be appropriated to the payment of the partnership debts, and the net proceeds of the individual estate of each partner to the payment of his individual debts." That structure—maintaining separate accounts and separate liability for partnership debts and individual debts, with cross-recovery permitted only through specific mechanisms such as marshaling, *see id.*—confirms that Congress and this Court have consistently treated partnership obligations and individual partner obligations as analytically distinct, to be joined only through defined procedural mechanisms, not through simple identity of assessment.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, and those stated in the opinion below, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
