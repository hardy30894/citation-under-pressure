ARGUMENT

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Anthony Perry's petition for review to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The proper forum for judicial review of the Merit Systems Protection Board's (MSPB) dismissal of Perry's mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is a federal district court.

The Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, as amended, governs the judicial review of MSPB decisions. Under 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(A), review of Board decisions generally lies in the Federal Circuit. However, an exception exists for "[c]ases of discrimination," as to which review lies in federal district court, 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). The statute defines such cases as those in which an employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by" enumerated federal laws, 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1).

The Supreme Court's decision in Kloeckner v. Solis, while not directly on point, provides guidance on the proper interpretation of the statutory scheme. Although Kloeckner involved a procedural dismissal, the Court's reasoning suggests that the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not dispositive. See Kloeckner, 133 S.Ct. at 604 (citing Harms v. IRS, 321 F.3d 1001 (10th Cir. 2003), and Downey v. Runyon, 160 F.3d 139 (2d Cir. 1998), which drew a distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals).

Pre-1970 case law supports the proposition that the Federal Circuit is not the exclusive forum for reviewing MSPB jurisdictional decisions. In United States v. ICC, 337 U.S. 426 (1949), the Supreme Court held that a statutory provision governing judicial review did not preclude review in a district court, even though the statute did not explicitly provide for it. Similarly, in Switchmen's Union of North America v. National Mediation Board, 320 U.S. 297 (1943), the Court held that a statutory provision governing judicial review did not bar review in a district court, even though the statute provided for review in a court of appeals.

The decision in Powell v. Department of Defense, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), relied upon by the D.C. Circuit, is not controlling. Powell was decided before Kloeckner, and its holding is not consistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Brown v. United States, 169 U.S. 644 (1898), and Stark v. Wickard, 321 U.S. 288 (1944), which emphasize the importance of considering the statutory scheme as a whole when determining the proper forum for judicial review.

Furthermore, the reasoning in cases such as Leedom v. Kyne, 358 U.S. 184 (1958), and Harmon v. Brucker, 355 U.S. 579 (1958), suggests that the availability of judicial review in a district court is not limited by the statutory provision governing review in the Federal Circuit. In Leedom, the Court held that a district court had jurisdiction to review a decision of the National Labor Relations Board, despite a statutory provision governing review in a court of appeals. Similarly, in Harmon, the Court held that a district court had jurisdiction to review a decision of the Secretary of the Army, despite a statutory provision governing review in a court of appeals.

The decision in United States v. American Trucking Associations, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), also supports the proposition that the statutory scheme governing judicial review of MSPB decisions should be interpreted in light of the overall statutory purpose. In American Trucking Associations, the Court held that a statutory provision should be interpreted in a way that is consistent with the statutory purpose, even if it requires a departure from a literal reading of the statute.

Additionally, the principles enunciated in cases such as Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), emphasize the importance of considering the statutory context and purpose when interpreting a statutory provision. These cases support the argument that the proper forum for judicial review of MSPB decisions should be determined in light of the overall statutory scheme, rather than a narrow reading of a single statutory provision.

Finally, the decision in Myers v. Bethlehem Shipbuilding Corp., 303 U.S. 41 (1938), highlights the importance of considering the availability of judicial review in determining the proper forum for review. In Myers, the Court held that a district court had jurisdiction to review a decision of the National Labor Relations Board, despite a statutory provision governing review in a court of appeals.

In light of these precedents, it is clear that the D.C. Circuit erred in transferring Perry's petition for review to the Federal Circuit. The proper forum for judicial review of the MSPB's dismissal of Perry's mixed case on jurisdictional grounds is a federal district court.
