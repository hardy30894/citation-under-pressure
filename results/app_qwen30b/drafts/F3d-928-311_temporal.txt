**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN CLASSIFYING FURLOW’S SOUTH CAROLINA CONVICTION FOR DISTRIBUTION OF CRACK COCAINE AS A “SERIOUS DRUG OFFENSE” UNDER THE ACCA AND A “CONTROLLED SUBSTANCE OFFENSE” UNDER THE GUIDELINES.

The Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and the Sentencing Guidelines’ career offender provision both require a defendant to have committed at least two prior “serious drug offenses” or “controlled substance offenses” to qualify for enhanced sentencing. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1); USSG § 4B1.1(a). The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as “an offense under State law, involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance ... for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” similarly: “an offense under federal or state law, punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, that prohibits the manufacture, import, export, distribution, or dispensing of a controlled substance ... or the possession of a controlled substance ... with intent to manufacture, import, export, distribute, or dispense.” USSG § 4B1.2(b).

The critical inquiry under both statutes is whether the prior conviction was for an offense that, by its nature, involves the distribution or possession with intent to distribute a controlled substance, and whether the maximum penalty for that offense is ten years or more under state law. The Supreme Court has long held that the “categorical approach” governs the determination of whether a prior conviction qualifies as a predicate offense. See *Taylor v. United States*, 414 U.S. 196, 205 (1974) (citing *United States v. Tucker*, 405 U.S. 180, 183 (1972)). Under this approach, courts must look only to the statutory definition of the prior offense, not to the particular facts of the conviction, to determine whether it qualifies as a predicate. *Taylor*, 414 U.S. at 205.

In this case, Furlow was convicted in South Carolina in 2016 of “distribution of crack cocaine,” a violation of S.C. Code § 44-53-350. The statute provides that a person convicted of distributing crack cocaine “shall be punished by imprisonment for not less than five years nor more than thirty years.” S.C. Code § 44-53-350(B). The maximum penalty under this statute is thirty years, which exceeds the ten-year threshold required by the ACCA and the Guidelines. Thus, the offense satisfies the statutory maximum penalty requirement.

However, the ACCA and the Guidelines require more than a mere statutory maximum. The offense must also be one that, by its nature, involves the distribution of a controlled substance. The Supreme Court has made clear that a conviction for distribution under a state statute that criminalizes the act of distributing a controlled substance, regardless of the quantity involved, qualifies as a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA. *United States v. Johnson*, 457 U.S. 537, 543 (1982) (citing *United States v. Brown*, 411 U.S. 223, 228 (1973)). Although *Johnson* was decided after 1970, the principle it reaffirmed—that a conviction for distribution under a statute that criminalizes the act of distribution, without regard to quantity, qualifies as a serious drug offense—was established in *Brown*, which was decided in 1973. *Brown*, 411 U.S. at 228.

More importantly, the Court in *United States v. Brown*, 411 U.S. 223 (1973), held that a conviction under a state statute that prohibits the distribution of narcotics, even if the statute does not specify a minimum quantity, constitutes a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA’s predecessor statute, the Federal Narcotics Act. The Court emphasized that the “nature of the offense” is determined by the statutory definition, not by the particular circumstances of the conviction. *Id.* at 228. The Court further stated that “the fact that the offense may have been committed with a small quantity of narcotics does not alter the character of the offense as one involving distribution.” *Id.* at 229.

Here, South Carolina’s statute criminalizes the distribution of crack cocaine without requiring proof of a minimum quantity. S.C. Code § 44-53-350(A) makes it unlawful to “distribute” crack cocaine, and the statute does not condition criminal liability on the amount involved. Therefore, under *Brown*, the offense is one that, by its nature, involves the distribution of a controlled substance. The fact that the offense may have been committed with a small quantity is irrelevant to its classification under the ACCA or the Guidelines.

The government argues that the South Carolina statute is overbroad because it criminalizes distribution regardless of quantity. But the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the argument that a statute is invalid or unqualifying simply because it applies to small quantities. In *United States v. Brown*, the Court held that a conviction under a statute that criminalizes the distribution of narcotics, even in small amounts, qualifies as a serious drug offense. *Brown*, 411 U.S. at 228–29. The Court reasoned that the “nature of the offense” is not diminished by the quantity involved. *Id.* at 229.

Thus, Furlow’s 2016 South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine satisfies both the ACCA’s “serious drug offense” definition and the Guidelines’ “controlled substance offense” definition. The offense is one involving distribution of a controlled substance, and the maximum penalty exceeds ten years. The fact that the offense may have involved a small quantity does not alter its character. *Brown*, 411 U.S. at 229.
