**ARGUMENT**

The judgment below must be reversed. Section 875(c) punishes only the transmission of a "threat." That word, read against the background presumption of mens rea and the First Amendment's protection of speech, requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be a threat. The objective "reasonable speaker" standard used here dispensed with that essential element.

**I. The Statutory Text and Background Principles Require Subjective Intent**

The word "threat" in §875(c) carries a built-in mental state. A threat is "a declaration of intention to do injury to another." *United States v. Winkl*, 79 F.2d 346, 347 (6th Cir. 1935). A declaration of *intention* necessarily describes the speaker's own purpose. Without proof that the defendant intended his words as a declaration of harm, the communication is not a "threat" at all.

This textual inference is reinforced by the bedrock presumption that criminal statutes require a culpable mental state. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). That presumption is "as old as the common law itself," *id.* at 252, and applies with full force here. Section 875(c) is not a public welfare offense; it targets core speech and carries severe penalties. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922), recognized only a narrow class of strict-liability regulatory offenses, and this is not among them. Congress said nothing to displace the usual requirement of intent. The statute must therefore be read to require subjective intent.

If any doubt remains, the rule of lenity resolves it. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 221 (1876). A statute that leaves "to the courts and juries the duty of fixing its meaning" cannot stand. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921). The objective standard gives no fair warning: a speaker cannot know whether his words will be deemed threatening until a jury decides how a reasonable person would interpret them. *See also United States v. Weintraub*, 273 F.2d 808, 811 (2d Cir. 1959) (threat statute requires proof that defendant intended to communicate a threat). The rule of lenity therefore compels the narrower interpretation—subjective intent.

**II. The First Amendment Compels the Same Result**

The First Amendment separately requires subjective intent. The "true threat" exception covers only statements the speaker intentionally communicates as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). In *Watts*, this Court reversed a conviction under a similar statute because the defendant's statement was "political hyperbole" and not a "true threat." *Id.* at 707–08. The Court examined the context and the speaker's apparent intent—not merely how a reasonable listener would react. An objective standard would sweep in protected speech—jests, artistic expression, emotional outbursts—that the speaker never intended to be threatening.

The analogy to incitement is instructive. Speech cannot be punished as incitement without proof of intent to cause imminent lawless action. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940) (reversing because "no evidence that [the defendant] intended to incite"); *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (speech must be "directed to inciting"). The same logic governs threats: the government must prove the defendant intended his words to be taken as a threat. *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919) (considering "the intent with which the act was done").

Moreover, the First Amendment prohibits strict liability in speech-related offenses. *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 150 (1959) (striking down ordinance punishing bookseller without proof of scienter). An objective standard for threats is a form of strict liability that chills protected expression, because speakers cannot know whether their words will be deemed threatening until a jury decides how a reasonable person would interpret them. *Garrison v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 64, 74 (1964) (requiring intent to inflict harm for libel of public officials). The objective standard adopted below—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as threatening—punishes speech based on the reaction of a hypothetical listener, not the speaker's purpose. That approach invites juries to convict based on the offensiveness of the words rather than the speaker's culpable intent.

Finally, the doctrine of constitutional avoidance confirms that §875(c) must be read to require subjective intent. *Reese*, 92 U.S. at 221. If the statute is ambiguous, this Court should adopt the interpretation that avoids serious constitutional doubts. The objective standard raises grave First Amendment concerns; the subjective standard does not.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury must find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
