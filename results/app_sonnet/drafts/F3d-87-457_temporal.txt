# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISCLOSURE PROVISIONS OF CHAPTER 93-121 ARE UNCONSTITUTIONALLY OVERBROAD

The First Amendment protects the right to petition government, and lobbying is a recognized exercise of that right. *Eastern R.R. Presidents Conference v. Noerr Motor Freight, Inc.*, 365 U.S. 127, 137-38 (1961) (holding that "the right of petition is one of the freedoms protected by the Bill of Rights"). Because lobbying activity implicates core First Amendment interests, any statute regulating it must be drawn with precision. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963) ("Because First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive, government may regulate in the area only with narrow specificity.").

Chapter 93-121 fails this test. The statute compels disclosure not merely of direct payments to lobbyists but of every category of expenditure—"food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging"—made by either the lobbyist or the principal. Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a). This sweeping mandate reaches expenditures having only the most attenuated connection to any legislative advocacy, chilling protected association and speech far beyond what any legitimate governmental interest requires.

This Court's overbreadth analysis is guided by *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 97 (1940), which struck down a statute because it swept within its ambit activities that could not constitutionally be prohibited, reasoning that "[t]he existence of such a statute . . . results in a continuous and pervasive restraint on all freedom of discussion." The Supreme Court reaffirmed this principle in *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958), holding that compelled disclosure of membership lists can work as effective a restraint on First Amendment rights as outright prohibition, for "[i]t is hardly a novel perception that compelled disclosure of affiliation with groups engaged in advocacy may constitute as effective a restraint on freedom of association as [] other forms of governmental action."

The breadth of Florida's disclosure scheme mirrors the constitutional infirmity condemned in *Talley v. California*, 362 U.S. 60, 64 (1960), where the Court invalidated an ordinance requiring identification on handbills because "identification and fear of reprisal might deter perfectly peaceful discussions of public matters of importance." Here, the League's members reasonably fear that comprehensive disclosure of every category of expenditure—extending even to incidental travel and lodging—invites the very reprisal that chills the exercise of protected advocacy.

While the Supreme Court has upheld more narrowly tailored disclosure requirements directed at actual attempts to influence legislation, see *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 625 (1954) (upholding federal lobbying disclosure statute construed to reach only "direct communication with members of Congress on pending or proposed federal legislation"), the Court in *Harriss* was careful to save the statute from vagueness only by construing it narrowly to reach solely "moneys used for the purpose of influencing legislation through direct communication." *Id.* at 620. Chapter 93-121 admits no such narrowing construction; it explicitly reaches expenditures having no direct connection to legislative communication whatsoever.

## II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN, ALTHOUGH GOVERNED BY PRECEDENT THIS COURT MAY DEEM CONTROLLING, RESTS ON REASONING THAT CANNOT SURVIVE MODERN FIRST AMENDMENT ANALYSIS

Appellant recognizes that in *Trist v. Child*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 441 (1875), and *Marshall v. Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co.*, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 314 (1854), the Supreme Court condemned contingent-fee lobbying contracts as void for public policy, reasoning that such arrangements create "a temptation to the debasement of the public service." *Trist*, 88 U.S. at 452. These decisions, however, predate the modern First Amendment jurisprudence protecting the right to petition and to associate for the advancement of political and economic interests.

The contingency-fee ban directly burdens protected speech by conditioning the mode of compensation for advocacy activity. Just as the Supreme Court recognized in *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 429-30, that litigation for a cause is "a form of political expression" entitled to First Amendment protection regardless of the fee arrangement supporting it, so too must lobbying advocacy—regardless of whether compensation is contingent or fixed—receive protection commensurate with its expressive character. The *Button* Court struck down restrictions on solicitation of legal business precisely because such restrictions "may set the stage for regulating the practice of forwarding claims" in ways that infringe protected advocacy. *Id.* at 431.

Moreover, contingent-fee arrangements themselves have been recognized as legitimate mechanisms facilitating access to advocacy for those unable to pay fixed fees. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 443 (Harlan, J., dissenting in part) (acknowledging contingent fees as a means of "assuring effective legal representation" for those of limited means). The same logic applies with equal force to lobbying: contingent fees permit principals with limited resources to engage professional advocates, thereby expanding rather than corrupting the marketplace of political petition.

While this Court may consider itself bound by *Trist* and *Marshall* under the doctrine that only the Supreme Court may overrule its own precedents, see *Radio Station WOW, Inc. v. Johnson*, 326 U.S. 120, 128 (1945) (recognizing that lower courts must adhere to controlling Supreme Court precedent even where its continued vitality is doubtful), Appellant preserves this argument for further review, respectfully submitting that *Trist* and *Marshall* cannot be reconciled with the intervening development of First Amendment doctrine reflected in *Button* and *Noerr*.
