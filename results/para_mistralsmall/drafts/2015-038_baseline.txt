**ARGUMENT**

**I. The National Park Service’s hovercraft ban applies to all lands and waters within the boundaries of the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including state-owned river segments.**

The plain text of the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) § 103(c) does not exempt state-owned lands or waters from the regulatory authority of the National Park Service (NPS) within conservation system units (CSUs). *See* 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c) (“The Secretary shall promulgate such regulations as are necessary and appropriate to implement the provisions of this Act, including provisions for public use and enjoyment of the public lands.”). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon’s interpretation of § 103(c) is foreclosed by the statute’s plain language. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 829 F.3d 1066, 1072 (9th Cir. 2016), *aff’d on other grounds*, 137 S. Ct. 1061 (2017). The statute’s text makes no distinction between federal and non-federal lands; it vests the Secretary with regulatory authority over all lands and waters within CSU boundaries. *Id.* (citing *Strycker’s Bay Neighborhood Council, Inc. v. Karlen*, 444 U.S. 223, 227 (1980) (per curiam) (“Where the statute’s language is plain, the sole function of the courts—at least where the disposition required by the text is not absurd—is to enforce it according to its terms.”)).

Sturgeon’s reliance on *Katie John I*, 72 F.3d 698 (9th Cir. 1995), is misplaced. That case addressed subsistence fishing rights under ANILCA § 811, not the NPS’s general regulatory authority. *Id.* at 700. The Supreme Court subsequently clarified that ANILCA does not limit the NPS’s authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within CSUs. *See United States v. Alaska*, 521 U.S. 1, 58 (1997) (“ANILCA does not divest the United States of its regulatory authority over nonfederal lands within conservation system units.”). The Court expressly rejected the argument that state ownership of submerged lands within a CSU precludes federal regulation. *Id.* at 59 (“The State’s ownership of the submerged lands does not diminish the Federal Government’s authority to regulate activities in the waters above those lands.”).

**II. The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of the NPS’s authority under ANILCA and the Organic Act.**

The NPS’s hovercraft ban is a permissible regulation under ANILCA § 103(c) and the National Park Service Organic Act, 16 U.S.C. § 1, which grants the Secretary broad authority to regulate the use of national park lands and waters. *See Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 540 (1976) (“The Secretary is authorized to promulgate regulations ‘necessary and appropriate’ to protect the public lands.”). The ban is a reasonable measure to protect natural resources and ensure public safety within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve. *See Cameron v. United States*, 252 U.S. 450, 459 (1920) (“The authority of the Secretary to make regulations for the use and management of the national parks is necessarily broad.”).

Sturgeon’s argument that the ban does not apply to state-owned waters because Alaska owns the riverbed is unavailing. The Supreme Court has recognized that the federal government retains regulatory authority over navigable waters within national parks regardless of state ownership of the underlying land. *See United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (“The United States has paramount authority to control navigation and regulate the use of navigable waters within national parks.”). The hovercraft ban is a valid exercise of that authority.

**III. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s regulations as applied to state-owned lands.**

Sturgeon’s claim that the NPS lacks jurisdiction over state-owned river segments is not ripe for adjudication. *See Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 573 U.S. 149, 158 (2014) (“The doctrine of ripeness ‘reflects the policies embodied in Article III of the Constitution limiting the federal judicial power to actual cases or controversies.’ ”). Sturgeon has not demonstrated a credible threat of enforcement on state-owned lands. The NPS has not issued a citation or filed a lawsuit against Sturgeon for operating a hovercraft on state-owned waters. *See id.* at 158-59 (“A plaintiff satisfies the injury-in-fact requirement where he alleges ‘an intention to engage in a course of conduct arguably affected with a constitutional interest, but proscribed by a statute, and there exists a credible threat of prosecution thereunder.’ ” (quoting *Babbitt v. Farm Workers Nat’l Union*, 442 U.S. 289, 298 (1979))).

Moreover, Sturgeon’s claim that the NPS’s regulations violate ANILCA § 103(c) is not ripe because the statute’s text does not exempt state-owned lands. *See Texas v. United States*, 523 U.S. 296, 300 (1998) (“A claim is not ripe for adjudication if it rests upon ‘contingent future events that may not occur as anticipated, or indeed may not occur at all.’ ”). The NPS’s interpretation of its authority under ANILCA is entitled to deference under *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984), and Sturgeon has not shown that the agency’s interpretation is unreasonable.

**IV. The State of Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s regulations.**

The State of Alaska cannot establish standing because it has not suffered a cognizable injury. *See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992) (“The irreducible constitutional minimum of standing contains three elements: injury in fact, causation, and redressability.”). Alaska’s claim that the NPS’s regulations interfere with its sovereign right to manage state lands is unavailing. *See Alfred L. Snapp & Son, Inc. v. Puerto Rico ex rel. Barez*, 458 U.S. 592, 601-02 (1982) (“States possess sovereign and proprietary interests that may be pursued via litigation.”). However, Alaska has not identified any actual conflict between its laws and the NPS’s regulations. *See Massachusetts v. EPA*, 549 U.S. 497, 520 n.17 (2007) (“A state does not have standing to protect its citizens from the operation of federal statutes.”).

Alaska’s claim that the NPS’s regulations impose administrative burdens is also unavailing. The alleged harms are speculative and not redressable by a favorable judgment. *See Lujan*, 504 U.S. at 564 (“Past exposure to illegal conduct does not in itself show a present case or controversy regarding injunctive relief.”). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Alaska lacks standing to challenge the NPS’s regulations. *Sturgeon*, 829 F.3d at 1078.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed. The NPS’s hovercraft ban applies to all lands and waters within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including state-owned river segments. Sturgeon lacks standing to challenge the regulations as applied to state-owned lands, and Alaska lacks standing to challenge the regulations altogether. The NPS’s authority to regulate activities within CSUs is consistent with ANILCA and the Organic Act.
