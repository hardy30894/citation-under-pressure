**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DOES NOT APPLY TO ALIENS WHO RE-ENTERED BEFORE ITS EFFECTIVE DATE**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries that occurred before its enactment. However, this Court has repeatedly held that "[s]tatutes are not to be given retroactive effect unless Congress has clearly manifested its intent to that effect." *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 260 (1994). Here, Congress did not clearly express such an intent.

The Tenth Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries—rests on an impermissible inference from silence. *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 445 F.3d 1171, 1177 (10th Cir. 2006). This approach disregards the well-settled rule that "[a]mbiguity concerning the temporal reach of a statute should be resolved in favor of prospective application." *Id.* at 1176 (citing *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 272). The reinstatement statute’s text, structure, and legislative history provide no clear indication that Congress intended it to apply retroactively. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001) (holding that ambiguous statutory text should not be construed to apply retroactively where doing so would attach "new legal consequences to events completed before [the statute’s] enactment").

**II. APPLYING THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE TO PRE-ENFORCEMENT RE-ENTRIES IS IMPERMISSIBLY RETROACTIVE**

Even if the statute’s text were ambiguous, its application to pre-enactment re-entries would violate the Constitution’s prohibition on retroactive legislation. *See generally Miller v. Florida*, 482 U.S. 423, 430 (1987) ("The ex post facto Clause forbids the application of a law that changes the legal consequences of acts completed before its effective date."). A law is retroactive if it "attaches a new disability, in respect to transactions or considerations already past." *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269 (quoting *Society for Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.)).

Here, Petitioner’s re-entry occurred in 1982—decades before the reinstatement statute’s 1997 enactment. Applying the statute to him now imposes a new legal consequence (deportation without a hearing) for conduct that was lawful at the time. *See St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321 ("[A] statute does not operate retroactively merely because some of the events it regulates occurred before its enactment."). The Tenth Circuit’s contrary holding—that Petitioner had no "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status—misapprehends retroactivity principles. *Fernandez-Vargas*, 445 F.3d at 1180. The relevant inquiry is whether the statute alters the legal consequences of past conduct, not whether the individual had a subjective expectation of future benefits. *See Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270 ("The fact that [a statute] upsets otherwise settled expectations is not a sufficient reason to find it retroactive.").

**III. THE REINSTATEMENT STATUTE DEPRIVES PETITIONER OF DUE PROCESS**

The reinstatement statute also violates due process by denying Petitioner an individualized hearing before deportation. *See Reno v. Flores*, 507 U.S. 292, 306 (1993) ("Due process entitles aliens to notice and a hearing before deportation."). The statute’s automatic reinstatement procedure—without an opportunity to present defenses—is constitutionally infirm. *See Kwong v. Mukasey*, 549 F.3d 1094, 1100 (9th Cir. 2008) ("[A]n alien facing deportation is entitled to a full and fair hearing.").

The government’s reliance on *INS v. Lopez-Mendoza*, 468 U.S. 1032 (1984), is misplaced. That case addressed the exclusion of illegally obtained evidence in deportation proceedings, not the wholesale denial of a hearing. *Id.* at 1050-51. Here, Petitioner is denied any opportunity to challenge his deportation, a deprivation far more severe than evidentiary rules. *See Kwong*, 549 F.3d at 1101 ("The reinstatement statute’s summary procedure violates due process.").

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Tenth Circuit’s decision and hold that the reinstatement statute does not apply to pre-enactment re-entries and that its application in this case is unconstitutionally retroactive.
