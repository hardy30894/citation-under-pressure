**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE FOURTEENTH AMENDMENT DOES NOT REQUIRE STATES TO ADMIT EXPERT MENTAL-STATE EVIDENCE SEPARATE FROM AN INSANITY DEFENSE.**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a criminal defendant a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s accusations. *In re Oliver*, 333 U.S. 257, 273 (1948). But this Court has never held that due process compels a State to admit expert psychiatric evidence on mens rea when that evidence is already channeled through an insanity defense. To the contrary, this Court has consistently recognized that States may define the boundaries of admissible evidence and allocate burdens of proof without offending the Constitution.

In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld an Oregon statute that required a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, rejecting the argument that due process required the State to bear that burden. The Court explained that “the definition of the elements of a crime, and the determination of the defenses thereto, are questions of state law.” *Id.* at 797. Because Oregon had chosen to treat insanity as an affirmative defense, the State could require the defendant to prove it. *Id.* at 798. The same logic applies here: Arizona has chosen to treat evidence of mental disease or defect as relevant only to the insanity defense, not to negate mens rea. That choice is a permissible exercise of state authority.

Similarly, in *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court held that a trial court did not err in excluding expert testimony on a defendant’s mental condition when that testimony was offered to show lack of premeditation. The Court observed that “the exclusion of evidence offered to prove a fact which is not in issue is not error.” *Id.* at 476. Arizona’s rule simply reflects the State’s determination that mental disease or defect, short of insanity, does not negate the specific intent required for the crime. That determination is a substantive rule of law, not a procedural bar to a fair trial.

**II. THE DEFENDANT’S PROPOSED RIGHT WOULD DISRUPT THE STATE’S SOVEREIGN AUTHORITY TO DEFINE CRIMES AND DEFENSES.**

This Court has long recognized that “the power to define criminal offenses and to prescribe the punishments to be imposed therefor resides wholly with the States.” *Alabama v. King & Boozer*, 314 U.S. 1, 9 (1941). Arizona has defined first-degree murder to require the specific intent to kill. *See Ariz. Rev. Stat. § 13-1105(A)(1)*. The State has also defined insanity as a complete defense, placing the burden on the defendant to prove it by clear and convincing evidence. *See State v. Schantz*, 98 Ariz. 200, 207, 403 P.2d 521, 525 (1965). Nothing in the Fourteenth Amendment requires Arizona to allow a defendant to use the same evidence of mental disease to attack the mens rea element while simultaneously invoking the insanity defense.

In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), this Court declined to constitutionalize a defense of chronic alcoholism, emphasizing that “the doctrines of *actus reus*, *mens rea*, insanity, mistake, justification, and duress have historically provided the tools for a constantly shifting adjustment of the tension between the evolving aims of the criminal law and changing religious, moral, philosophical, and medical views of the nature of man.” *Id.* at 536. The Court warned against “writing into the Constitution” a particular view of criminal responsibility. *Id.* at 535. The same caution applies here: Arizona’s rule reflects a considered judgment about the proper role of mental-disease evidence, and this Court should not displace that judgment.

**III. THE DEFENDANT RECEIVED A FAIR OPPORTUNITY TO PRESENT HIS DEFENSE.**

The defendant was permitted to present expert evidence of his paranoid schizophrenia to support his insanity claim. He was not barred from presenting any evidence; he was merely required to use it within the framework Arizona has established. This Court has never held that due process entitles a defendant to present the same evidence for two different purposes when the State has chosen to treat that evidence as relevant only to one. *See Stein v. New York*, 346 U.S. 156, 196 (1953) (holding that “the Fourteenth Amendment does not require that a defendant be permitted to present any evidence he chooses”). The defendant’s conviction and sentence were the result of a fair trial in which he had the opportunity to prove insanity. He failed to do so. The Constitution requires no more.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
