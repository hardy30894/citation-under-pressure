ARGUMENT

I. The Mandatory Preliminary Injunction Exceeded the Bounds of Equitable Discretion Because Shapiro Failed to Establish Irreparable Injury for Which There Was No Adequate Remedy at Law

The district court ordered Cadman Towers to hand over, immediately, a scarce ground-floor parking space that dozens of other residents had waited years to obtain. Such relief is not the ordinary preservation of a status quo pending trial; it is a mandatory injunction that itself grants Shapiro the final relief she seeks. This Court's predecessors have long insisted that "the basis for injunctive relief in the federal courts has always been irreparable injury and the inadequacy of legal remedies." Beacon Theatres, Inc. v. Westover, 359 U.S. 500, 506-07 (1959). Shapiro's own difficulties finding street parking, while sympathetic, do not establish that no remedy at law — including damages or an accelerated but orderly placement on the waiting list — could redress her injury.

Nor did Shapiro demonstrate the kind of ongoing, demonstrable harm that justifies extraordinary equitable intervention. As the Supreme Court explained in United States v. W.T. Grant Co., 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953), a court granting injunctive relief must be satisfied "that there exists some cognizable danger of recurrent violation, something more than the mere possibility." Here the record showed sporadic urinary accidents attributable to a general citywide parking shortage, not a specific, continuing violation by Cadman Towers of any duty owed uniquely to Shapiro ahead of every other similarly situated applicant. Mandatory injunctions altering the parties' existing rights, rather than merely preserving them, warrant particular caution, for such decrees compel affirmative action before the merits are finally adjudicated. See Virginian Ry. Co. v. System Federation No. 40, 300 U.S. 515, 552 (1937) (recognizing that compulsory decrees ordering affirmative performance are an extraordinary exercise of the equity power, appropriate only where the statutory right invoked leaves no other means of enforcement).

II. Even if Injunctive Relief Were Appropriate, the District Court Was Obliged to Balance the Equities and Mold Relief So As Not to Displace the Settled Expectations of Other Waiting-List Residents

Equity has never operated by rigid rule; it has always required the chancellor to weigh the competing interests of all affected parties. The Supreme Court's teaching in Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 321 U.S. 321, 329-30 (1944), remains controlling: "The essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case. Flexibility rather than rigidity has distinguished it." The district court's order did the opposite — it treated Shapiro's request as though it existed in isolation, ignoring the hardship inflicted on the numerous other tenants who had patiently followed the first-come/first-served system for years in reliance on its evenhandedness.

Similarly, in sustaining wartime price-control injunctions, the Supreme Court in Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 440-41 (1944), recognized that the propriety of injunctive relief depends upon a careful weighing of the harm to the party enjoined and to third parties against the benefit to the moving party. That balancing was never performed below. The court did not ask, and Shapiro did not prove, why her hardship should outweigh the reasonable, settled expectations of every other cooperator awaiter — some of whom may have disabilities or hardships of their own — who would be displaced by immediate reassignment of the only ground-floor space.

III. The Reasonable-Accommodation Mandate of the Fair Housing Amendments Act Must Be Construed According to Its Plain Terms and Cannot Be Read to Require Cadman Towers to Displace a Neutral Allocation System

The statutory duty is to make "reasonable accommodations," not to guarantee an applicant's preferred outcome regardless of the burden imposed on others. Where a statute's language is plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). The qualifier "reasonable" cannot be read out of the statute; it necessarily contemplates limits tied to the burden an accommodation places on the housing provider and on other residents. As the Supreme Court observed in United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542 (1940), "[t]here is, of course, no more persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute than the words by which the legislature undertook to give expression to its wishes." Congress's chosen word — "reasonable" — expresses precisely such a limitation, and it forecloses a construction that would require Cadman Towers to override a neutral, uniformly applied waiting-list system to satisfy any individual accommodation request instantly.

A first-come/first-served system, administered without regard to any resident's disability, race, or other protected characteristic, does not itself offend anti-discrimination principles merely because its neutral operation produces delay for a disabled applicant along with everyone else. Cf. Railway Mail Ass'n v. Corsi, 326 U.S. 88, 93-94 (1945) (recognizing that laws forbidding discrimination protect equality of treatment, not entitlement to preferred treatment). Cadman Towers offered to accommodate Shapiro's disability once she reached the top of the list by assigning her the nearest available space — precisely the kind of reasonable, proportionate accommodation the statute contemplates.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the preliminary injunction should be vacated.
