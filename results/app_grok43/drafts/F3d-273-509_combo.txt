The release executed by Bancorp does not bar its claims. Its terms release the FDIC only from claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language does not encompass the FDIC’s subsequent conduct as receiver in paying itself post-insolvency interest, charging expenses to the estate, or selling assets below franchise value. Releases are construed strictly according to their terms. Garrett v. Moore-McCormack Co., 317 U.S. 239, 246 (1942); Callen v. Pennsylvania R. Co., 332 U.S. 625, 629 (1948); Union Pac. R. Co. v. United States, 254 U.S. 38, 46 (1920). A release of claims arising from one transaction does not discharge claims arising from later, distinct acts. Meeker v. Lehigh Valley R. Co., 236 U.S. 412, 423 (1915).

The district court also erred in holding the claims time-barred. A receiver owes fiduciary duties of loyalty and accounting to those interested in the estate. Pepper v. Litton, 308 U.S. 295, 306-07 (1939). Those duties continue until the receivership terminates. Michoud v. Girod, 45 U.S. (4 How.) 503, 552-53 (1846). The cause of action for breach therefore accrues, and the statute begins to run, only upon termination. Kirby v. Lake Shore & Michigan Southern R. Co., 120 U.S. 130, 139 (1887); Burnet v. Willingham Loan & Trust Co., 282 U.S. 437, 439 (1931). The receivership ended November 1, 1995; the complaint filed the preceding day is timely.

Bancorp’s claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and waste rest on the FDIC’s post-closure conduct as receiver, not on the OCC’s 1985 closure decision. The release therefore affords no defense, and the statute of limitations did not begin to run until the receivership terminated.
