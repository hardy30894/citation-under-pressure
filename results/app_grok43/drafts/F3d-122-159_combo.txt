The Bureau of Prisons exceeds its authority by treating a sentencing enhancement as transforming the offense of conviction into a crime of violence. As explained in Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803), courts must declare “what the law is.” The statute permits reduction only for prisoners convicted of a nonviolent offense, 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B), and the offense here—conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance—contains no element of force. United States v. Fisher, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 358, 386 (1805), requires that “the words of the statute” control.

The regulation and Program Statement nevertheless reclassify the conviction on the basis of a two-level enhancement. This construction cannot stand. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 421 (1819), teaches that a power “must be exercised in subordination to the great fundamental principle” that the statute itself supplies. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 189 (1824), confirms that administrative action “must be consistent with the letter and spirit” of the congressional enactment.

Plain meaning supplies the limit. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 490 (1917), holds that “where the language is plain and admits of no more than one meaning the duty of interpretation does not arise.” The offense of conviction is nonviolent; the enhancement is not an element of that offense. Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States, 143 U.S. 457, 459 (1892), likewise directs that the statute be read according to “the ordinary meaning of its words.”

An agency may not enlarge its own power by redefining statutory terms. Manhattan General Equipment Co. v. Commissioner, 297 U.S. 129, 134 (1936), states that “a regulation which is inconsistent with the statute is without force.” Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944), adds that administrative interpretations deserve respect only to the extent they possess “power to persuade.” The Program Statement lacks that power because it contradicts the statute’s focus on the offense of conviction rather than sentencing factors.

Finally, the rule of lenity, recognized in United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820), requires that ambiguity be resolved in favor of the prisoner. The Bureau therefore may not withhold eligibility.
