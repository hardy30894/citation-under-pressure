ARGUMENT

I. The Disclosure Provisions Are Facially Overbroad and Violate the First Amendment

The First Amendment shields lobbying—core political speech—from unduly burdensome disclosure. *Buckley v. Valeo*, 424 U.S. 1, 14 (1976) (per curiam) (recognizing that “discussion of public issues” is “core” speech). Florida’s sweeping disclosure requirements, covering all expenditures “whether made by the lobbyist or by the principal,” Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a), and including categories such as “research, communication, media advertising, publications,” id., unconstitutionally chill association and expression. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (compelled disclosure of membership lists “may constitute an effective restraint on the exercise of First Amendment rights”). The statute imposes no threshold or nexus to corruption, making it substantially overbroad. *Bates v. City of Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516, 524 (1960) (invalidating disclosure requirement that “bears no rational relationship to the purpose for which it is sought”).

Appellant’s members face a Hobson’s choice: refrain from protected activity or risk administrative sanctions. *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 152–53 (1967). Because the statute reaches substantial protected speech, the facial challenge is proper. *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 745 (1987) (facial invalidity requires showing “no set of circumstances” under which the law is valid). Here, the disclosure mandate is not narrowly tailored to serve a compelling state interest. *McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm’n*, 514 U.S. 334, 357 (1995) (strict scrutiny applies to content-based disclosure laws). Even if the state’s anticorruption interest is legitimate, the statute’s overbreadth is fatal. *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 366–67 (2010) (disclosure is permissible only if “it is justified by a sufficient governmental interest” and “narrowly tailored”). The district court erred in granting summary judgment.

II. The Contingency-Fee Ban Unconstitutionally Restricts Protected Speech and Association

Florida’s absolute prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying, Fla. Stat. § 11.047, “bars lobbyists from receiving fees contingent on their success in affecting legislative or executive outcomes,” directly burdening the speaker’s incentive and ability to engage in advocacy. *Meyer v. Grant*, 486 U.S. 414, 422–23 (1988) (ban on paid petition circulators “imposes a burden on political expression” that cannot survive strict scrutiny). The Supreme Court has never upheld a blanket ban on contingent compensation for lobbying. The old pronouncements cited by the district court are inapposite. *United States v. Harriss*, 347 U.S. 612, 624 (1954) (sustaining disclosure, not a ban on compensation structures). More recent precedent confirms that restrictions on the “manner of compensation” for speech are subject to rigorous review. *Riley v. Nat’l Fed’n of the Blind of N.C., Inc.*, 487 U.S. 781, 801 (1988) (invalidating state limits on fees paid to professional fundraisers because “the State’s interest in protecting charities and their donors is not sufficient to justify the First Amendment burden”).

The contingency-fee ban is a content-based restriction that discriminates based on the speaker’s financial interest in the outcome. *Arkansas Writers’ Project, Inc. v. Ragland*, 481 U.S. 221, 230 (1987) (discriminatory tax on magazines violated First Amendment). The state’s asserted interest in preventing corruption is not furthered by a blanket prohibition; less restrictive alternatives exist, such as disclosure or caps. *FEC v. National Conservative Political Action Comm.*, 470 U.S. 480, 500 (1985) (citing “less drastic means” of regulating campaign contributions). Moreover, the ban operates as a prior restraint on speech, a “forbidden intrusion into the First Amendment.” *Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson*, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931). The district court’s reliance on outdated precedent ignores the Supreme Court’s evolving scrutiny of economic regulations that chill political expression. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 438–39 (1963) (litigation cannot be “proscribed” by “vague and unlimited” prohibitions on compensation arrangements). The Eleventh Circuit should reverse.

CONCLUSION

The district court erred in sustaining Chapter 93-121. The disclosure provisions are facially overbroad, and the contingency-fee ban is an unconstitutional burden on core political speech. This Court should reverse summary judgment and remand for entry of judgment in favor of the Florida League of Professional Lobbyists.
