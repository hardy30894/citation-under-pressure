**ARGUMENT**

**I. Standard of Review**

Because Hill did not object at trial, this Court reviews for plain error. Under that standard, reversal is warranted where the error is obvious and affects substantial rights. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (appellate courts may notice unobjected-to errors "if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings"). Plain-error review does not excuse errors that strike at the heart of the jury's fact-finding function; it exists precisely to correct them. *Johnson v. United States*, 318 U.S. 189 (1943).

**II. Agent Jones's Testimony That Hill Was Lying Invaded the Jury's Exclusive Province to Assess Credibility**

The determination of whether a witness is telling the truth belongs to the jury alone. This principle is not new; it is the bedrock of Anglo-American trial practice and has long been enforced by the federal courts. In *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Ward*, 140 U.S. 76 (1891), the Supreme Court made clear that the weight and credibility of testimony are matters committed to the jury's judgment, not to be supplanted by the opinion of the court or of any witness. The Court reaffirmed and sharpened this rule in *Quercia v. United States*, 289 U.S. 466 (1933), reversing a conviction because the trial judge told the jury that the defendant's nervous gesture while testifying was a sign that he was lying. The Court held that even a trial judge—whose "lightest word or intimation is received with deference" by the jury—may not tell the jury which witnesses to believe or why; that determination is the jury's and the jury's alone. *Id.* at 470.

If a judge may not comment on a defendant's truthfulness, an FBI agent certainly may not. Yet that is precisely what occurred here. Agent Jones did not merely describe Hill's demeanor for the jury to evaluate; he told the jury, in the guise of expertise, that Hill's answers "did not make sense," that Hill exhibited behaviors "common among the criminal element," that he had "never" seen an innocent person express Hill's sentiments, and that Hill's invocations of God were themselves "deceptive statements." This was not evidence; it was a verdict on credibility delivered from the witness stand, dressed in the authority of federal law enforcement training. Under *Quercia* and *Aetna Life*, that testimony usurped the jury's exclusive function and was error.

**III. Agent Jones's Deception-Detection Testimony Was Inadmissible Absent a Showing of General Scientific Acceptance**

Agent Jones's testimony was, in substance, expert lie-detection testimony: he claimed specialized "training" enabling him to "identify deception in statements and truths in statements." Such testimony has long been held inadmissible unless the technique underlying it has gained general acceptance in the relevant scientific community. In the seminal decision *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), this Court's sister circuit excluded expert testimony based on a deception-detection device because "the thing from which the deduction is made must be sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field in which it belongs." *Id.* at 1014. No such foundation was laid for Agent Jones's asserted ability to discern truth from deception based on an interviewee's demeanor, word choice, or references to faith. There was no showing that his methodology for detecting deception—as opposed to ordinary investigative experience—satisfied any recognized scientific standard. Absent that showing, his opinion that Hill was lying was no more competent than the polygraph evidence excluded in *Frye*, and it should not have reached the jury.

**IV. The Error Was Plain and Affected Hill's Substantial Rights, Requiring Reversal**

The error here was not a technical misstep; it went to the heart of the jury's task. The government's case against Hill depended heavily on circumstantial evidence and on the jury's assessment of his own statements during interrogation—statements that Agent Jones told the jury were the marks of a guilty man. This is not a case, as in *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935), where the prosecution's excesses were harmless; there, as here, the prosecutor's duty is "to refrain from improper methods calculated to produce a wrongful conviction," and where such methods are employed, the "conviction... resting on error" cannot stand undisturbed. *Id.* Agent Jones's testimony told the jury exactly what conclusion to reach regarding the central contested issue at trial—Hill's truthfulness and, by extension, his guilt. That the jury previously deadlocked on identical charges against Hill at his first trial, where no such testimony was admitted, underscores how closely balanced the evidence was and how significant the improper testimony must have been to the verdict obtained at the second trial.

Because the erroneous admission of Agent Jones's testimony was plain, invaded the jury's exclusive credibility-determination function under *Quercia* and *Aetna Life*, lacked the scientific foundation required by *Frye*, and affected the fairness of Hill's trial within the meaning of *Atkinson*, this Court should vacate Hill's conviction and remand for a new trial.
