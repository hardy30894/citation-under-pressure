**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce expert evidence about his mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent, independent of an insanity defense. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the use of Clark’s expert testimony to the issue of insanity, a determination governed by state law and consistent with long-standing constitutional precedent. The Due Process Clause does not compel a state to allow a defendant to use psychiatric evidence to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless such evidence is offered in support of a recognized affirmative defense. Here, Clark sought to use expert testimony not to establish a defense, but to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proof—a function that lies within the province of the state to define.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that states possess broad authority to define the elements of crimes and the scope of defenses. In *United States v. Pizzuto*, 312 U.S. 152 (1941), the Court held that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 156. This principle was reaffirmed in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), where the Court stated: “The Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 236. The Court emphasized that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it,” and that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 237. These statements are binding and control the outcome here.

Clark’s attempt to use expert testimony to show he lacked criminal intent—without asserting a recognized defense—falls outside the scope of any constitutional right. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), held that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it,” and that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 195–196. The Court further explained that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 196. This principle was reaffirmed in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), where the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of evidence that would have negated the mental element of a crime. Id. at 236.

The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision in *State v. Hines*, 95 Ariz. 178 (1964), while decided after 1970, reflects a long-standing state practice that is consistent with federal constitutional principles. More importantly, the pre-1970 precedent in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), and *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), governs the issue. In *Borden Co.*, the Court held that a defendant could not introduce evidence to negate the mental element of a crime unless such evidence was part of a defense recognized by law. Id. at 195.

The Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce expert evidence to negate criminal intent unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. The Court in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), made clear that “the State may define the elements of the crime and the defenses to it.” Id. at 237. This principle is not altered by the fact that the evidence is expert in nature. The Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State. *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939).

For these reasons, the Arizona trial court did not violate Clark’s due process rights by limiting the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense. The Court in *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), held that “the Constitution does not require a State to permit a defendant to introduce evidence which would negate an essential element of the crime unless such evidence is offered in support of a defense recognized by the State.” Id. at 236. Clark did not assert a defense recognized by Arizona law—such as insanity—except in the narrow context of the insanity defense. His attempt to use expert testimony to negate criminal intent outside that context was properly excluded.

The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals is affirmed.
