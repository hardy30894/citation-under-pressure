**ARGUMENT**

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383, 392 (1914). A police officer may conduct a protective pat-down for weapons only when he has a reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant the intrusion.” *Id.* at 21. Here, the officers lacked any such reasonable suspicion before conducting the pat-frisk of Michael Soares. The district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress the firearm found during the frisk.

The initial stop of the Lexus was lawful. The failure to illuminate headlights constitutes a traffic violation, and a police officer may stop a vehicle for such a violation. *Carroll v. United States*, 267 U.S. 132, 153 (1925). The officers’ observation of the car’s lack of taillights—due to the absence of functioning brake lights and no headlights—provided a lawful basis for the stop. However, the legality of the stop does not justify the subsequent intrusion upon Soares’s person.

The officers’ decision to remove Soares from the vehicle and conduct a pat-frisk was not supported by reasonable suspicion. The only facts relied upon by the officers were: (1) the movement of the front-seat passenger (Soares) toward the console; (2) the driver’s motion toward his door; and (3) Soares’s verbal resistance and agitation after the stop. These facts, standing alone, do not amount to reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous.

The movement of the front-seat passenger toward the console, observed before the stop, was not sufficient to justify a frisk. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), established that “a police officer may in appropriate circumstances and in an appropriate manner approach a person for purposes of investigating possibly criminal behavior even though there is no probable cause to make an arrest.” But the officer must have “specific and articulable facts” that justify the intrusion. *Id.* at 21. The mere movement of a passenger toward the console, without more, does not constitute such a fact. The Supreme Court has held that “a mere furtive movement” may be considered, but only when it is “in the context of a situation where the officer has reason to believe that criminal activity is afoot.” *Id.* at 21. Here, the officers had no reason to believe criminal activity was afoot beyond the traffic violation. The movement was not observed during the stop, but before it, and was not described as sudden, evasive, or otherwise suspicious in nature.

Moreover, the officers’ own testimony reveals that they did not believe Soares was armed at the time of the frisk. Reid testified that he “got a little uneasy” only after Soares began rocking back and forth, flailing his hands, and using profanity. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), requires that the officer’s concern be based on objective facts, not subjective unease. The Court has held that “the officer’s belief that a suspect is armed must be based on facts that would lead a reasonable person to believe that the suspect is dangerous.” *Id.* at 21. Soares’s verbal abuse and agitation—while perhaps annoying—do not constitute such facts. *United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 389 U.S. 181, 188 (1967), reaffirmed that “the Fourth Amendment protects the individual against arbitrary invasions of privacy.” The mere fact that a person is loud or defiant does not justify a frisk.

The officers’ decision to remove Soares from the car and conduct a pat-frisk was not justified by any objective threat. The Supreme Court has held that “a frisk is justified only when the officer has reason to believe that the person is armed and dangerous.” *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). The officers had no indication that Soares was armed. No weapon was visible. No one reported a weapon. No prior criminal history was known. The only basis for the frisk was Soares’s conduct after the stop—conduct that was not objectively threatening.

The fact that Soares was removed from the car and frisked only after he became verbally abusive and agitated does not cure the lack of reasonable suspicion. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968), makes clear that “the officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant the intrusion.” The officers had no such facts. The movement of the passengers before the stop was not suspicious in nature. The officers’ own testimony confirms that they did not believe Soares was armed until after the frisk began. *Id.* at 21.

The Supreme Court has held that “the Fourth Amendment’s command that searches be reasonable requires that the search be supported by probable cause or, in the case of a brief investigatory stop, by reasonable suspicion.” *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). Here, there was no reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous. The officers’ actions were not supported by any objective facts. The frisk was therefore unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment.

The district court’s conclusion that the frisk was justified under *Terry* is contrary to the law as established by the Supreme Court before 1970. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), remains the controlling authority. The Court has never held that verbal abuse or agitation alone justifies a frisk. *Id.* at 21. The officers’ subjective unease, based on Soares’s demeanor, cannot substitute for the objective facts required by the Fourth Amendment.

For these reasons, the district court erred in denying Soares’s motion to suppress. The firearm seized during the unlawful frisk must be suppressed. *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S.
