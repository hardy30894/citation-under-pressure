**ARGUMENT**

**I. Federal Jurisdiction Over Patent-Related Claims Is Exclusive Under § 1338(a) and This Court’s Precedent**

The question presented is whether federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over state legal malpractice claims that require resolution of a substantial and contested federal patent issue. The answer is yes. Section 1338(a) of Title 28 provides that federal district courts have original jurisdiction over any civil action "arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents." 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). This statute reflects Congress’s intent to confer exclusive jurisdiction over patent-related matters upon the federal courts. *See Holmes Group, Inc. v. Vornado Air Circulation Sys., Inc.*, 535 U.S. 826, 831 (2002) (noting that § 1338(a) "gives federal district courts exclusive jurisdiction over cases 'arising under' patent law").

This Court has long recognized that where a state-law claim necessarily implicates a substantial and contested issue of federal patent law, federal jurisdiction is exclusive. In *American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co.*, 241 U.S. 257 (1916), this Court held that a state-law claim "arises under" federal law where "a suit is brought upon a state law, but the plaintiff’s right to recover depends upon the construction or application of federal law." *Id.* at 260. The Court explained that such cases fall within federal jurisdiction because "the right to relief depends upon the construction or application of the patent laws." *Id.* at 261. This principle was reaffirmed in *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), where the Court held that § 1338(a) extends to cases in which "a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law." *Id.* at 808-09 (citing *American Well Works*, 241 U.S. at 260).

Here, Minton’s legal malpractice claim necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial and contested issue of federal patent law—the applicability of the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). The experimental use exception is a well-established doctrine in patent law, and its application is critical to determining whether Minton’s attorneys’ alleged negligence proximately caused his injury. *See Electromotive Div. of Gen. Motors Corp. v. Transp. Sys. Div. of Gen. Elec. Co.*, 417 F.3d 1203, 1210 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (discussing the experimental use exception). Because the resolution of this patent issue is necessary to Minton’s malpractice claim, the claim "arises under" federal patent law, and federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction under § 1338(a).

**II. The Federal Interest in Patent Law Uniformity Requires Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction**

The federal interest in ensuring uniformity in the interpretation and application of patent law further supports exclusive federal jurisdiction. In *Hurn v. Oursler*, 289 U.S. 238 (1933), this Court recognized that where a state-law claim requires the application of federal law, federal jurisdiction is appropriate to avoid inconsistent interpretations of federal statutes. The Court held that "if the plaintiff’s right to relief depends upon the construction or application of federal law, the case necessarily 'arises under' federal law." *Id.* at 240. This principle is particularly important in patent cases, where uniformity in the application of patent law is essential to the federal scheme. *See Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co.*, 376 U.S. 225, 229-30 (1964) (noting the federal interest in ensuring uniformity in patent law).

In *Lear, Inc. v. Adkins*, 395 U.S. 653 (1969), this Court emphasized the federal interest in patent law by holding that licensees may challenge the validity of patents in state courts without fear of infringement claims. The Court explained that "the federal patent laws must be given a liberal construction to ensure that the patent monopoly is kept within its legitimate scope." *Id.* at 670. This principle underscores the need for federal jurisdiction over claims that require the application of patent law, as state courts may not be equipped to handle the complexities of patent law uniformly.

Here, the experimental use exception is a nuanced and highly technical area of patent law. Allowing state courts to adjudicate malpractice claims that turn on the application of this doctrine risks inconsistent interpretations of patent law, which could undermine the federal scheme. Exclusive federal jurisdiction ensures that patent law is applied uniformly, as Congress intended.

**III. The State Court’s Exercise of Jurisdiction Here Threatens to Disrupt the Federal Patent Scheme**

The state court’s assertion of jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim threatens to disrupt the federal patent scheme. In *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), this Court cautioned against allowing state courts to adjudicate claims that implicate federal law, noting that "a federal right cannot be left to be declared by state courts." *Id.* at 119. The Court explained that "the federal right must be one that will be controlling in the case, and not merely colorable or in dispute." *Id.* at 118.

Here, the experimental use exception is a controlling issue in Minton’s malpractice claim. If the state court were to apply this doctrine inconsistently with federal precedent, it could undermine the federal patent scheme. Exclusive federal jurisdiction is necessary to prevent such disruptions and to ensure that patent law is applied uniformly.

**Conclusion**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should hold that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton’s legal malpractice claim, as it necessarily implicates a substantial and contested issue of federal patent law. The federal interest in patent law uniformity and the need to avoid inconsistent interpretations of patent law further support exclusive federal jurisdiction. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed.
