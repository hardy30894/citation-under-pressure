Argument

I. The ADEA excludes appointed state judges from its coverage.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri judges appointed under the Non-Partisan Court Plan exercise precisely that function. “Policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Their decisions “establish either general standards or specific rules of decision that may be relied upon in the future,” id., and they exercise “considerable policymaking responsibility in the supervision of the Missouri court system.” 898 F.2d at 605.

Because the statute’s plain text excludes them, the ADEA does not apply. “In traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved.” United States v. Bass, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971). Congress gave no such clear statement here.

II. The mandatory retirement provision satisfies equal protection.

Age is not a suspect classification. “This Court’s decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental.” Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976). The provision therefore need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest. Missouri’s rule “eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence” and “makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary.” 898 F.2d at 608 (quoting Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen., 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988)). It also “reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges.” Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). These rationales suffice. “One might disagree with some or all of these rationales on either empirical or philosophical grounds and yet be unable properly to conclude that a state which thinks otherwise lacks a rational basis.” 898 F.2d at 608.

The judgment should be affirmed.
