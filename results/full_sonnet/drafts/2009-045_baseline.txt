# ARGUMENT

## THE LODESTAR MAY BE ENHANCED IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CASES WHERE SPECIFIC EVIDENCE DEMONSTRATES SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS

This Court has never held that enhancement of the lodestar for quality of performance and results obtained is categorically forbidden. To the contrary, in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), this Court expressly left room for such enhancements, explaining that "the special skill and experience of counsel should be reflected in the reasonableness of the hourly rates charged" but that "an upward adjustment" remains available "in the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.* at 899. The Court denied enhancement in *Blum* not because such adjustments are per se impermissible, but because "the District Court's finding of exceptional results" was not accompanied by the requisite specific evidentiary support. *Id.* This case-specific rejection is the mirror image of, not a bar to, enhancement where such evidence exists.

This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), stating that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee" and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435. *Blum* quoted this language with approval. 465 U.S. at 901. The Court could not have "reject[ed] petitioner's argument that an upward adjustment to an attorney's fee is never appropriate under § 1988," *id.*, while simultaneously holding that such adjustments are categorically unavailable.

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) ("*Delaware Valley I*"), confirms rather than forecloses this rule. The Court there held only that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar." *Id.* at 566 (emphasis added). The word "ordinarily" preserves the extraordinary case. And the Court expressly reaffirmed that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). Had this Court intended to overrule *Blum*'s carefully preserved exception, it would have said so; instead it cited *Blum* with approval throughout its opinion. *Id.* at 564, 568.

Every circuit to address the question has read *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* the same way respondents do here. As the Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized below, the Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits all permit enhancement for exceptional performance and results upon a showing of specific evidence. *See, e.g.*, *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000) (enhancement "justified only in the rare case where there is specific evidence that the quality of service was superior in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional"); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999) (affirming enhancement "on account of the exceptional results obtained"). This unanimity among the courts of appeals reflects the correct understanding that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*, read together, preserve the district court's discretion to enhance in the truly rare case.

Sound fee-shifting policy compels this same conclusion. The purpose of statutes like 42 U.S.C. § 1988 is to enable victims of civil rights violations "to secure legal assistance" adequate to enforce their rights. *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565. A categorical bar on enhancement would prevent district courts from accounting for the rare case in which counsel's performance and the result achieved genuinely exceed what the lodestar, calculated on prevailing hourly rates, could have been expected to produce. The district court here made precisely the kind of "detailed findings" this Court's precedents require, finding that "the superb quality of counsel's representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee." Pet. App. 34a. That finding rested on the court's own extensive familiarity with the Atlanta legal market, built over "58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge," *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1290 (N.D. Ga. 2006), as well as specific record evidence concerning the extraordinary comprehensiveness of the consent decree obtained on behalf of foster children.

Petitioners' position — that quality and results may *never* justify enhancement — would require this Court to disavow the plain language of *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* alike, language every circuit has understood to preserve, not eliminate, the district court's discretion in the extraordinary case. Nothing in this Court's precedents demands that result. The lodestar remains "presumed to be the reasonable fee," *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565, and enhancement remains available only upon the specific evidentiary showing this record supplies. The judgment below should be affirmed.
