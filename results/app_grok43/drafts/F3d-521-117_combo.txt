The Fourth Amendment forbids unreasonable searches and seizures. Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961). A traffic stop, though lawful at inception, does not authorize a pat-frisk absent specific, articulable facts supporting a reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous. Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 27 (1968). The officer must point to “particular facts from which he reasonably inferred that the individual was armed and dangerous.” Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968). Mere nervousness or verbal protest does not supply that inference. Beck v. Ohio, 379 U.S. 89, 91 (1964).

Here the officers observed only that the front-seat passenger moved his hands and later became agitated, rocking and using profanity while the driver’s license was checked. These acts, viewed in the light most favorable to the ruling below, fall short of the “reasonably warrant[ed]” suspicion required before a frisk may occur. Terry, 392 U.S. at 27. The record contains no testimony that Soares reached for a weapon, made a threatening gesture, or was known to carry firearms. Compare Brinegar v. United States, 338 U.S. 160, 162-63 (1949) (requiring facts that would “warrant a man of reasonable caution” to believe criminal activity is afoot).

The government may not justify the frisk by later-discovered evidence or by the general character of the neighborhood. Johnson v. United States, 333 U.S. 10, 16-17 (1948). Nor does the presence of backup officers convert routine agitation into reasonable fear; the Fourth Amendment measures the justification at the moment the frisk is initiated. Chimel v. California, 395 U.S. 752, 762-63 (1969). Because the officers lacked the requisite particularized suspicion, the frisk violated the Fourth Amendment.

The handgun discovered as a direct result of that unlawful frisk must be suppressed. Weeks v. United States, 232 U.S. 383, 393 (1914); Carroll v. United States, 267 U.S. 132, 156 (1925). The district court’s denial of the motion therefore cannot stand.
