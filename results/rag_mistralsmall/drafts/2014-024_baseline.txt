**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Amendment’s “True Threat” Exception Requires Proof of the Defendant’s Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

The First Amendment does not protect “true threats.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). But this Court has repeatedly held that a “true threat” is one made with the *intent* to threaten. *Id.* at 360 (“[T]rue threats … require[] some showing of intent.”); *id.* at 365 (O’Connor, J., concurring in the judgment) (“[T]rue threats … encompass statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence.”). The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—under which a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable speaker would foresee it would be interpreted as a threat—is inconsistent with this Court’s precedents.

In *Black*, this Court expressly rejected the objective approach adopted by the Third Circuit in *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991). *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359-60. The Court held that the First Amendment requires proof of the defendant’s *subjective* intent to threaten. *Id.* at 360. The Court explained that an objective standard would “‘[run] contrary to common sense’” and “‘fail[] to protect individuals from the kind of harm the “true threat” exception seeks to prevent.’” *Id.* (quoting *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360). The Third Circuit’s rule, which Elonis challenges here, is precisely the kind of objective standard this Court rejected in *Black*.

The government’s contrary arguments are unavailing. The government relies on *Sexton v. California*, 189 U.S. 319 (1903), but that case involved a state extortion statute and did not address the First Amendment’s “true threat” exception. *Sexton*, 189 U.S. at 321. Similarly, *Brown v. Pierce*, 74 U.S. 205 (1869), addressed common-law duress, not the First Amendment. *Brown*, 74 U.S. at 207. Neither case supports the government’s position.

The government also cites *Mary Gormley v. Director, Connecticut State Department of Adult Probation*, 449 U.S. 1023 (1980), but that case involved a state telephone harassment statute and did not address the First Amendment’s “true threat” exception. *Gormley*, 449 U.S. at 1023. The government’s reliance on *Waley v. Johnston*, 316 U.S. 101 (1942), is equally misplaced, as that case involved a habeas corpus petition alleging coercion to plead guilty, not a First Amendment challenge to a “true threat” conviction. *Waley*, 316 U.S. at 101.

This Court’s decision in *Black* controls. The Third Circuit’s objective standard is inconsistent with *Black* and must be reversed.

**II. The Government’s Failure to Prove Subjective Intent Violates the First Amendment and Due Process.**

The government concedes that Elonis’s posts were “rap lyrics” and that he did not subjectively intend them as threats. *See* Brief for the United States at 10. But the government argues that an objective standard suffices. This argument is foreclosed by *Black*. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. The government’s reliance on *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Echazabal*, 536 U.S. 73 (2002), is misplaced, as that case involved the Americans with Disabilities Act, not the First Amendment. *Echazabal*, 536 U.S. at 75.

The government’s position also violates due process. The Fifth Amendment requires proof of *mens rea* for criminal convictions. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The government’s objective standard dispenses with this requirement, rendering the statute unconstitutionally vague. *See* *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 358 (1983) (holding that a statute is unconstitutionally vague if it fails to give fair warning of the conduct it proscribes).

**III. The Third Circuit’s Decision Conflicts with This Court’s Precedents and Must Be Reversed.**

The Third Circuit’s decision in this case conflicts with *Black* and this Court’s other precedents. The court held that a “true threat” requires only an objective intent to threaten, but *Black* requires proof of the defendant’s *subjective* intent. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360. The Third Circuit’s decision must be reversed to comport with this Court’s precedents.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be reversed.
