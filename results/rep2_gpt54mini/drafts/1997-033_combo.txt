**Argument**

Section 1407(a) does not permit a transferee court, while acting only in the course of “pretrial proceedings,” to assign the case to itself for trial under § 1404(a). The statute’s text is explicit: civil actions may be transferred for “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Congress used mandatory language, and courts must give effect to that command. **Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399 (1821)**; **Green v. Bock Laundry Machine Co., 490 U.S. 504, 509 (1989)** (statutory text controls where clear). Although the present question concerns venue, the point is the same: a court may not “fill gaps” in a statute when Congress has spoken directly. **Iselin v. United States, 270 U.S. 245, 250-51 (1926)**.

This Court has long refused to treat judicial convenience as a license to disregard enacted venue rules. In **Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58-60 (1949)**, the Court stressed that venue statutes must be enforced according to their terms; and in **United States v. National City Lines, Inc., 337 U.S. 78, 81-82 (1949)**, it reiterated that courts may not rework venue legislation under the guise of construction. Those principles control here. Section 1407(a) does not merely permit pretrial coordination; it separately commands remand to the transferor court. A transferee judge therefore lacks authority to convert a temporary pretrial assignment into a trial assignment of his own making.

The transferee court’s resort to § 1404(a) does not cure the problem. Section 1404(a) authorizes a district court to transfer a civil action “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only when venue is otherwise proper. **Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, 349 U.S. 29, 32 (1955)**. That general transfer statute cannot override a later-enacted, case-specific mandate that multidistrict actions “shall be remanded” after pretrial proceedings conclude. **Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp., 353 U.S. 222, 228-29 (1957)** (general venue provisions do not displace specific statutory limits); **United States v. Oklahoma Gas & Electric Co., 318 U.S. 206, 210-11 (1943)** (specific statutory direction governs over broader general language). If Congress intended transferee judges to keep cases for trial, it would have said so. It did not.

Nor can § 1404(a) be used to accomplish indirectly what § 1407(a) forbids directly. Courts have repeatedly recognized that venue statutes are not tools for judicial preference. **Ex parte Chas. Pfizer & Co., 225 U.S. 377, 383 (1912)**, and **Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 507-08 (1947)**, both reflect that the plaintiff’s chosen forum deserves substantial respect unless Congress has authorized transfer under defined standards. Here, Congress defined the limits: pretrial coordination only, followed by remand. A transferee court’s sua sponte self-assignment for trial nullifies that limit and makes the mandatory remand clause a dead letter.

The better reading is confirmed by the historic understanding that a case may be moved only as the statute authorizes, not as judicial economy suggests. **Ex parte Simons, 247 U.S. 231, 239-40 (1918)**, recognized that transfer power is statutory, not inherent; and **Hill v. United States ex rel. Wampler, 298 U.S. 460, 463 (1936)**, teaches that courts may not enlarge their authority beyond the statute’s words. Section 1407(a) gives transferee courts power over “pretrial proceedings,” but not trial. Once pretrial proceedings end, the case must go back. That conclusion follows from the statute’s text and from the settled rule that courts cannot treat convenience as a substitute for authority. **Cohens, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) at 399**; **Iselin, 270 U.S. at 250-51**.

Respondent’s contrary position would collapse the statutory distinction between pretrial coordination and trial. But Congress drew that distinction deliberately. The Court should enforce it as written and hold that a district court conducting § 1407(a) pretrial proceedings may not invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case for trial.
