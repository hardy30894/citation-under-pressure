**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Amendment Confers Upon Mrs. Davis a Qualifying Income Interest for Life Under § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)**

The sole statutory requirement at issue is whether the surviving spouse is “entitled to all the income from the property, payable annually or at more frequent intervals.” I.R.C. § 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(I). Although the marital deduction is a matter of legislative grace, *United States v. Stapf*, 375 U.S. 118, 128 (1963), the deduction is allowed when the trust instrument, read as a whole, gives the spouse an unqualified right to all income. *Jackson v. United States*, 376 U.S. 503, 508-09 (1964). The Tax Court erred by rewriting the Amendment’s unambiguous mandate that the trustee “pay to or apply for the benefit of the surviving spouse . . . all of the net income from the trust estate.”

**A. The Trustee’s Discretion Is Ministerial, Not a Power to Withhold or Accumulate Income**

The Amendment directs that the trustee “shall pay” all net income to or for the spouse in quarter-annual or more frequent installments. The phrase “as the trustee, in the trustee’s reasonable discretion, shall determine to be proper for the health, education, or support, maintenance, comfort and welfare” qualifies the *method* of payment, not the *amount*. A trustee who must distribute all income quarterly cannot accumulate or divert income; the discretion goes only to whether to pay the spouse directly or apply the income for her benefit. This reading comports with the principle that fiduciary discretion of a ministerial nature does not defeat an otherwise absolute income interest. *Cf. Jennings v. Commissioner*, 161 F.2d 74, 76 (2d Cir. 1947) (holding that a power to invade for support is an ascertainable standard, not a general power of appointment).

The Commissioner’s contrary interpretation would render the words “all of the net income” surplusage. Where, as here, a trust instrument expressly bestows all income on a sole beneficiary, the courts do not infer a power to withhold absent an express accumulation clause. *See Estate of Mittleman*, 52 T.C. 542, 548-49 (1969) (holding that a trust fails the “all income” requirement only where the trustee is *expressly authorized* to accumulate income). The Amendment contains no such clause.

**B. The “Other Sources” Guideline Is Not a Substantive Limitation**

The clause permitting the trustee to “consider any other income or resources” of the spouse is a discretionary guideline for determining how to pay the income, not whether to pay it. Unlike the trust in *Huesman v. Commissioner*, 198 F.2d 133, 134-35 (9th Cir. 1952), which allowed the trustee to withhold income if the spouse’s other resources were adequate, the Amendment does not condition the spouse’s entitlement on need. A trustee’s consideration of other resources in the exercise of a narrowly confined fiduciary duty does not transform the spouse’s right into an aspirational hope. *Cf. Suter v. Commissioner*, 29 T.C. 244, 251-52 (1957) (disqualifying a trust because the trustee could distribute income to dependents). Here, no person other than the spouse may receive income, and the Amendment expressly provides that the surviving spouse is the sole income beneficiary.

**C. The Support and “Comfort and Welfare” Standard Is Ascertainable**

The Commissioner may argue that “comfort and welfare” render the discretion unascertainable. But this Court need not reach that question because the primary standard is “health, education, or support,” which is a classic ascertainable standard. *Jennings*, 161 F.2d at 76. Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that even a power to invade for “comfort” is sufficiently measurable for actuarial purposes where the trustee is constrained by the beneficiary’s accustomed standard of living. *Ithaca Trust Co. v. United States*, 279 U.S. 151, 154-55 (1929). The Amendment expressly ties the distributions to the spouse’s “accustomed manner of living,” providing an objective benchmark. Thus, even if “comfort and welfare” go beyond bare subsistence, they remain ascertainable standards, not unfettered discretion. *See Canfield v. Commissioner*, 306 F.2d 1, 5-6 (8th Cir. 1962) (distinguishing a standard of “comfort” alone from a standard of support).

**D. No Person Has a Power to Appoint Any Part of the Property to Anyone Other Than the Surviving Spouse**

Section 2056(b)(7)(B)(ii)(II) prohibits any power to appoint property to a person other than the spouse. The Amendment gives the trustee no such power. The invasion-of-principal clause permits principal to be distributed only “for the benefit of surviving spouse” and only after the spouse’s own trust is exhausted. The successor beneficiaries cannot receive anything during the spouse’s lifetime. This satisfies the clear statutory requirement.

**II. The Tax Court’s Construction Violates the Command to Interpret Trust Language According to Its Plain Meaning**

Federal tax law looks to the legal rights created by the trust instrument. *Stapf*, 375 U.S. at 130. The Tax Court’s decision rewrites the plain meaning of “all of the net income” in order to infer an ambiguity. But the Amendment’s text, in no uncertain terms, makes Mrs. Davis the mandatory recipient of all trust income. The Commissioner’s strained reading finds no support in the instrument or in the tax code. *See Jackson*, 376 U.S. at 511 (holding that the marital deduction is not defeated by a trustee’s incidental administrative powers). If the Commissioner wished to challenge the adequacy of the standard, she could, but the statutory scheme requires no more than what the Amendment provides. The decision below should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the estate’s claimed marital deduction under § 2056(b)(7) should be allowed. The judgment of the Tax Court should be reversed.
