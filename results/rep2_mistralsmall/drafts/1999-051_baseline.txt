**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

Respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court has long held that “[t]he cause-and-prejudice test is a prerequisite to federal habeas review of procedurally defaulted claims.” *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). The Court has further explained that “[t]he cause requirement is satisfied when the prisoner shows that some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986). However, the Court has never held—and indeed, has explicitly rejected the notion—that an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim itself may serve as cause to excuse a procedural default.

In *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446 (2000), this Court squarely addressed whether a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could excuse the default of another claim. The Court held that it could not, explaining that “[a]lthough a prisoner may raise an ineffective-assistance claim as cause for a procedural default, the ineffective-assistance claim itself must be untainted by the procedural default.” *Id.* at 451. The Court reasoned that “[i]f the ineffective-assistance claim is itself procedurally defaulted, the prisoner cannot rely on it to excuse the default of another claim.” *Id.* The Court further cautioned that “[a]llowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to serve as cause for another default would permit a prisoner to circumvent the cause-and-prejudice test by bootstrapping one default to another.” *Id.* at 452.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary holding in this case directly conflicts with *Edwards v. Carpenter*. The court below erroneously concluded that an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim, regardless of whether the ineffective-assistance claim itself was procedurally defaulted. This holding is irreconcilable with this Court’s decision in *Edwards v. Carpenter*, which explicitly forecloses such a result.

**II. Even if an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim were cognizable as cause, Carpenter’s claim fails under the cause-and-prejudice test.**

Even assuming, arguendo, that an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as cause to excuse a procedural default, Carpenter’s claim fails under the cause-and-prejudice test. To establish cause, a petitioner must show that “some objective factor external to the defense impeded counsel’s efforts to comply with the State’s procedural rule.” *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. at 488. Carpenter has not made such a showing. His claim of ineffective assistance is based solely on his appellate counsel’s failure to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim on direct appeal. This is not an “objective factor external” to the defense; it is merely a strategic decision by counsel that, at most, could give rise to a claim of ineffective assistance, not cause to excuse a procedural default.

Moreover, even if Carpenter could establish cause, he has not demonstrated prejudice. To establish prejudice, a petitioner must show that “there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different.” *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 694 (1984). Carpenter has not shown that there is a reasonable probability that the outcome of his direct appeal would have been different had his appellate counsel raised the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim is meritless, as the evidence against Carpenter was overwhelming. Therefore, even if Carpenter could establish cause, he has not demonstrated prejudice.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Sixth Circuit and hold that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.
