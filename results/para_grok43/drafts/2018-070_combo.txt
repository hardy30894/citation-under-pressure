The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), declares that the civil and criminal laws of each adjacent State “are declared to be the law of the United States” only “[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” This language incorporates state law solely as surrogate federal law to supply deficiencies where federal statutes and regulations leave a void. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969) (purpose of the Act was “to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures”); United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286, 293 (1958) (assimilation statutes operate only where Congress has not “provide[d] a complete body of law”).

Federal enclave doctrine confirms the same limitation. When the United States acquires exclusive jurisdiction, “only state laws existing at the time of the acquisition remain enforceable. Subsequent state laws are only enforceable if Congress provides for assimilation.” Howard v. Commissioners of Sinking Fund, 344 U.S. 624, 626-27 (1953); see also Pacific Coast Dairy, Inc. v. Department of Agriculture, 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943) (“the state may not legislate with respect to matters within the exclusive jurisdiction of the United States”). The OCSLA’s prospective incorporation of later-enacted state law therefore functions identically: it fills gaps, not supplements comprehensive federal schemes.

This Court has repeatedly required an actual deficiency before borrowing state law. In James Stewart & Co. v. Sadrakula, 309 U.S. 94, 100 (1940), state workmen’s compensation applied on a federal enclave only because “Congress had not undertaken to cover the subject.” Likewise, in Murray v. Joe Gerrick & Co., 291 U.S. 315, 318 (1934), state law governed only “in the absence of legislation by Congress.” Where Congress has acted, “the state law is superseded.” Arlington Hotel Co. v. Fant, 278 U.S. 439, 454 (1929); Chicago, R.I. & P. Ry. Co. v. McGlinn, 114 U.S. 542, 546 (1885) (state law continues only “until displaced by federal legislation”).

The Fair Labor Standards Act supplies a complete regulatory regime for wages and hours, including an express savings clause for more protective state laws that operates only within the territorial jurisdiction of the States. Because that regime contains no gap on the outer Continental Shelf, California wage-and-hour statutes cannot be adopted as surrogate federal law under § 1333(a)(2)(A). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent”—contradicts the text and the settled enclave principle that state law operates solely to remedy federal incompleteness.
