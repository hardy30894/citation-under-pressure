**ARGUMENT**

**I. State courts retain jurisdiction over this legal-malpractice action because it fails the Grable test for arising-under jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a).**

Congress has provided federal courts with exclusive jurisdiction over civil actions “arising under” federal patent law. 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). In Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 808-09 (1988), the Court held that § 1338(a) reaches a state-law claim only when “a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff’s right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law.” Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005), refined that inquiry: federal jurisdiction exists only if the state claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.”

**A. Resolution of a federal patent issue is not necessary to Minton’s malpractice claim.**

Minton’s complaint alleges that Gunn’s failure to assert the experimental-use exception caused the loss of his patent-infringement suit. The experimental-use exception is an affirmative defense to the on-sale bar, 35 U.S.C. § 102(b). Because Minton could prevail on alternative theories of negligence that do not require adjudication of that defense, the federal issue is not necessary. See Christianson, 486 U.S. at 809 (jurisdiction turns on what “necessarily appears in the plaintiff’s well-pleaded complaint”).

**B. The federal issue is not substantial within the meaning of Grable.**

Even if the experimental-use question were necessary, it is not “substantial” in the sense required for arising-under jurisdiction. Grable, 545 U.S. at 313. The issue arises only in the context of a hypothetical “case within a case” that would never itself confer federal jurisdiction. See Grable, 545 U.S. at 313 (federal issue must be “a substantial one, indicating a serious federal interest in claiming the advantages thought to be inherent in a federal forum”). State adjudication of such embedded questions does not threaten the uniformity of patent law, because the resulting judgment binds only the parties and does not alter patent rights or create precedent on patent validity.

**C. Federal jurisdiction would upset the congressionally approved balance between state and federal courts.**

Legal-malpractice claims have traditionally been the province of state courts. See Grable, 545 U.S. at 314. Shifting every malpractice suit that touches patent litigation into federal court would “herald a potentially enormous shift of traditionally state cases into federal courts.” Id. at 319. Congress has not indicated any intent to effect such a shift. See id. at 318 (absence of a federal cause of action is “worth some consideration” in the balance inquiry).

Because Minton’s claim fails three of the four Grable factors, the Texas courts possessed subject-matter jurisdiction. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed.
