**ARGUMENT**

**OSHA HAS JURISDICTION UNDER SECTION 4(b)(1) OF THE ACT TO CITE UNINSPECTED VESSELS BECAUSE THE COAST GUARD HAS NOT EXERCISED STATUTORY AUTHORITY TO PRESCRIBE OR ENFORCE COMPREHENSIVE OCCUPATIONAL SAFETY STANDARDS FOR SUCH VESSELS**

Congress enacted the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 "to assure so far as possible every working man and woman in the Nation safe and healthful working conditions." *American Textile Mfrs. Institute, Inc. v. Donovan*, 452 U.S. 490, 493 (1981) (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 651(b)). That comprehensive remedial purpose counsels against reading narrow, judge-made exceptions into the statute where Congress has not clearly required one. Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA jurisdiction only "with respect to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies . . . exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1) (emphasis added). The statute's plain text conditions the exemption not on the mere existence of some other agency's jurisdictional authority, but on the actual *exercise* of that authority to prescribe or enforce safety and health standards for the working conditions at issue.

This Court's decision in *Gade v. National Solid Wastes Management Assn.*, 505 U.S. 88 (1992), confirms that the § 4(b)(1) inquiry turns on whether a "[federal] standard is in effect," not on abstract statutory authority to regulate. *Id.* at 96 (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 667(a)). Congress structured the Act around the premise that occupational safety regulation would be governed by actual, promulgated standards—hence the elaborate state-plan mechanism of § 18(b), under which pre-emption occurs only when "a Federal standard has been promulgated." *Gade*, 505 U.S. at 96 (quoting 29 U.S.C. § 667(b)). The same logic applies with equal force to § 4(b)(1): an exemption from OSHA coverage requires that the other agency have actually promulgated and be actively enforcing standards addressing the working conditions in question, not merely that it possess latent statutory authority it has chosen not to exercise as to a particular category of vessel.

Here, the Coast Guard has not exercised comprehensive statutory authority over the working conditions aboard uninspected vessels like Rig 52. The Fifth Circuit's own opinion below confirms that the Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are narrow and enumerated—covering only fire extinguishers, life preservers, flame arrestors, ventilation, and signaling equipment. *See* 46 U.S.C. § 4102; 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 *et seq.*, 26.01 *et seq.* These piecemeal equipment requirements bear no resemblance to the "comprehensive regulation and supervision" that this Court and the courts of appeals have recognized as the predicate for displacing another agency's jurisdiction. Where, as here, the Coast Guard has not promulgated the kind of pervasive occupational safety and health standards that would actually "prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health" for the hazards at issue—here, the specific drilling and blowout-control hazards that caused the explosion on Rig 52—§ 4(b)(1) simply does not apply.

This construction accords with the Act's design as recognized in *Whirlpool Corp. v. Marshall*, 445 U.S. 1 (1980), where the Court read the Act's protective mechanisms broadly to protect workers from "employment conditions believed to pose an emergent threat of death or serious injury." *Id.* at 11. It would be anomalous for this Court to construe § 4(b)(1)'s exemption so broadly that an entire category of maritime workers—those aboard uninspected barges performing essentially industrial oil-and-gas drilling operations—would fall into a regulatory gap where neither OSHA's comprehensive standards nor the Coast Guard's narrow equipment rules provide meaningful protection. Congress could not have intended § 4(b)(1) to create such a vacuum.

Moreover, this Court's maritime-jurisdiction precedents recognize that offshore drilling operations are properly understood as a "hybrid industry," sharing characteristics of both maritime and land-based industrial work. *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 422 (1985) (recognizing offshore drilling as "an onshore industry that initially crept out over the water" that also shares maritime concerns). Rig 52 was not navigating or engaged in maritime transport; it was performing stationary oil and gas exploration in Louisiana's territorial waters, precisely the kind of industrial workplace activity the Act was designed to reach. The vessel's status as a "vessel in navigation" for admiralty purposes does not answer the distinct statutory question whether the Coast Guard has *exercised* authority over the occupational hazards of drilling operations sufficient to trigger § 4(b)(1).

Finally, the structure of the Act as a whole—including its administrative enforcement mechanisms upheld in *Atlas Roofing Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm'n*, 430 U.S. 442 (1977), and its inspection authority sustained in relevant part in *Marshall v. Barlow's, Inc.*, 436 U.S. 307 (1978)—reflects Congress's judgment that a "drastic" national problem of workplace death and injury, *Atlas Roofing*, 430 U.S. at 444, required comprehensive federal enforcement. Permitting a categorical exemption for uninspected vessels based on the Coast Guard's minimal equipment regulations would undermine that congressional judgment and leave workers like those aboard Rig 52 without the protection Congress intended.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
