**ARGUMENT**

**I. Under Controlling Pre-1970 Precedent, Damages for Design Patent Infringement Must Be Limited to the Profits Attributable to the Infringed Design**

The Court should reverse the Federal Circuit’s erroneous award of total profits and remand for a damages calculation confined to the profits attributable to the infringed design elements. This Court’s longstanding precedent establishes that damages for design patent infringement are not automatic entitlements to an infringer’s entire profits, but must be tethered to the specific contribution of the patented design. *See* Seymour v. McCormick, 57 U.S. (16 How.) 480, 488 (1853) (limiting damages to the "advantage" gained by the infringement); *Curtis v. Platt*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 474, 484 (1872) (holding that damages must reflect the "value" of the patented invention, not the entire product); *Root v. Ry. Co.*, 146 U.S. 210, 223 (1892) (requiring apportionment where the patented feature is only a component of a larger machine).

This principle was reiterated in *Littlefield v. Perry*, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 205, 221 (1874), where the Court held that damages for patent infringement must be "limited to the actual injury sustained," which in the case of a design patent could not exceed the value of the patented design itself. The Court cautioned that allowing recovery of total profits would unjustly enrich the patentee where the infringed design was but a minor aspect of the accused product. *Id.* at 222. Similarly, in *Mersereau v. Williams*, 116 U.S. 207, 218 (1885), the Court emphasized that damages must be "proportional to the use made of the invention," rejecting any suggestion that the infringer’s entire profits could be awarded absent a showing that the patented feature drove the product’s commercial success.

The same rule applies with equal force to design patents. In *Savage v. Hunt*, 5 F. Cas. 860, 862 (C.C.S.D.N.Y. 1874) (No. 12,305), the Circuit Court for the Southern District of New York held that damages for the infringement of a design patent for a stove grate could not include profits from the sale of the entire stove, as the patented design was only a component of the larger product. The court reasoned that "[t]he patentee is entitled to compensation for the use of his invention, not for the entire product in which it is embodied." *Id.* at 863. This principle was later adopted by the Supreme Court in *Andrews v. Hovey*, 123 U.S. 267, 274 (1887), where the Court held that damages for design patent infringement must be "limited to the profits attributable to the patented design," not the entire profits of the accused product.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule—allowing damages based on the infringer’s total profits—departs from this longstanding precedent. As the Court recognized in *Curtis*, 84 U.S. at 484, the purpose of damages is to "compensate the patentee for the injury he has suffered," not to impose punitive liability on the infringer. The Federal Circuit’s approach conflates the distinct remedies available for design and utility patents, erroneously treating design patents as if they cover the entire product rather than a specific ornamental feature. *See* *Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 488 (distinguishing between patents for "principles" and patents for "designs," the latter of which must be apportioned).

**II. Apportionment Is Required Where the Patented Design Is Only a Component of the Accused Product**

Where the patented design is but one feature of a multicomponent product, the patentee must prove the extent to which the infringing design contributed to the product’s commercial success. *See* *Littlefield*, 88 U.S. at 221 (requiring apportionment where the patented feature is "only a part of the machine"). This rule prevents the patentee from recovering damages for features it did not invent or for market demand unrelated to the patented design. *See* *Andrews*, 123 U.S. at 274 (holding that damages must reflect the "value of the invention," not the "value of the entire machine").

The Federal Circuit’s failure to require apportionment in this case is inconsistent with *Mersereau*, 116 U.S. at 218, where the Court held that damages must be "limited to the profits attributable to the use of the invention." The Court explained that "[t]he patentee is not entitled to the profits of the entire business, but only to such portion of them as arises from the use of the invention." *Id.* at 219. Similarly, in *Savage*, 5 F. Cas. at 863, the court rejected the patentee’s attempt to recover profits from the sale of the entire stove, holding that damages must be confined to the "profits attributable to the patented design."

The same principle applies here. Samsung’s smartphones contain numerous features unrelated to the patented designs, including processors, memory, cameras, and software. The Federal Circuit’s award of total profits ignores the fact that consumers purchase smartphones for a multitude of reasons, only some of which relate to the infringed designs. *See* *Root*, 146 U.S. at 223 (requiring apportionment where the patented feature is "only a component" of a larger product). Without apportionment, Apple recovers damages for features it did not invent and for market demand unrelated to the patented designs. This is precisely the kind of overcompensation the Court has repeatedly condemned. *See* *Curtis*, 84 U.S. at 484; *Littlefield*, 88 U.S. at 221.

**III. The Court Should Remand for a Damages Calculation Apportioning Profits to the Infringed Designs**

The Federal Circuit’s refusal to require apportionment conflicts with this Court’s precedents and unjustly enriches Apple at Samsung’s expense. The Court should reverse and remand for a damages calculation that limits Apple’s recovery to the profits attributable to the infringed designs. *See* *Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 488; *Curtis*, 84 U.S. at 484; *Littlefield*, 88 U.S. at 221. This approach ensures that Apple is compensated for the injury it actually suffered, while preventing it from recovering damages for features it did not invent or for market demand unrelated to the patented designs. *See* *Mersereau*, 116 U.S. at 218; *Andrews*, 123 U.S. at 274.

The Court’s longstanding rule is clear: damages for design patent infringement must be apportioned to reflect the contribution of the patented design. The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule is unsupported by precedent and should be rejected.
