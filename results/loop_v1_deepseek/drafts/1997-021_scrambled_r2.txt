**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR POLLING IS IRRATIONAL AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.**

The National Labor Relations Board requires an employer to possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” before polling employees. That standard is arbitrary because it demands the same quantum of evidence needed to withdraw recognition—a far more drastic step. The Board’s rule creates a logical dead end: if an employer has enough evidence to withdraw recognition, it has no need to poll; if it lacks that evidence, it cannot poll. This renders the polling mechanism illusory, defeating the Act’s purpose of promoting industrial peace through informed employer decision-making. *See NLRB v. Falk Corp.*, 308 U.S. 453, 458–59 (1940) (Act aims to “eliminate the causes of labor disputes” and Board must act consistently with that purpose).

The Supreme Court has recognized that an employer may poll employees “if he has a good-faith doubt as to the union’s majority status.” *NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co.*, 395 U.S. 575, 610 n.27 (1969). *Gissel* did not condition polling on objective evidence of a loss of majority support; a sincere, good-faith doubt suffices. The Board’s heightened requirement conflicts with this Court’s guidance and with the Act’s design. The Board’s interpretation is unreasonable and therefore not entitled to deference. *See Falk*, 308 U.S. at 458–59 (Board’s orders must be “consistent with the Act” and not arbitrary); *NLRB v. J.H. Rutter-Rex Mfg. Co.*, 396 U.S. 258, 265 (1969) (Board must exercise discretion consistent with the Act’s policies).

Moreover, the Board’s preference for Board-conducted RM elections does not justify a uniform evidentiary threshold. RM elections are supervised; employer polls are not. Yet the Board applies the same standard to both, while applying the same standard to outright withdrawal of recognition—which lacks any procedural safeguards. This inconsistency is irrational. *See NLRB v. Wooster Div. of Borg-Warner Corp.*, 356 U.S. 342, 350 (1958) (Board must act “within the boundaries of the Act”); *NLRB v. Local 449, Int’l Union of Operating Engineers*, 353 U.S. 313, 318 (1957) (Board’s rules must be “carefully tailored” to the Act’s objectives). The Board’s standard fails that test.

**II. EVEN UNDER THE BOARD’S STANDARD, ITS FACTUAL FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED A REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.**

The record contains overwhelming, unrefuted evidence that a majority of the 32 employees no longer supported the union. At least seven employees made direct statements repudiating the union. Additional reports from employees Bloch and Mohr—that the night shift of five or six employees and the entire service department opposed the union—provided objective corroboration. The Board improperly discounted these statements under rigid rules that ignore the totality of circumstances. The substantial evidence standard requires the Board to consider the record as a whole. *NLRB v. American Mfg. Co.*, 328 U.S. 273, 279 (1946) (Board’s findings must be supported by substantial evidence on the record considered as a whole). Here, the Board selectively credited only direct, unqualified statements and ignored circumstantial evidence that corroborated the employer’s doubt.

The Board’s rejection of statements from employees Ridgick and Marsh as “equivocal” or mere “dissatisfaction” is arbitrary. In *American Mfg. Co.*, 328 U.S. at 279, this Court held that an employer may rely on employee statements indicating opposition to the union. Ridgick’s statement that “there was no need for a union” and Marsh’s complaint about paying dues without value are clear expressions of anti-union sentiment. The Board’s hypertechnical parsing of employee language finds no support in the Act.

The Board also excluded employees hired after January 25, 1991, yet the employer’s doubt was based on pre-existing information. The Board’s arbitrary date choice ignores the dynamic nature of the bargaining unit. *See Rutter-Rex*, 396 U.S. at 265 (Board must exercise discretion consistent with the Act’s purposes). The Board may not penalize an employer for acting on information available at the time of its decision.

**III. THE BOARD’S STANDARD UNDERMINES THE ACT’S POLICY OF EMPLOYEE FREE CHOICE AND INDUSTRIAL PEACE.**

The Act guarantees employees the right to select or reject a bargaining representative. The Board’s rule discourages employers from testing employee sentiment, forcing them to either recognize a union that may no longer have majority support or risk an unfair labor practice by withdrawing recognition. This Catch-22 frustrates the Act’s fundamental objective of industrial peace. *See Falk*, 308 U.S. at 458–59. The Board’s standard is a departure from the flexibility inherent in the Act and should be rejected.
