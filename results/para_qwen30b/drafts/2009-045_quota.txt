**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorney’s fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The Supreme Court has consistently held that the lodestar method, which calculates fees by multiplying hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is the presumptively correct measure of a “reasonable” fee under statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988. Any enhancement beyond the lodestar must be justified not by general praise of counsel’s work or the magnitude of the outcome, but only in the rare and exceptional case where the fee applicant offers specific, record-based evidence that the lodestar itself fails to reflect the true value of the services rendered. The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed a $4.5 million enhancement based on the district judge’s personal observations and subjective commendations of counsel’s “stellar performance,” violates this settled principle and must be reversed.

**I. The Supreme Court Has Repeatedly Declared That Fee Enhancements Based on Quality or Results Are Permissible Only in the “Rare” and “Exceptional” Case, and Only When Supported by Specific Evidence.**

The foundation of the fee-shifting regime is the lodestar, which is “presumed to be the reasonable fee.” *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897, 104 S.Ct. 1541, 1549, 79 L.Ed.2d 891 (1984). The Court in *Blum* explicitly rejected the notion that “the quality of representation” or “the results obtained” could serve as an independent basis for increasing the fee unless the applicant provided “specific evidence” demonstrating that the lodestar did not reflect the exceptional nature of the work or the outcome. *Id.* at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549. The Court emphasized that “the lodestar figure includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance in order to serve the statutory purpose of enabling plaintiffs to secure legal assistance.” *Delaware Valley Citizens Council for Clean Air v. Pennsylvania*, 478 U.S. 546, 566, 106 S.Ct. 3088, 3098, 92 L.Ed.2d 439 (1986) (emphasis added).

This principle was reaffirmed in *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435, 103 S.Ct. 1933, 1940, 76 L.Ed.2d 40 (1983), where the Court stated that “where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee. Normally this will encompass all hours reasonably expended on the litigation, and indeed in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* (emphasis added). The Court did not hold that such enhancements are automatic or even common; rather, it recognized that they may be justified in “some cases” of “exceptional success”—a phrase that has been interpreted to mean only those rare instances where the results were so extraordinary that the lodestar would otherwise undercompensate counsel. *See also* *Carr v. City of New York*, 530 F.3d 135, 142 (2d Cir.2008) (“An enhancement is appropriate only when the results obtained were so exceptional that the lodestar alone would not adequately compensate counsel.”).

**II. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Not Supported by Specific Evidence, But Rather by the Judge’s Subjective, Unreviewable Observations.**

The district court in this case awarded a $4.5 million enhancement based on its own “substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta,” and its “observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1290 (N.D.Ga.2006). This is precisely the kind of unreviewable, self-serving testimony that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* prohibit. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the district court must make specific findings justifying any enhancement,” and that “the fee applicant must offer specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged.” *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, 104 S.Ct. at 1549; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565, 106 S.Ct. at 3098.

Here, the district court offered no such evidence. It did not compare the quality of counsel’s work to that of other attorneys in similar cases. It did not cite to specific legal arguments, motions, or trial strategies that were particularly innovative or effective. It did not identify any particular obstacles overcome or risks taken. Instead, it relied on its own “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to conclude that the performance was “stellar.” This is not a proper basis for an enhancement. *See* *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir.1999) (holding that an enhancement must be supported by “specific evidence that the quality of service rendered and the results obtained were superior to what one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and the number of hours expended”); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir.2000) (same).
