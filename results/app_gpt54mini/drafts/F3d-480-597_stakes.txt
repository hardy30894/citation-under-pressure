## Argument

### I. The machine-gun conviction cannot stand because the government failed to prove the knowledge required by *Staples*.

A conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o) may not rest on mere possession of a firearm later determined to be a machine gun. The Supreme Court has held that, where a statute criminalizes possession of an item that is otherwise “quintessentially lawful,” the government must prove that the defendant knew the facts that made the conduct illegal. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 619, 114 S. Ct. 1793, 128 L. Ed. 2d 608 (1994). In *Staples*, the Court rejected the notion that “ignorance of the law is no excuse” was enough; instead, it required proof that the defendant knew the weapon possessed the features that brought it within the statute. *Id.* at 619. This Court has likewise recognized that criminal statutes should not be construed to dispense with a mens rea requirement absent a clear indication from Congress. *United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.*, 513 U.S. 64, 70-73, 115 S. Ct. 464, 130 L. Ed. 2d 372 (1994); *United States v. O’Brien*, 435 F.3d 36, 41-42 (1st Cir. 2006).

The evidence here was insufficient under that standard. The government proved, at most, that Nieves-Castaño possessed a rifle concealed in a golf bag, that she had opened the bag once, and that she told an agent she knew the rifle was an AK-47. That proof does not establish beyond a reasonable doubt that she knew the rifle was capable of automatic fire, or otherwise knew the particular characteristics that made it a machine gun under federal law. See *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 602, 619. Knowledge that a weapon is an “AK-47” is not knowledge that the weapon is a machine gun; many semiautomatic rifles are styled as AK-type rifles, and the government introduced no evidence that Nieves-Castaño knew of any automatic-fire capability. Cf. *United States v. Thomas*, 15 F.3d 381, 384-85 (5th Cir. 1994) (requiring proof the defendant knew of the weapon’s automatic capabilities).

Nor did the surrounding circumstances supply the missing mens rea. The rifle was hidden in a golf bag at the request of another person, Alexis; the search of the apartment uncovered no drugs or drug paraphernalia; and the government relied entirely on post-arrest statements and the inference that concealment implies guilty knowledge. But *Staples* specifically rejected the idea that possession plus suspicious circumstances automatically permits a conviction without proof of the defendant’s awareness of the weapon’s illegal characteristics. 511 U.S. at 615-18. At most, the government showed that Nieves-Castaño knowingly possessed a rifle and knew it was something she was asked to hide. That is not enough to prove she knew it was a machine gun. See *United States v. Nieves-Castaño*, 480 F.3d 597, 600-01 (1st Cir. 2007) (discussing *Staples* requirement of knowledge of weapon’s characteristics).

Because the evidence failed to establish the requisite mens rea, the judgment of conviction on the § 922(o) count must be reversed. See *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319, 99 S. Ct. 2781, 61 L. Ed. 2d 560 (1979) (conviction may stand only if, viewing the evidence most favorably to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements beyond a reasonable doubt).

### II. The school-zone conviction should also be reversed because § 922(q)(2)(A) is void for vagueness as applied here.

The Due Process Clause requires criminal statutes to give ordinary people fair notice of what conduct is prohibited and to prevent arbitrary enforcement. *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357-58, 103 S. Ct. 1855, 75 L. Ed. 2d 903 (1983); *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108-09, 92 S. Ct. 2294, 33 L. Ed. 2d 222 (1972). A statute is void for vagueness when it “fails to provide a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited,” or is so standardless that it invites arbitrary enforcement. *United States v. Williams*, 553 U.S. 285, 304, 128 S. Ct. 1830, 170 L. Ed. 2d 650 (2008).

Section 922(q)(2)(A) makes possession unlawful “in a school zone,” and defines a school zone as the grounds of a school or “within a distance of 1,000 feet from the grounds of a public, parochial or private school.” 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(25). But Congress did not specify how that 1,000-foot distance is to be measured. The absence of any measurement rule leaves an ordinary person without notice whether the distance is measured from the nearest point of the school grounds, the school building, the property line, or by some other method. It also invites arbitrary enforcement, because different officers or courts could use different methodologies. See *Kolender*, 461 U.S. at 357-58; *Grayned*, 408 U.S. at 108-09.

This is not a case where a statute’s meaning is clarified by settled judicial usage or common understanding. Cf. *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 266, 117 S. Ct. 1219, 137 L. Ed. 2d 432 (1997). The government may argue that geometry supplies the answer, but the statute itself says nothing about measurement method, and criminal liability cannot rest on post hoc judicial or prosecutorial choice. Because the statute fails to provide clear notice and clear standards of enforcement, it is unconstitutionally vague as applied here. See *United States v. Batchelder*, 442 U.S. 114, 123, 99 S. Ct. 2198, 60 L. Ed. 2d 755 (1979).

For these reasons, both convictions should be reversed.
