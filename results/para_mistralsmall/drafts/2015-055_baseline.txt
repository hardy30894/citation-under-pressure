**ARGUMENT**

I. **Johnson’s Rule Regarding the Residual Clause Applies Retroactively to Cases on Collateral Review**

The Supreme Court has long held that a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure applies retroactively to cases on collateral review if it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 307 (1989) (plurality opinion). The Court has further explained that a new rule applies retroactively if it is “dictated by precedent” or if it is a “watershed rule of criminal procedure” implicating fundamental fairness. *Id.* at 301. The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) is unconstitutionally vague, is a new rule that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review.

The Court in *Johnson* held that the residual clause of the ACCA, which included crimes that “otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” failed to give fair notice of the conduct it proscribed and thus violated the Due Process Clause. *Id.* at 2556-57. The Court reasoned that the residual clause’s “imprecise” and “highly context-dependent” nature rendered it unconstitutionally vague. *Id.* at 2557. This holding was a new rule of constitutional law, as prior decisions had not explicitly invalidated the residual clause on vagueness grounds. *See id.* at 2556.

The rule announced in *Johnson* is a procedural rule that implicates fundamental fairness and thus applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *See Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 322-23 (1987) (holding that new rules of criminal procedure apply retroactively to cases on direct review and cases pending on collateral review). The Court has recognized that rules invalidating vague criminal statutes are procedural rules that apply retroactively because they “enhance[] the accuracy of the criminal process.” *See Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004) (citing *Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347 (1964)). The rule in *Johnson* is no different. It ensures that individuals are not convicted under a statute that fails to provide fair notice of the conduct it proscribes, a core concern of due process. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion in *In re Griffin*, 795 F.3d 1190 (11th Cir. 2015), is unpersuasive. The court in *Griffin* held that *Johnson* did not apply retroactively because it did not announce a new rule of constitutional law. *Id.* at 1193. This conclusion is incorrect. The Court in *Johnson* explicitly held that the residual clause was unconstitutionally vague, a holding that was not dictated by prior precedent. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. The rule announced in *Johnson* is thus a new rule that applies retroactively to cases on collateral review.

II. **Petitioner’s ACCA Sentence Was Imposed in Violation of Due Process**

Petitioner’s ACCA sentence was imposed in violation of due process because his prior conviction for Florida “strong arm” robbery was not a violent felony under the ACCA. The ACCA defines a “violent felony” as a crime that “has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against the person of another,” or that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B). The residual clause of the ACCA, which includes crimes that “otherwise involve[] conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” was held unconstitutionally vague in *Johnson*. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57.

Under the categorical approach, Petitioner’s Florida robbery conviction does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA. The Florida robbery statute under which Petitioner was convicted criminalized the taking of property from another’s person or custody “by the use of force, violence, assault, or putting in fear.” Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996). However, Florida courts have held that the degree of force required to sustain a robbery conviction under this statute could be “slight” or “minimal.” *See, e.g., Goldsmith v. State*, 573 So. 2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d Dist. Ct. App. 1991) (holding that the “slight force used ... to remove the bill from [the victim’s] hand” was “insufficient to constitute the crime of robbery”). This minimal force is not sufficient to satisfy the ACCA’s elements clause, which requires the use of “physical force” capable of causing physical pain or injury. *See Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133, 140 (2010) (holding that “physical force” means “force capable of causing physical pain or injury to another person”).

Moreover, Petitioner’s conviction does not qualify as a violent felony under the residual clause, which was held unconstitutionally vague in *Johnson*. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. Even if Petitioner’s conviction could be construed as presenting a serious potential risk of physical injury, the residual clause’s vagueness renders it unconstitutional. *Id.* at 2557. Thus, Petitioner’s prior conviction for Florida “strong arm” robbery does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA, and his ACCA sentence was imposed in violation of due process.

III. **Petitioner Is Entitled to Resentencing**

Because Petitioner’s ACCA sentence was imposed in violation of due process, he is entitled to resentencing. The Court has held that a defendant whose sentence was imposed in violation of the Constitution is entitled to resentencing. *See, e.g., United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220, 244 (2005) (holding that the Federal Sentencing Guidelines are advisory, not mandatory). Petitioner’s ACCA sentence was imposed under a statute that was later held unconstitutionally vague, and thus his sentence was imposed in violation of due process. *See Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2556-57. Petitioner is therefore entitled to resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.
