**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Protection Clause Prohibits Discrimination Against Groups, Not Individual Animus.**

The Equal Protection Clause commands that no State shall “deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws.” This Court has consistently interpreted that command to require that “all persons similarly situated should be treated alike.” *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373 (1886). But the Clause does not guarantee that every individual will receive identical treatment from the government. Rather, it prohibits the State from making “invidious” classifications that discriminate against a group or class. *See Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944) (holding that “the unlawful administration by state officers of a state statute fair on its face, resulting in unequal application to those who are entitled to be treated alike, is not a denial of equal protection unless there is shown to be present in it an element of intentional or purposeful discrimination”). The key inquiry is whether the discrimination is based on some “unjustifiable standard” such as race, religion, or other arbitrary classification. *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962).

In every case where this Court has found an equal protection violation, the plaintiff has alleged discrimination against a definable class. *See, e.g., Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373 (Chinese laundry owners); *Skinner v. Oklahoma*, 316 U.S. 535, 541 (1942) (criminals convicted of certain offenses). The Clause is not a general safeguard against arbitrary government action; that protection is provided by the Due Process Clause. *See Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954) (applying due process to federal action). To allow a plaintiff to bring an equal protection claim based solely on a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him,” as the court below did, would transform the Clause into a font of tort liability for any disgruntled citizen.

**II. The “Class of One” Theory Is Unsupported by Precedent.**

The court below held that a plaintiff may state an equal protection claim by alleging that state action was motivated solely by a “spiteful effort to ‘get’ him.” That standard finds no support in this Court’s decisions. In *Snowden v. Hughes*, the Court rejected a similar claim by a candidate who alleged that election officials refused to certify his nomination while certifying others. 321 U.S. at 8-9. The Court held that the plaintiff failed to allege intentional discrimination, but it did not suggest that a single individual could sue without showing that the discrimination was based on an impermissible classification. *Id.* at 9-10. Similarly, in *Oyler v. Boles*, the Court held that the conscious exercise of selectivity in enforcement is not a constitutional violation absent a showing of intentional discrimination based on an “unjustifiable standard.” 368 U.S. at 456. The Court has never recognized a claim where the plaintiff merely alleges that she was singled out for unfavorable treatment without any connection to a class.

The only context in which this Court has allowed a single plaintiff to challenge discriminatory treatment without alleging membership in a class is in tax assessment cases. *See, e.g., Sunday Lake Iron Co. v. Township of Wakefield*, 247 U.S. 350, 352-53 (1918); *Sioux City Bridge Co. v. Dakota County*, 260 U.S. 441, 445-46 (1923). But those cases are *sui generis*, resting on the unique nature of property taxation and the requirement of uniformity. They do not support a general “class of one” theory applicable to all government actions. Moreover, even in those cases, the Court required a showing of intentional and systematic discrimination, not merely a single instance of different treatment. *Sunday Lake*, 247 U.S. at 353.

**III. The Court of Appeals’ Standard Would Undermine Rational Basis Review.**

The Equal Protection Clause does not require that all laws or government actions be uniform. *See Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (“The Equal Protection Clause does not require that all persons be dealt with identically.”). Instead, it permits classifications that have a rational basis. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911). Under the court below’s standard, any person who can show that a government decision was motivated by spite could bring an equal protection claim, regardless of whether the decision had any rational basis. That would effectively replace rational basis review with a subjective inquiry into governmental motive, contrary to this Court’s repeated admonition that the Equal Protection Clause is not a “license to courts to judge the wisdom of legislative classifications.” *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483, 488 (1955).

Here, respondent alleged only that the Village demanded a 33-foot easement from her while requiring only a 15-foot easement from others. She did not allege that she was a member of any class or that the Village’s action was based on any invidious classification. Her claim, if any, sounds in due process or state tort law, not equal protection. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
