ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in affirming petitioner's convictions under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) because that statute requires proof of subjective intent to threaten. Criminal statutes are construed to incorporate the common-law requirement of mens rea. United States v. Balint, 258 U.S. 250, 251 (1922). 

The First Amendment likewise demands subjective intent before speech may be punished as a threat. The clear-and-present-danger test protects expression unless the speaker intends to produce imminent lawless action. Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925). An objective standard would criminalize protected advocacy. Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 372 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring).

Punishment of speech without regard to the speaker's purpose chills expression the Amendment safeguards. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931); Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713-714 (1931). "The safeguarding of free speech . . . demands that the state show a clear and present danger" arising from the speaker's intended meaning. Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 258 (1937). An objective test fails this requirement. Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941).

Even "fighting words" require that the speaker intend to provoke violence. Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 315 U.S. 568, 572 (1942). The same principle governs threats under § 875(c). Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 503 (1951). Because the jury was instructed only on an objective standard, the convictions cannot stand.
