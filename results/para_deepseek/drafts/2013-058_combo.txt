**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts only “retirement funds” held in a tax-qualified account. An inherited individual retirement account does not contain the debtor’s retirement funds; it contains funds that once belonged to the original owner and must be distributed to the beneficiary before her own retirement. This Court has long held that exemptions from creditors’ claims are to be strictly construed, as they are in derogation of the common law. *Stratton v. Comm’r*, 283 U.S. 318, 321 (1931) (“exemptions from taxation are not to be read into the statute by implication”); *United States v. Stewart*, 311 U.S. 60, 71 (1940) (exemption provisions “must be strictly construed”); *Helvering v. Stockholms Enskilda Bank*, 293 U.S. 84, 87 (1934) (exemption “cannot be extended by implication”). The same principle applies to bankruptcy exemptions. *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903) (homestead exemption “must be strictly construed because it is in derogation of the rights of creditors”).

The term “retirement funds” in § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) describes funds that the debtor holds for her own retirement, not funds that were once set aside by another. The inherited IRA at issue here is a “time-limited tax-deferral vehicle” that must begin distributing assets within one year of the original owner’s death and complete payout in as little as five years. Those funds are not reserved for the debtor’s retirement; they are available for immediate consumption. The exemption is therefore inapplicable.

This Court has consistently held that the character of property for exemption purposes depends on how the debtor uses it, not on how a predecessor used it. *Hughes v. Union Trust Co.*, 191 U.S. 477, 489 (1903) (homestead exemption does not apply to property the debtor rents out, even if it was once the debtor’s home); *Bryant v. Bryant*, 137 U.S. 420, 424 (1890) (exemption “depends upon the use of the property”). Just as a house inherited from a parent is not the debtor’s “homestead” unless the debtor actually resides there, *McDonald v. Hovey*, 110 U.S. 619, 622 (1884), so funds inherited from a parent’s retirement account are not the debtor’s “retirement funds” unless the debtor holds them for her own retirement. The inherited IRA is not so held.

The statutory language reinforces this conclusion. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts “retirement funds” without any temporal qualifier, but that does not mean the funds need never have been the debtor’s retirement funds. It means that new contributions to the debtor’s own retirement account during bankruptcy are exempt—a point irrelevant to inherited accounts. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 241 (1934) (exemption provisions are to be given “a fair and reasonable interpretation” but not extended beyond their plain meaning). The debtor’s inherited IRA is not a “retirement fund” by any economic measure; it is a short-term tax-deferral vehicle that must be drained before the debtor reaches retirement age.

The argument that any funds that once qualified as retirement funds in the hands of the original owner remain exempt forever would lead to absurd results. *Edwards v. Kearzey*, 96 U.S. 595, 604 (1877) (exemption laws “are not to be extended by construction”). If the original owner had withdrawn the money and given it to the debtor, the cash would not be exempt. *In re Tune*, 115 F.2d 306, 308 (6th Cir. 1940) (pension fund exemption lost when funds are withdrawn and commingled with personal assets). There is no principled difference between that scenario and the instant one. The inherited IRA is simply a tax-deferred bequest, not a retirement savings vehicle for the beneficiary.

The debtor’s reliance on the phrase “inherited IRA” is misplaced. The Internal Revenue Code’s use of the term “IRA” in that phrase designates the source of the funds, not their current character. *May v. Henderson*, 268 U.S. 111, 115 (1925) (an exemption “must be determined by the actual condition of the property at the time of the bankruptcy filing”). At the time of filing, the debtor’s inherited IRA was not a fund for her retirement; it was a fund that had to be distributed to her immediately. The exemption does not apply.

This Court has long recognized that exemptions are designed to protect the debtor’s necessary means of support, not to shelter assets that can be freely used for current consumption. *Local Loan Co.*, 292 U.S. at 243 (exemption of wages is to protect the “necessary expenditure for the support of the debtor and his family”). Allowing a debtor to exempt an inherited IRA that must be spent before retirement would convert the exemption into a windfall for the debtor’s creditors’ detriment. The bankruptcy judge correctly held that the inherited IRA is not exempt.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
