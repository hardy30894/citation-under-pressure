**Argument**

The bankruptcy court had jurisdiction to decide whether Kirkland’s student-loan principal had been paid in full and remained excepted from discharge. But it lacked jurisdiction to go further and liquidate ECMC’s alleged post-petition interest and collection costs after the Chapter 13 case had been closed and the discharge entered. Federal jurisdiction in bankruptcy extends only to matters “arising under,” “arising in,” or “related to” a case under title 11. 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b); see **Celotex Corp. v. Edwards**, 514 U.S. 300, 307–08 (1995); **Pacor, Inc. v. Higgins**, 743 F.2d 984, 994 (3d Cir. 1984). A dispute is “related to” a bankruptcy case only if its outcome could conceivably affect the estate being administered. **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 308; **Valley Historic Ltd. P’ship v. Bank of N.Y.**, 486 F.3d 831, 836 (4th Cir. 2007). Once the Chapter 13 estate was fully administered, the case was closed, and discharge entered, there was no estate left to affect. See **In re Kieffer-Mickes, Inc.**, 226 B.R. 204, 208 (B.A.P. 8th Cir. 1998); **In re Querner**, 7 F.3d 1199, 1201–02 (5th Cir. 1993).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that bankruptcy jurisdiction does not become a roving commission to resolve every dispute touching a debtor or creditor after administration ends. **Celotex**, 514 U.S. at 308; **Stern v. Marshall**, 564 U.S. 462, 478–79 (2011). Here, ECMC’s claim for post-petition interest and collection costs did not belong to the estate, did not alter the confirmed plan, and could not affect distribution to creditors, because the case was closed. See **In re Resorts Int’l, Inc.**, 372 F.3d 154, 164–67 (3d Cir. 2004). At most, the dispute concerned the amount of Kirkland’s post-bankruptcy personal liability. That is a state-law or nonbankruptcy collection issue, not a matter “arising in” or “arising under” the bankruptcy case. See **Valley Historic**, 486 F.3d at 836; **New Horizon of N.Y. LLC v. Jacobs**, 231 F.3d 143, 152–53 (4th Cir. 2000).

The bankruptcy court’s authority was also limited by the nature of the adversary proceeding. Kirkland sought a declaration that the principal had been paid and discharged except for non-dischargeable interest accrued during the case. ECMC never asked the court to adjudicate collection costs, and the parties did not litigate a separate allowance or disallowance of those costs under the Bankruptcy Code. A bankruptcy court may determine dischargeability under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a), but it may not enlarge its jurisdiction by addressing issues not necessary to that determination. See **In re Heckert**, 272 F.3d 253, 258–60 (4th Cir. 2001); **In re CACI Eng’g, Inc.**, 318 F.3d 480, 486–87 (4th Cir. 2003). The question before the court was whether the debt was discharged, not whether ECMC could recover an unpled post-petition fee item.

Nor did the court have ancillary jurisdiction to award post-petition interest and collection costs simply because it could determine dischargeability. Ancillary jurisdiction exists to enable a court to manage its proceedings and effectuate its decrees, not to create new substantive liability after the underlying bankruptcy administration has concluded. **Kokkonen v. Guardian Life Ins. Co. of Am.**, 511 U.S. 375, 379–80 (1994); **Travelers Indem. Co. v. Bailey**, 557 U.S. 137, 151–54 (2009). Here, the court’s determination that the principal remained owed did not require it to quantify additional items ECMC had never properly placed at issue. See **In re Resorts Int’l**, 372 F.3d at 165–67.

The post-petition interest award was especially improper because the record shows the parties only agreed that “some amount of interest has accrued,” not that ECMC’s submitted figure was uncontested in any jurisdictional sense. A bankruptcy court may determine the existence of a nondischargeable debt, but the creditor bears the burden to establish the amount owed. See **Grogan v. Garner**, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991); **In re CACI Eng’g**, 318 F.3d at 486–87. The same is true of collection costs: absent a statutory, contractual, or factual basis established in the adversary record, the court had no basis to fix them. See **Travelers Cas. & Sur. Co. of Am. v. Pac. Gas & Elec. Co.**, 549 U.S. 443, 449–50 (2007); **Law v. Siegel**, 571 U.S. 415, 421–22 (2014).

The judgment should therefore be affirmed insofar as it held the principal nondischargeable, but reversed to the extent it adjudicated ECMC’s post-petition interest and denied collection costs in a closed case beyond the scope of the adversary proceeding and outside the bankruptcy court’s post-closing jurisdiction.
