The Ninth Circuit erred in expanding Exemption 2 beyond its text. That provision exempts only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase reaches no farther than rules governing “employment” matters such as hiring, leave, and discipline. Boske v. Comingore, 177 U.S. 459, 470 (1900). It does not encompass technical data used to site explosives near civilian communities.

Statutes creating exceptions to public access are construed narrowly. United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen, 340 U.S. 462, 468 (1951). The Navy’s ESQD arcs are not “solely” internal; they directly affect public safety and land use. When information serves both internal and external purposes, disclosure is required. United States v. Reynolds, 345 U.S. 1, 6 (1953).

The “predominantly internal” gloss adopted below finds no support in the statute’s language or in contemporaneous decisions interpreting analogous disclosure provisions. Jencks v. United States, 353 U.S. 657, 668 (1957). Courts may not engraft additional qualifiers that Congress omitted. Palermo v. United States, 360 U.S. 343, 350 (1959).

The risk-of-circumvention rationale likewise exceeds the exemption’s scope. Pre-FOIA decisions permitted withholding only upon a concrete showing of harm to a recognized privilege, not upon speculation about future misuse. Roviaro v. United States, 353 U.S. 53, 60-61 (1957). The Navy’s affidavits assert only generalized security concerns; such assertions do not justify nondisclosure. Campbell v. United States, 365 U.S. 85, 92 (1961).

FOIA’s structure confirms that Exemption 2 was never intended to shelter operational safety data. Pittsburgh Plate Glass Co. v. United States, 360 U.S. 395, 399 (1959). Where Congress wished to protect sensitive information, it enacted separate, specific exemptions. The arcs at issue fall outside those provisions and must be released.

The judgment should be reversed.
