**ARGUMENT**

Section 1113(2) of ERISA provides that an action for breach of fiduciary duty under 29 U.S.C. § 1104 may not be commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The three-year period began to run before October 29, 2012, because the undisputed record establishes that Sulyma received disclosures identifying the precise asset allocations and investment strategy he now challenges.

This Court has long held that the limitations period is triggered by knowledge of the underlying transaction, not by knowledge that the transaction violated ERISA. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the Court stated that section 1113 “is triggered by the [plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” Id. at 991-92. The same principle controlled in Meagher v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), where receipt of reduced pension checks supplied the requisite knowledge even though the plaintiff “may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.” Id. at 1423. Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990), confirmed that actual knowledge exists once the plaintiff is informed of the facts that later form the basis of the claim. Id. at 552.

The disclosures Intel furnished—Fund Fact Sheets, the Qualified Default Investment Alternative Notice, the Summary Plan Description, and annual disclosures—identified the exact allocations to hedge funds, private equity, and commodities that Sulyma alleges were imprudent. Those documents therefore supplied “actual knowledge of the breach or violation” within the meaning of section 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary requirement—that a plaintiff must also have appreciated the imprudence of the disclosed facts—finds no support in the statute or in this Court’s precedents and would effectively revive the constructive-knowledge provision Congress repealed in 1987.

Because the record contains no genuine dispute that Sulyma possessed the information constituting the alleged breach more than three years before suit, the district court correctly entered summary judgment. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
