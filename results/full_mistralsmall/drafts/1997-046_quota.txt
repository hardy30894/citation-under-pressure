**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN WHICH PETITIONER HAS A DUE PROCESS RIGHT TO PARTICIPATE WITHOUT WAIVING HIS FIFTH AMENDMENT PRIVILEGE.**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Ohio’s clemency process does not itself create a protected liberty interest. *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 465 (1981) (“[P]ardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.”). However, once a State chooses to create a clemency mechanism, due process attaches to its procedures. *See Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985) (due process applies to state-created appellate procedures integral to adjudication). Here, Ohio’s clemency process—mandated by statute and regulation—functions as the final phase of the State’s penal system. *See Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07*; Ohio Death Penalty Clemency Procedure, Ohio Admin. Code § 5120:1-1-15. Because clemency is the last opportunity to avoid execution, it is a critical component of the State’s deprivation of liberty. *See Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 471 (Stevens, J., dissenting) (“The decision to grant or withhold commutation ... constitutes a regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process employed by the State ... to determine the magnitude of its deprivation of the prisoner’s liberty.”).

**II. THE STATE MAY NOT CONDITION PARTICIPATION IN CLEMENCY ON A WAIVER OF THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PRIVILEGE AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Ohio’s clemency interview does not compel a Fifth Amendment waiver. The Fifth Amendment provides that “[n]o person ... shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The State may not impose a Hobson’s choice: participate in clemency and risk self-incrimination, or refuse and forfeit the process entirely. *See Lefkowitz v. Cunningham*, 431 U.S. 801, 808 (1977) (“[A] State may not impose substantial penalties because a witness elects to exercise [Fifth Amendment] rights.”). The mere *option* to speak does not cure the constitutional defect where the State structures the process to extract information as a condition of participation. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976) (prison disciplinary hearings may not compel testimony, though silence may be considered).

**III. THE COURT OF APPEALS ERRED IN MINIMIZING THE RISK OF SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit dismissed the Fifth Amendment claim as premature, reasoning that the regulation does not *require* testimony. However, the Court must consider the *practical effect* of the process. *See Lefkowitz*, 431 U.S. at 808 (penalty imposed for exercising rights need not be explicit to violate the Fifth Amendment). Here, the clemency interview is structured to elicit statements that could later be used against Petitioner in unrelated proceedings. *See Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 324 (acknowledging risk of self-incrimination in disciplinary hearings). The State’s interest in efficiency does not outweigh Petitioner’s constitutional right. *See id.* at 322 (“[T]he Fifth Amendment ... protects a state witness against being compelled to furnish evidence which may tend to incriminate him.”).

**IV. THE COURT SHOULD REJECT OHIO’S ATTEMPT TO DISTINGUISH DUM SCHAT ON PROCEDURAL GROUNDS.**

Ohio relies on *Dumschat* to argue that clemency is unreviewable, but that case addressed only whether a liberty interest existed—not whether the process could compel self-incrimination. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. The Fifth Amendment question is distinct and remains unanswered. *See id.* at 471 (Stevens, J., dissenting) (noting clemency’s role in determining liberty deprivation). The Court should not permit Ohio to immunize its clemency process from constitutional scrutiny.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s judgment and hold that Ohio’s clemency process violates Petitioner’s Fifth and Fourteenth Amendment rights.
