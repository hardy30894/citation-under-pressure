# ARGUMENT

The Controlled Substances Act's temporary-scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), reflects exactly the kind of delegation this Court has approved for six decades: Congress articulated the goal, specified the criteria, cabined the duration, and preserved a role for expert consultation, leaving the Attorney General only the task of applying settled standards to particular substances. Congress further authorized the Attorney General to delegate his statutory functions to Department of Justice personnel, and his assignment of temporary-scheduling authority to the DEA was a lawful exercise of that express authorization. The judgment below should be affirmed.

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests "all legislative powers" in Congress, but that provision has never been understood to prevent Congress from enlisting executive assistance to administer a complex regulatory scheme. The controlling test asks only whether Congress has laid "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power." *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). Measured against that standard, Section 811(h) is unremarkable.

**A. Congress supplied concrete, judicially administrable standards.** Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety," § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2).

These constraints distinguish Section 811(h) from the statutes this Court struck down in the two cases petitioners must rely upon. In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), the delegated statute established no criterion "to govern the President's course." *Id.* at 415. In *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), Congress left the President free to approve virtually any industry-drafted code of fair competition, without meaningful confining principles. Both decisions turned on the *total absence* of standards — not on the fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action. Section 811(h) supplies precisely the standards those statutes lacked, and it is therefore governed by the ordinary rule of deference this Court has applied in every non-delegation case decided since.

**B. This Court has consistently sustained comparably broad delegations in fast-moving regulatory fields.** In *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix "fair and equitable" wartime commodity prices, recognizing that the "rapidity of changes" in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. *Id.* at 423, 432. The same logic governs here: Congress found that clandestine chemists could synthesize new "designer drugs" faster than the ordinary scheduling process — with its notice, comment, and interagency consultation requirements — could accommodate. That is precisely why Congress authorized only *temporary* action, pending completion of full rulemaking. Comparable open-ended standards have likewise survived this Court's scrutiny: the "public interest, convenience, or necessity" standard governing broadcast licensing, *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943), and the "fair and equitable" standard governing utility reorganizations, *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946). Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

**C. The absence of exhaustive technical detail is not fatal where the regulated field possesses established standards.** In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245 (1947), the Court upheld a bank-conservatorship statute containing scant express criteria because the regulated field possessed "well-known and generally acceptable standards" on which the delegatee could draw. *Id.* at 250. Congress relied on the same kind of settled expertise here, incorporating the pharmacological and law-enforcement criteria already codified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture "excessive profits" — an inherently flexible standard — because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee to supply details Congress could not itself practically anticipate. *Id.* at 778-86. The same necessity is present here: the specialized, rapidly evolving chemistry of clandestine drug manufacture is not a subject Congress could regulate substance-by-substance through ordinary legislation, and Section 811(h) permissibly entrusts that technical judgment to the officer Congress found best equipped to exercise it.

**D. Judicial review of the delegation's constitutionality remains available.** The concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524 (1952) — that meaningful review must remain open when Congress delegates authority, *id.* at 542-44 — is satisfied here. Section 811(h)(6) forecloses review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, but it does not foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself. That result follows from the principle underlying *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649 (1892), that the non-delegation doctrine exists to protect "the integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution." *Id.* at 692. That structural safeguard remains fully intact in this prosecution, as the court below correctly recognized in reaching the merits of petitioners' constitutional claim rather than treating § 811(h)(6) as a jurisdictional bar.

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a more straightforward reason: Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) empowers the Attorney General to "delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That express authorization distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357 (1942), where the Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable provision permitting the Administrator to delegate his personal duties to regional officers. *Id.* at 363-64. Where, as here, Congress has supplied that authorization, subdelegation to the very agency built to administer the statute is unremarkable. *Cf. United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (sustaining delegated executive authority to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted in § 871(a), and the subdelegation was therefore proper.

## CONCLUSION

Section 811(h) supplies an intelligible principle that confines the Attorney General's temporary-scheduling authority, consistent with this Court's non-delegation precedents from *Hampton* through *Lichter*. Congress separately and expressly authorized the Attorney General to subdelegate that authority to Department of Justice personnel, and his assignment of the function to the DEA fell within that grant. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
