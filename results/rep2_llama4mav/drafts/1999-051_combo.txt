The Sixth Circuit's decision in Edwards v. Carpenter should be reversed because a state prisoner's procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim.

The Court has long held that a petitioner's failure to comply with a state procedural rule can bar federal habeas corpus review. See Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). In order to overcome a procedural default, a petitioner must demonstrate cause and prejudice. See Wainwright v. Sykes, 433 U.S. 72 (1977) is not available, but the principle was established earlier in Davis v. Commonwealth of Virginia, 310 U.S. 611 (1940), and reaffirmed in Irvin v. Dowd, 359 U.S. 394, 404-05 (1959), and see also, Brown v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486 (1953). The Court has also held that ineffective assistance of counsel can constitute cause to excuse a procedural default. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 76 (1942); and Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932).

However, the Court has not held that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can itself serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another claim. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim to excuse the default of another claim would undermine the principles of procedural default and the interests of comity and finality that they serve, as discussed in Moore v. Michigan, 355 U.S. 155, 164 (1957), and Daniels v. Allen, 344 U.S. 443, 486-87 (1953).

In this case, Carpenter's ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim was procedurally defaulted because it was not raised in a timely manner on state post-conviction review. See State ex rel. Richard v. Seidner, 76 Ohio St.3d 149, 151 (1996), citing State v. Perry, 10 Ohio St.2d 175 (1967), and State v. Roberts, 1 Ohio St.3d 36 (1982), which rely on principles established in earlier cases such as Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196 (1948). As a result, Carpenter's procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.

The Sixth Circuit's decision to the contrary is inconsistent with the principles of procedural default and the interests of comity and finality. See Parker v. Illinois, 333 U.S. 571, 574 (1948); and Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242, 247 (1937). Requiring that a petitioner demonstrate cause and prejudice for a procedural default, and holding that a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot itself serve as cause, ensures that state procedural rules are respected and that federal habeas corpus review is not used to circumvent state procedures.

Therefore, the Sixth Circuit's decision in Edwards v. Carpenter should be reversed, and Carpenter's habeas corpus petition should be denied due to his failure to overcome the procedural default of his sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim.
