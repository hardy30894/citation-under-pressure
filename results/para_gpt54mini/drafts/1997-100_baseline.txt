**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding. The Clause protects against a second prosecution for the “same offense,” U.S. Const. amend. V, but the Supreme Court has repeatedly distinguished sentencing from the adjudication of guilt. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), the Court upheld a judge’s use of out-of-court information at sentencing and explained that due process does not require sentencing procedures to be “freeze[d]” in trial form. *Id.* at 251. The Court later reaffirmed that principle in *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995), and *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 153–54 (1997) (per curiam). Those cases establish that sentencing, unlike guilt adjudication, has never been subject to the full panoply of constitutional trial protections.

That distinction controls here. In *Monge v. California*, 16 Cal. 4th 826, 848–52, 67 Cal. Rptr. 2d 546, 941 P.2d 41 (1997), the California Supreme Court correctly held that a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital case is part of sentencing, not a separate offense. The United States Supreme Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies generally to such proceedings. To the contrary, its cases show the opposite. In *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18–19 (1919), the Court allowed a harsher sentence on retrial after a murder conviction was reversed. And in *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969), while the Court recognized protection against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, it did not suggest that sentencing enhancements are themselves offenses for double jeopardy purposes.

Petitioner relies on *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), and *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), but those cases were limited to capital sentencing. In *Bullington*, the Court held that a capital sentencing hearing with “the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence” could trigger double jeopardy protection. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. *Rumsey* followed the same reasoning in a capital case. *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209–10. The Court emphasized the unique structure of capital sentencing in *Monge v. California*, 524 U.S. 721, 731–32 (1998), explaining that “the death penalty is different,” *id.* at 732, and that the concerns driving *Bullington* do not extend to noncapital sentence enhancements. See also *Spaziano v. Florida*, 468 U.S. 447, 459–60 (1984), overruled on other grounds by *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 92 (2016).

This case is materially different from *Bullington*. A prior conviction allegation under California’s Three Strikes law does not expose the defendant to a separate punishment for a new offense; it is a recidivism determination that affects the sentence for the current offense. The Supreme Court has consistently treated recidivism findings as sentencing factors, not substantive charges. See *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728, 732 (1948); *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 747 (1994). Because the proceeding is a sentencing phase, not a trial of guilt, a failure of proof at the first hearing does not amount to an acquittal of an “offense” within the meaning of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

Nor does the state-law right to have prior convictions tried to a jury transform the proceeding into a constitutionally protected criminal trial. A state may provide additional procedures without importing every constitutional trial guarantee. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251; *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967). California’s statutory choice to submit prior conviction allegations to a jury, see Cal. Penal Code § 1025, does not alter the federal double jeopardy analysis. As the Supreme Court explained in *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 729–30, “the pronouncement of sentence has never carried the finality that attaches to an acquittal.” That principle forecloses petitioner’s claim.

Finally, allowing retrial here does not offend any core double jeopardy value. The Clause guards against repeated attempts to convict after acquittal, multiple punishments for the same offense, and prosecutorial harassment. *Brown v. Ohio*, 432 U.S. 161, 165 (1977). Retrial of a sentencing allegation after appellate reversal for insufficient proof of the prior conviction’s qualifying nature implicates none of those concerns. The defendant has already been convicted of the substantive drug offenses; the only question is whether his sentence should be enhanced based on a prior serious felony conviction. Under *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 734, retrial is permissible.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
