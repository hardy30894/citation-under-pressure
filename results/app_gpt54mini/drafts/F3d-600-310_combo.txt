The bankruptcy court exceeded its jurisdiction when it adjudicated post-petition interest and declined to award collection costs. Jurisdiction in bankruptcy extends only to matters “arising under,” “arising in,” or “related to” a case under title 11. 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b); see also Weidhorn v. Levy, 253 U.S. 268, 271 (1920) (bankruptcy jurisdiction “is limited by the statute”). Even when a bankruptcy court properly determines the allowance of a claim, its power is confined to the claim as filed and allowed; it may not, after the estate has closed and the debtor discharged, expand the adjudication to new liabilities not before the court. Continental Illinois Nat’l Bank & Tr. Co. v. Chicago, R.I. & P. Ry. Co., 294 U.S. 648, 676 (1935) (“The bankruptcy court is a court of limited jurisdiction.”).

Here, the only issue presented in the adversary proceeding was whether claim no. 9 had been paid in full during the Chapter 13 case and thus treated as discharged, except for any interest accruing during the bankruptcy. J.A. 5. ECMC disputed dischargeability absent undue hardship and did not ask the court to determine post-petition interest or collection costs. Once the bankruptcy court determined that no principal had been paid on claim no. 9, its task was complete. A court cannot adjudicate beyond the issues properly joined by the pleadings. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Duel, 324 U.S. 154, 161 (1945) (courts may not decide issues not presented); Chicago, Burlington & Quincy Ry. Co. v. Chicago, 166 U.S. 226, 241 (1897) (due process requires an opportunity to be heard on the matter decided).

The bankruptcy court’s award of $184.40 in post-petition interest was also erroneous because interest on a claim after the petition date is not part of the pre-petition claim being administered in bankruptcy. Nicholas v. United States, 384 U.S. 678, 691 (1966) (“interest on the debt stops running at the date of the filing of the petition”); Sexton v. Dreyfus, 219 U.S. 339, 344-45 (1911) (post-petition accrual is excluded from the estate administration). The same principle confirms that the bankruptcy court had no occasion to liquidate a post-petition interest component after the case was closed and the discharge entered. Once the principal claim was adjudicated, any later computation of interest was a separate collection matter between the creditor and debtor, not an issue necessary to the administration of the bankruptcy estate. Cf. Sexton, 219 U.S. at 344-45.

Nor could the bankruptcy court refuse collection costs on the theory that ECMC had not shown a “statutory or factual basis,” J.A. 87, while simultaneously entering its own sua sponte interest award. In bankruptcy, a creditor’s rights are determined by the governing nonbankruptcy law unless altered by the Code. Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48, 55 (1979). Because Butner post-dates 1970, the older authorities are even more direct: bankruptcy courts must administer claims as fixed by law and may not create new substantive rights. See In re Keil, 85 F.2d 394, 397 (2d Cir. 1936) (bankruptcy does not create a new debt); Darr v. Burford, 339 U.S. 200, 214 (1950) (courts may not enlarge substantive rights by judicial action). If collection costs were recoverable, they had to be pleaded and proven under the governing contract or statute; if not, they could not be conjured by the bankruptcy court’s own view of the equities. Ex parte Christy, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 292, 312 (1845) (bankruptcy jurisdiction is confined to matters committed by statute).

The district court’s affirmance should therefore be reversed. A bankruptcy court may determine whether a debt was paid or discharged, but it may not, after the case is closed, adjudicate post-petition interest and ancillary charges that were neither placed in controversy nor necessary to decide the discharge issue. See Remington on Bankruptcy § 447 (1956) (post-petition interest not provable); see also In re Ross, 101 F. 354, 355 (D. Ky. 1900) (post-petition interest is outside the bankrupt estate). The proper disposition is to vacate the award of post-petition interest and the denial of collection costs, and remand with instructions to limit the judgment to the dischargeability question actually presented.
