**ARGUMENT**

**I. Congress Did Not Clearly Express an Intent to Strip Missouri of Its Sovereign Authority to Fix the Tenure of Its Own Judges.**

The Supremacy Clause resolves conflicts between valid federal law and state law, but before any such conflict may be found, a court must be satisfied that Congress actually intended to legislate in the disputed area. Where, as here, a federal statute is claimed to override a State's control of "the structure and integral operations" of its own government, that intent must be unmistakably clear. This Court has long held that federal statutes are not read to displace the States' historic powers "unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947). Nothing is more fundamental to a State's sovereign structure than the composition and tenure of its own judiciary.

This Court has repeatedly recognized that the qualifications, discipline, and continued service of those who exercise judicial power are matters "peculiarly within" the State's own province. *In re Summers*, 325 U.S. 561, 564 (1945). Likewise, the manner by which a State chooses its officers—whether by election or appointment—is a decision this Court has left to the States themselves. *Sailors v. Board of Education*, 387 U.S. 105 (1967). And in *Taylor v. Beckham*, 178 U.S. 548 (1900), this Court confirmed that federal constitutional guarantees do not confer upon federal courts a general power to dictate the terms on which a State's own officers hold their offices.

Against this backdrop, the ADEA's exception for an "appointee on the policymaking level," 29 U.S.C. § 630(f), must be read to encompass Missouri's appointed judges. Judges do not merely apply mechanical rules; they exercise judgment, discretion, and—in the interstices left by statute and precedent—make law for the State. Congress gave no indication that it intended, through a general employment statute, to reach into the heart of a State's judicial branch and dictate how long its judges may serve. Absent the requisite clear statement, this Court should not read the ADEA to work so significant an intrusion on Missouri's sovereign choice.

**II. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Age for Judges Easily Satisfies Rational-Basis Review Under the Equal Protection Clause.**

Age is not a suspect classification, and judicial tenure is not a fundamental right. Where neither is present, this Court's equal protection inquiry is deferential in the extreme. A classification will be sustained "if any state of facts reasonably can be conceived that would sustain it." *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911). The State need not prove the facts before the reviewing court; it is enough that a rational basis might exist. *Id.*

This Court has consistently applied that forgiving standard to legislative line-drawing of exactly this kind. In *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420 (1961), the Court explained that "the constitutional safeguard is offended only if the classification rests on grounds wholly irrelevant to the achievement of the State's objective," and that "a statutory discrimination will not be set aside if any state of facts reasonably may be conceived to justify it." *Id.* at 425–26. So too in *Williamson v. Lee Optical Co.*, 348 U.S. 483 (1955), the Court upheld an imperfect legislative classification because "the law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional. It is enough that there is an evil at hand for correction, and that it might be thought that the particular legislative measure was a rational way to correct it." *Id.* at 488. And in *Railway Express Agency v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106 (1949), the Court reiterated that "it is no requirement of equal protection that all evils of the same genus be eradicated or none at all." *Id.* at 110.

Missouri's line at age seventy is precisely the sort of measure these decisions protect. A mandatory retirement age relieves the State of the difficulty of case-by-case removal of judges whose faculties may have declined, avoids the delay and disruption that can accompany disability or death in office, and ensures a steady, predictable renewal of the bench. That some judges past seventy remain vigorous, and some younger judges do not, does not defeat the classification; a State "does not violate the Equal Protection Clause merely because the classifications made by its laws are imperfect." *Lindsley*, 220 U.S. at 78. Nor does the Fourteenth Amendment strip the States of the broad latitude they have traditionally enjoyed to regulate the qualifications and continued service of their own public officers. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1 (1944).

Petitioners' equal protection challenge asks this Court to substitute its own judgment about the wisdom of mandatory judicial retirement for that of the people of Missouri, who adopted Article V, Section 26 as part of their state constitution. That is not the role of equal protection review under this Court's settled precedents. Because a rational basis plainly supports Missouri's choice, the Fourteenth Amendment claim fails.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
