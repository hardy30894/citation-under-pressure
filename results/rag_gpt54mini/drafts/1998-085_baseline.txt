**Argument**

Punitive damages under Title VII do not require proof that the employer’s conduct was separately “egregious” or “outrageous” apart from the state of mind Congress specified. The statute authorizes punitive damages when the plaintiff shows that the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That text supplies the governing standard. Courts may not graft onto it an additional threshold of outrageousness that Congress did not write. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994) (describing the Civil Rights Act of 1991 as expanding monetary relief for intentional discrimination); Curtis v. Loether, 415 U.S. 189, 195-96 (1974) (recognizing punitive damages as a routine form of relief in civil rights damages actions).

This Court’s punitive-damages jurisprudence has never required proof of “egregious” conduct as an independent element. In Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), the Court held that punitive damages are available when the defendant acts with a culpable mental state, and it rejected the contention that “actual malicious intent” must be shown in every case. Id. at 50-51. The Court explained that punitive damages are not awarded as of right, but it did not make their availability depend on conduct that is separately “egregious” or “outrageous.” Id. at 52. Rather, the inquiry turns on the defendant’s state of mind. Id. at 56. Section 1981a(b)(1) adopts the same sort of mental-state formulation. The question is whether the employer acted with the requisite malice or reckless indifference, not whether its conduct crosses some extra-textual outrage threshold.

Nothing in Title VII suggests Congress intended to impose a stricter punitive-damages standard than the statute’s words. To the contrary, the 1991 Act was enacted because Congress concluded that “additional remedies under Federal law are needed to deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination in the workplace.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a note. Congress thus created a damages remedy for intentional discrimination, and it capped that remedy by employer size. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3). That structure confirms that Congress chose calibration through text and caps, not through an added judicial requirement that the conduct be independently outrageous. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 252-55.

The proper focus on state of mind also accords with the Court’s treatment of civil-rights statutes more generally. In United States v. Burke, 504 U.S. 229, 237-38 (1992), the Court emphasized that Title VII protects against “fundamental injury” to personal rights, and that discrimination in employment is “no less injurious” than other forms of discrimination. Punitive damages are designed to punish and deter knowing or reckless violations of those rights. Smith, 461 U.S. at 54-56. Where an employer acts with conscious disregard for federally protected rights, the statutory standard is satisfied; no separate showing that the facts are “egregious” in some free-floating sense is needed.

Respondent’s position also preserves the line Congress drew between liability and remedy. Title VII liability is established by intentional discrimination, which may be proven by a prima facie case and pretext evidence. McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); St. Mary’s Honor Center v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993). Punitive damages then require the additional statutory showing of malice or reckless indifference. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). That is already an enhanced threshold. Requiring plaintiffs to prove something further—an undefined level of “egregiousness”—would add a third layer Congress did not enact.

The contrary view misreads the statutory term “reckless indifference.” Reckless indifference is itself a measure of culpability, and it is more than negligence or a good-faith mistake. Smith, 461 U.S. at 40-41, 52-56. If an employer consciously disregards an employee’s federally protected rights, punitive damages are authorized. If Congress had wanted to limit punitive damages to “extraordinarily egregious cases,” it could have said so. It did not.

The district court therefore erred in refusing to submit punitive damages to the jury on the ground that the ADA’s conduct was not sufficiently “egregious” or “outrageous.” The correct inquiry under 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) is whether the ADA acted with malice or reckless indifference to Kolstad’s federally protected rights. Because the jury found intentional sex discrimination, and because the evidence would permit a finding of the requisite state of mind, the punitive-damages claim should have gone to the jury. See Curtis, 415 U.S. at 195-96; Smith, 461 U.S. at 50-56; Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 252-55.
