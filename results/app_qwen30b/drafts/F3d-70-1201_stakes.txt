**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in instructing the jury to disregard testimony concerning post-accident design changes to the Eeger Beever wood chipper, despite the fact that such evidence was properly admitted for a limited, permissible purpose—impeachment of Morbark’s trial strategy. That instruction, coupled with the court’s failure to correct the resulting prejudice, constituted reversible error under Federal Rule of Evidence 407 and established principles of evidentiary fairness. The court’s ruling effectively nullified the jury’s ability to assess the credibility of Morbark’s repeated assertions that the seventeen-inch infeed chute was safe and unchanged, thereby undermining the integrity of the trial and warranting reversal.

Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence provides that “evidence of subsequent remedial measures… is not admissible to prove negligence, culpable conduct, a defect in a product, a need for a warning, or a need for a safety device.” *Fed. R. Evid. 407*. However, the rule explicitly permits such evidence “to prove ownership, control, or feasibility of precautionary measures.” *Id.* This narrow exception is not a mere technicality—it is a foundational principle of evidentiary fairness, allowing a party to rebut a misrepresentation or implication that a product’s design was unimprovable or inherently safe. As this Court has recognized, “the rule is designed to encourage manufacturers to improve their products after accidents, without fear that such improvements will be used against them in litigation.” *Barnes v. General Motors Corp.*, 878 F.2d 1357, 1360 (11th Cir. 1989).

Here, Morbark’s counsel repeatedly implied to the jury that the seventeen-inch infeed chute remained unchanged and was still in use by the City of DeFuniak Springs and other governmental agencies. In opening statement, counsel stated: “the evidence will indicate that after Hurricane Andrew the Army Corps of Engineers ordered thirty machines just like the one that is involved in this case.” *R4-141-22*. This statement was not a mere factual assertion—it was a calculated effort to suggest that the design had been validated by government use and that no improvements were deemed necessary. The court’s pretrial ruling excluding evidence of subsequent remedial measures was properly grounded in Rule 407, but it did not immunize Morbark from the consequences of its own trial tactics.

When Morbark’s counsel elicited testimony from John Infinger that the same machine was still in use, the court correctly concluded that Morbark had “opened the door” to rebuttal. *See United States v. Hines*, 887 F.2d 1517, 1522 (11th Cir. 1989) (“A party who introduces evidence that creates a misleading impression may be required to allow the opposing party to correct it.”). The court’s own warning to Morbark’s counsel—“if you change that here, then don’t expect those rulings to apply any longer”—confirmed that the pretrial exclusion of remedial measures evidence was conditional upon the party’s adherence to its trial strategy. *R4-141-56*. Once Morbark invited the jury to infer that the design was safe and unchanged, the jury had a right to hear that the infeed chute had, in fact, been lengthened through welding—a change that directly contradicted Morbark’s narrative.

The court’s subsequent instruction to the jury to “disregard” the testimony about the modified chute was legally erroneous. The testimony was not offered to prove defect or negligence; it was offered solely to impeach Morbark’s misleading implication that the design had remained static. *See Fed. R. Evid. 407 advisory committee note* (“The rule does not bar evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered for a purpose other than to prove negligence or defect.”). The jury was entitled to consider the modification as evidence of feasibility and as a response to a known hazard—precisely the purpose for which Rule 407 permits such evidence. To instruct the jury to disregard this testimony, without any limiting instruction on its proper use, deprived Wood of a fair opportunity to counter a deliberate misrepresentation.

This Court has long held that “a trial court’s error in admitting or excluding evidence is reversible if it affects the substantial rights of a party.” *United States v. Mendoza*, 978 F.2d 1205, 1210 (11th Cir. 1992). Here, the error was not merely procedural—it was prejudicial. Morbark’s strategy was to portray the seventeen-inch chute as safe and unimprovable. The jury was left with no means to assess the credibility of that claim, despite clear evidence that the design had been altered post-accident. The court’s instruction effectively shielded Morbark from the consequences of its own conduct, undermining the very purpose of Rule 407 and the fairness of the trial.

For these reasons, the district court’s instruction to disregard the testimony concerning the modified infeed chute was an abuse of discretion and a clear error of law. The jury was deprived of critical context necessary to evaluate Morbark’s credibility and the safety of the product. The error was not harmless, and the denial of Wood’s motion for a new trial was therefore improper.

The judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial consistent with this opinion.
