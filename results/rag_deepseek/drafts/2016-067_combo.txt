ARGUMENT

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act ("FDCPA") regulates only "debt collectors," defined as those who collect debts "owed or due *another*." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). A creditor collecting its own debt is not a debt collector. Santander Consumer USA purchased the petitioners' defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collected on its own behalf. Because Santander was collecting its own debt, it was not a "debt collector" subject to the FDCPA. The Fourth Circuit correctly affirmed dismissal.

**I. A Purchaser of Debt Collecting for Itself Is a Creditor, Not a Debt Collector.**

The FDCPA's text draws a fundamental line between those who collect for themselves and those who collect for others. This distinction is rooted in the common law of principal and agent. *See Wilson v. Edmonds*, 130 U.S. 472, 474 (1889) (recognizing that one who collects on his own account is a "principal in final settlement," not an agent). A debt collector acts as an agent for a creditor-principal; a creditor acts for itself. *See Chapman v. Forsyth & Limerick*, 43 U.S. 202, 204 (1844) (distinguishing a factor who sells "for others" from one who sells his own goods). The FDCPA codifies this ancient distinction.

When Santander purchased the petitioners' loans, it became the owner of the debts—the creditor. It no longer collected "for another"; it collected what was owed to itself. This Court has long held that the purchase of a debt transfers the right to collect it as principal. *See United States v. Erie Ry. Co.*, 107 U.S. 1, 3 (1883) (a debt owed by the company is a "debt which the company owes" to the holder of the obligation); *Mason v. Gamble*, 62 U.S. 390, 393 (1859) (the "collector of the port" collects duties for the government, not for himself). A purchaser of debt stepping into the shoes of the original lender is no more a "debt collector" than the original lender would be.

**II. The Default Status of the Debt Does Not Convert a Creditor into a Debt Collector.**

The petitioners argue that because the debts were in default when Santander purchased them, Santander must be a debt collector. The statute contains no such rule. The definition of "debt collector" turns on *for whom* the debt is collected, not on *when* it was acquired. *See East St. Louis v. Amy*, 120 U.S. 600, 603 (1887) (a creditor's "debt" and the obligation to pay "when due" are the same regardless of when the debt arose). The exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) for debt not in default applies only to persons who are *already* debt collectors—it does not create a new category of debt collectors from creditors who purchase defaulted debt.

This Court has consistently treated the status of the person collecting as determinative. *Ivanhoe Bldg. & Loan Ass'n v. Orr*, 295 U.S. 243, 246 (1935) (a creditor who forecloses and buys in the property "may prove for the full amount of the debt" as a creditor, not as a collector for another). Santander, like the mortgagee in *Ivanhoe*, acquired the debt and then sought to collect the balance. It acted as a creditor.

**III. Collecting One's Own Debt Is Not "Collecting for Another."**

The petitioners contend that Santander "regularly collects" debts for others because it sometimes services loans for other entities. But the FDCPA regulates specific conduct, not general corporate character. When Santander collected on debts it owned, it was not collecting for CitiFinancial Auto. *Libby v. Hopkins*, 104 U.S. 303, 305 (1881) (mutual credit and setoff require that the parties' debts be due to each other "in the same right"). A company that is both a creditor and a debt collector in different transactions is regulated only when acting as a debt collector. *Western Tie & Timber Co. v. Brown*, 196 U.S. 502, 507 (1905) (an obligation to remit proceeds "for account of" the owner exists only when the collector is acting as an agent, not when it owns the debt).

Similarly, *Selden v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 94 U.S. 419, 421 (1877), held that an entity lending "its own capital" on mortgage securities is a principal, not a banker collecting for others. Santander invested its capital to purchase loans; it did not undertake to collect for CitiFinancial Auto. *See also Nixdorff v. Smith*, 41 U.S. 132, 134 (1842) (a partner who "assumed the whole of the debts" became the principal debtor, not an agent for collection).

**IV. The Petitioners' Interpretation Would Create an Anomaly.**

If the mere fact of default converted every purchaser of defaulted debt into a debt collector, then every bank or finance company that bought a nonperforming loan would be subject to the FDCPA even when collecting its own asset. This would sweep in countless creditors Congress never intended to regulate. *Chapman*, 43 U.S. at 204 (rejecting a construction that would treat all "commission merchants" as fiduciaries regardless of ownership). The FDCPA's purpose is to curb third-party collection abuses, not to regulate creditors' own collection efforts.

Because Santander was collecting debts it owned, it was a creditor, not a debt collector. The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
