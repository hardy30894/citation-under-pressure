The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, as established in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969), precludes retrial on a prior conviction allegation in noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal, as stated in North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969).

Although the Court has not directly addressed whether the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, its precedents suggest that the Clause's protections extend beyond traditional trials. In Ex parte Lange, 85 U.S. 163, 173-74 (1873), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause prohibits not only multiple punishments for the same offense but also multiple prosecutions. This principle is relevant to sentencing proceedings, where a defendant is subject to increased punishment based on prior convictions.

The Court has also recognized that sentencing proceedings can be akin to trials, with the government bearing the burden of proof. In United States ex rel. Vajtauer v. Commissioner of Immigration, 273 U.S. 103, 106 (1927), the Court noted that a deportation proceeding, though not a traditional trial, required proof of facts to justify the government's action. Similarly, in noncapital sentencing proceedings, the government must prove the existence of prior convictions to enhance a defendant's sentence.

The historical context of the Double Jeopardy Clause, as discussed in Kepner v. United States, 195 U.S. 100, 128-29 (1904), and United States v. Perez, 22 U.S. 579, 580 (1824), demonstrates that the Clause was intended to protect defendants from repeated prosecutions and punishments. Applying this principle to noncapital sentencing proceedings, where a defendant faces increased punishment based on prior convictions, is consistent with the Clause's historical purpose.

Furthermore, the Court has acknowledged that the Double Jeopardy Clause is not limited to capital cases. In Green v. United States, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957), the Court applied the Clause to a noncapital case, holding that a defendant could not be retried for a greater offense after being acquitted of that offense in a prior trial.

In Stroud v. United States, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919), the Court addressed a situation where a defendant was retried and received a harsher sentence. Although the Court ultimately upheld the sentence, it did so on the grounds that the initial sentence was not a final acquittal of the greater punishment. This reasoning implies that, in certain circumstances, sentencing proceedings can be subject to Double Jeopardy protections.

Citing these precedents, it is clear that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies to noncapital sentencing proceedings, including those involving prior conviction allegations. The Clause's protections against multiple prosecutions and punishments for the same offense are fundamental to the American scheme of justice, as noted in Benton v. Maryland, 395 U.S. at 794. Therefore, retrial on a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding is barred by the Double Jeopardy Clause.
