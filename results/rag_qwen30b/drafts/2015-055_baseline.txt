**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 130 S. Ct. 1265 (2010), applies retroactively to invalidate predicate convictions used under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA)—is one of profound consequence for the integrity of the criminal justice system. The Supreme Court has long recognized that when a new rule of constitutional law “clarifies” a prior ambiguity in the law, it is “not only appropriate but necessary” to apply that rule retroactively to ensure that “the rule of law is not undermined by the passage of time.” *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989). The rule in *Johnson* did not create a new constitutional standard; it merely clarified that the ACCA’s residual clause—defining a “violent felony” as one that “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another”—was unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. *Johnson*, 130 S. Ct. at 1271. Because the clause failed to provide “fair notice” of the conduct it punished, it violated the fundamental principle that “no man shall be held criminally responsible for conduct which the law does not make criminal.” *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377, 382 (1922).

The Court in *Johnson* did not announce a new rule of criminal law; it applied settled constitutional principles to a statute that had long been unconstitutionally vague. The residual clause, as interpreted by lower courts, required judges to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a crime—a task that was “arbitrary and unpredictable.” *Johnson*, 130 S. Ct. at 1271. This lack of clarity rendered the clause “so vague that it failed to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punished.” Id. The Court emphasized that “a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits.” *Id.* at 1270. That warning was absent in the ACCA’s residual clause, which allowed for the imposition of life-altering sentences based on a standard that no reasonable person could understand.

The rule in *Johnson* is therefore a “clarification” of existing constitutional law, not a new rule. It did not expand the scope of the law but instead invalidated a provision that had been unconstitutionally overbroad and unconstitutionally vague. As the Court has held, “a decision that overrules a prior precedent is not necessarily a new rule for purposes of retroactivity.” *Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987). In *Griffith*, the Court held that a new rule of constitutional law “clarifying” the meaning of the Constitution must be applied retroactively to all cases pending on direct review or not yet final. *Id.* at 328. The Court reasoned that “the rule of law must be applied consistently,” and that “the integrity of the judicial process depends on the finality of judgments.” *Id.* at 327. The same principle applies here: *Johnson* did not create a new rule; it restored the constitutional balance by invalidating a clause that had been unconstitutionally vague.

The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that *Johnson* does not apply retroactively—undermines the very purpose of the exclusionary rule and the Due Process Clause. If a defendant is sentenced under a statute that is later declared unconstitutionally vague, the sentence must be vacated. To hold otherwise would allow the government to benefit from a law that was never constitutionally valid. As the Court stated in *United States v. Booker*, 543 U.S. 220, 245 (2005), “the Constitution requires that criminal penalties be imposed only under laws that are clear and predictable.” The ACCA’s residual clause failed that test. Its application to Welch’s 1996 conviction for Florida “strong arm” robbery was therefore unconstitutional.

The Court has repeatedly held that when a statute is declared unconstitutionally vague, its application to past conduct must be invalidated. In *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437 (1965), the Court held that a statute that failed to give fair notice could not be applied retroactively. Similarly, in *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259 (1997), the Court emphasized that “a criminal statute must give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits.” *Id.* at 266. The residual clause failed that test. It allowed for the imposition of a 15-year mandatory minimum sentence based on a standard that no reasonable person could understand.

The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit must be reversed. The rule in *Johnson* applies retroactively to all cases not yet final. Welch’s sentence, based on a conviction that was rendered under an unconstitutionally vague statute, must be vacated. The Constitution demands no less.
