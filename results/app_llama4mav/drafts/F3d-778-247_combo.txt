ARGUMENT

I. The Search Warrant Failed to Supply a Nexus to the Place Being Searched

The Fourth Amendment requires that a search warrant be supported by probable cause, describing with particularity the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. Nathanson v. United States, 290 U.S. 41, 47 (1933). To establish probable cause, there must be a nexus between the items to be seized and the place to be searched. Dumbra v. United States, 268 U.S. 435, 441 (1925). The affidavit supporting the warrant must provide a sufficient basis for the issuing judge to conclude that evidence of the crime will be found at the specified location. Steele v. United States, 267 U.S. 498, 504 (1925).

Here, the affidavit failed to establish a sufficient nexus between the items to be seized and Joubert's parents' home. The affidavit merely stated that Joubert lived at the residence and that he had destroyed his computer tower. However, it did not provide any information linking the residence to the alleged crimes or the items to be seized. The officer-affiant's conclusory statement that persons engaged in the molestation and exploitation of minors often maintain possession of physical or electronic documents is insufficient to establish a nexus. Byars v. United States, 273 U.S. 28, 29 (1927). The warrant was therefore invalid, and the evidence seized should have been suppressed.

II. The District Court Abused its Discretion in Admitting Testimony of Uncharged Child Molestation

The admission of evidence of uncharged crimes is governed by the principles of relevance and prejudice. Boyd v. United States, 142 U.S. 450, 458 (1892). While evidence of other crimes may be admissible to show motive, intent, or a common scheme, it must be carefully scrutinized to avoid undue prejudice. People v. Zackowitz, 172 N.E. 466, 468 (N.Y. 1930). In this case, the testimony of uncharged child molestation was highly prejudicial and lacked probative value. The district court failed to properly weigh the prejudicial effect of this testimony against its probative value, as required by Michelson v. United States, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948).

III. The Commerce Clause Cannot Support Application to Joubert of the Federal Criminal Statutes

The Commerce Clause grants Congress the power to regulate commerce among the states. United States v. E.C. Knight Co., 156 U.S. 1, 12 (1895). However, this power is limited to regulating activities that have a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Carter v. Carter Coal Co., 298 U.S. 238, 303 (1936). The statutes under which Joubert was indicted, 18 U.S.C. § 2251(a) and 18 U.S.C. § 2252A(a)(5)(B), exceed Congress's Commerce Clause authority as applied to Joubert. The government failed to demonstrate that Joubert's actions had a substantial effect on interstate commerce. Hammer v. Dagenhart, 247 U.S. 251, 276 (1918).

IV. Joubert's 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

The Eighth Amendment prohibits excessive and cruel punishments. Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910). A sentence is substantively unreasonable if it is disproportionate to the offense committed. O'Neil v. Vermont, 144 U.S. 323, 331 (1892). Joubert's 480-month sentence is excessive and disproportionate to his crimes. The district court failed to properly consider the sentencing factors, as required by Ballew v. United States, 160 U.S. 187, 202 (1895).

For the foregoing reasons, Joubert's convictions and sentence should be reversed.
