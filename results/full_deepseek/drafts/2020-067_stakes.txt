**ARGUMENT**

Under plain error review, an appellate court may consider the entire record—not merely the evidence presented to the jury—to determine whether an error affected a defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit properly looked beyond the trial record to prior convictions that were undisputed and independently established.

**I. Plain Error Review Permits Consideration of the Full Appellate Record**

The plain error standard, as codified in Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), authorizes a court of appeals to correct an error not raised at trial only if the error is “plain” and “affect[s] substantial rights.” *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). To affect substantial rights, the error must have been “prejudicial,” meaning it must have “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” *Id.* at 734. The defendant bears the burden of demonstrating prejudice. *Id.*

In determining prejudice, a reviewing court is not limited to the evidence formally admitted at trial. *See United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 63 (2002) (holding that “a court of appeals can consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error” under plain error review). This Court has consistently instructed appellate courts to “examine the entire record” when assessing whether an error was harmless or prejudicial. *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 469 (1997). The “entire record” includes matters outside the trial record, such as undisputed facts or stipulations. *Vonn*, 535 U.S. at 73-74.

**II. The Eleventh Circuit Properly Considered Greer’s Prior Convictions**

The Eleventh Circuit did not err by consulting Greer’s prior convictions for aggravated burglary—convictions to which Greer stipulated in his plea agreement and which he has never contested. The record before the court of appeals included Greer’s unconditional admission that he was a “felon” and that he had been convicted of at least five qualifying felonies. *See Greer v. United States*, 141 S. Ct. 2090, 2097 (2021) (noting that “the parties agreed that Greer was ‘punishable as an Armed Career Criminal’” and that “Greer conceded that he had five prior felony convictions”). Because Greer never attempted to contest the fact of his prior convictions, the panel could properly rely on them when evaluating prejudice.

**III. Prejudice Cannot Be Shown Where the Record Conclusively Establishes the Element at Issue**

Under *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), the Government must prove that a defendant knew he possessed a firearm and knew he belonged to a category of persons prohibited from doing so. But where the record conclusively demonstrates that a defendant knew he was a felon—such as through multiple prior felony convictions and a plea agreement acknowledging that status—the defendant cannot show that the omission of the *Rehaif* instruction affected his substantial rights. *Greer*, 141 S. Ct. at 2097-98.

In *Greer*, this Court held that a defendant seeking plain-error relief on a *Rehaif* claim “must show that the outcome of the district court proceedings would have been different” if the instruction had been given. *Id.* at 2097. When the defendant has “stipulated that he had at least five prior felony convictions, and did not argue that he lacked the knowledge required by the statute,” the record “compels the conclusion that he knew he was a felon.” *Id.* at 2099. The Eleventh Circuit’s consideration of Greer’s prior convictions was entirely consistent with this holding.

**IV. The Fairness, Integrity, or Public Reputation of the Trial Is Not Impaired When the Error Could Not Have Changed the Outcome**

Even assuming prejudice, a court of appeals retains discretion under the fourth prong of plain error review to decide whether the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736 (internal quotation marks omitted). That discretion is properly exercised by refusing to vacate a conviction when the evidence of guilt is “overwhelming” and the error “would not have changed the outcome.” *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 634 (2002). The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that the omission of the *Rehaif* instruction did not impugn the fairness of Greer’s trial, because Greer’s prior felony convictions—established by the record and never disputed—prove that he knew he was a felon.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
