**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 1407(a) Mandates Remand to the Transferor District for Trial and Precludes Self-Assignment Under Section 1404(a)**

The plain text of 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) requires that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel . . . at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” This Court has long recognized that statutory commands are not optional. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55, 61 (1949) (“Congress has removed the compulsion which determined the [earlier] case, and the [state] court should no longer regard it as controlling.”). The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) lacks authority to retain transferred actions for trial, and district courts lack authority to invoke § 1404(a) to circumvent the remand mandate. *Id.*; *Missouri ex rel. Southern Railway Co. v. Mayfield*, 340 U.S. 1, 4 (1950) (Section 1404(a) “speaks to federal courts” and “addresses itself only to that federal forum in which a lawsuit has been initiated”).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary conclusion rests on an erroneous interpretation of § 1404(a) and ignores the jurisdictional limits of § 1407(a). *Pope v. Atlantic Coast Line R.R.*, 345 U.S. 379, 383 (1953) (“Section 1404(a) . . . provides for the transfer of [actions] to a more convenient federal court. It does not speak to [the power of] some court other than the forum where a lawsuit is initiated.”). A transferee court may not arrogate jurisdiction over a case that the statute commands be remanded.

**II. The Statutory Scheme Forecloses Self-Assignment as a Permissible “Pretrial Proceeding”**

Section 1407(a) authorizes transfer only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings.” This Court has repeatedly emphasized that “pretrial” means just that—proceedings leading up to trial, not the trial itself. *Herb v. Pitcairn*, 325 U.S. 77, 79 (1945) (holding that an action is “commenced” for limitations purposes when filed in a court with jurisdiction to proceed to judgment, not merely to conduct pretrial matters). The JPML’s rule purporting to authorize self-assignment exceeds its statutory mandate. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. at 61 (“Congress has removed the compulsion which determined the [earlier] case, and the [court] should no longer regard it as controlling.”).

Moreover, § 1404(a) does not empower a transferee court to retain a case for trial. *Kilpatrick v. Texas & Pac. Ry. Co.*, 337 U.S. 75, 78 (1949) (transfer under § 1404(a) must be to a district “where it might have been brought,” not to a district where the action could not have been filed initially). Here, Lexecon’s action could not have been filed in the District of Arizona for trial; thus, § 1404(a) provides no basis for the court’s self-assignment.

**III. The Legislative History and Policy of § 1407(a) Confirm That Remand Is Mandatory**

Congress enacted § 1407(a) to centralize pretrial proceedings in complex litigation while preserving plaintiffs’ choice of forum for trial. *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 638 (1964) (explaining that § 1407(a) balances efficiency with litigant rights). The statute’s remand requirement ensures that plaintiffs are not deprived of their constitutional and statutory right to select their trial forum. *Hoffman v. Blaski*, 363 U.S. 335, 343 (1960) (holding that transfer under § 1404(a) is permissible only to a district where the action “might have been brought,” preserving litigant choice).

The Ninth Circuit’s approach—allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial—undermines this balance. *Pope*, 345 U.S. at 383. It permits forum shopping by transferee judges and frustrates the statute’s purpose. *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. at 61.

**IV. This Court’s Pre-1970 Precedent Forecloses the Argument That § 1404(a) Permits Self-Assignment**

This Court has consistently rejected expansive readings of § 1404(a) that would permit courts to override statutory limits. *Parsons v. Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co.*, 375 U.S. 71, 73 (1963) (per curiam) (reversing a court of appeals that ordered transfer under § 1404(a) where the transferee district was not one “where it might have been brought”). The same principle applies here: the transferee court lacked authority to retain the case for trial under § 1404(a).

Similarly, *Continental Grain Co. v. Barge FBL-585*, 364 U.S. 19, 26 (1960), confirms that transfer under § 1404(a) must serve the convenience of parties and witnesses, not the convenience of the transferee judge. The court below acted outside its statutory authority by invoking § 1404(a) to retain the case.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court erred in invoking § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial. Section 1407(a) mandates remand to the transferor district, and § 1404(a) does not authorize self-assignment. This Court’s pre-1970 precedent confirms that the transferee court lacked jurisdiction to retain the case. The judgment below should be reversed.
