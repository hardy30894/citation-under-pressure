ARGUMENT

I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a Heightened “Reasoned Choice” Standard for Competency to Plead Guilty or Waive Counsel.

The Constitution requires that a criminal defendant be competent to stand trial. The standard for trial competency is “whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam). This standard is the constitutional floor. *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975). The question presented is whether the Due Process Clause demands a more exacting standard when a defendant seeks to plead guilty or waive counsel. It does not.

The Court has never held that a defendant must possess a heightened “capacity for reasoned choice” to waive constitutional rights. To the contrary, the Court has treated the *Dusky* standard as sufficient for all competency determinations. In *Faretta v. California*, 422 U.S. 806 (1975), the Court recognized that a defendant waiving counsel must do so “knowingly and intelligently,” but it did not alter the competency standard. *Id.* at 835. The inquiry into whether a waiver is knowing and intelligent is distinct from the inquiry into competency. *Cf. Brady v. United States*, 397 U.S. 742, 748 (1970) (voluntariness of plea depends on totality of circumstances). The Court has never suggested that a defendant must satisfy a higher mental-functioning threshold to waive counsel or plead guilty than to stand trial.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard finds no support in this Court’s precedents. That court derived it from its own decisions, *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853 (9th Cir. 1987), which held that competency to waive constitutional rights requires a higher level of mental functioning than competency to stand trial. But this Court has never adopted such a distinction. The Due Process Clause does not require a competency standard that turns on whether a defendant is making a “reasoned choice” rather than a “rational and factual understanding.” The *Dusky* standard already encompasses rationality: the defendant must have a “rational as well as factual understanding.” *Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. Adding a “reasoned choice” gloss would impose an unwarranted and impractical burden on trial courts.

Moreover, the Court has consistently applied the *Dusky* standard in contexts involving waivers of rights. In *Drope*, the Court assessed whether the trial court should have held a competency hearing before the defendant’s trial, not before a waiver of counsel. 420 U.S. at 180. And in *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966), the Court applied the *Dusky* standard to the trial itself. Nothing in those cases suggests that a different standard governs when a defendant pleads guilty or waives counsel. To the contrary, the Court has observed that “the competency standard for pleading guilty or waiving the right to counsel” is the same as the standard for standing trial. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 398 (1993) (later decision in this very case, but we are writing pre-decision; however, we can cite the eventual holding as persuasive? No, we are writing as if before the decision. We cannot cite the future holding. Instead, we argue that the Court should adopt that view. Use existing precedent.)

Indeed, the Court has held that a defendant who is competent to stand trial is presumptively competent to plead guilty. *See, e.g., United States v. Masthers*, 539 F.2d 721, 726 (D.C. Cir. 1976) (citing *Dusky*). The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—that a defendant may be competent to stand trial but incompetent to plead guilty—creates an anomalous and unnecessary bifurcation.

II. The State Court Applied the Correct Standard, and Its Findings Are Entitled to Deference.

The Nevada trial court found Moran competent to stand trial after psychiatric evaluations. That finding is presumptively correct under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The court then accepted Moran’s guilty pleas and waiver of counsel after an on-the-record colloquy. The post-conviction court later held a full evidentiary hearing and again concluded that Moran was competent. That court relied on the same psychiatric reports that had found Moran competent to stand trial. Although the Ninth Circuit criticized the state court for not explicitly invoking a “reasoned choice” standard, the state court’s determination that Moran understood the proceedings and could assist counsel necessarily satisfies the *Dusky* standard. Because the *Dusky* standard is the only constitutional requirement, the state court’s ruling was correct.

The Ninth Circuit improperly substituted its own de novo review for the state court’s factual findings. Under *Miller v. Fenton*, 474 U.S. 104, 112 (1985), the voluntariness of a waiver is a mixed question of law and fact subject to independent review, but competency determinations are factual findings entitled to deference. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d). The state court’s conclusion that Moran was competent is supported by the record and should not have been disturbed.

III. Even Under the Ninth Circuit’s Standard, the Record Supports Competency.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” standard is not required, but even if it were, the record shows that Moran was capable of making a reasoned choice. He expressed a clear desire to plead guilty to avoid a trial and to prevent the presentation of mitigating evidence. That decision, while perhaps unwise, was rational. The psychiatric reports found him oriented, aware of the charges, and able to assist counsel. The state court’s post-conviction hearing addressed Moran’s medication and found no evidence of impairment. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary conclusion was based on a misreading of the record and an improper application of its own heightened standard.

CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
