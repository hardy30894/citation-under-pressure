The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's facilities, although not traditional public forums like streets and parks, as described in Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939), were opened for use by the general public for certain purposes, thereby creating a limited public forum.

When the government creates a limited public forum, it is bound by the same standards as those applicable to traditional public forums in terms of content-based exclusions. The exclusion of speech from a limited public forum based on content is permissible only if it serves a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that interest, as required by Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940). The Supreme Court has consistently held that a state may not exclude speech from a public forum based on its content unless the exclusion is necessary to serve a compelling state interest, as seen in Lovell v. Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 451 (1938), and Staub v. City of Baxley, 355 U.S. 313, 321-22 (1958).

The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series, described as "Family oriented movie from a Christian perspective," was based on the religious content of the speech. Local Rule No. 7, which prohibits the use of school premises "by any group for religious purposes," constitutes a content-based exclusion. The rule is not viewpoint-neutral, as it discriminates against religious speech while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes. This is inconsistent with the principles of equal access to public forums, as emphasized in Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941).

The District has not demonstrated a compelling state interest in excluding Lamb's Chapel's film series. In fact, allowing the film series would not have violated the Establishment Clause, as the Court noted in Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), where it stated that "we are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being." The District's actions are more akin to a prior restraint on speech, which is not justified simply because there have been no previous instances of religious uses, as seen in Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 294 (1951), and Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951).

The lower court's reliance on the fact that the District had not previously allowed religious groups to use the facilities is misplaced. The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not justified by a history of prior religious uses, as there is no evidence that the District had previously allowed such uses. The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral and does not serve a compelling state interest.

In conclusion, the District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral and does not serve a compelling state interest.
