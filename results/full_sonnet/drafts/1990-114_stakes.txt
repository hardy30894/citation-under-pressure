# ARGUMENT

## I. THE ADEA DOES NOT REACH MISSOURI'S APPOINTED JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE "APPOINTEE[S] ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL"

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act excludes from its definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri's appointed judges fall squarely within that exclusion.

Judging is not merely mechanical application of settled rules; it is, in substantial part, an exercise of policymaking authority. As the First Circuit explained in a decision the Eighth Circuit adopted below, "'[p]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988). Missouri's own courts confirm this reality. The Missouri Supreme Court has not hesitated to displace settled doctrine when it determined that policy required a different rule, discarding the common-law doctrine of last clear chance in favor of comparative fault. *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc). And Missouri judges resolve questions of the first order for the entire polity, including questions of life and death. *See Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc). Beyond adjudication, Missouri's Chief Justice, appellate chief judges, and circuit presiding judges hold general administrative and rulemaking authority over the courts, including the power to resolve intractable disputes within the judicial hierarchy. *See In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). Judges who make common law for an entire State and who supervise the administration of justice are policymakers in every meaningful sense.

Petitioners' contrary reading—that the "policymaking level" exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees—cannot be squared with the statute's structure. If that exception excludes judges entirely, then in every State that appoints rather than elects its judges, no exception in § 630(f) would ever apply to the judiciary, even though Congress plainly exempted elected judges from ADEA coverage altogether. It is untenable to conclude that Congress meant appointed judges to receive less deference to state prerogative than elected judges performing the identical function. This anomaly "weighs heavily against such a narrow reading of the policymaking level exception." *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460–61 (D. Mass. 1988), *aff'd*, 858 F.2d 52 (1st Cir. 1988).

## II. PRINCIPLES OF FEDERALISM REQUIRE A CLEAR STATEMENT BEFORE THE ADEA IS READ TO OVERRIDE A STATE'S CONSTITUTIONAL QUALIFICATIONS FOR ITS OWN JUDGES

Even were the text less clear, this Court's federalism precedents compel the same result. The qualifications a State establishes for the judges who exercise its sovereign judicial power lie at the core of state sovereignty. This Court has long required that Congress "explicitly and unequivocally" express an intent to intrude upon such traditionally state functions before a federal statute will be read to do so. *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (explaining that "[i]n traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision"). Nothing in the ADEA's text or history reflects that Congress ever confronted, let alone resolved, the question whether it meant to displace a State's chosen method of ensuring vitality on its own bench. Absent such a clear statement, the Act should not be construed to conflict with Article V, Section 26 of the Missouri Constitution.

## III. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT AGE FOR JUDGES SATISFIES RATIONAL-BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

Age is not a suspect classification, and the right to hold judicial office past a certain age is not fundamental. This Court has squarely held that "[t]his Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental." *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). Mandatory retirement statutes for public employees, including judges, therefore need only be rationally related to a legitimate state interest. *Id.* at 314–15.

Missouri's rule easily clears that bar. A mandatory retirement age spares the State the "unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges" through individualized, adversarial fitness proceedings. *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980). It reflects the judgment that "the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode" and that mandatory retirement "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges." *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979). It also serves the State's interest in judicial turnover and diversity on the bench. *See Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988) (age limit "makes it possible to increase the proportion of minority group members and women in the judiciary"). That reasonable minds might debate the wisdom of these judgments does not deprive Missouri of a rational basis for its choice. Missouri need not prove its rule optimal—only rational. It has done so.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
