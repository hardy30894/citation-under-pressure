# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT BECAUSE APPELLANTS ESTABLISHED A GENUINE ISSUE OF MATERIAL FACT ON EVERY ELEMENT OF THEIR FRSA RETALIATION CLAIM

The Federal Railroad Safety Act protects an employee who "notif[ies], or attempt[s] to notify, the railroad carrier ... of a work-related personal injury or work-related illness of an employee." 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C). To defeat summary judgment, an employee need only show that protected activity was "a contributing factor" in the adverse action; the burden then shifts to the carrier to prove by clear and convincing evidence that it would have taken the same action absent the protected activity. 49 U.S.C. § 42121(b)(2)(B)(ii); *Araujo v. New Jersey Transit Rail Operations, Inc.*, 708 F.3d 152, 157 (3d Cir. 2013) (describing the "contributing factor" standard as "broad and forgiving"). This is a lenient, employee-protective standard. *See Kuduk v. BNSF Ry. Co.*, 768 F.3d 786, 789 (8th Cir. 2014) ("The contributing-factor standard was intended to be a broad standard requiring a lesser causal connection than 'motivating factor.'").

### A. Appellants Engaged in Protected Activity

There is no genuine dispute that Foster's recorded statement identifying the bridge as a hazardous location BNSF employees had previously reported, and Kline's and Snyder's written and oral statements describing the missing handrails and lighting, constitute notification of a "work-related personal injury" under § 20109(a)(1)(C). This Court has recognized that reports concerning the circumstances of an on-the-job injury—including statements identifying unsafe conditions contributing to that injury—fall squarely within the statute's protection. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 791. Moore's injury was unquestionably work-related, and each Appellant reported facts material to that injury to BNSF supervisory and claims personnel within days of the accident.

### B. A Reasonable Jury Could Find That the Protected Activity Was a Contributing Factor in BNSF's Discipline

The temporal proximity between Appellants' statements and BNSF's decision to open a disciplinary investigation is itself probative of causation. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 792 (noting that "very close" temporal proximity may support an inference of contributing factor). Here, BNSF issued its investigation notice on April 6—the day after Foster's recorded statement questioning why the crew change occurred at a location "previously ... reported to BNSF" as dangerous, and mere days after Kline and Snyder's written statements flagged the absence of side rails and lighting. This sequence permits the inference that BNSF's investigation, and the discipline that followed, was motivated at least in part by Appellants' criticism of the company's safety practices rather than by neutral rule enforcement.

The district court erred by treating BNSF's stated rationale—rule violations such as failure to use a whistle or flashlight—as conclusively negating causation at the summary judgment stage. The contributing-factor standard does not require that protected activity be the *sole* or even primary cause of the adverse action; it requires only that it play *some* role. *Araujo*, 708 F.3d at 158. A jury could reasonably conclude that BNSF's disciplinary apparatus was set in motion, or at minimum shaped in its severity, by Appellants' safety complaints, particularly where the officials who investigated and disciplined Appellants—Knutstrom and Dixon—were the very individuals responsible for the crew-change instructions that Appellants' statements implicitly criticized.

### C. Genuine Factual Disputes Preclude Summary Judgment on BNSF's Same-Action Defense

Even if BNSF could point to some evidence of legitimate rule infractions, the "clear and convincing" affirmative defense is not susceptible to resolution on summary judgment where, as here, the underlying facts are sharply contested. The parties dispute the very location BNSF instructed crews to use for the change—Appellants testified they were told to stop *west* of the bridge, while Knutstrom and Dixon testified crews were orally instructed to change *east* of it. This is a classic credibility dispute inappropriate for resolution by the court rather than a jury. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) ("Credibility determinations, the weighing of the evidence, and the drawing of legitimate inferences from the facts are jury functions, not those of a judge."); *Torgerson v. City of Rochester*, 643 F.3d 1031, 1042 (8th Cir. 2011) (en banc) (summary judgment is improper where reasonable jurors could reach different conclusions on the evidence).

Because the location instruction bears directly on whether Appellants actually committed the charged infractions, and because BNSF's clear-and-convincing burden is heightened, not diminished, at summary judgment, the district court should have permitted a jury to weigh this conflicting testimony rather than accepting BNSF's version as a matter of law. *See Kuduk*, 768 F.3d at 791 (carrier's affirmative defense remains a jury question where material facts are contested).

### D. The Post-Hearing Surveillance Constitutes Cognizable Adverse Action

Finally, the district court erred in dismissing the increased operations testing as non-actionable. This Court has recognized that heightened scrutiny and surveillance, even absent formal discipline, may constitute adverse action where it is "sufficient to dissuade a reasonable worker" from engaging in protected conduct. *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). Kline and Snyder's testimony that they altered their work assignments—accepting less lucrative shifts—to avoid the risk of being blamed for others' errors under BNSF's covert observation regime satisfies this standard and creates a triable issue of fact.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
