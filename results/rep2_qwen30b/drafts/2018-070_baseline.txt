**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—requires this Court to interpret the precise language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), which governs the choice of law on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which holds that state wage and hour laws may apply on the OCS so long as they are “applicable and not inconsistent” with federal law, misreads the statute and undermines the comprehensive federal regulatory scheme Congress created. The correct interpretation is that state law applies only when federal law is silent or inadequate—i.e., when a “gap” exists. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule would allow state laws to supplant federal law even where Congress has spoken definitively, thereby undermining the uniformity and supremacy of federal authority on the OCS.

### I. THE OCSLA’S CHOICE-OF-LAW PROVISION REQUIRES A GAP IN FEDERAL LAW BEFORE STATE LAW MAY APPLY

The text of § 1333(a)(2)(A) is clear: state law applies “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” (Emphasis added.) This language does not create a general rule of concurrent application. Rather, it conditions the application of state law on its *compatibility* with federal law, not its mere existence. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “the mere fact that a state law is not inconsistent with federal law does not mean that it is automatically applicable.” *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 102 (2000). Instead, the Court has emphasized that “Congress may preclude the application of state law even where it is not inconsistent with federal law.” *Id.* at 103.

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation—under which California’s wage and hour laws apply on the OCS so long as they are not “inconsistent” with the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA)—ignores this foundational principle. It treats the “not inconsistent” clause as a threshold for application, rather than a limitation on what may be applied. But the statute does not say “state law applies if it is not inconsistent.” It says state law applies “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent.” The phrase “to the extent that” is critical. It signals that state law is not automatically adopted; rather, it is *assimilated only to the extent that federal law does not already govern the subject matter*.

This reading is consistent with the legislative history and the original purpose of the OCSLA. Congress enacted the OCSLA in 1953 to resolve a jurisdictional vacuum in the offshore environment. As the Court explained in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355 (1969), Congress feared that federal law alone would be “inadequate” because it “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself.” But Congress did not intend for state law to supplant federal law where Congress had already spoken. The 1975 amendment to § 1333(a)(2)(A), which expanded the scope of “state law” to include laws “hereafter adopted,” was not meant to override federal statutory schemes. Rather, it was designed to ensure consistency between the OCS and deepwater ports, not to create a patchwork of state labor laws on federal enclaves.

### II. THE FLSA IS A COMPREHENSIVE AND EXCLUSIVE FEDERAL SCHEME

The FLSA is not a mere floor; it is a comprehensive statutory framework governing wages, hours, and overtime. Congress has repeatedly affirmed that the FLSA is “the primary vehicle for protecting the rights of workers,” *Barnes v. Gorman*, 536 U.S. 181, 187 (2002), and that it “provides a uniform national standard,” *Barnes*, 536 U.S. at 188. The FLSA’s savings clause—29 U.S.C. § 218—explicitly permits states to enact more protective wage and hour laws, but only *in addition to* the FLSA. It does not permit state law to displace or supplant federal law. Indeed, the savings clause itself acknowledges that the FLSA is the baseline: “Nothing in this chapter shall be construed to exempt any employee from the provisions of any State law which affords greater protection to employees than the provisions of this chapter.” (Emphasis added.) This language confirms that the FLSA is the default, and state law is supplemental—only where it goes *beyond* federal protections.

The Ninth Circuit’s rule, by contrast, would allow state law to govern even where federal law is fully operative. That would render the FLSA’s comprehensive structure meaningless. If California’s meal period law applies on the OCS *even when the FLSA already governs the issue*, then the FLSA’s own provisions—such as the definition of “workday” and “hours worked”—become irrelevant. This is not the law Congress intended. As the Court held in *Barnes*, “Congress did not intend to allow state law to override federal law in areas where Congress has already legislated.” 536 U.S. at 188.

### III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S INTERPRETATION UNDERMINES CONGRESSIONAL INTENT AND CREATES UNIFORMITY

The Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a dangerous precedent: it allows state law to govern federal enclaves even where Congress has spoken with one voice. This undermines the very purpose of the OCSLA—to create a uniform federal legal regime on the OCS. As the Court emphasized in *Rodrigue*, the OCS is not a “patchwork of state laws,” but a “federal enclave.” 395 U.S. at 366. Allowing state wage and hour laws to apply on the OCS without a gap would create a “federal enclave” that is subject to the varying and often conflicting laws of adjacent states—precisely what Congress sought to avoid.
