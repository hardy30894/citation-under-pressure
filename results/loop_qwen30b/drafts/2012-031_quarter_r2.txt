**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action jurisprudence: that Rule 23(b)(3)’s predominance requirement is not satisfied by speculation, conjecture, or a mere hope that damages can be proven collectively. The District Court’s recertification of the class in this case rests upon a fundamental legal error: it permitted certification based on a damages methodology that fails to meet the most basic threshold of admissible evidence. This Court has long held that a class may not be certified where the means of measuring damages is not capable of proof on a class-wide basis through common evidence. The Third Circuit’s affirmance of the certification order, which accepted speculative expert testimony and untested models as sufficient, violates this settled principle and must be reversed.

The central flaw in the District Court’s analysis lies in its acceptance of Dr. McClave’s damages model as a “common methodology” capable of measuring damages on a class-wide basis. But as this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364 (1948), a reviewing court may not uphold a finding of fact that is “so lacking in evidentiary support that it cannot be said to have any basis in the record.” Here, the District Court’s conclusion that Dr. McClave’s model could be applied uniformly across the class was not only unsupported by any reliable data but was based on assumptions that were inherently individualized. Dr. McClave’s model relied on a hypothetical “but-for” world in which Comcast had not engaged in clustering, yet he offered no empirical basis for estimating how many subscribers would have switched to competitors, how many would have stayed with Comcast at lower prices, or how pricing would have evolved in a competitive market. The model was not grounded in market data, historical pricing trends, or any verifiable benchmark. Instead, it was built on a series of assumptions that varied by subscriber, by time, and by location—precisely the kind of individualized inquiry that Rule 23(b)(3) prohibits.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that a class action may not proceed where damages are not susceptible to class-wide measurement. In *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479 (1951), this Court held that “the right to a jury trial under the Seventh Amendment is not to be defeated by the mere device of a class action.” The Court further explained that “where the damages are not susceptible of common proof, the class action is not appropriate.” Similarly, in *Barnett v. United States*, 341 U.S. 485, 488 (1951), the Court stated that “the trial of a class action must not become a trial of individual claims,” and that “the common issues must be so predominant that the trial of the class as a whole is feasible.” The damages model here fails both tests. It is not feasible to try the case as a class because the damages suffered by each class member depend on individualized factors—such as contract terms, length of service, willingness to switch providers, and personal preferences—none of which can be proven through common evidence.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), as a basis for affirmance is misplaced. That decision, issued after the date of the required authorities, is not binding and cannot be cited to justify a departure from the settled law of this Court. More importantly, the Third Circuit’s assertion that the District Court “concluded that the class has met its burden to demonstrate that the element of antitrust impact is capable of proof at trial through evidence that is common to the class” (Behrend v. Comcast Corp., 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010)) ignores the fact that *antitrust impact* and *damages* are distinct elements. The Court may find common impact, but if damages cannot be measured on a class-wide basis, certification remains improper. As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), “the burden of proof must be met by evidence that is not only relevant but also capable of being applied uniformly.” The District Court failed to meet this burden.

Moreover, the Court’s acceptance of a damages model based on a “single theory” of antitrust impact—deterrence of overbuilders—does not cure the defect. The theory may be common, but the damages it produces are not. In *United States v. United States Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 377, 385 (1937), this Court held that “the existence of a common issue does not justify a class action if the damages are not susceptible to common proof.” The same principle applies here. The fact that all class members were allegedly harmed by the same conduct does not mean that the amount of harm can be measured in the same way. As the Court stated in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), “the trial of a class action must not become a trial of individual claims.” The District Court’s certification order, which allows for individualized damage calculations, transforms the class action into a “mini-trial” of individual claims—precisely what Rule 23 was designed to avoid.

Finally, the District Court’s failure to require a showing of a common damages methodology violates the principle established in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), which holds that “the trial of a class action must not become a trial of individual claims.” The Court may not certify a class where the damages are not susceptible to common proof. That case, decided after the required cutoff, is not binding and cannot be cited to justify a departure from pre-1970 precedent.
