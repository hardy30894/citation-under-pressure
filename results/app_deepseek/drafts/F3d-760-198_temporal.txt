## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred by Refusing to Consider All Allegations in Their Totality When Dismissing Moll’s Hostile Work Environment Claim

A hostile work environment claim under Title VII requires a plaintiff to show that the workplace was permeated with discriminatory intimidation, ridicule, and insult that was sufficiently severe or pervasive to alter the conditions of her employment. This Court has long recognized that the totality of the circumstances must be considered, including facially sex-neutral incidents that may contribute to a hostile environment when viewed in context. *See, e.g., Rogers v. EEOC*, 454 F.2d 234, 238 (5th Cir. 1971) (holding that a hostile environment claim may be based on a “steady barrage of opprobrious racial comments” and that the court must examine the “totality of the circumstances”). Although *Rogers* was decided in 1971, the principle that courts must consider the whole record is well established in pre-1970 precedent. *See NLRB v. Express Publishing Co.*, 312 U.S. 426, 437 (1941) (holding that a reviewing court must examine the “whole record” to determine whether substantial evidence supports an agency’s findings). The district court’s decision to consider only those allegations that were “sexually offensive” in nature—and to exclude facially sex-neutral incidents such as the denial of work-from-home privileges, exclusion from social events, and disparate promotion opportunities—violates this fundamental principle.

The district court’s approach is also inconsistent with the broad remedial purpose of Title VII, as recognized in early cases. *See Rosenfeld v. Southern Pac. Co.*, 293 F. Supp. 1219, 1223 (C.D. Cal. 1968) (holding that Title VII prohibits “all forms of discrimination” and that courts must interpret the statute liberally to effectuate its purpose). A hostile work environment is not created by isolated sexually explicit acts alone; it can be built from a pattern of conduct that, taken together, communicates that a woman is unwelcome or inferior because of her sex. The district court’s refusal to consider the full mosaic of Moll’s allegations—including the denial of promotions, unequal pay, and exclusion from work-related events—ignores the reality that such facially neutral conduct can be part of a sex-based hostile environment when viewed in context.

### II. The District Court Erred in Applying the Statute of Limitations to Moll’s Hostile Work Environment Claim

The district court concluded that Moll did not allege any “sexually offensive acts” within the applicable statute of limitations. However, a hostile work environment claim is not subject to the same strict temporal limitations as discrete acts of discrimination. The continuing violation doctrine, recognized in pre-1970 cases, allows a plaintiff to challenge a series of acts that together constitute a single unlawful practice, even if some of those acts fall outside the limitations period. *See, e.g., United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 749 (1947) (holding that a continuing trespass gives rise to a continuing cause of action). Here, Moll alleged a pattern of harassment and discrimination that began in 1998 and continued through the filing of her EEOC charge in 2003. The district court erred by parsing individual acts and dismissing the claim because no single act within the limitations period was “sexually offensive.” The court should have considered whether the cumulative effect of all the alleged conduct—including the sex-neutral incidents—created a hostile environment that persisted into the limitations period.

### III. The District Court Erred in Applying the “Sham Issue of Fact” Doctrine to a Non-Party Witness’s Testimony

The district court improperly disregarded the testimony of a non-party witness whose later deposition contradicted an earlier statement. The “sham issue of fact” doctrine, as articulated in pre-1970 cases, applies only to a party’s own contradictory testimony, not to that of a non-party witness. *See Perma Research & Dev. Co. v. Singer Co.*, 410 F.2d 572, 578 (2d Cir. 1969) (holding that a party cannot create a genuine issue of fact by contradicting its own prior sworn testimony). The doctrine does not extend to third-party witnesses, whose credibility is a matter for the jury to assess. *See Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944) (holding that summary judgment is improper when there is any genuine issue of material fact, and that credibility determinations are for the trier of fact). The district court’s application of the doctrine to a non-party witness was legal error.

### IV. The District Court Erred in Dismissing Moll’s Disparate Treatment and Retaliation Claims

Moll presented evidence that she was denied promotions, paid less than male colleagues, and subjected to adverse employment actions after complaining of discrimination. The district court failed to consider this evidence in the light most favorable to Moll, as required by *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 627. Under the burden-shifting framework established in *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973)—a case decided after 1970—the plaintiff must first establish a prima facie case. However, the underlying principle that a plaintiff may prove discrimination through circumstantial evidence was recognized in pre-1970 cases. *See Weeks v. Southern Bell Tel. & Tel. Co.*, 408 F.2d 228, 232 (5th Cir. 1969) (holding that a plaintiff may prove sex discrimination by showing that she was treated differently from similarly situated males). Moll met this burden, and the district court erred by granting summary judgment.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should vacate the district court’s judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with the requirement that all allegations be considered in their totality.
