**ARGUMENT**

The Center Moriches Union Free School District’s categorical exclusion of religious groups from its after-hours facilities violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a religiously themed film series—on the grounds that the content is “church related”—constitutes impermissible content-based discrimination in a forum that, by policy and practice, has been opened to nonreligious expressive activity. This case falls squarely within the principle established in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981), and *Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226, 247 (1990): when a public school creates a limited public forum for student and community groups, it may not exclude speech based on its religious content. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel access, while permitting secular groups to use its facilities, is not viewpoint-neutral and thus violates the First Amendment.

The District’s facilities are not nonpublic forums, as the Second Circuit concluded. Rather, they are limited public forums, created by the District’s own policy and consistent practice of allowing nonreligious community groups to use school property after hours. The District has permitted a wide range of secular programs—including a jazz ensemble, a lecture series on parapsychology, and a community theater group—on school premises during noninstructional time. These uses were not isolated or incidental; they were part of a sustained practice of opening the facilities to the public for expressive purposes. As this Court has held, “a forum is a limited public forum when the government has opened the forum for use by certain speakers or for certain purposes.” *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983). The District’s policy of allowing nonreligious groups to use its auditorium and gymnasium for cultural, educational, and recreational events demonstrates that it has created such a forum.

Once a limited public forum is created, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a state university could not deny access to student religious groups when it had opened its campus to other student organizations. The Court emphasized that “the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and that “the First Amendment prohibits the government from excluding speech from such a forum solely because of its religious content.” Id. at 267, 273. The same principle applies here. The District has opened its facilities to secular groups for expressive purposes, and it may not exclude religious speech simply because it is religious.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Deeper Life* cases is misplaced. Those decisions involved the use of school facilities by religious groups in the absence of any prior practice of opening the forum to nonreligious groups. See *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 977 F.2d 72, 75 (2d Cir. 1992) (en banc). But here, the District has repeatedly permitted secular groups to use its facilities. The District’s own policy—reflected in its “Rules and Regulations for Community Use of School Facilities”—does not prohibit all religious use; it merely prohibits “religious purposes.” Yet the District has allowed secular groups to use the facilities for events with religious overtones, such as a lecture series on “parapsychology” and “transpersonal psychology,” which included references to spiritual concepts. The District’s own actions demonstrate that it does not draw a bright line between religious and secular expression. It permits some religiously tinged speech while banning others based on content. That is precisely the kind of content-based discrimination the First Amendment forbids.

The District’s argument that the film series is “church related” because it promotes Christian values is irrelevant. The First Amendment does not permit the government to suppress speech simply because it is religious in character. *Mergens*, 496 U.S. at 247. The Equal Access Act, 20 U.S.C. § 4071(b), which the Court interpreted in *Mergens*, explicitly prohibits schools from discriminating against student groups based on the content of their speech. While the Act applies to student groups, the First Amendment’s protection against content-based discrimination extends to all speakers, including outside religious organizations. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities, while permitting secular groups to do so, is a classic example of viewpoint discrimination. The District is not neutral; it is favoring secular expression over religious expression.

The District’s claim that it has not opened the forum to religious groups is contradicted by the record. The District has allowed secular groups to use its facilities for events that include religious themes, such as the “Psychology and The Unknown” lecture series, which discussed “the unconscious emotional system” and “metaphysics.” The District’s own staff acknowledged that the lectures “had a spiritual component,” yet still permitted them. If the District allows secular events with spiritual content, it cannot then deny access to a religious group seeking to present a film series on family values from a Christian perspective. The District’s policy is not content-neutral; it is content-based.

The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel access violates the First Amendment’s guarantee of free speech. The government may not create a forum for expression and then exclude speech based on its religious content. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273. The Second Circuit’s conclusion that the District’s actions were constitutional is contrary to binding precedent and must be reversed.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Second Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded for entry of judgment in favor of Lamb’s Chapel.
