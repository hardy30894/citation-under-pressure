**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is one of statutory interpretation, and the answer must be grounded in the text, structure, context, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that TPS confers an “admission,” thereby rendering petitioners eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255(a). That conclusion is contrary to the plain meaning of “admission,” inconsistent with the INA’s framework, and undermines Congress’s deliberate design of the immigration system.

### I. THE TEXT OF THE INA CLEARLY DISTINGUISHES “ADMISSION” FROM “LAWFUL STATUS”

Congress defined “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive. It refers to the *physical act* of entering the country lawfully, following inspection at a port of entry. As this Court has held, “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country,” which is distinct from the subsequent *status* an alien may hold. Hanif v. Att’y Gen., 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); see also Taveras v. Att’y Gen., 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013) (“The words ‘entry’ and ‘into’ plainly indicate that ‘admission’ involves physical entrance into the country…”).

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that “[t]he alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” This language does not say the alien was “admitted” or “inspected and admitted.” It speaks only to *status*, not *entry*. To equate “lawful status” with “admission” would render the distinction between those terms meaningless—a result this Court has long rejected. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004) (“A statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous…”).

Petitioners argue that because TPS recipients are “considered” to be in lawful status, they must therefore be deemed “admitted.” But that argument conflates two separate concepts. The fact that an alien is *in* lawful status does not mean he was *admitted* into the country. As the Board of Immigration Appeals has recognized, “a grant of asylum places the individual in valid immigration status but is not an ‘admission.’” In re H-G-G-, 27 I. & N. Dec. 617, 635 (AAO 2019). Similarly, a grant of benefits under the Family Unity Program confers “status” but not “admission.” Id. (citing In re Fajardo Espinoza, 26 I. & N. Dec. 603, 605 (BIA 2015)). If a grant of asylum or family unity relief does not constitute admission, then neither does TPS.

### II. THE STRUCTURE AND CONTEXT OF THE INA REINFORCE THE DISTINCTION

Congress structured the INA to treat “admission” and “status” as independent requirements. Section 1255(a) explicitly requires that an alien “was inspected and admitted or paroled into the United States” before eligibility for adjustment of status can be considered. 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a). This threshold requirement is not mere formality—it reflects Congress’s intent that only those who entered lawfully may adjust their status.

Congress did not exempt TPS recipients from this rule. Instead, it created a separate exception for certain categories of aliens—such as special immigrants under § 1101(a)(27)(J) and those eligible for visas—by expressly including them in § 1255(h) and (i). The absence of such an exception for TPS recipients is telling. As this Court has held, “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.” INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987).

Moreover, § 1254a(h)(2) establishes a supermajority requirement for Congress to pass legislation adjusting the status of TPS recipients. This provision exists precisely because Congress intended TPS to be a temporary, non-adjustable status. If a grant of TPS were equivalent to admission under § 1255(a), then TPS recipients could adjust status without congressional action—rendering § 1254a(h)(2) superfluous. Such a reading would violate the principle that “no part [of a statute] will be inoperative or superfluous.” Hibbs, 542 U.S. at 101.

### III. THE PURPOSE OF TPS CONFIRMS THAT IT DOES NOT CONSTITUTE ADMISSION

TPS is designed to provide temporary protection from removal during extraordinary conditions abroad—such as natural disasters or armed conflict. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). Its purpose is not to confer permanent residence, but to shield individuals already present in the United States from deportation while conditions in their home countries remain unsafe. See Serrano v. Att’y Gen., 655 F.3d 1260, 1265 (11th Cir. 2011) (per curiam) (“That an alien with Temporary Protected Status has ‘lawful status as a nonimmigrant’ for purposes of adjusting his status does not change § 1255(a)’s threshold requirement…”).
