**ARGUMENT**

This case asks whether a defendant may be convicted under 18 U.S.C. §875(c) for words a jury finds threatening in hindsight, even though the defendant himself never intended those words as a threat. Both the statute and the Constitution answer no. Ordinary principles of statutory construction require this Court to read a scienter element into §875(c), and the First Amendment's true-threats doctrine independently demands proof that the speaker intended his words to convey a threat. An objective standard collapses both protections into a test of listener perception, punishing carelessness rather than wrongdoing.

**I. The Presumption Favoring Scienter Requires §875(c) to Be Construed to Demand Proof of Subjective Intent to Threaten.**

Section 875(c) does not specify the mental state required for conviction, but that silence cannot be read to dispense with intent altogether. This Court has explained that the requirement of a guilty mind "is no provincial or transient notion. It is as universal and persistent in mature systems of law as belief in freedom of the human will and a consequent ability and duty of the normal individual to choose between good and evil." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). Where Congress enacts a criminal statute without addressing mens rea, this Court has instructed that the traditional intent requirement should be presumed to survive rather than assumed abolished by omission. *Id.* at 263.

That presumption applies with special force here, because §875(c) is not the kind of regulatory "public welfare" offense in which this Court has tolerated strict liability. *Compare United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251–252 (1922) (upholding strict liability for narcotics sales under a comprehensive regulatory scheme). A prosecution for transmitting a "threat" is a true crime carrying felony punishment and lasting stigma — precisely the context in which this Court has insisted intent be proven, not presumed. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260. Nor should ambiguous criminal statutes be extended by inference to reach conduct Congress did not clearly intend to punish, particularly where doing so would penalize a defendant who had no fair warning that his words crossed a legal line. *Boyce Motor Lines v. United States*, 342 U.S. 337, 340 (1952). Where an objective standard would convict a defendant for failing to foresee how listeners might interpret his words, rather than for what he actually intended, the statute should instead be construed to require proof that the defendant himself meant his communication as a threat.

**II. The First Amendment's True-Threats Doctrine Requires Proof of the Speaker's Intent, Not Merely a Listener's Reaction.**

Apart from ordinary rules of construction, the Constitution independently compels a subjective standard. Only narrowly defined categories of speech fall outside First Amendment protection, and this Court has cautioned that such categories must be defined with precision because they form "no essential part of any exposition of ideas." *Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 315 U.S. 568, 571–572 (1942). Applying the true-threat concept, this Court in *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969) (per curiam), refused to treat as punishable a statement a listener might have found alarming, holding that "we do not believe that the kind of political hyperbole indulged in by [the] petitioner fits within that statutory term." *Id.* at 708. *Watts* thus confirms that the inquiry cannot turn solely on how a reasonable listener might construe ambiguous words; it must account for what the speaker actually meant to convey.

An objective standard risks chilling a wide swath of protected expression — hyperbole, dark humor, artistic composition such as song lyrics, or the venting of anger and grief — because speakers cannot reliably predict how an unknown audience will receive ambiguous language. This Court has recognized that First Amendment freedoms "need breathing space to survive," and that laws touching speech must not be construed to reach protected activity simply because some listener might take alarm. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 433 (1963). Just as this Court required a scienter element to prevent booksellers from facing strict liability for the content of material they had no intent to distribute unlawfully, lest such liability "impose a severe limitation on the public's access to constitutionally protected matter," *Smith v. California*, 361 U.S. 147, 154 (1959), so too must speakers be protected from liability that turns solely on how their words are received rather than on what they intended. Absent a scienter requirement, individuals will "steer far wider of the unlawful zone" than the Constitution demands, curbing protected speech out of undue caution. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 526 (1958). And where advocacy or expression is at stake, this Court has required proof that speech was intended to produce a proscribed result before liability may attach. *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502 (1951).

**III. An Objective Standard Punishes Carelessness, Not Wrongdoing, and Cannot Be Reconciled With the Constitution's Demand for Fair Notice.**

Dispensing with subjective intent transforms §875(c) into a negligence offense, punishing defendants not for what they meant but for failing to anticipate how their words might be perceived by others. This Court has cautioned against construing ambiguous criminal statutes to impose liability for such unwitting conduct, particularly where a defendant's fair notice of the legal consequences of his own words is at stake. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 101–102 (1945) (plurality opinion); *Boyce Motor Lines*, 342 U.S. at 340. A rule that criminalizes speech based on a jury's after-the-fact assessment of how a hypothetical reasonable listener would have reacted gives speakers no reliable guide for conforming their conduct to the law, and instead invites conviction for what amounts to an unfortunate choice of words rather than a criminal state of mind.

Both the statute and the Constitution require more than a showing that some reasonable person might have felt threatened. The judgment below should be reversed, and this Court should hold that a conviction under §875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication to be understood as a threat.
