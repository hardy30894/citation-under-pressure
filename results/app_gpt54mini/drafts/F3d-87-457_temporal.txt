**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE DISCLOSURE REQUIREMENTS BURDEN POLITICAL EXPRESSION AND CANNOT SURVIVE FACIAL FIRST AMENDMENT REVIEW**

The Act compels lobbyists and their principals to disclose all lobbying expenditures and the source of funds for those expenditures, including itemized categories such as food, entertainment, research, media advertising, travel, and lodging. That compelled reporting is not a mere administrative formality; it reaches the financing and organization of efforts to influence public officials on matters of public concern. The First Amendment protects not only speaking, but also the “right to petition the Government for a redress of grievances,” U.S. Const. amend. I, and that protection extends to concerted efforts to secure governmental action. *Thomas v. Collins*, 323 U.S. 516, 531-32 (1945); *United Mine Workers of America, Dist. 12 v. Illinois State Bar Ass’n*, 389 U.S. 217, 222-26 (1967).

Compelled disclosure that targets association and advocacy is constitutionally suspect because it deters participation by exposing supporters and advocates to public scrutiny and retaliation. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 462-63 (1958). The Court has repeatedly recognized that compelled disclosure may operate as a restraint on First Amendment freedoms where it chills association or expression. *Bates v. City of Little Rock*, 361 U.S. 516, 523-24 (1960); *Gibson v. Florida Legislative Investigation Comm.*, 372 U.S. 539, 546-57 (1963). The challenged statute requires broad financial disclosures without any showing that the State has tailored the requirement to a specific, demonstrated need sufficient to overcome the chilling effect on protected advocacy. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. at 463-64; *Bates*, 361 U.S. at 524-25.

Nor can the statute be upheld merely because lobbying touches government processes. The Court has insisted that regulations burdening First Amendment activity must be closely examined where they reach political advocacy and association. *Thomas*, 323 U.S. at 532-33; *United Mine Workers*, 389 U.S. at 222-26. Here, the disclosure regime sweeps far beyond the State’s legitimate interest in identifying who is communicating with officials. It compels disclosure of all expenditures by both lobbyists and principals, regardless of whether any item is directly tied to a specific communication. Such overbreadth chills protected activity on its face. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 432-33 (1963); *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 486-87 (1965).

The State may argue that it has an interest in informing the public and deterring corruption. But those interests do not permit a statute that reaches protected advocacy more broadly than necessary. In the First Amendment context, “precision of regulation” is essential where speech and association are implicated. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 438. Because the Act’s disclosure provisions operate on their face to burden lawful advocacy across the board, they are unconstitutional.

II. **THE CONTINGENT-FEE BAN IS AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL PRIOR RESTRAINT ON PETITIONING AND POLITICAL ADVOCACY**

Section 11.047 flatly prohibits lobbyists from receiving compensation contingent on legislative or executive success. That prohibition bars a common and often necessary means of retaining effective advocates, and it does so before any misconduct occurs. A rule forbidding compensation arrangements for advocacy is a direct burden on the right to petition and to associate for political purposes. *Thomas*, 323 U.S. at 531-32; *United Mine Workers*, 389 U.S. at 222-26.

The Supreme Court has long treated contingent-fee arrangements in advocacy-related matters with caution, but it has not held that the State may impose categorical bans on compensation structures merely because the subject matter involves public affairs. To the contrary, the Court has made clear that restrictions on advocacy must be justified by a substantial governmental interest and narrowly confined. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 438-39; *Brotherhood of R.R. Trainmen v. Virginia ex rel. Virginia State Bar*, 377 U.S. 1, 7-8 (1964). The Act’s ban is absolute. It forbids parties from structuring compensation in a way that aligns counsel or lobbyist incentives with the client’s desired political result, even where no fraud, coercion, or corruption is shown.

The State’s asserted anti-corruption rationale does not support this sweeping prohibition on its face. The Court has recognized that the Constitution does not permit the government to suppress protected advocacy out of generalized fears about influence or effectiveness. *Thomas*, 323 U.S. at 530-31; *Button*, 371 U.S. at 438. If a particular contingent arrangement were shown to produce bribery or other unlawful conduct, the State could proceed against that abuse directly. But a categorical ban on contingent compensation for lobbying reaches far beyond any proven evil and suppresses lawful petitioning at its core.

III. **BECAUSE THE ACT REACHES A SUBSTANTIAL AMOUNT OF PROTECTED ACTIVITY, IT IS FACIALLY INVALID**

A facial challenge is warranted where a statute’s overbreadth substantially chills protected expression and association. *Thornhill v. Alabama*, 310 U.S. 88, 97-98 (1940); *Button*, 371 U.S. at 432-33. This Court has recognized that litigants need not expose themselves to penalties before seeking relief from a law that threatens constitutional rights. *Dombrowski*, 380 U.S. at 486-87. The League’s members are therefore entitled to facial relief because the Act forces them to choose between foregoing protected advocacy and risking sanctions. *Bates*, 361 U.S. at 523-24; *Abbott v. United States*, 338 U.S. 408, 412 (1949).

The Legislature could have adopted narrower disclosure rules and targeted anti-corruption measures. It did not. Instead, it enacted a regime that broadly compels disclosure of protected political activity and categorically bans a lawful means of compensating those who engage in petitioning. Under controlling First Amendment principles, that statute cannot stand. *NAACP v. Alabama*, 357 U.S. at 462-64; *Button*, 371 U.S. at 438-39.
