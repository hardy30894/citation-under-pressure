Respondent should prevail because the navigability-for-title inquiry is fixed at the moment of statehood. Under the equal-footing doctrine, a state acquires title to the beds of waters that were navigable in fact when it entered the Union. See Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229 (1845); Shively v. Bowlby, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). The governing constitutional test asks whether the river was “used, or [was] susceptible of being used” as a highway of commerce at the relevant historical moment. The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870); The Montello, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430, 441-42 (1874). Because title turns on that historical condition, a trial court must determine navigability as of statehood, not at some later or generalized period.

This Court has repeatedly made clear that navigability for title is not a free-floating inquiry into a waterway’s present utility. In United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931), the Court held that title depends on whether the river was navigable “at the time of the admission of the State into the Union.” The Court explained that segments must be evaluated as they existed then, because “the question of navigability is to be determined as of the time of statehood.” Id. at 75-76. Similarly, Oklahoma v. Texas, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922), treated navigability as a historical fact relevant to sovereignty and title, not merely a contemporary condition. These cases leave no room for a trial court to ignore the statehood date.

The reason is constitutional. The equal-footing doctrine gives each new state the same sovereign rights enjoyed by the original states, including title to submerged lands beneath navigable waters. Pollard’s Lessee, 44 U.S. at 229-30; Shively, 152 U.S. at 57. That title vests upon admission, if at all. Once title is fixed, later changes in the river’s course, condition, or use cannot alter the sovereign ownership originally conferred. St. Anthony Falls Water-Power Co. v. St. Paul Water Com’rs, 168 U.S. 349, 371-72 (1897). A contrary rule would make title depend on post-statehood developments, defeating the equal-footing principle.

PPL’s suggestion that a court may assess navigability without anchoring the inquiry to statehood also conflicts with the settled distinction between navigability for title and other navigability doctrines. The Court has long recognized that the test for federal regulatory power is not the same as the test for title. United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co., 311 U.S. 377, 406-08 (1940), addressed navigability in relation to federal authority and stated that “once found to be navigable, a waterway remains so.” But Appalachian did not alter the title rule; to the contrary, it distinguished the “question of navigability for title purposes” from the broader commerce-power inquiry. Id. at 407-08. The same is true of Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States, 256 U.S. 113, 122-23 (1921), which reiterated that navigability for title depends on whether the stream was navigable at statehood.

Respondent’s position is also supported by the Court’s treatment of evidence. Because the relevant facts are historical, courts may consider historical records, surveys, and testimony regarding conditions at statehood. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. at 83-84; The Montello, 87 U.S. at 441-42. But the point of admitting such evidence is to reconstruct the river’s condition at the statehood date, not to substitute a later snapshot. The inquiry thus remains anchored in 1889 for Montana.

The Court should therefore hold that the constitutional test for navigability for title requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood. That rule follows directly from Pollard’s Lessee, Shively, The Daniel Ball, The Montello, and United States v. Utah, and it preserves the constitutional allocation of title between the United States and the States at the moment of admission.
