**ARGUMENT**

I. **SANTANDER IS NOT A “DEBT COLLECTOR” UNDER THE FDCPA BECAUSE IT COLLECTS DEBTS OWED TO ITSELF, NOT “ANOTHER.”**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA) regulates “debt collectors,” defined in relevant part as persons who “regularly collect[ ] or attempt[ ] to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due ... another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander does not qualify as a debt collector because it collects debts that it owns. *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 814 F.3d 131, 135 (4th Cir. 2016). This interpretation is consistent with the statutory text and controlling Supreme Court and circuit precedent.

The FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector” distinguishes between those who collect debts *owed to another* and those who collect debts *owed to themselves*. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 294 (1995) (emphasizing that the Act regulates “third persons who regularly collect debts *for others*”). When Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, it became the owner of those debts and thus collected them for itself, not “another.” *Id.* (holding that a lawyer who both collects debts for clients and collects a debt owed to himself is not a “debt collector” when acting as to the latter). The Fourth Circuit properly applied this principle to Santander’s conduct. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 135.

The petitioners’ contrary argument—that Santander collects debts “owed or due another” because the debts were originally owed to CitiFinancial Auto—is foreclosed by the statutory text and this Court’s precedent. The phrase “owed or due another” refers to the *status of the debt at the time of collection*, not its origin. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1318 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[O]ur inquiry under § 1692a(6) is ... whether [the defendant] regularly collects on debts owed or due another *at the time of collection*”). The debts at issue were not “owed or due another” when Santander sought to collect them; they were owed to Santander itself. *Id.*; *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (same).

II. **THE FDCPA’S CREDITOR EXCLUSION CONFIRMS THAT SANTANDER IS NOT A DEBT COLLECTOR.**

The FDCPA expressly excludes from the definition of “debt collector” a “creditor” collecting debts “in his own name and ... for his own account.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Santander undisputedly meets this definition. By purchasing the defaulted loans, Santander became the creditor to whom the debts were owed and collected them for its own account. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 135. The Fourth Circuit correctly recognized that this exclusion applies to Santander’s conduct. *Id.*

The petitioners’ attempt to circumvent this exclusion by arguing that the debts were in default when purchased is unavailing. The exclusion applies to any creditor collecting debts for itself, regardless of when the debt went into default. *Id.* The statute’s structure makes clear that the default status of a debt is irrelevant to whether an entity is a creditor or debt collector; what matters is whether the entity collects debts for itself or for another. *Id.* at 134–35.

III. **THE FDCPA’S “REGULARLY COLLECTS” LANGUAGE DOES NOT ALTER THIS CONCLUSION.**

The petitioners argue that Santander “regularly collects” debts and thus falls within the FDCPA’s definition of “debt collector.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This argument conflates the *regularity* of collection activity with the *nature* of that activity. The FDCPA regulates those who regularly collect debts *for others*, not those who regularly collect debts *for themselves*. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 294; *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 135. Santander’s collection activity—undertaken as the owner of the debts—does not trigger FDCPA liability.

The petitioners’ reliance on *Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355 (6th Cir. 2012), and similar cases is misplaced. Those cases involved entities that collected debts for others, not for themselves. *Id.* at 359. Santander, by contrast, collects debts it owns. The Fourth Circuit’s decision in *Henson* directly rejects the petitioners’ interpretation. 814 F.3d at 135.

IV. **THE FDCPA’S PURPOSE AND STRUCTURE SUPPORT THIS INTERPRETATION.**

The FDCPA was enacted to curb abusive practices by *third-party debt collectors*, not creditors collecting their own debts. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), *reprinted in* 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. The statute’s definitions and exclusions reflect this intent. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 294. Applying the FDCPA to creditors like Santander would contravene Congress’s purpose by subjecting ordinary lending and collection activities to FDCPA liability. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 135.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed. Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA because it collects debts owed to itself, not “another,” and falls within the statute’s creditor exclusion. The Fourth Circuit’s decision correctly interprets the FDCPA and should be sustained.
