# ARGUMENT

## THE CONFERRAL OF TEMPORARY PROTECTED STATUS CONSTITUTES AN "ADMISSION" UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255

### A. The Plain Text of § 1254a(f)(4) Satisfies § 1255(a)'s Admission Requirement

Section 1254a(f)(4) directs that upon receiving TPS, "the alien shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4). This Court has long recognized that "nonimmigrant" status under the INA is definitionally tied to admission. The statutory categories of nonimmigrants enumerated in § 1101(a)(15) each describe aliens who have been admitted for a particular purpose—students, temporary workers, visitors—and Congress's choice to deem TPS recipients as occupying "lawful status as a nonimmigrant" necessarily imports the admission that accompanies every other nonimmigrant classification.

This Court has instructed that statutes must be read according to their "ordinary, contemporary, common meaning." *Perrin v. United States*, 444 U.S. 37, 42 (1979). The ordinary meaning of being placed in "nonimmigrant status" is that one has been admitted in that status; Congress does not create fictional categories of persons who possess a status without the entry event that defines it. Just as this Court held in *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987), that Congress's use of different statutory language in adjacent provisions is presumed intentional, so too should Congress's use of *identical* language—"lawful status as a nonimmigrant"—be presumed to carry identical meaning across the INA. When Congress uses a term of art like "nonimmigrant status" that elsewhere in the Act invariably follows admission, it does not silently strip that term of its ordinary incident.

### B. The Statutory Structure Confirms That TPS Functions as a Statutorily Created Admission

Congress enacted the TPS program against the backdrop of an admission-based immigration system and expressly chose to graft TPS recipients onto that system by deeming them nonimmigrants. This Court has recognized that when Congress legislates against a well-established statutory backdrop, it is presumed to incorporate the ordinary consequences of the terms it employs. *See Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 21 (1999) (courts presume Congress intends to incorporate common-law and statutory backgrounds absent contrary indication).

The Government's position—and the Third Circuit's holding below—rests on the proposition that "status" and "admission" are wholly severable concepts such that one may possess nonimmigrant status without ever having been admitted. But no other provision of the INA contemplates a person holding nonimmigrant status in the ether, disconnected from any entry event. The categories of nonimmigrants in § 1101(a)(15) are exhaustive descriptions of admitted persons. By deeming TPS recipients to hold "lawful status as a nonimmigrant," § 1254a(f)(4) necessarily deems them to have undergone the admission that produces that status. This is not, as the court below suggested, an argument that "status" and "admission" are identical concepts in the abstract; rather, it is the narrower and textually compelled proposition that this *particular* status—nonimmigrant status—cannot exist without an admission, and Congress's fiction in § 1254a(f)(4) supplies that admission by operation of law.

### C. The Purpose of the TPS Program Supports Petitioners' Reading

Congress created TPS to provide humanitarian relief to nationals of countries suffering armed conflict, environmental disaster, or other extraordinary conditions. 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1). This Court has repeatedly instructed that remedial humanitarian statutes should be construed to effectuate their protective purpose. *See, e.g., Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 689 (2001) (immigration statutes should be construed with an eye toward avoiding results manifestly at odds with statutory purpose). Congress could not have intended to grant TPS recipients temporary refuge while simultaneously foreclosing them—based solely on the manner of their original entry, an event that predates and is irrelevant to the humanitarian conditions triggering TPS eligibility—from ever regularizing their status through the ordinary adjustment mechanism available to every other nonimmigrant.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017), correctly recognized that "[b]y the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted." That court's reasoning—that treating TPS recipients differently based on mode of entry would produce anomalous results for TPS holders who later marry United States citizens—remains sound. The Sixth Circuit likewise reached this conclusion in *Flores v. USCIS*, 718 F.3d 548, 553-54 (6th Cir. 2013), holding that a grant of TPS satisfies § 1255(a)'s inspection-and-admission requirement.

### D. The Third Circuit's Contrary Reasoning Does Not Withstand Scrutiny

The court below relied on the INA's general definition of "admission" as "lawful entry . . . after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer," 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A), reasoning that this definition requires a physical border-crossing event. But this Court has recognized that Congress may define terms to apply fictionally in specified statutory contexts without disturbing their operation elsewhere. *Cf. Astoria Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass'n v. Solimino*, 501 U.S. 104, 108 (1991) (statutory terms take meaning from context). Section 1254a(f)(4) is precisely such a specialized directive: it instructs that, "for purposes of adjustment of status under section 1255," TPS recipients are to be treated as nonimmigrants. The general definition in § 1101(a)(13)(A) does not override this specific statutory command tailored to adjustment proceedings.

The court below also invoked the canon against surplusage, suggesting that treating TPS as admission would render § 1255(c)(2)'s "lawful status" bar superfluous. But this concern misapprehends the statute's operation: § 1255(c)(2) continues to perform independent work for the many nonimmigrants who are admitted through ordinary channels but subsequently fall out of lawful status—a concern entirely distinct from the admission requirement in § 1255(a). No surplusage results from recognizing that TPS satisfies the latter while the former retains full force for other categories of nonimmigrants.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and hold that a grant of Temporary Protected Status constitutes an admission under 8 U.S.C. § 1255.
