Petitioner was denied access to school premises because its proposed film series was “church related.” That is content discrimination, and under the First Amendment it cannot stand where the District had opened its facilities for community expressive use. See Marsh v. Alabama, 326 U.S. 501, 506-09 (1946); Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939); Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 160-62 (1939).

This Court has long held that when the government makes property generally available for expressive activity, it may not exclude speech because officials dislike its subject matter or viewpoint. In Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948), the Court invalidated a licensing regime that left speech subject to the uncontrolled discretion of officials. In Kovacs v. Cooper, 336 U.S. 77, 87-89 (1949), the Court reaffirmed that regulation must be justified by neutral, legitimate concerns, not hostility to expression. And in Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 271-72 (1951), the Court held that denial of a permit could not rest on “dislike” of the applicants’ views. The District’s refusal here, based on the religious character of the films, is the same kind of constitutionally forbidden suppression.

The District’s premises functioned as a forum for community speech. Where the State has by policy or practice opened property for public expression, it cannot arbitrarily exclude a speaker whose use falls within the forum’s general purposes. See Hague, 307 U.S. at 515-16; Schneider, 308 U.S. at 160-62; Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 271-72. The District invited outside groups to use its facilities after hours for presentations and meetings. Having created that opportunity, it could not carve out religion for exclusion. As the Court explained in Fowler v. Rhode Island, 345 U.S. 67, 69-70 (1953), the government may not suppress religious expression in a public setting while permitting comparable secular activity. Petitioner’s film series—addressing family values and child rearing—was expressive activity, not worship services. The exclusion therefore turned on content, not on any legitimate time, place, or manner concern.

Nor can the District justify its exclusion by invoking a blanket policy against “religious purposes.” A rule that singles out religious speech for disfavored treatment conflicts with the settled principle that the First Amendment protects expression from discrimination because of its subject matter. In Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713-20 (1931), the Court condemned censorship aimed at suppressing disfavored expression. In Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-07 (1940), the Court held that officials may not use discretionary power to burden religious speech. In West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 638-42 (1943), the Court rejected compelled orthodoxy and reaffirmed that freedom of expression does not yield to official preference. The District’s rule here operates as a categorical exclusion of religious perspective from otherwise available expressive facilities. That is precisely what the First Amendment forbids.

The Court should also reject any suggestion that granting access would establish religion. Equal access to a forum open to community speakers is not establishment; exclusion is the constitutional problem. In Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), the Court held that the Establishment Clause does not require hostility to religion. And in Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), the Court explained that the Constitution permits accommodation, not suppression, of religious exercise. Denying petitioner access because its films were religious reverses that principle and imposes impermissible discrimination under the guise of neutrality.

In short, the District opened its facilities for expressive use by the community, then excluded petitioner solely because the proposed films were religiously oriented. Under Marsh, Hague, Schneider, Niemotko, Fowler, Cantwell, Barnette, and Everson, that selective exclusion violates the First Amendment. The judgment below should be reversed.
