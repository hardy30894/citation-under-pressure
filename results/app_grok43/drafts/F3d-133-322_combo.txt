Mrs. Parker’s convictions rest on an erroneous construction of 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). That statute reaches only a public official who is induced “to do or omit to do any act in violation of his official duty.” Because Mrs. Parker possessed no authority to approve, deny, or reinstate SSI benefits, the acts charged fell outside any official duty and the indictment therefore failed to state an offense.

An “official act” is one that the officer is legally empowered to perform. United States v. Worrall, 2 U.S. 384, 389 (C.C.D. Pa. 1798). The duty must arise from “some law, or from some settled usage or practice.” Burton v. United States, 196 U.S. 283, 302 (1905). Where no statute, regulation, or established practice confers power to act, the officer’s conduct cannot constitute an official act. United States v. Van Wert, 195 F. 974, 976 (N.D. Iowa 1912).

Mrs. Parker’s duties were limited to assisting the ALJ, recording hearings, and typing decisions. She held no power to grant benefits or to sign the ALJ’s name. Acts performed without such authority lie beyond the reach of the bribery statute. United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 229 (1914) (official act must be “within the scope of the official’s authority”). The same limitation appears in United States v. Gibson, 47 F. Supp. 849, 851 (D. Md. 1942), and Kemler v. United States, 133 F.2d 235, 238 (1st Cir. 1942).

Nor can “settled usage” supply the missing authority. Usage cannot enlarge an office beyond the powers conferred by law. United States v. Sutter, 160 F.2d 754, 756 (7th Cir. 1947). The government’s evidence showed only that Mrs. Parker forged letters and signatures; it did not show that any established practice permitted a clerk to exercise the ALJ’s adjudicative power. See United States v. Quinn, 111 F. Supp. 870, 874 (E.D.N.Y. 1953).

The same defect defeats the conspiracy count. Conspiracy to commit bribery requires an agreement to perform an act that would itself violate § 201(b)(2)(C). Crain v. United States, 162 U.S. 625, 645 (1896). Because no such substantive violation could occur, the conspiracy charge likewise fails. Rosen v. United States, 161 U.S. 29, 33 (1896).

The convictions must be reversed.
