# ARGUMENT

Federal courts do not possess exclusive jurisdiction over every case that happens to touch on patent law. The question is not whether a patent issue is present, but whether resolution of that issue as a *necessary, disputed, and substantial* federal question—one that will not disturb the congressionally approved balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities—is required. *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005). Applying that test here, this case does not belong in federal court.

## I. Section 1338(a) Requires More Than the Mere Presence of a Patent Question

Congress vested federal district courts with jurisdiction "of any civil action arising under any Act of Congress relating to patents," 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), and made that jurisdiction exclusive of the states. This Court has long cautioned against reading "arising under" expansively. In *Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800 (1988), the Court held that § 1338(a) jurisdiction extends only to cases in which "a well-pleaded complaint establishes either that federal patent law creates the cause of action or that the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law." *Id.* at 808-09. Minton's malpractice claim is a creature of Texas law—it is not "created" by the Patent Act. The only remaining question is whether the patent issue embedded in that state cause of action is "substantial" enough to displace state jurisdiction entirely.

This Court's decisions caution against readily finding such substantiality. As the Court explained in *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), courts must engage in "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Id.* at 117-18. Similarly, in *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Court held that a suit does not arise under federal law "unless it really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Id.* at 569.

*Grable* refined this inquiry into four discrete requirements: federal jurisdiction exists only where "a state-law claim necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial, which a federal forum may entertain without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." 545 U.S. at 314. Minton's claim fails at least three of these four requirements.

## II. The Patent Issue in This Malpractice Suit Is Not "Substantial" Within the Meaning of Grable

Even accepting that resolution of the on-sale bar and the experimental-use exception is "necessary" to Minton's case-within-a-case, and that it is "actually disputed," the issue is not "substantial" in the sense *Grable* requires. In *Grable* itself, the substantiality of the federal issue arose because the case presented a *pure* question of the construction of a federal statute—the meaning of an IRS notice provision—an issue that would control the outcome of numerous other quiet-title actions and implicated "the Government's strong interest in the procedures followed in tax collection." 545 U.S. at 315. The federal question there was abstract, dispositive across many future cases, and directly concerned the government's own interest in uniform administration of a federal scheme.

By contrast, resolving whether Minton's TEXCEN lease fell within the experimental-use exception is a backward-looking, case-specific factual inquiry that has no continuing effect on the body of patent law. The '643 patent was already declared invalid; nothing in this malpractice action can restore, alter, or affect Minton's patent rights or any other patent. As numerous courts examining nearly identical malpractice claims have recognized, "[w]hen patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action... federal courts do not have jurisdiction of the case pursuant to § 1338." *New Tek Manufacturing, Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 346 (Neb. 2005). Likewise, the California Court of Appeal held that "to the extent that the subject matter of patent law is relevant to the determination of the professional negligence claim, it does not present a question of patent law that is substantial." *E-Pass Technologies, Inc. v. Moses & Singer LLP*, 117 Cal. Rptr. 3d 516, 524 (Cal. Ct. App. 2010).

The Fifth Circuit's own test confirms this conclusion. In *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 334 (5th Cir. 2008), the court held that federal question jurisdiction requires that the federal issue be substantial in the *Grable* sense—not merely necessary to a state claim—and found no exclusive federal jurisdiction over a similar malpractice claim implicating patent and trademark law. *Id.* at 340.

## III. Exercising Federal Jurisdiction Here Would Disturb the Congressionally Approved Balance Between State and Federal Courts

*Grable*'s fourth requirement independently defeats federal jurisdiction. Legal malpractice is a core area of state concern, traditionally and comprehensively regulated by state courts applying state standards of care, causation, and damages. Unlike the "rare" tax dispute in *Grable*, where allowing federal jurisdiction would not upset the state-federal balance "given the rarity of disputes" of that kind, 545 U.S. at 315, malpractice actions arising from patent litigation are commonplace and recurring. Sweeping every such claim into federal court simply because a hypothetical patent-validity question must be resolved as part of the case-within-a-case would work exactly the kind of jurisdictional imbalance *Grable* forbids.

Several courts have recognized this danger directly. The federal district court in *Roof Technical Services, Inc. v. Hill*, 679 F. Supp. 2d 749 (N.D. Tex. 2010), held that "even if the court must decide patent law issues, those decisions will not create or destroy any patent rights such that uniformity in the way patents are issued or enforced will be threatened." *Id.* at 753-54. The same reasoning applies with full force here: no patent will be issued, invalidated, or enforced differently because a Texas jury decides whether Gunn was negligent.

## CONCLUSION

Because the patent issue embedded in Minton's malpractice claim is not substantial within the meaning of *Grable*, and because exercising exclusive federal jurisdiction over routine state malpractice claims would disturb the balance between state and federal judicial responsibilities, this Court should hold that the Texas courts possessed jurisdiction to decide this case.
