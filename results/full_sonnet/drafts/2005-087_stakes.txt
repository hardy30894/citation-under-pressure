**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Affords States Broad Latitude to Define the Insanity Defense and to Structure the Presentation of Mental-State Evidence.**

This Court has long recognized that "it is normally within the power of the State to regulate procedures under which its laws are carried out, including the burden of producing evidence and the burden of persuasion, and its decision in this regard is not subject to proscription under the Due Process Clause unless it offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental." *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197, 201–02 (1977) (internal quotation marks omitted). Applying that standard, the Court in *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), upheld an Oregon rule requiring the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt, explaining that the allocation of proof on insanity is a matter "traditionally" left to the States. *Id.* at 798–99. *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437 (1992), confirmed that a defendant challenging a state procedural rule bears the burden of showing that the rule "offends some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," and that courts should be "reluctant to expand the concept of constitutionally mandated procedure in the area of criminal law." *Id.* at 443, 445–46.

These decisions establish the framework for resolving this case. Arizona has not relieved the State of its burden to prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt; it has simply directed that evidence of mental disease bearing on moral capacity be channeled into the insanity inquiry, where the defendant bears the burden the State constitutionally may impose. That legislative choice falls comfortably within the "wide leeway" States retain "in the definition of criminal law." *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 210.

**II. Arizona's Channeling Rule Reflects a Rational Line-Drawing Judgment That Does Not Offend a Fundamental Principle of Justice.**

*Montana v. Egelhoff*, 518 U.S. 37 (1996), controls the analysis. There, a plurality of this Court upheld a Montana statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication evidence on the question whether the defendant possessed the mental state required for the offense, even though such evidence was concededly relevant to mens rea. The plurality reasoned that a State may, without violating due process, decide that certain categories of mental-impairment evidence should not be permitted to negate the mental element of a crime, because the "resolution of ... difficult ... questions ... is entrusted to the political branches." *Id.* at 43, 56 (plurality opinion). If Montana could exclude intoxication evidence altogether from the mens rea inquiry, Arizona's more modest choice—to permit mental-disease evidence to be considered, but only within the insanity framework where it has always resided—cannot be constitutionally infirm.

That conclusion is reinforced by *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354 (1983), and *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968) (plurality opinion). In *Jones*, the Court emphasized the "substantial discretion" of the political branches "in the disposition of insanity acquittees," 463 U.S. at 365, and in *Powell* the plurality stressed that "the whole problem of the relationship between mental illness and moral responsibility ... is [one] to which no scientific consensus" has emerged, 392 U.S. at 536–37, counseling judicial restraint in constitutionalizing any particular allocation of psychiatric evidence. Arizona's rule—distinguishing observation evidence bearing directly on what the defendant perceived or intended from expert opinion evidence about mental disease and capacity, and reserving the latter for the insanity defense—is precisely the sort of considered line-drawing these decisions leave to the States.

**III. Chambers v. Mississippi Does Not Require a Different Result Because Arizona's Rule Is Neither Arbitrary Nor Disproportionate to Legitimate State Interests.**

Petitioner's reliance on the general right to present a defense, recognized in *Chambers v. Mississippi*, 410 U.S. 284 (1973), is misplaced. This Court there held that the right to present evidence "may, in appropriate cases, bow to accommodate other legitimate interests in the criminal trial process," *id.* at 295, and struck down Mississippi's rule only because it was applied mechanistically to exclude reliable, corroborated evidence with no offsetting state interest. Arizona's rule bears no resemblance to that arbitrary exclusion. The State has a substantial interest in preventing juries from repurposing capacity-based psychiatric evidence—evidence about whether a defendant's mental illness rendered him unable to appreciate the wrongfulness of his conduct—to manufacture reasonable doubt about a discrete mens rea element the evidence was never designed to negate. Confining such evidence to the insanity defense, where its probative weight and the reliability concerns surrounding psychiatric testimony can be properly managed within a coherent burden-of-proof framework, is a legitimate and rational means of achieving that end. Petitioner remained free to, and did, introduce all of the same expert testimony; Arizona merely determined the legal significance that testimony would bear.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, and because Arizona's rule offends no principle "so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental," *Patterson*, 432 U.S. at 202, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
