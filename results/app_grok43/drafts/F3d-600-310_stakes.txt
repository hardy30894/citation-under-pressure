The bankruptcy court lacked subject matter jurisdiction to fix the amount of post-petition interest or collection costs. Those obligations arose after the Chapter 13 estate closed and the discharge entered; they therefore neither “aris[e] under title 11” nor “aris[e] in or relate[] to” a case under title 11 within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. § 1334(b). See Celotex Corp. v. Edwards, 514 U.S. 300, 307 (1995) (bankruptcy jurisdiction exists only where the proceeding could “conceivably have any effect on the estate being administered”); In re A.H. Robins Co., 788 F.2d 994, 1002 (4th Cir. 1986) (same). Once the estate closed, the debtor’s remaining liability on the nondischargeable student loan was a matter between private parties outside the bankruptcy court’s limited authority. See In re Resorts Int’l, Inc., 372 F.3d 154, 164-65 (3d Cir. 2004) (post-confirmation disputes must have “a close nexus to the bankruptcy plan or proceeding”); accord Valley Historic Ltd. P’ship v. Bank of N.Y., 486 F.3d 278, 287 (4th Cir. 2007).

The adversary complaint sought only a declaration that the principal had been discharged. Neither party requested adjudication of post-petition interest or collection costs, and the bankruptcy court’s sua sponte determination of those amounts therefore exceeded the “core” authority conferred by 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2). See Northern Pipeline Constr. Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co., 458 U.S. 50, 71 (1982) (bankruptcy courts may not exercise “all essential attributes” of judicial power over matters outside the estate). Because the loan was concededly nondischargeable under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(8) absent an undue-hardship finding, the court had no occasion to quantify the surviving obligation. See Tenn. Student Assistance Corp. v. Hood, 541 U.S. 440, 450 (2004) (nondischargeable debts “pass through the bankruptcy unaffected”).

The district court’s contrary conclusion—that jurisdiction existed merely because the complaint was styled a “core proceeding”—misapprehends the statutory limits. Jurisdiction cannot be manufactured by labeling; it depends on whether the dispute affects the estate or the administration of the Bankruptcy Code. See Celotex, 514 U.S. at 308. The post-petition interest and collection costs at issue here do not. Accordingly, the judgment should be reversed insofar as it affirms the bankruptcy court’s quantification of those items.
