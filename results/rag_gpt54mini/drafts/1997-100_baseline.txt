**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial only when the State seeks a second prosecution for the “same offense,” U.S. Const. amend. V, and the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that sentencing proceedings in noncapital cases are fundamentally different from trials on guilt or innocence. In *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949), the Court explained that due process does not freeze “the evidential procedure of sentencing in the mold of trial procedure.” That principle has been reaffirmed in modern cases. *Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995); *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 151-52 (1997) (per curiam). Because a noncapital sentencing enhancement is not itself a separate offense, the Double Jeopardy Clause does not convert a failed enhancement proof into an acquittal that forecloses retrial.

This Court’s capital-sentencing cases do not compel a different result. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 438-46 (1981), the Court held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applied where the capital sentencing proceeding “in all relevant respects” resembled a trial on guilt or innocence. The Court extended that reasoning in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-11 (1984), because the sentencing hearing there shared the “hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” But the Court has never held that *Bullington* governs ordinary noncapital recidivism proceedings. To the contrary, in *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28, 30 (1985) (per curiam), the Court reiterated that “a sentencing in a noncapital case does not have the qualities of constitutional finality” associated with an acquittal. And in *Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33, 42 (1988), the Court held that retrial of an enhancement allegation is not barred where a reviewing court sets aside the sentence after concluding the proof was insufficient only because evidence was erroneously admitted. That is fatal to respondent’s theory, because it confirms that enhancement findings do not receive the same finality as guilt-phase acquittals. See also *United States v. DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. 117, 136-39 (1980).

The California Supreme Court correctly recognized that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were tied to capital sentencing. *Monge v. California*, 16 Cal. 4th 826, 837-40, 67 Cal. Rptr. 2d 546, 941 P.2d 352 (1997). That distinction matters. Capital sentencing is unique because the sentencer must choose between life and death, a determination that “in all relevant respects” mirrors a trial verdict. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 438. Noncapital recidivist proceedings are different in both function and form. They are part of the sentencing process, not a separate adjudication of criminal liability. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 246, 251; *DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. at 137-39. As the Court observed in *DiFrancesco*, a defendant “has no expectation of finality” in a sentence the law expressly permits to be revisited. 449 U.S. at 136.

Respondent’s claim also misreads *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1 (1978), and the general rule that an evidentiary insufficiency after a trial on guilt bars retrial. That rule protects against repeated attempts to convict of the same offense after an acquittal. *Burks*, 437 U.S. at 16-18. It does not govern a noncapital sentence enhancement, where the State is not seeking to relitigate guilt. *DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. at 134-39; *Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. at 30. Likewise, *Sanford* teaches that when proceedings end without a conviction or acquittal on the charged offense, retrial is ordinarily permitted. *United States v. Sanford*, 429 U.S. 14, 15-16 (1976) (per curiam).

Nor does the fact that California provides procedural protections for prior-conviction allegations transform them into “offenses” for double jeopardy purposes. States may choose to grant jury trial and proof-beyond-a-reasonable-doubt safeguards without incorporating every constitutional attribute of a guilt trial. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251; *Monge*, 16 Cal. 4th at 833-36. A State’s decision to afford greater process does not create greater double-jeopardy finality than the Federal Constitution requires. *DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. at 130-31, 134-39.

Finally, petitioner’s rule would create perverse consequences. If any evidentiary failure in a noncapital enhancement proceeding barred retrial, the State would lose the ability to correct ordinary trial error even where the failure stemmed from evidentiary confusion, an erroneous ruling, or an incomplete record. That is precisely the type of rigid finality the Court rejected in sentencing contexts. *DiFrancesco*, 449 U.S. at 135-39; *Lockhart*, 488 U.S. at 42.

Because the Double Jeopardy Clause does not extend to noncapital sentencing enhancements, the judgment below should be reversed.
