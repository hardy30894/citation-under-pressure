**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) adopts state law as surrogate federal law only to fill a gap in federal law, not whenever state law is merely “not inconsistent” with federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding misreads the statute’s text, ignores this Court’s precedent, and would disrupt the uniform federal regime Congress designed for the Outer Continental Shelf.

**I. The Text and Structure of OCSLA Require a Gap-Filling Approach**

OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision declares that state law applies “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). The phrase “to the extent that” is inherently limiting. It directs courts to adopt state law only when federal law does not already cover the field. As this Court explained in *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365 (1969), OCSLA “adopts state law as the law of the United States” only “where federal law is not applicable.” The statute does not create a concurrent regime; it fills a void.

The Ninth Circuit erred by treating “applicable” as a mere synonym for “pertinent.” But “applicable” in this context means “capable of being applied” because federal law leaves room. The Fifth Circuit correctly recognized this in *Cont’l Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), holding that state law applies only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. That interpretation respects the statutory text and the OCSLA’s purpose: to provide a complete body of law for the OCS, not to invite state-law supplementation whenever Congress has already spoken.

**II. This Court’s Precedent Confirms the Gap-Filling Rule**

*Rodrigue* is the Court’s only direct application of § 1333(a)(2)(A). There, the Court held that state wrongful-death law applied because the Death on the High Seas Act did not cover accidents on fixed platforms. 395 U.S. at 366. The Court did not ask whether state law was merely “not inconsistent” with federal law; it asked whether federal law provided a remedy. Because it did not, state law filled the gap. *Id.* at 365–66.

In *Shell Oil Co. v. Iowa Dep’t of Revenue*, 488 U.S. 19, 27 (1988), the Court reiterated that OCSLA “adopts state law as federal law to fill the gaps in federal law.” The Court noted that Congress enacted OCSLA because “federal law standing alone would be inadequate.” *Id.* at 26–27. That rationale—inadequacy, not mere compatibility—drives the statute. Where federal law is adequate, state law has no role.

The Ninth Circuit relied on *Rodrigue* for the proposition that state law applies whenever it is “applicable and not inconsistent.” But *Rodrigue* never adopted that broad standard. It applied state law only after finding that federal law left a gap. The Court’s reasoning confirms that the absence of federal law is a prerequisite.

**III. The FLSA Leaves No Gap for State Wage-and-Hour Law**

The Fair Labor Standards Act (“FLSA”) is a comprehensive federal scheme governing wages, hours, and meal periods. 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219. It establishes minimum wage and overtime requirements, defines the workweek, and provides enforcement mechanisms. The Department of Labor has issued detailed regulations interpreting the FLSA, including rules on meal periods. 29 C.F.R. § 785.19. There is no void.

The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves state law that provides greater protections *within the states’ territorial jurisdiction*. But that clause does not extend state law to the OCS. The OCS is an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). The savings clause cannot override OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision, which limits state law to gap-filling. As the district court correctly held, the FLSA’s comprehensive coverage leaves no room for California’s wage-and-hour laws to operate as surrogate federal law.

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Rule Would Undermine Uniformity**

Congress designed OCSLA to create a uniform federal regime for the OCS. *See* S. Rep. No. 83-411, at 2 (1953). The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow each adjacent state’s law to apply whenever it is not preempted, producing a patchwork of conflicting obligations. That result is precisely what Congress sought to avoid by making federal law primary and state law supplemental only when necessary.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
