Arizona’s rule is constitutional because due process guarantees a fair opportunity to contest the State’s proof, not a freestanding right to use psychiatric evidence for every theory the defense might prefer. The Fourteenth Amendment requires the prosecution to prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt, but it does not dictate that the States must allow expert mental-health evidence to operate as a separate diminished-capacity defense. See In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358 (1970); Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952); Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895).

This Court’s insanity cases make clear that mental responsibility is a matter the States may regulate. In Davis, the Court required the federal government to prove sanity beyond a reasonable doubt once the issue was raised, 160 U.S. at 484-85, but that holding turned on federal criminal practice and did not constitutionalize a separate right to introduce psychiatric evidence to negate intent. Leland rejected the claim that due process forbids placing the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, upholding Oregon’s rule that the accused prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt. 343 U.S. at 798-99. If due process permits a State to put the entire burden of insanity on the defendant, it necessarily permits the lesser choice Arizona made here: allowing psychiatric proof for insanity, but not as an independent way to disprove mens rea. See Salinger v. United States, 272 U.S. 542, 548-49 (1926).

The older cases petitioner might invoke do not support his claim. Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14 (1967), struck down a rule that barred the accused from calling a witness because of his status as an accomplice; the Court held the defendant had been denied “the testimony of witnesses to establish a defense.” Id. at 19. But Washington addressed the wholesale exclusion of testimony, not a State’s authority to define the legal relevance of admitted evidence. Arizona did not bar Clark from presenting a defense; it limited one category of expert evidence to the insanity issue, while still requiring the State to prove intent through the ordinary proof of the shooting and its circumstances. That is a fundamentally different constitutional question.

The competency and confession cases are also inapposite. Blackburn v. Alabama, 361 U.S. 199 (1960), involved an involuntary confession from a mentally ill defendant under coercive circumstances. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), addressed competence to stand trial. Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), required a hearing when the record raised doubt about competency. None of those decisions recognizes a due process right to present psychiatric evidence to negate intent apart from insanity. They protect the reliability of the process and the defendant’s ability to participate in it; they do not require States to adopt diminished-capacity doctrines.

Nor does general due process doctrine help petitioner. Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97, 105-06 (1934), held that due process is violated only by procedures that offend “some principle of justice so rooted in the traditions and conscience of our people as to be ranked as fundamental.” No such principle requires a State to recognize psychiatric evidence as a separate route to acquittal on mens rea. To the contrary, the common-law tradition has long permitted legislatures and courts to confine mental-condition evidence to insanity. Holt v. United States, 218 U.S. 245, 252-53 (1910), recognized the distinct treatment of mental evidence in criminal responsibility determinations, and Fisher v. United States, 328 U.S. 463, 476 (1946), reiterated that constitutional protections do not disable the States from structuring the administration of justice.

Petitioner’s theory would convert a policy choice about the scope of insanity into a federal constitutional command. But this Court has never held that due process requires States to recognize diminished capacity, much less to permit expert psychiatric testimony for that purpose whenever a defendant also asserts insanity. Before 1970, the Court had approved broad state authority over insanity burdens and evidentiary use of mental-condition proof, while insisting only that the defendant receive a fair trial. Arizona satisfied that requirement here. The conviction should be affirmed.
