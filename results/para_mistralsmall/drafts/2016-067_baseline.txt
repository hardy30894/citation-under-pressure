**ARGUMENT**

I. **SANTANDER IS NOT A “DEBT COLLECTOR” UNDER THE FDCPA BECAUSE IT COLLECTS DEBTS OWED TO ITSELF, NOT “ANOTHER.”**

The Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (“FDCPA”) regulates “debt collectors,” defined in relevant part as those who “regularly collect[ ] or attempt[ ] to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due ... another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander does not qualify as a debt collector because it collects debts that it owns—i.e., debts owed to *itself*, not “another.” *Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc.*, 814 F.3d 131, 135 (4th Cir. 2016). This interpretation is consistent with the statutory text and controlling Supreme Court and circuit precedent.

The Supreme Court has emphasized that the FDCPA’s definitional structure distinguishes between “debt collectors” and “creditors” based on whether the entity collects debts *for others* or *for itself*. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 294 (1995) (“The Act’s definition of the term ‘debt collector’ includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] ... another’”). The Court’s focus on the phrase “owed ... another” confirms that the critical inquiry is the *identity of the creditor at the time of collection*, not the status of the debt when acquired. *Id.* (emphasizing that the Act regulates third-party collectors, not creditors collecting their own debts).

This Court has likewise recognized that the FDCPA does not apply to creditors collecting on their own behalf. *FTC v. Absolute Collection Serv., Inc.*, 521 F.3d 1314, 1317 (11th Cir. 2008) (“The FDCPA does not regulate creditors attempting to collect debts owed to them in the first instance”). The same principle applies here: Santander purchased the defaulted loans and thereafter sought to collect the balances owed to *it*, not to CitiFinancial Auto. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 135. Because Santander was collecting its *own* debts, it acted as a creditor, not a debt collector. *Id.*

The Fourth Circuit’s decision aligns with this interpretation. It rejected the argument that the default status of the debt at the time of purchase transforms a purchaser into a debt collector, noting that the FDCPA’s definition hinges on *who* the debt is owed to at the time of collection, not *when* the debt was acquired. *Id.* at 136-37. The court further observed that the plaintiffs’ interpretation would improperly expand the FDCPA’s reach to entities that purchase defaulted debt and collect it for their own account. *Id.* at 137.

II. **THE FDCPA’S EXCLUSIONS DO NOT ALTER SANTANDER’S STATUS AS A CREDITOR.**

The FDCPA excludes from the definition of “debt collector” certain persons who collect debts on behalf of others, including those who collect non-defaulted debts. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). The Fourth Circuit correctly held that this exclusion does not apply to Santander because it does not collect debts “owed ... another” at all—it collects debts owed to *itself*. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 136. The exclusion only removes from the definition of “debt collector” those who collect debts *for others* when the debts are not in default. *Id.* (citing S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977), *reprinted in* 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698). Santander, by contrast, collects its *own* debts, regardless of default status.

The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355 (6th Cir. 2012), is misplaced. In *Bridge*, the Sixth Circuit held that a loan servicer that purchased defaulted loans and then collected on them for its own account was not a debt collector. *Id.* at 360. The court reasoned that the servicer was collecting its *own* debts, not debts “owed ... another.” *Id.* The Fourth Circuit expressly followed *Bridge* in *Henson*, distinguishing cases like *Ruth v. Triumph P’ships*, 577 F.3d 790 (7th Cir. 2009), which involved entities collecting debts *for others*. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 136.

III. **SANTANDER’S PRE-PURCHASE COLLECTION ACTIVITIES DO NOT TRANSFORM IT INTO A DEBT COLLECTOR.**

The plaintiffs argue that Santander’s prior role as a servicer for CitiFinancial Auto rendered it a debt collector even after purchasing the loans. This argument conflates Santander’s *status* with its *activities*. The FDCPA regulates conduct *by* debt collectors, not entities that *sometimes* act as debt collectors. *Heintz*, 514 U.S. at 294. Santander’s purchase of the loans extinguished its agency relationship with CitiFinancial Auto and transformed it into the creditor. *Henson*, 814 F.3d at 137. The Fourth Circuit correctly rejected the notion that an entity could evade the FDCPA’s creditor exemption by purchasing the debt it was previously collecting for another. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA because it collects debts owed to itself, not “another.” The Fourth Circuit’s decision should be affirmed.
