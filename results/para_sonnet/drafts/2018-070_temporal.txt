**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) borrows the law of adjacent States only where necessary to fill a void in federal coverage. That limited, gap-filling function follows from the structure of OCSLA's choice-of-law provision, this Court's treatment of Shelf installations as federal enclaves, and the comprehensive character of the federal wage-and-hour scheme at issue here.

**A. OCSLA's Choice-of-Law Provision Borrows State Law Only to Fill Gaps in Federal Law.**

Section 1333(a)(2)(A) declares that the laws of an adjacent State become "the law of the United States" for the Shelf only "[t]o the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent" with federal law. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Congress designed this provision to complete, not compete with, the body of federal law governing platforms on the Shelf. As the Fifth Circuit held in Continental Oil Co. v. London Steamship Owners' Mutual Insurance Association, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), state law is received into the OCSLA scheme "as surrogate federal law" only where necessary "to fill a significant void or gap" left by existing federal law. Id. at 1036. That formulation reflects the statute's true office: a mechanism for completing federal law, not an open door for any state regulatory scheme not flatly contradicted by a specific federal command.

The statute's own terms confirm this. Because § 1333(a)(2)(A) makes the Constitution and laws of the United States govern the Shelf "to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction," 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1), Congress contemplated a body of law in which federal law is primary and state law merely supplemental. The word "applicable" does independent work only if it asks whether federal law has left room—a gap—for state law to operate; otherwise a bare "not inconsistent" test would suffice.

**B. Rodrigue Confirms That Shelf Installations Are Treated as Federal Enclaves, Where State Law Fills Only Interstitial Gaps.**

This Court's decision in Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969), confirms that Congress modeled OCSLA's choice-of-law regime on federal enclave law. The Court explained that "[t]he purpose of the Act was to define a body of law applicable to the seabed, the subsoil, and the fixed structures … on the outer Continental Shelf," id. at 355, and held that such structures must be treated as artificial islands within a landlocked jurisdiction rather than as vessels, so that maritime remedies did not extend to accidents on them, id. at 360, 366. Because Congress extended federal law to the Shelf "as if" it were "an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction," 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1), Rodrigue teaches that the Shelf's legal status parallels an upland federal enclave, where federal law is the baseline and state law is received only where federal law is silent.

That enclave analogy is significant because Congress structured § 1333(a)(2)(A) through the same device long used for federal enclaves—prospective incorporation of state law into territory of exclusive federal jurisdiction. This Court sustained Congress's power to adopt state law prospectively for such enclaves in United States v. Sharpnack, 355 U.S. 286 (1958), removing the constitutional doubt that had previously confined Congress to borrowing only law "now in effect." Id. at 293–94. Sharpnack did not suggest that such assimilated state law was meant to run concurrently alongside a comprehensive federal scheme; the assimilation of state law into federal enclaves has traditionally served only the narrower purpose of supplying rules where none otherwise exist. Rodrigue's holding that state wrongful-death law was available on the Shelf turned precisely on the absence of any governing federal remedy—the Death on the High Seas Act did not reach fixed-platform accidents because they "involved no collision with a vessel," and the platforms "were not navigational aids." 395 U.S. at 360. Only because no federal remedy existed did the Court hold that "any obstacle to the application of state law by incorporation as federal law through [§ 1333(a)(2)]" was removed. Id. at 366. Rodrigue thus stands for the proposition that state law fills the void OCSLA leaves—not that it operates as an independent, freestanding source of law whenever a State chooses to regulate similar subject matter.

**C. A Comprehensive Federal Scheme Leaves No Gap for State Law to Fill.**

The same principle that limited state law's role in Rodrigue to the absence of a federal remedy governs here. Where Congress has already legislated comprehensively over a subject on lands under exclusive federal jurisdiction, there is no room—no "significant void or gap," Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036—for a State's parallel regulatory scheme to be assimilated as surrogate federal law. The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., comprehensively addresses minimum-wage and overtime protection for covered employees, including a savings clause allowing more protective state minimum-wage and overtime law to operate within its own terms. That the FLSA speaks directly and comprehensively to wage-and-hour protection is itself proof that no "void or gap," Continental Oil, 417 F.2d at 1036, exists on the Shelf for California's separate meal-period and overtime requirements to fill under § 1333(a)(2)(A).

Because OCSLA borrows state law only interstitially—consistent with its text, with the enclave framework this Court recognized in Rodrigue, and with the settled understanding of prospective state-law assimilation approved in Sharpnack—the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
