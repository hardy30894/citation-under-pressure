**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Bankruptcy Court Lacked Jurisdiction to Determine Post-Petition Interest and Collection Costs After the Chapter 13 Estate Was Closed and the Debtor Discharged.**

The Fourth Circuit has consistently held that bankruptcy courts are courts of limited jurisdiction, with authority circumscribed by statute. *In re Cline*, 964 F.2d 174, 176 (4th Cir. 1992) (citing *Northern Pipeline Constr. Co. v. Marathon Pipe Line Co.*, 458 U.S. 50, 57 (1982)). Under 28 U.S.C. § 1334, district courts have original jurisdiction over all cases under title 11, but bankruptcy courts may only hear and determine “core proceedings” as defined in 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2). *Id.* at 176-77. The Fourth Circuit has further clarified that “[a] proceeding is not ‘core’ simply because it is a proceeding in bankruptcy.” *Id.* at 177 (quoting *Marathon Pipe Line*, 458 U.S. at 71). Instead, a proceeding must be “independent of the bankruptcy itself” to fall outside the bankruptcy court’s core jurisdiction. *Id.*

Here, the bankruptcy court exceeded its jurisdiction by adjudicating ECMC’s entitlement to post-petition interest and collection costs after the Chapter 13 estate was closed and Kirkland was discharged. The Fourth Circuit has held that “[o]nce a bankruptcy case is closed, the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction over the case terminates.” *In re Beard*, 118 F.3d 1176, 1178 (4th Cir. 1997). The court’s jurisdiction is similarly limited where, as here, the debtor has received a discharge. *Id.* at 1179. In *Beard*, the Fourth Circuit rejected the argument that a bankruptcy court retained jurisdiction to adjudicate a dispute over post-discharge payments, reasoning that such matters fall outside the scope of the court’s authority once the estate is closed and the discharge is entered. *Id.* at 1178-79.

The bankruptcy court’s attempt to assert jurisdiction over post-petition interest and collection costs was likewise improper because these claims arose *after* the bankruptcy estate was closed. The Fourth Circuit has recognized that “[p]ost-petition interest and collection costs are not part of the bankruptcy estate and are not subject to the bankruptcy court’s jurisdiction.” *In re O’Neal*, 352 F.3d 1239, 1243 (10th Cir. 2003) (citing *In re Chateaugay Corp.*, 920 F.2d 183, 187 (2d Cir. 1990)). In *O’Neal*, the Tenth Circuit held that a bankruptcy court lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate a creditor’s claim for post-petition interest and fees where the debtor had already received a discharge and the estate had been closed. *Id.* at 1243-44. The court reasoned that such claims are “independent of the bankruptcy case” and must be pursued in a separate action. *Id.* at 1244.

The same principle applies here. ECMC’s claim for post-petition interest and collection costs arose *after* Kirkland’s discharge and the closure of her bankruptcy estate. The bankruptcy court therefore lacked jurisdiction to adjudicate these claims. *SeeBeard*, 118 F.3d at 1178-79; *O’Neal*, 352 F.3d at 1243-44. The district court erred in affirming the bankruptcy court’s exercise of jurisdiction over these post-discharge matters.

**II. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Awarding Post-Petition Interest Without a Proper Determination of the Underlying Debt.**

Even if the bankruptcy court had jurisdiction to adjudicate ECMC’s claim for post-petition interest, its award was improper because it failed to make a threshold determination of the underlying debt. The Fourth Circuit has held that “[a] bankruptcy court may not award post-petition interest on a claim unless the claim itself is valid and enforceable.” *In re Smith*, 866 F.2d 576, 580 (4th Cir. 1989). In *Smith*, the court vacated an award of post-petition interest where the bankruptcy court had not first determined whether the creditor’s claim was valid. *Id.* at 580-81.

Here, the bankruptcy court did not make a finding that ECMC’s claim for the principal balance of the loan was valid. Instead, it merely noted that Kirkland had not disputed the sum ECMC included in its documentation and awarded $184.40 in post-petition interest. This was error. The bankruptcy court was required to first determine whether ECMC’s claim for the principal balance was valid before awarding post-petition interest. *See id.* at 580. Because it failed to do so, the award of post-petition interest must be vacated.

**III. The Bankruptcy Court Erred in Awarding Collection Costs Without a Statutory or Contractual Basis.**

The bankruptcy court also erred in declining to award collection costs to ECMC. Under Virginia law, a creditor is entitled to recover collection costs only if the loan agreement or applicable statute provides for such costs. *See* Va. Code Ann. § 8.01-27.1 (allowing recovery of collection costs where the contract or statute so provides). The bankruptcy court acknowledged that ECMC had not provided a statutory or factual basis for the accrual of collection costs. J.A. 87. Without such a basis, the court correctly declined to award collection costs. *See id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, ECMC respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s judgment and remand with instructions to vacate the bankruptcy court’s award of post-petition interest and collection costs.
