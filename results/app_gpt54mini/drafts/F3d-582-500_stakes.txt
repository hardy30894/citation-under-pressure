**Argument**

The District Court erred in granting summary judgment because a reasonable jury could find that Erdman satisfied the FMLA’s eligibility threshold and, in any event, Nationwide’s conduct interfered with and retaliated against her exercise of FMLA rights. Summary judgment is reviewed de novo, and all reasonable inferences must be drawn in Erdman’s favor. McTernan v. City of York, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009); Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986).

**I. Erdman raised a genuine dispute of material fact as to whether she had at least 1,250 hours of service.**

The FMLA applies to an “eligible employee,” meaning an employee who has been employed “for at least 12 months” and who has completed “at least 1,250 hours of service with such employer during the previous 12-month period.” 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A). Congress directed that the statute be construed to fulfill its remedial purpose. See 29 U.S.C. § 2601(b); Nevada Department of Human Resources v. Hibbs, 538 U.S. 721, 724-25 (2003).

The record permits a finding that Erdman met the hours requirement. Nationwide’s supervisors acknowledged that Erdman regularly worked extra hours outside the office, and Sarno consistently authorized payment or “comp” time for those hours. Once an employee has actually worked hours, those hours count toward the FMLA threshold; the question is factual, not semantic. The District Court resolved that factual dispute against Erdman, contrary to the summary-judgment standard. See Anderson, 477 U.S. at 255; Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322-23 (1986).

Nationwide may argue that Erdman’s extra hours were unauthorized or later disallowed. But the FMLA defines “hours of service” by reference to hours actually worked, and employers cannot avoid the statute by characterizing previously tolerated work as nonexistent. See 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(C); Ragsdale v. Wolverine World Wide, Inc., 535 U.S. 81, 87-89 (2002). At minimum, the record evidence that Sarno repeatedly approved extra hours, that Getgen initially made no objection to Erdman’s comp-time practice, and that Erdman specifically sought clarification before being told she could no longer use comp time creates a triable issue as to how many hours she had accumulated during the relevant period.

**II. Erdman presented sufficient evidence of FMLA interference.**

An employer violates the FMLA if it “interfere[s] with, restrain[s], or den[ies] the exercise of or the attempt to exercise” FMLA rights. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(1). To prevail, a plaintiff need only show that she was entitled to benefits under the Act and was denied them. See Callison v. City of Philadelphia, 430 F.3d 117, 119-20 (3d Cir. 2005); Sommer v. The Vanguard Group, 461 F.3d 397, 399-400 (3d Cir. 2006).

Erdman requested leave for July 7 to August 29, and a human resources employee responded that, “as far as the FMLA, I probably don’t see any problems with this.” A reasonable jury could conclude that Nationwide’s later termination prevented Erdman from taking leave to which she was entitled. The employer’s after-the-fact explanation that it terminated Erdman for misconduct is a classic factual dispute that cannot be resolved on summary judgment where the evidence supports competing inferences. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); Fuentes v. Perskie, 32 F.3d 759, 764-65 (3d Cir. 1994).

**III. Erdman also presented sufficient evidence of retaliation.**

The FMLA separately prohibits an employer from discharging or discriminating against an employee for opposing unlawful FMLA practices or for exercising FMLA rights. 29 U.S.C. § 2615(a)(2); 29 C.F.R. § 825.220(c). Retaliation claims under the FMLA are analyzed under the familiar burden-shifting framework. Conoshenti v. Public Service Electric & Gas Co., 364 F.3d 135, 146-48 (3d Cir. 2004); Lichtenstein v. University of Pittsburgh Medical Center, 691 F.3d 294, 302-04 (3d Cir. 2012).

Erdman’s request for FMLA leave was followed closely by adverse treatment: Nationwide eliminated her part-time position, pressured her to return full-time, and then discharged her within weeks of her leave request. Temporal proximity can support an inference of retaliatory motive when combined with other evidence of hostility. See Jalil v. Avdel Corp., 873 F.2d 701, 708 (3d Cir. 1989); Kachmar v. SunGard Data Systems, Inc., 109 F.3d 173, 177-78 (3d Cir. 1997). The record also shows that Erdman had longstanding approval to work extra hours and use comp time until she sought FMLA leave, after which Nationwide abruptly changed course. A jury could infer that Nationwide’s stated reliance on misconduct was pretextual. See Fuentes, 32 F.3d at 764-65.

**Conclusion**

Because genuine disputes of material fact exist as to Erdman’s FMLA eligibility, interference claim, and retaliation claim, the judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
