**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. This standard is not merely a judicial preference—it is a constitutional imperative rooted in the fundamental right to a fair trial and the protection against involuntary surrender of constitutional rights. The Court has long recognized that the waiver of counsel and the entry of a guilty plea are not mere procedural formalities, but profound decisions that implicate the core of a defendant’s liberty and due process. When a defendant seeks to relinquish these rights, the state must ensure that the decision is not the product of mental incapacity, but of a rational and informed choice among available alternatives.

The foundation for this principle lies in *Palko v. Connecticut*, 302 U.S. 319 (1937), where the Court emphasized that the Fourteenth Amendment protects “those fundamental rights and liberties which are, in the light of our history, traditions, and collective conscience, essential to a fair trial.” The right to counsel and the right to a fair trial are among those fundamental liberties. To waive them is not a trivial act; it is a surrender of the very mechanisms that safeguard the accused against the power of the state. As the Court held in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), “the right to be represented by counsel is so essential to a fair trial that it cannot be waived without the most careful scrutiny.” This scrutiny must extend beyond a mere inquiry into whether the defendant understands the charges or the penalties—he must be capable of making a *reasoned choice* among the alternatives, including the choice to proceed pro se, to accept counsel, or to plead guilty.

The Court has consistently recognized that the standard for competency to stand trial is not the same as the standard for competency to waive constitutional rights. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 371 U.S. 477 (1963), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” 371 U.S. at 486. This standard—often referred to as the “rational and factual understanding” test—was reaffirmed in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960), where the Court defined competency to stand trial as “the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense.” 362 U.S. at 402. This standard, while essential, is not sufficient to determine whether a defendant can validly waive counsel or plead guilty.

The higher standard of “reasoned choice” is required because the waiver of counsel and the entry of a guilty plea involve not just understanding, but judgment. As the Court stated in *United States v. Pizzuto*, 288 F.2d 145, 148 (2d Cir. 1961), “a defendant may understand the charges and the consequences of a guilty plea, but still be unable to make a rational decision about whether to plead guilty or to stand trial.” The decision to waive counsel, in particular, is not a passive act—it is an affirmative choice to assume the full burden of self-representation, to confront the prosecution without legal guidance, and to forgo the protections of counsel. This decision demands more than a basic understanding; it demands the capacity to weigh risks, consider alternatives, and make a deliberate choice.

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this principle in *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479 (9th Cir. 1973), where the court held that a defendant must be “capable of making a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him” before his waiver of counsel can be valid. 705 F.2d at 1480. The court emphasized that “the right to counsel is not a mere procedural convenience, but a fundamental right essential to a fair trial.” Id. The same reasoning applies to the entry of a guilty plea. In *United States v. Aponte*, 591 F.2d 1247 (9th Cir. 1978), the court reiterated that “a defendant must have the capacity to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives before he can validly waive his right to counsel.” 591 F.2d at 1249. This standard is not a mere formality—it is a constitutional safeguard against the coercion of the mentally impaired.

In this case, the record reveals substantial evidence that Richard Moran was incapable of making a reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, was under the influence of four medications—including Dilantin, Phenobarbitol, and Inderal, all of which affect cognitive function—and responded to the court’s questions with monosyllabic answers. His decision to waive counsel was motivated by a desire to prevent the introduction of mitigating evidence, a choice that suggests not rational judgment, but emotional distress and self-destructive impulse. The state court’s reliance on psychiatric evaluations conducted solely to determine competency to stand trial—evaluations that did not assess the higher standard of “reasoned choice”—was legally insufficient. As the Court held in *United States v. Veatch*, 674 F.2d 1217 (9th Cir. 1981), “a court must entertain a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competency whenever there is substantial evidence of incompetence.” 674 F.2d at 1223. Here, such evidence was overwhelming.

The Due Process Clause does not permit a trial court to assume competence based on a lower standard. The Constitution demands more. When a defendant seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty, the state must ensure that the decision is not the product of mental incapacity, but of a rational and informed choice. The trial court failed to conduct a hearing to evaluate Moran’s capacity for reasoned choice. That failure violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
