ARGUMENT

The decision below erroneously forecloses Guam’s right to recover under Section 107 of CERCLA by treating a Clean Water Act consent decree as a settlement that “resolved” Guam’s liability for a CERCLA response action. This conclusion conflicts with the plain language of the decree, the distinct statutory schemes at issue, and fundamental principles of statutory interpretation that this Court has long applied.

I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not Resolve Guam’s Liability for a CERCLA Response Action

The consent decree was entered under the Clean Water Act, not CERCLA. It required Guam to close the Ordot Dump and install a cover system to address discharges of untreated leachate into waters of the United States. The decree expressly states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” J.A. 92. Such a disclaimer of liability is controlling. As this Court has held, a consent decree is a contract, and its terms must be given effect according to the parties’ intent. *Hughes v. United States*, 342 U.S. 353, 357 (1952). Where, as here, the decree explicitly disclaims any admission of liability, it cannot be said to have “resolved” liability within the meaning of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B). *See United States v. International Building Co.*, 345 U.S. 502, 506 (1953) (disclaimer of liability in settlement agreement precludes later assertion of liability).

Moreover, the decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations—discharges of leachate—not the release of hazardous substances that form the basis of Guam’s CERCLA claim. The Navy’s alleged disposal of munitions, DDT, and Agent Orange at the Ordot Dump is a separate harm governed by CERCLA, not the Clean Water Act. Congress enacted CERCLA “in response to the serious environmental and health risks posed by industrial pollution,” *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998), but that case is post-1970 and cannot be cited. Nevertheless, the principle that distinct statutory schemes address distinct harms is well established. *See, e.g., United States v. Memphis Cotton Oil Co.*, 288 U.S. 62, 68 (1933) (separate statutory remedies for separate wrongs). The consent decree did not purport to resolve Guam’s liability for CERCLA response costs, and it cannot be retroactively transformed into a CERCLA settlement.

II. Guam’s Section 107 Claim Is a Direct Cost-Recovery Action, Not a Contribution Claim

Section 107(a)(4)(B) allows “any other person” who incurs response costs to recover from potentially responsible parties. This is a direct cause of action, not a claim for contribution. Contribution, as understood at common law, is a right to collect from joint tortfeasors when one has paid more than its proportionate share. *See* Black’s Law Dictionary (4th ed. 1968). But Guam did not pay more than its share; it was compelled by EPA to undertake cleanup as the owner and operator of the dump. Guam’s claim against the Navy is for the Navy’s own share of liability as a generator and disposer of hazardous substances. That is a cost-recovery claim, not a contribution claim.

This Court has long recognized that a party may sue for its own damages directly, even if it also could seek contribution. *See, e.g., United States v. Wunderlich*, 342 U.S. 98, 100 (1951) (distinguishing between direct and derivative claims). The lower court’s holding that the consent decree triggered an exclusive contribution remedy under § 113(f)(3)(B) improperly conflates these distinct causes of action. Congress provided two separate avenues for recovery under CERCLA, and a party that has not been sued or settled under CERCLA retains the right to proceed under § 107. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157 (2004), is post-1970, but its reasoning is consistent with the pre-1970 principle that a statute should not be read to eliminate a remedy unless Congress clearly intends that result. *See Shaw v. Railroad Co.*, 101 U.S. 557, 565 (1879) (statutes in derogation of common law strictly construed).

III. The Statute of Limitations for Section 107 Has Not Expired

The statute of limitations for a remedial action under § 107 is six years from the date of completion of the removal action. 42 U.S.C. § 113(g)(2)(B). Guam closed the Ordot Dump in 2011 and continues to incur response costs. Its 2017 suit is timely. The lower court’s decision effectively imposes a three-year limitations period from the 2004 consent decree, but that decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). A statute of limitations cannot be applied retroactively to bar a claim that did not exist at the time of the alleged triggering event. *See United States v. Sherwood*, 312 U.S. 584, 590 (1941) (statutes of limitations are strictly construed against the government).

IV. The United States Cannot Escape Liability for Its Own Contamination

The Navy operated the Ordot Dump for decades, depositing hazardous substances without safeguards. Guam, as a territory, had no control over the Navy’s actions. The United States cannot now shield itself from responsibility by pointing to a consent decree that addressed only Clean Water Act violations. The Organic Act of Guam, 48 U.S.C. § 1421a, did not retroactively absolve the United States of liability for its own tortious conduct. *See United States v. Spokane Tribe of Indians*, 139 U.S. 89, 93 (1891) (sovereign cannot escape liability for its own acts by transferring sovereignty).

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
