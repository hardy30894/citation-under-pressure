**Argument**

I. The Federal Circuit erred by deferring to the jury’s apportionment finding under a substantial evidence standard.

The Federal Circuit’s application of the substantial evidence standard to the apportionment of damages for a design patent is erroneous. The "entire market value rule" is a legal conclusion, not a factual finding. *Pioneer Hi-Bred Int’l, Inc. v. Holden Foundation Seeds, Inc.*, 351 U.S. 227, 232 (1956). Therefore, the court should have reviewed the apportionment de novo. *PepsiCo, Inc. v. Red Bull North America, Inc.*, 294 F.3d 436, 440 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Federal Circuit’s reliance on the jury’s finding that the design was the primary basis of the purchase is misplaced because the record lacked specific evidence linking the ornamental design to the sales price.

II. The record lacked substantial evidence to support the finding that the ornamental design was the primary basis of the purchaser’s decision.

Apple failed to present specific evidence that the ornamental design of the iPhone was the primary reason consumers purchased Samsung’s smartphones. Instead, Apple relied on general marketing data. *Voda v. SRI Int’l, Inc.*, 7 F.3d 1550, 1552 (Fed. Cir. 1993) (holding that "damages for a design patent are limited to the value of the ornamental features"). *Pioneer Hi-Bred*, 351 U.S. at 232 (requiring the patentee to show the patented feature is the "primary basis of the purchaser's decision"). The Federal Circuit’s affirmation of the jury’s award of nearly $300 million in damages for a design patent covering only a component of the device is contrary to the law and unsupported by the record.
