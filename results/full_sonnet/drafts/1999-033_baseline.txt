# ARGUMENT

## THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A PLAINTIFF TO ALLEGE MEMBERSHIP IN A CLASS OR GROUP, AND RESPONDENT HAS STATED A COGNIZABLE CLASS-OF-ONE CLAIM

### I. The Text and History of the Equal Protection Clause Protect Individuals, Not Merely Classes

The Fourteenth Amendment commands that no State shall "deny to *any person* within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1 (emphasis added). This Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause's protections "extend to anyone, citizen or stranger, who is subject to the laws of a State." *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886). In *Yick Wo*, this Court struck down a facially neutral ordinance administered with "an evil eye and an unequal hand" against Chinese laundry operators, holding that even where "the discrimination is applied by [officials] with a mind so unequal and oppressive as to amount to a practical denial by the State of that equal protection," the guarantee is violated. *Id.* at 373-74. Critically, the plaintiffs in *Yick Wo* did not need to establish a suspect classification—the arbitrary and irrational application of government power to a single individual was itself the constitutional harm.

### II. This Court Has Never Required Allegation of Class Membership as a Prerequisite to an Equal Protection Claim

Respondent's claim fits comfortably within this Court's traditional rational-basis framework, which asks only whether governmental action bears "a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest." *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 432, 446 (1985). In *Cleburne*, this Court held that a city's discriminatory permit requirement violated equal protection because it "rest[ed] on an irrational prejudice against the mentally retarded" and lacked any rational basis. *Id.* at 450. Nothing in *Cleburne*'s analysis—or in the century of rational-basis jurisprudence preceding it—suggests that the equal protection inquiry changes based on whether one plaintiff or many are singled out for arbitrary treatment.

Indeed, this Court's precedents confirm that irrational, invidious treatment of even a single individual states a constitutional violation. In *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. Commission of Webster Cty.*, 488 U.S. 336 (1989), this Court held that a county assessor's practice of assessing petitioner's property at substantially higher values than comparable neighboring properties violated equal protection, even absent any claim that petitioner belonged to a protected class. This Court explained that the Equal Protection Clause requires "rough equality in tax treatment," *id.* at 343, and found the disparate treatment unconstitutional based solely on the arbitrary disparity itself—precisely the theory Respondent advances here.

### III. The Seventh Circuit Correctly Recognized That Arbitrary Governmental Action Against a Single Person States a Cognizable Claim

The Seventh Circuit properly held that Respondent stated a claim by alleging that the Village's demand for a 33-foot easement—when it required only 15 feet from other property owners—was "irrational and wholly arbitrary." This formulation tracks the rational-basis standard articulated in *Cleburne* and applied in *Allegheny Pittsburgh*. The Village's demand for more than double the easement width required of similarly situated property owners, without any articulated justification, is precisely the type of arbitrary governmental action the Equal Protection Clause forbids.

Petitioner's contrary argument—that equal protection claims require allegation of class-based animus—finds no support in this Court's precedent and would create an anomalous rule: the Constitution would protect *groups* of citizens from irrational governmental abuse while leaving *individuals* without recourse against the very same arbitrary conduct. Such a distinction has no grounding in either the text of the Fourteenth Amendment or this Court's equal protection jurisprudence. As *Yick Wo* makes clear, the constitutional concern is with the "unequal hand" of government action itself, not with any particular characteristic of its victim. 118 U.S. at 373.

### IV. Recognizing Class-of-One Claims Does Not Transform Every Zoning or Regulatory Dispute Into a Constitutional Case

Petitioner's floodgates argument is unpersuasive. The rational-basis standard already provides substantial deference to governmental decisionmaking. *Cleburne*, 473 U.S. at 440 (explaining that legislation is presumed valid and will be upheld if the classification bears a rational relationship to a legitimate governmental purpose). Plaintiffs asserting class-of-one claims must still surmount this deferential standard by demonstrating that the differential treatment lacks any conceivable rational basis—a demanding burden that will screen out routine regulatory disagreements while preserving a remedy for truly arbitrary and irrational governmental action, such as that alleged here.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit and hold that the Equal Protection Clause permits a class-of-one claim where a plaintiff alleges that she was intentionally treated differently from others similarly situated, and that there is no rational basis for the difference in treatment.
