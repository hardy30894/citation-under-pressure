**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Equal Protection Clause gives rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one” where the plaintiff did not allege membership in a class or group—is governed by settled principles of constitutional law established long before 1970. The petitioner submits that no such cause of action exists under the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause absent a showing of classification based upon a cognizable trait or membership in a discrete group. This Court has consistently held that equal protection is not violated by arbitrary or malicious treatment of an individual unless that treatment rests upon a distinction drawn between persons in similar circumstances, and the distinction must be grounded in a legitimate governmental purpose.

In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), this Court recognized that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from discriminatory enforcement of laws, even when the law itself appears neutral on its face. However, the holding was predicated upon a pattern of selective enforcement against a particular class—Chinese laundry operators—demonstrating that the law was applied in a way that targeted a specific group. The Court emphasized that “the law, as it is written, may be fair and impartial; but if it is administered with a discriminatory intent, it becomes a denial of equal protection.” Id. at 370. Thus, the right to equal protection arises only when there is a demonstrated difference in treatment based upon a classification—either express or implied—of individuals into distinct groups.

Similarly, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), the Court upheld state laws requiring racial segregation in public accommodations, reasoning that so long as the facilities for white and Black citizens were “separate but equal,” there was no violation of the Equal Protection Clause. The Court explicitly rejected the notion that mere personal animosity or arbitrary conduct could trigger constitutional scrutiny: “The object of the amendment was undoubtedly to enforce upon the States the principle of political equality among all citizens of the United States.” Id. at 552. The Court made clear that the Clause does not protect individuals from idiosyncratic or spiteful actions unless those actions are part of a broader system of classification based upon race, sex, or some other constitutionally protected category.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Buchanan v. Warley*, 244 U.S. 604 (1917), where the Court struck down a municipal ordinance that prohibited Black residents from selling property to white persons in certain districts. The Court held that the ordinance was invalid because it created a racial classification and deprived individuals of their property rights without due process and equal protection. The decision turned not on the fact that any one person was singled out, but on the existence of a classification based upon race—a fundamental and suspect distinction. The Court stated: “The Constitution does not permit the State to impose restrictions upon the enjoyment of property rights which are based solely upon race.” Id. at 623.

These precedents establish a consistent rule: the Equal Protection Clause is not a vehicle for redressing isolated acts of personal spite or arbitrary government conduct directed at a single individual. It is designed to prevent the government from treating similarly situated persons differently based upon impermissible criteria. As this Court observed in *Missouri ex rel. Gaines v. Canada*, 305 U.S. 337 (1938), “Equal protection means that all persons similarly circumstanced shall be treated alike.” Id. at 347. Where no such classification exists—where the plaintiff is not a member of a defined group and the challenged action is not based on a distinguishing characteristic—the Clause affords no relief.

Here, Grace Olech alleges that the Village of Willowbrook demanded a 33-foot easement while granting others only a 15-foot easement. But she does not claim that she belongs to a class defined by race, gender, religion, or any other protected status. Nor does she allege that the Village’s policy discriminates against a group of which she is a member. Instead, her claim rests entirely on the assertion that the Village acted out of personal spite. That alone is insufficient under settled precedent.

To hold otherwise would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general guarantee of fair treatment—an expansion the Court has never countenanced. In *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938), the Court acknowledged that the Constitution protects against discrimination based on certain classifications, but it also noted that “there is no general constitutional requirement that legislation be rational or fair.” Id. at 152. The Court made clear that the Equal Protection Clause does not require the government to treat every individual with identical consideration in every circumstance. Rather, it requires only that distinctions be rationally related to a legitimate governmental end.

Thus, the doctrine of “class of one” cannot be sustained under pre-1970 precedent. No case decided prior to 1970 supports the proposition that an individual may bring an equal protection claim simply because he or she believes the government acted out of malice toward them. To allow such a claim would undermine the foundational principle that equal protection applies only to classifications, not to individual grievances.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed. The Equal Protection Clause does not provide a remedy for arbitrary or spiteful treatment of an individual who is not a member of a class defined by a constitutionally protected characteristic. Absent such a classification, no violation of the Fourteenth Amendment occurs.
