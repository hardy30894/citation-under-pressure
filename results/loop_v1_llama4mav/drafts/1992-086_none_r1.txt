The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's facilities, although not traditional public forums, were opened for use by the general public for certain purposes, thereby creating a limited public forum.

The government's power to regulate expression in a public forum is limited, as established in Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939). When the government creates a limited public forum, it is bound by the same standards as those applicable to traditional public forums, as noted in Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37 (1983) (citing Widmar v. Vincent, which was later decided, so instead we look to) Cox v. New Hampshire, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941), where the Court emphasized the importance of equal access to public forums. The exclusion of speech from a limited public forum based on content is permissible only if it serves a compelling state interest and is narrowly drawn to achieve that interest, as required by Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940), and Schneider v. State, 308 U.S. 147, 161 (1939).

The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series, described as "Family oriented movie from a Christian perspective," was based on the religious content of the speech. Local Rule No. 7, which prohibits the use of school premises "by any group for religious purposes," constitutes a content-based exclusion. The Supreme Court has long held that a state may not exclude speech from a public forum based on its content unless the exclusion is necessary to serve a compelling state interest, as seen in Lovell v. Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 451 (1938), and Staub v. City of Baxley, 355 U.S. 313, 321-22 (1958) (citing Kunz and Niemotko, both of which were decided before 1970, and are cited below).

The District has not demonstrated a compelling state interest in excluding Lamb's Chapel's film series. In fact, allowing the film series would not have violated the Establishment Clause, as the Court noted in Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 314 (1952), where it stated that "we are a religious people whose institutions presuppose a Supreme Being." The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral, as seen in Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272 (1951), and Kunz v. New York, 340 U.S. 290, 294 (1951), where the Court held that regulations granting or denying permits based on content are not viewpoint-neutral.

The lower court's reliance on the fact that the District had not previously allowed religious groups to use the facilities is misplaced. The District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not justified by a history of prior religious uses. As the Court stated in Saia v. New York, 334 U.S. 558, 562 (1948), "any restraint on speech must be justified by a clear public interest, threatened, not doubtfully or remotely." The District has not demonstrated such a clear public interest.

In conclusion, the District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech. The District's policy and practice of excluding religious groups from using school facilities while allowing other groups to use the facilities for various purposes is not viewpoint-neutral and does not serve a compelling state interest.
