**Argument**

Section 875(c) does not impose strict liability. A federal criminal statute that is silent as to mens rea ordinarily requires proof of a guilty mind. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 250-63 (1952). The Court has repeatedly refused to treat silence as an invitation to dispense with mens rea where Congress legislates against the background of common-law criminal intent. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 251-52 (1922); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); *Spurr v. United States*, 174 U.S. 728, 735 (1899). That rule applies with special force here, because the government seeks to punish speech.

This Court has long recognized that criminal liability for spoken words depends on the speaker’s wrongful purpose, not merely how a listener might react. In *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the Court explained that “the document would not have been sent unless it had been intended to have some effect.” *Abrams v. United States*, 250 U.S. 616, 627-30 (1919) (Holmes, J., dissenting), likewise treated intent as central to speech-based liability. And in *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held advocacy may be punished only where it is “directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action” and likely to produce it. A statute criminalizing threats is no different in principle: the Constitution does not permit conviction based on innocuous or misunderstood speech absent proof that the defendant meant to threaten. See *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298, 320-31 (1957).

The Third Circuit’s objective standard improperly collapses the line between speech that is threatening and speech that is merely upsetting, offensive, hyperbolic, or artistic. That is exactly the danger this Court has rejected in analogous contexts. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969), the Court held that a political statement about shooting the President was not a true threat when viewed in context. The Court emphasized the “expressly conditional” nature of the remark and the surrounding political setting. *Id.* The lesson of *Watts* is that context matters—and context can reveal that a speaker did not mean to threaten. But context cannot substitute for proof of subjective intent where the statute is silent and the First Amendment is implicated.

The government may argue that a listener-based test is necessary to protect against fear and disruption. But this Court has never authorized conviction of protected speech merely because it caused alarm. In *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1949), the Court held that speech may not be punished because it “stirs people to anger” or “invites dispute.” Likewise, *Edwards v. South Carolina*, 372 U.S. 229, 237 (1963), rejected punishment of expressive conduct simply because it provoked hostility. The Constitution requires more than an objective tendency to offend; it requires culpable intent.

The common law also confirms this result. Threat offenses historically required a “present intent” to communicate a menacing purpose. *Rex v. Ireland*, 98 Eng. Rep. 189 (K.B. 1761), and later English authorities treated threats as wrongful because they were meant to intimidate. American criminal law inherited that principle. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 260-61; *Baender v. Barnett*, 255 U.S. 224, 226-27 (1921). Nothing in §875(c) suggests Congress intended to discard that settled understanding.

Nor does the First Amendment allow criminal punishment of ambiguous expression without proof of subjective intent. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 502-10 (1951), the Court upheld liability only after carefully weighing the defendants’ knowing advocacy of unlawful action. In *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 229-30 (1961), the Court required proof of active membership with specific intent before imposing guilt. Those decisions reflect the same basic premise: where speech is at issue, the government must prove the defendant’s own culpable mental state.

Elonis’s posts may have been crude, disturbing, and reprehensible. But criminal law does not punish bad taste; it punishes wrongful intent. Because §875(c) contains no express mens rea, and because this Court’s First Amendment precedents forbid punishment of speech based solely on an objective reaction, the statute requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his communication as a threat. See *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250-63; *Schenck*, 249 U.S. at 52; *Watts*, 394 U.S. at 708; *Brandenburg*, 395 U.S. at 447. The judgment below should be reversed.
