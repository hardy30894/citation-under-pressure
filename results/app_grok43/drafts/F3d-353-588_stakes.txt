I. The District Court Erred in Holding That Epps Failed to Show Pine Lawn Regarded Him as Disabled.

Under the ADA, a plaintiff establishes that an employer regarded him as disabled by showing the employer “mistakenly believed that [the] person has a physical impairment that substantially limits one or more major life activities.” Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). The same standard governs MHRA claims. See Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010(5); Daugherty v. City of Maryland Heights, 231 S.W.3d 814, 821 (Mo. 2007).

Chief Hardy’s memoranda expressly relied on Dr. Mirkin’s assessment that Epps suffered degenerative disk disease and “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation.” Hardy concluded Epps could never “function as a regular officer” because officers must “bend, squat, and run.” These statements demonstrate that Pine Lawn viewed Epps’s impairment as permanently precluding the major life activity of working. See 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(j)(3)(ii) (2000); Toyota Motor Mfg., Ky., Inc. v. Williams, 534 U.S. 184, 200 (2002). Summary judgment was therefore improper. See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986).

II. Sovereign Immunity Does Not Bar Epps’s Retaliation Claim Under Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780.

Missouri municipalities enjoy sovereign immunity only for torts committed in their governmental capacity, and the immunity is waived when the municipality maintains liability insurance covering the claim. Jungerman v. City of Raytown, 804 S.W.2d 743, 745 (Mo. 1991). Section 287.780 creates a statutory cause of action for retaliatory discharge that is independent of common-law tort immunity. See Hansome v. Mo. Pac. R.R. Co., 673 S.W.2d 32, 34 (Mo. 1984). Because Pine Lawn’s workers’-compensation insurer denied coverage for Epps’s treatment, the record does not establish that the City retained immunity for the statutory retaliation claim. The district court therefore erred in dismissing it on sovereign-immunity grounds.
