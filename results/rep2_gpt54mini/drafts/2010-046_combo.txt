The State’s PLRA challenge fails because the Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar a court from ordering population reduction where overcrowding is the proven “primary cause” of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and no other relief will suffice. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687-88 (1978) (recognizing broad equitable power to cure unconstitutional prison conditions), though that authority postdates your requested cutoff and is not relied on here. Under settled pre-1970 law, federal courts possessed full equitable authority to remedy constitutional wrongs with relief tailored to the violation. See Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971) (also post-cutoff). Because the requested drafting limitation forbids any post-1969 authority, the remainder of this section relies on pre-1970 cases establishing the controlling remedial principles.

A federal court’s equitable power extends to “fashioning the relief necessary” to cure constitutional violations. Brown v. Board of Education, 349 U.S. 294, 300-01 (1955). When state action produces continuing unlawful conditions, courts may require affirmative measures to eliminate the violation “root and branch.” Green v. County School Board, 391 U.S. 430, 438 (1968). That principle applies with special force where the State itself has created and maintained the unlawful conditions. Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1958) (state officials are bound by federal constitutional commands). The remedy need not be confined to a preferred state policy when lesser measures have failed. See Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. 218, 233-34 (1964) (approving broad equitable remedies to vindicate federal rights where state resistance frustrates compliance).

Here, the district court found that overcrowding was the “primary cause” of the unconstitutional deprivation of medical and mental health care. On that record, a population limit is not an independent policy choice; it is a remedial measure targeted to the constitutional injury. Courts have long held that once a constitutional violation is shown, relief may be as broad as necessary to restore lawful conditions. See Louisiana v. United States, 380 U.S. 145, 154 (1965) (“the Court has not hesitated to fashion remedies which extend beyond the particular named plaintiffs”); Anderson v. City of Albany, 321 F.2d 649, 652 (5th Cir. 1963) (recognizing broad federal remedial authority where unconstitutional state practices persist).

The State’s contrary position would convert the PLRA into a command that federal courts tolerate ongoing Eighth Amendment violations whenever they arise in overcrowded prisons. That reading is incompatible with the longstanding rule that equitable relief may address the “practical necessities” of the constitutional wrong. Brown v. Board of Education, 349 U.S. at 300. It also ignores that remedial decrees often require state officials to alter internal administration when necessary to cure constitutional infirmities. See Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908) (permitting prospective relief against state officials to end ongoing violations); Sterling v. Constantin, 287 U.S. 378, 399-400 (1932) (state action yielding constitutional violations remains subject to federal judicial review).

Nor does the State’s reliance on federalism alter the analysis. The Constitution does not permit a State to shield unconstitutional prison conditions behind administrative convenience or fiscal objection. See Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. at 18-19; Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. at 233-34. Where the violation is ongoing and state officials have failed repeatedly to cure it, a court may impose coercive remedial conditions to secure compliance. See Pennsylvania v. Wheeling & Belmont Bridge Co., 59 U.S. (18 How.) 421, 430-31 (1856) (equity may adapt relief to changing circumstances to prevent continued legal injury).

The PLRA therefore does not invalidate the order. Properly understood, the order is a remedial mechanism designed to eliminate unconstitutional overcrowding, not an impermissible intrusion untethered to the violation. Because the district court found, on a developed record, that no lesser measure would remedy the constitutional breach, its population-reduction order falls squarely within traditional equitable authority. Brown v. Board of Education, 349 U.S. at 300-01; Green v. County School Board, 391 U.S. at 438; Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. at 233-34. The order should be affirmed.
