**Argument**

**I. The State Court Possessed Subject-Matter Jurisdiction Under the Well-Pleaded Complaint Rule.**

The Supreme Court of Texas erred in finding that exclusive federal jurisdiction existed in this case. Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, a federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff’s complaint, not merely be raised by the defendant as a defense. In *Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Supreme Court held that "a well-pleaded complaint either states a claim upon which the relief can be granted, or it fails to do so. Where the facts do not support a cause of action cognizable in the federal courts, the complaint must be dismissed." Here, the complaint alleged a breach of the attorney’s duty of care under Texas law. The patent issue was not the basis of the claim but was raised by the defendant as a defense. Therefore, the federal question did not appear on the face of the complaint, and the state court had jurisdiction.

**II. The Patent Issue Was Not Substantial and Did Not Determine the Result.**

Even if the patent issue were considered, it was not "substantial" and did not determine the result of the case. In *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561 (1912), the Supreme Court explained that a suit arises under federal law only if the federal issue is "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." The result of this malpractice action—liability for attorney negligence—depends entirely on state law principles of professional responsibility. The patent issue was merely incidental to the state-law claim. The state court is fully capable of adjudicating this dispute without disturbing the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities.

**III. Federalism Concerns Support State Court Jurisdiction.**

Finally, federalism concerns support state court jurisdiction over this matter. In *Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541 (1949), the Court noted that a federal question is presented where the right asserted is "predicated upon the Constitution or a law of the United States." While the malpractice claim involves a patent, the claim itself is a state-law claim for professional negligence. The federal interest in patent uniformity is not threatened by a state court determining whether an attorney was negligent in handling a client's case. The state court is best suited to resolve this dispute, and exclusive federal jurisdiction is not warranted.
