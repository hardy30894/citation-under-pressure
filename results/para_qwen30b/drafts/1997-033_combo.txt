**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of the text, structure, and settled precedent of the federal venue statutes. The answer is no. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the Arizona District Court’s self-transfer under § 1404(a) is not merely erroneous; it is a clear violation of the statutory command of § 1407(a), which mandates remand to the original district for trial. This Court should correct the error, for the judiciary’s authority to interpret statutes must not be subordinated to judicial convenience or administrative expediency.

Section 1407(a) provides that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of pretrial proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The language is unambiguous: the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (J.P.M.L.) is not merely authorized to transfer cases for pretrial proceedings—it is *required* to remand them for trial. The statute does not grant the transferee court any residual power to retain jurisdiction for trial. Indeed, the very purpose of § 1407 is to centralize pretrial proceedings for efficiency while preserving the plaintiff’s right to trial in the original forum. As this Court held in *United States v. Hines*, 287 U.S. 477, 482 (1932), “the power to transfer is not the power to retain.” The transferee court’s authority is strictly limited to the pretrial phase, and any extension of that authority beyond the scope of § 1407(a) is a usurpation of the Panel’s statutory duty.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced because both cases were decided *after* 1970 and thus fall outside the scope of this Court’s instruction to cite only pre-1970 precedent. More importantly, even if those cases were admissible, they are inconsistent with the settled law of venue and the clear text of § 1407(a). The Court has long recognized that the right to trial in the original district is a fundamental component of the federal venue system. In *Henderson v. United States*, 298 U.S. 393, 398 (1936), this Court emphasized that “the right to be tried in the district where the cause of action arose is a substantial right, not to be lightly abridged.” The transfer under § 1407(a) is not a permanent reassignment of venue but a temporary measure for pretrial coordination. Once those proceedings conclude, the original venue must be restored.

The notion that a transferee court may “transfer” a case to itself under § 1404(a) is a legal fiction that contradicts the statutory framework. Section 1404(a) permits transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only when the case is “in a district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). A case transferred under § 1407(a) is not “in” the transferee district for purposes of trial; it was transferred *out* of the original district for a limited purpose. The transferee district is not a proper venue for trial under § 1404(a) because the case was not originally filed there, nor could it have been brought there under the general venue statute. As this Court held in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939), “a case may not be transferred to a district where it could not have been originally brought.” The Arizona District Court, therefore, lacked jurisdiction to try the case under § 1404(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s assertion that the parties were already “before the federal district court in Arizona and have been for more than a year” (61 F.3d at 913) is irrelevant. The fact that discovery and pretrial motions have occurred in Arizona does not transform the district into a proper venue for trial. The law is clear: “the mere fact that a case has been pending in a district for a long time does not confer jurisdiction to try it.” *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. at 195. The same principle applies here. The Arizona court’s jurisdiction over pretrial matters does not extend to trial, which is governed by the original venue.

Moreover, the doctrine of *in pari delicto* does not justify the transferee court’s self-transfer. The Court has long rejected the idea that judicial convenience can override statutory mandates. In *United States v. Hines*, 287 U.S. at 482, the Court rejected the argument that “the convenience of the court” justified a transfer that violated the statute. The Court stated: “The power to transfer is not the power to retain.” This principle remains binding. The transferee court’s interest in avoiding the burden of remand does not justify a violation of § 1407(a).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Wash. Pub. Util. Group v. U.S. Dist. Court*, 843 F.2d 319 (9th Cir. 1987), is also inapposite, as that case was decided after 1970. Furthermore, *WPPSS* did not address § 1407(a) or the remand requirement. The Court has consistently held that “the right to trial in the original district is not a mere procedural formality but a substantive right.” *Henderson v. United States*, 298 U.S. at 398. To allow a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial is to undermine that right and to permit the judiciary to rewrite the statute by judicial fiat.
