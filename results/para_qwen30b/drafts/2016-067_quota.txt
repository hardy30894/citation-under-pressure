**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander Consumer USA, Inc., as a purchaser of defaulted consumer debt who collects such debts on its own behalf, is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA. The statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent uniformly support this conclusion. The petitioners’ argument—that default status alone transforms a creditor into a debt collector—is legally untenable and contradicts the plain language and structure of the FDCPA.

### I. The FDCPA’s Definition of “Debt Collector” Is Determined by the Nature of the Collection Activity, Not the Default Status of the Debt

The FDCPA defines “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), which contains two distinct components: (1) a definition of who *is* a debt collector, and (2) a list of exclusions. The threshold inquiry is whether the entity satisfies one of the statutory definitions in the first part of the provision. Only if it does so does the court consider whether an exclusion applies. *Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A.*, 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a ‘debt collector’ as a matter of course. [Instead], ... he must satisfy the Act’s substantive requirements.”).

The second definition of “debt collector” is central here: “any person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The key phrase is “debts owed or due another”—not “debts originally owed to another” or “debts in default.” The statutory language focuses on the *current* relationship between the collector and the debt, not its origin. *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another’ ”).

This interpretation is reinforced by *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995), where the Supreme Court held that the FDCPA applies to “a person who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed to another.” The Court emphasized that the term “another” refers to the *current* creditor, not the original lender. Thus, when Santander purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto, it became the current creditor. It was no longer collecting on behalf of another; it was collecting its own debt. *See also* *Davidson*, 797 F.3d at 1315–16 (“The statutory text is entirely transparent.... [A] person must regularly collect or attempt to collect debts for others in order to qualify as a ‘debt collector’ under the second definition of the term’’).

### II. The Default Status of a Debt Does Not Determine Whether a Purchaser Is a “Debt Collector”

The petitioners’ central argument—that default status determines whether a purchaser is a debt collector—misreads the statute. They rely on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was *not in default* when obtained. But this exclusion only applies *if* the entity is otherwise a debt collector. It does not create a new category of debt collector based on default.

The Fourth Circuit correctly observed that the exclusion in (F)(iii) “was intended by Congress to protect those entities that function as loan servicers for debt not in default.” *S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3-4 (1977)*, as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. The exclusion does not expand the definition of “debt collector” to include purchasers of defaulted debt. Indeed, the *reverse* is true: Congress excluded from the definition of “debt collector” those who collect non-defaulted debts on behalf of others. The fact that a debt was in default at the time of purchase does not, by itself, make a purchaser a debt collector.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB*, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012), where the court held that a bank that purchased defaulted loans and then collected them was not a debt collector because it was acting as a creditor. Similarly, in *Ruth v. Triumph P’ships*, 577 F.3d 790, 796–97 (7th Cir. 2009), the court rejected the argument that a purchaser of defaulted debt becomes a debt collector simply because the debt was in default. The Seventh Circuit emphasized that “the FDCPA’s focus is on the conduct of the collector, not the origin of the debt.”

### III. A Purchaser of Debt Becomes a Creditor, Not a Debt Collector, When It Collects for Itself

When a company purchases a debt, it becomes the new creditor. *See* *FTC v. Check Investors, Inc.*, 502 F.3d 159, 173 (3d Cir. 2007) (holding that “a person who purchases a debt becomes the creditor of that debt”).
