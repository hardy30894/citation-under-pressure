**ARGUMENT**

The Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution protects individuals against unreasonable searches and seizures. *U.S. Const. amend. IV*. A police officer may conduct a protective pat-down for weapons—commonly known as a “Terry frisk”—only when the officer has a reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21 (1968). Here, the district court erred in concluding that the officers had reasonable suspicion to justify the pat-frisk of Michael Soares. The totality of the circumstances, as viewed through the lens of the Fourth Amendment, did not support the intrusion. The officers’ actions exceeded the bounds of permissible investigative stops and violated Soares’s constitutional rights.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “a police officer may in appropriate circumstances and in an appropriate manner approach a person for purposes of investigating possibly criminal behavior even though there is no probable cause to make an arrest.” *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. But such an encounter must be supported by “specific and articulable facts” that, taken together with rational inferences, give rise to reasonable suspicion. *Id.* at 21. The Court has also emphasized that “the officer must be able to point to specific and articulable facts which, taken together with rational inferences from those facts, reasonably warrant the intrusion.” *Id.* at 21. The mere fact that a person is in a high-crime area or that a vehicle is stopped for a traffic violation does not, by itself, justify a frisk. *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 231 (1985).

In this case, the officers’ decision to frisk Soares was based on a combination of the vehicle’s traffic violations, the observation of “furtive movements” in the car, and Soares’s subsequent conduct. However, the “furtive movements” observed by Officer Reid—specifically, the front-seat passenger (Soares) and the driver moving their hands toward the console and door—were not, in themselves, sufficient to justify a frisk. The Supreme Court has held that “mere nervousness, fidgeting, or other vague gestures” do not constitute reasonable suspicion. *United States v. Sokolow*, 490 U.S. 1, 10 (1989). Moreover, the movements were brief and ambiguous, and no weapon was visible. The officers did not observe any object being passed, concealed, or drawn. The fact that the movements occurred in a high-crime area does not, standing alone, justify a frisk. *United States v. Brignoni-Ponce*, 422 U.S. 873, 885 (1975).

Even more critical is the fact that the officers had not yet completed the traffic stop when they decided to frisk Soares. The stop was based on a violation of Massachusetts law requiring headlights and functioning brake lights. *Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 85, § 11*. The officers had not yet issued a citation or completed their investigation when they decided to remove Soares from the car. At that point, the only facts known to the officers were: (1) the car was driving without headlights; (2) the brake lights were malfunctioning; (3) the occupants made brief, ambiguous movements; and (4) Soares became verbally abusive and agitated after being ordered to keep his hands visible.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against allowing subjective fear or discomfort to justify a frisk. *United States v. Place*, 462 U.S. 696, 707 (1983). The officers’ “uneasiness” or “nervousness” cannot substitute for reasonable suspicion. *Id.* at 707. Here, Officer Reid testified that he “got a little uneasy” and that Soares’s “rocking back and forth” and “flailing” hands made him feel “uneasy.” But such subjective reactions, without more, do not satisfy the objective standard of reasonable suspicion. *United States v. Arvizu*, 534 U.S. 266, 273 (2002) (“The determination of reasonable suspicion must be based on the totality of the circumstances, not on the officer’s hunches or gut feelings.”).

Furthermore, the officers’ decision to remove Soares from the car and conduct a pat-frisk was not justified by any immediate threat to their safety. The Supreme Court has held that a frisk is permissible only when the officer reasonably believes that the individual is armed and dangerous. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21. There was no evidence that Soares had a weapon, no indication that he was reaching for anything, and no threat of violence. The fact that Soares was “verbally abusive” and “rocked back and forth” does not, by itself, justify a frisk. *United States v. Brown*, 510 F.3d 57, 62 (1st Cir. 2007) (holding that “verbal hostility” and “restlessness” are insufficient to justify a frisk absent additional indicia of danger).

The First Circuit has recognized that “a suspect’s demeanor, while relevant, is not dispositive.” *United States v. Soares*, 451 F. Supp. 2d 282, 286 (D. Mass. 2006). In *United States v. Brown*, the court held that “a suspect’s nervousness, fidgeting, or verbal resistance” does not, standing alone, justify a frisk. 510 F.3d at 62. Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 453 F.3d 1, 6 (1st Cir. 2006), the court emphasized that “the mere fact that a suspect is agitated or uncooperative does not justify a frisk.” The officers here had no reason to believe Soares was armed or dangerous beyond their own subjective discomfort.

The district court’s conclusion that the frisk was justified under *Terry* is therefore erroneous. The totality of the circumstances did not support a reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous.
