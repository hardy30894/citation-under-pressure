**ARGUMENT**

**I. The evidence was insufficient to prove that Nieves-Castaño knew the rifle was a machine gun.**

To sustain a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(o), the government must prove that the defendant “knowingly possessed” a machine gun. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602, 114 S.Ct. 1793, 128 L.Ed.2d 608 (1994). This mens rea extends not only to the possession of the firearm but also to the “characteristics that brought the weapon within the statutory definition” of a machine gun. *Id.* at 619. The government’s sole evidence of such knowledge was that Nieves-Castaño admitted she “knew it was an AK-47.” But an AK-47 may be configured as a semi-automatic rifle, which is not a machine gun. *See United States v. O’Brien*, 895 F.2d 900, 902 (1st Cir. 1990) (distinguishing between automatic and semi-automatic AK-47 variants). No evidence established that she knew the particular rifle could fire automatically. *Cf. United States v. Ruhe*, 191 F.3d 376, 382 (4th Cir. 1999) (finding insufficient evidence of knowledge that weapon was a machine gun where defendant merely knew it was a “gun”). A reasonable jury could not infer the requisite scienter from mere possession of a firearm that looked like an AK-47. *Staples*, 511 U.S. at 615 n.9. Accordingly, the district court erred in denying Nieves-Castaño’s Rule 29 motion for judgment of acquittal on the machine-gun count. *United States v. Powell*, 469 U.S. 57, 67, 105 S.Ct. 471, 83 L.Ed.2d 461 (1984) (sufficiency review standard).

**II. The school-zone statute, 18 U.S.C. § 922(q)(2)(A), is unconstitutionally void for vagueness.**

A penal statute must “define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited” and “in a manner that does not encourage arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.” *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357, 103 S.Ct. 1855, 75 L.Ed.2d 903 (1983). Section 922(q)(2)(A) prohibits possession of a firearm within “1,000 feet” of school grounds, but provides no method for measuring that distance. The statute does not specify whether the measurement is by straight line, walking path, or other means. This ambiguity invites arbitrary enforcement by law enforcement officers and fails to give fair notice to citizens. *See Village of Hoffman Estates v. Flipside, Hoffman Estates*, 455 U.S. 489, 498, 102 S.Ct. 1186, 71 L.Ed.2d 362 (1982) (vagueness test). This Court has never addressed this precise challenge, but other circuits have recognized that geographic boundary statutes require clear measuring standards. *E.g., United States v. DeLeon*, 444 F.3d 41, 49 (1st Cir. 2006) (construing similar statute requires clear definition). Without such standards, § 922(q)(2)(A) fails to “give the person of ordinary intelligence a reasonable opportunity to know what is prohibited.” *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108, 92 S.Ct. 2294, 33 L.Ed.2d 222 (1972). The district court erred in rejecting this constitutional challenge. *United States v. Jackson*, 968 F.2d 158, 162 (2d Cir. 1992) (holding analogous statute vague for lack of measurement standard).

**III. The evidence was insufficient to prove that the firearm was within 1,000 feet of a school.**

Even if the statute is not void for vagueness, the government failed to prove that Nieves-Castaño possessed the rifle within a school zone. The trial evidence established only that the apartment was in a housing project; no witness testified to the distance to any school, nor was any map or survey introduced. *United States v. Nelson*, 277 F.3d 164, 175 (2d Cir. 2002) (government must prove location element beyond a reasonable doubt). Because the jury could only speculate about the proximity, the conviction on this count must also be reversed. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319, 99 S.Ct. 2781, 61 L.Ed.2d 560 (1979).

**IV. The district court’s jury instruction on the machine-gun count erroneously omitted the mens rea requirement.**

The court instructed the jury that the government must prove that the defendant “knowingly possessed” a firearm, but did not instruct that she must know it was a machine gun. *Staples* requires such an instruction. *See United States v. Rogers*, 94 F.3d 1519, 1524 (11th Cir. 1996) (reversing for failure to instruct on knowledge of automatic-fire capability). The error was not harmless because the evidence of knowledge was weak. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734, 113 S.Ct. 1770, 123 L.Ed.2d 508 (1993).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the convictions should be reversed.
