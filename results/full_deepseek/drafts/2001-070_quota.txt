**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment does not categorically forbid the execution of mentally retarded persons. This Court squarely held in *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302, 340 (1989), that mental retardation is a mitigating factor to be weighed by the sentencer, not a per se bar to capital punishment. That holding remains sound, and the Court should adhere to it.

**I. The Evolving Standards of Decency Do Not Support a Categorical Ban**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment is measured by “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion). The primary evidence of those standards is the legislation enacted by state legislatures. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 331. At the time of *Penry*, only two states—Georgia and Maryland—barred execution of the mentally retarded. *Id.* at 334. By the time of petitioner’s sentencing, only a handful of additional states had joined them. This modest legislative movement falls far short of the “national consensus” required to overturn this Court’s precedent. *See Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 370-71 (1989) (plurality opinion) (rejecting a categorical bar on juvenile executions where only 15 states prohibited the practice). The federal government, which has enacted no such prohibition, further confirms the absence of consensus. *See id.* at 373-74.

Moreover, the practice of executing mentally retarded persons is not “cruel” in the constitutional sense. The death penalty itself is not per se unconstitutional. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 187 (1976) (plurality opinion). A punishment is cruel only if it is “excessive” in relation to the crime or the offender’s culpability. *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977) (plurality opinion). Mental retardation does not automatically reduce culpability to a level that renders death disproportionate. As *Penry* recognized, mental retardation is a “spectrum” condition; some individuals with mild retardation possess sufficient cognitive capacity to be fully responsible for their actions. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 338-39. A categorical exemption would sweep too broadly, exempting those whose retardation does not significantly impair their moral culpability.

**II. Individualized Sentencing, Not Categorical Rules, Is the Appropriate Framework**

The Eighth Amendment requires that capital sentencing be “individualized,” allowing the sentencer to consider all relevant mitigating evidence. *Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 604 (1978) (plurality opinion). *Penry* itself held that the sentencer must be able to consider and give effect to evidence of mental retardation as a mitigating factor. *Penry*, 492 U.S. at 328. That requirement is fully satisfied by Virginia’s sentencing scheme, which permits the jury to weigh any mitigating circumstance. *See* Va. Code Ann. § 19.2-264.4(B). The jury in petitioner’s case heard expert testimony regarding his mental retardation and nonetheless concluded that death was the appropriate sentence. This Court should not second-guess that factual determination.

A categorical bar would also undermine the principle of federalism and respect for state legislative judgments. *See McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279, 305-06 (1987) (“[T]he Constitution does not require that a State’s capital punishment scheme be ‘perfect’ in the sense that it treats all similarly situated defendants alike.”). States are better positioned to assess the nuances of mental retardation and to craft appropriate procedures. *See Thompson v. Oklahoma*, 487 U.S. 815, 822 n.4 (1988) (plurality opinion) (noting that “the Court has recognized that the States serve as laboratories for social experimentation”). The fact that some states have chosen to ban the practice while others have not reflects a healthy democratic debate, not a constitutional imperative.

**III. *Penry* Remains Good Law and Should Not Be Overruled**

Stare decisis counsels against overturning *Penry* absent a “special justification.” *See Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 965 (1991) (opinion of Scalia, J.). Petitioner offers no such justification. The legislative trend since *Penry* is modest and does not demonstrate that the Court’s prior decision was “unworkable” or “badly reasoned.” *Id.* at 965. To the contrary, *Penry* provides a flexible, case-by-case approach that respects both the severity of the death penalty and the need for individualized sentencing. *See Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 410 (1986) (holding that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the insane, but only because insanity renders the defendant unaware of the punishment’s meaning—a distinct rationale not applicable to mental retardation).

Finally, any concerns about the reliability of capital sentencing for mentally retarded defendants are adequately addressed by existing procedural safeguards, including the right to present mitigating evidence and the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *See Walton v. Arizona*, 497 U.S. 639, 650 (1990) (upholding state’s burden of proof scheme), overruled on other grounds by *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584 (2002). The Constitution does not require a categorical exemption.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
