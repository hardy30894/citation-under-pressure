# ARGUMENT

## I. THE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING PROVISION, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), IS A CONSTITUTIONAL DELEGATION OF AUTHORITY TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL

Article I, Section 1 of the Constitution vests "[a]ll legislative Powers" in Congress, and this Court has long recognized that Congress may not delegate that power to another branch. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 371-72 (1989) (quoting *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892)). But the nondelegation doctrine does not prevent Congress from enlisting the assistance of the other branches. *Id.* at 372. Congress satisfies Article I so long as it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [act] is directed to conform." *Id.* (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)).

This Court has upheld delegations far broader than the one at issue here. In *Mistretta*, the Court sustained Congress's grant of authority to the Sentencing Commission to promulgate binding Guidelines, emphasizing that "Congress simply cannot do its job absent an ability to delegate power under broad general directives." 488 U.S. at 372. Similarly, in *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414 (1944), the Court upheld a delegation authorizing price controls that were "generally fair and equitable," recognizing that Congress may rely on "the necessities of the total war effort" to justify broad delegations addressing rapidly changing conditions. *Id.* at 423-24. Section 811(h) is considerably more constrained than either of these approved delegations.

Congress provided the Attorney General with specific, substantive criteria to guide temporary scheduling decisions. The statute limits the Attorney General's discretion to three enumerated factors: the drug's "history and current pattern of abuse," the "scope, duration, and significance of abuse," and "what, if any, risk there is to the public health." 21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6), incorporated by § 811(h)(3). The Attorney General may act only when necessary "to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety." *Id.* § 811(h)(1). This standard is materially indistinguishable from those this Court has repeatedly approved. *See Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947) (upholding delegation where "standards prescribed for [the delegate's] use are found in the customs and practices" of the regulated field).

Congress further cabined the delegation by imposing procedural safeguards: the Attorney General must publish notice in the Federal Register and consider comments from the Secretary of Health and Human Services. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(4). And critically, the delegated power is temporally limited—an order may remain in effect for only one year, extendable by six months. *Id.* § 811(h)(2). This built-in expiration distinguishes § 811(h) from the unconstrained, indefinite delegations struck down in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), and *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935)—the only two delegations this Court has ever invalidated.

Petitioners' reliance on those Depression-era cases is misplaced. In *Panama Refining*, the statute "did not declare any policy" and established "no criterion to govern the President's course." 293 U.S. at 415, 430. In *Schechter Poultry*, the President could approve virtually any industry-proposed code with no guiding standard beyond the vague goal of "fair competition." 295 U.S. at 541-42. Section 811(h), by contrast, channels the Attorney General's discretion through enumerated statutory factors, mandatory consultation requirements, and a firm temporal limit. That the delegated authority here relates to criminal penalties does not demand a more exacting test. This Court has never held that the "intelligible principle" standard varies based on the consequences of the delegated authority; *Mistretta* itself sustained a delegation directly affecting criminal sentences. 488 U.S. at 371-79.

Nor does the limitation on judicial review in § 811(h)(6) alter the constitutional analysis. That provision bars only review of the *particular scheduling decision*—not review of the delegation's facial constitutionality. As the Third Circuit correctly recognized, "[e]very court that has considered whether section 811(h)(6) precludes consideration of the constitutionality of the delegation... has explicitly or implicitly concluded that constitutional review is not precluded." Pet. App. 12a (citing *United States v. Emerson*, 846 F.2d 541, 544 (9th Cir. 1988); *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426 (10th Cir. 1987)). This Court's own precedent counsels that statutory preclusion of review must be shown by "clear and convincing" evidence, particularly where constitutional questions are at stake. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977). Nothing in § 811(h)(6) evinces intent to insulate the statute from facial constitutional challenge, and the provision's narrow focus on individual scheduling orders in no way undermines the intelligible principles Congress supplied to constrain the delegated authority itself.

## II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL WAS AUTHORIZED TO SUBDELEGATE TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA

Congress expressly authorized subdelegation. Title 21 U.S.C. § 871(a) provides that "[t]he Attorney General may delegate any of his functions under this subchapter to any officer or employee of the Department of Justice." This unambiguous statutory text forecloses petitioners' claim that subdelegation to the DEA was unauthorized.

The Attorney General's subdelegation followed the precise mechanism Congress contemplated. Pursuant to § 871(a), the Attorney General promulgated 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b), assigning to the DEA Administrator the "[f]unctions vested in the Attorney General by the Comprehensive Drug Abuse Prevention and Control Act of 1970, as amended." This regulation, properly promulgated through notice-and-comment rulemaking, effectuated exactly the kind of delegation Congress authorized when it enacted § 871(a). There is no requirement that Congress specify by name which subordinate officer must exercise delegated authority; it is sufficient that Congress authorized the Attorney General to select the appropriate delegate from within the Department of Justice, and the DEA—the agency Congress itself has recognized as the primary enforcer of federal drug laws—is a paradigmatic example of such a delegate.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
