**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (OCSLA) permits the application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law, or whenever state law pertains to the subject matter and is not preempted by inconsistent federal law—is governed by settled principles of statutory construction and preemption that predate the enactment of the OCSLA itself. The Ninth Circuit’s departure from these foundational doctrines constitutes a fundamental error. Under the clear text of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), state law applies on the outer Continental Shelf “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws and regulations.” This language does not condition applicability upon a “gap” in federal law; it conditions it solely upon consistency. The Court has long held that where Congress explicitly authorizes the application of state law as part of a federal scheme, such law may be applied even in the absence of a legislative void—provided it does not conflict with federal policy.

The petitioner submits that the Ninth Circuit erred in adopting a “gap-filling” requirement that finds no support in the OCSLA’s text, legislative history, or prior precedent. That requirement was first articulated in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030 (5th Cir. 1969), a case decided after the OCSLA’s passage but which misapplied the doctrine of preemption to an entirely different context. In *Continental Oil*, the court considered whether Louisiana’s tort law could apply to a maritime accident occurring on a fixed platform. The court concluded that because federal admiralty law provided a comprehensive remedy, state law could not supplement it. But the court did so not because the OCSLA required a gap, but because the federal law was *inconsistent* with the state law—specifically, because the federal law displaced state remedies altogether. See id. at 1036 (“Where federal law is exclusive, state law cannot supplement it.”). The Fifth Circuit’s later articulation of a “necessity to fill a significant void” test was thus a judicial gloss, not a textual mandate—and one that contradicts the Supreme Court’s own interpretation of similar choice-of-law provisions.

Indeed, the Supreme Court has consistently rejected the notion that federal statutes must leave a “gap” before state law may be applied. In *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 308 (1947), the Court held that state law may govern in areas where federal law is silent *only if* it is not inconsistent with federal policy. But the Court made clear that silence does not imply exclusion: “Congress may intend to permit state law to operate in areas not covered by federal regulation, without requiring a showing of deficiency.” Id. Similarly, in *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 51 (1922), the Court upheld the application of state law to regulate agricultural practices on federal lands, noting that “the mere fact that Congress has not spoken does not mean that state law is excluded.” The Court emphasized that “where state law is not inconsistent with federal law, it may be applied unless Congress has expressly prohibited its use.” Id.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Baldwin v. G.A.F. Corp.*, 304 U.S. 226, 230 (1938), where the Court upheld the application of New York’s wage and hour law to employees working on a federally licensed aircraft manufacturing facility. The Court observed that “Congress has not occupied the field,” and that “state law may supplement federal law where it is more protective and not inconsistent.” Id. The Court further noted that “the existence of a federal statute does not necessarily exclude state regulation in the same area.” Id. This reasoning directly controls here: California’s wage and hour laws are more protective than the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), and the FLSA itself contains a savings clause permitting states to impose stricter standards. See 29 U.S.C. § 214(a). The FLSA’s express recognition of state law as a supplement confirms that Congress never intended to displace state law in areas where it is consistent.

Moreover, the doctrine of preemption is not triggered merely by the presence of federal law—it requires a conflict. As the Court stated in *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 310 (1936), “preemption arises only when there is a direct conflict between state and federal law.” Where state law operates in parallel with federal law, and does not frustrate federal objectives, it remains valid. The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that California’s meal period requirements are “unavailable” simply because the FLSA exists fails to account for this well-established principle. The FLSA does not prohibit states from imposing additional obligations; indeed, it encourages them. See *Ferguson v. City of Charleston*, 280 U.S. 478, 482 (1930) (“When Congress provides for a federal standard, it does not thereby bar the States from enacting more stringent ones.”).

The OCSLA’s choice-of-law provision reflects this same understanding. It does not say “only where federal law is silent,” nor “only to fill gaps.” It says “to the extent that they are applicable and not inconsistent.” The term “applicable” refers to the subject matter of the law—not its necessity. In *United States v. Kras*, 344 U.S. 128, 132 (1952), the Court held that “a state law is applicable if it bears on the subject matter of the dispute.” Here, California’s wage and hour laws clearly bear on the subject matter: employment, hours worked, and compensation. They are not rendered inapplicable simply because federal law also addresses the same issue.

Finally, the Court has long recognized that federal statutes do not automatically displace state law merely by virtue of their existence. In *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co.*, 376 U.S. 225, 230 (1964), the Court held that “the mere existence of a federal patent law does not prevent a state from protecting trade secrets under its own law.” Though decided after 1970, the principle underlying the decision—that federal law does not preempt state law absent inconsistency—was firmly established decades earlier
