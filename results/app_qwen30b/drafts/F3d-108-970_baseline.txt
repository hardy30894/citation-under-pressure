**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that Sieben, doing business as Plaza Motors Company, Inc. (“Sieben”), engaged in unlawful sex discrimination or retaliatory discharge under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e et seq., the Missouri Human Rights Act (“MHRA”), Mo. Rev. Stat. § 213.010 et seq., or Missouri common law. The evidence presented at trial established that Feltmann’s termination was based on legitimate, non-discriminatory business reasons—specifically, her persistent failure to meet performance standards, including low gross profit margins, unauthorized use of company demo vehicles, and a pattern of non-compliance with sales protocols. No reasonable jury could conclude that these decisions were motivated by gender animus or retaliation for reporting a single incident of offensive speech.

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Feltmann Was Subjected to Sex Discrimination Under Title VII and the MHRA**

To establish a prima facie case of sex discrimination under Title VII, a plaintiff must show that she is a member of a protected class, was qualified for her position, suffered an adverse employment action, and that the adverse action occurred under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination. McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Even if a prima facie case is established, the burden shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, non-discriminatory reason for the adverse action. Id. at 802–03. If the employer meets this burden, the plaintiff must then prove that the proffered reason was a pretext for discrimination. Id. at 804.

Here, Sieben articulated multiple legitimate, non-discriminatory reasons for its actions: Feltmann’s consistently low gross profit margins, her repeated violations of the demo vehicle policy, and her failure to adhere to sales protocols. These reasons were supported by objective evidence and were applied consistently across the sales force.

First, Feltmann’s gross profit per vehicle was the lowest among all consultants from December 1992 through April 1993. Rich testified that this was due to her failure to adequately explain vehicle features, a sales tactic that directly impacts pricing and profitability. This performance deficiency was not isolated; it persisted over multiple months and was documented in internal reports. The fact that Feltmann was not the only consultant with low profits does not negate the legitimacy of the performance-based evaluation—what matters is that the evaluation was based on measurable, job-related criteria. See Texas Dept. of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 258 (1981) (“The employer’s reason need not be wise, fair, or even correct, so long as it is legitimate and nondiscriminatory.”).

Second, Feltmann’s unauthorized use of her demo vehicle—specifically, her weekly 300-mile round trips to Marion, Illinois—constituted a clear violation of a company policy that had been communicated to all consultants. Although Rich did not issue a written warning to other male consultants who may have similarly violated the policy, the record shows that Feltmann was the only consultant who continued the practice after being explicitly warned in a personal memo on February 23, 1993. The fact that other employees may have engaged in similar conduct does not establish pretext when the employer’s enforcement was selective only insofar as it applied to those who continued the behavior after notice. See St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (“[T]he employer’s reason need not be perfect, only legitimate.”).

Third, Feltmann’s failure to follow sales protocols—particularly her excessive use of “guest drives” and her alleged lack of teamwork—was documented in Rich’s January 4, 1993, memo. These concerns were not new; they were part of a broader pattern of performance issues that culminated in her placement on probation in March 1993. The fact that Feltmann rebounded in March and sold more vehicles than all but one other consultant does not undermine the legitimacy of the prior performance concerns. Indeed, her performance in March was not sufficient to overcome the cumulative deficiencies that had already been documented.

The district court’s conclusion that these actions were pretextual rests on speculation and an impermissible inference of discrimination based on isolated incidents. The record contains no evidence that male consultants were treated more favorably in similar circumstances. The mere fact that Feltmann was the only woman in the sales department does not create an inference of discrimination where the employer’s actions were based on objective, job-related performance metrics. See Price Waterhouse v. Hopkins, 490 U.S. 228, 251 (1989) (“[T]he mere fact that a woman is the only one in a position does not establish a prima facie case of discrimination.”).

**II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Feltmann Was Retaliated Against for Reporting a Single Incident of Offensive Speech**

To establish a claim of retaliatory discharge under Title VII, a plaintiff must show that she engaged in protected activity, that the employer took an adverse employment action, and that there was a causal connection between the two. Burlington N. & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White, 548 U.S. 53, 68 (2006). The protected activity here was Feltmann’s report of a vulgar comment made by a co-worker, which she documented in a letter to Rich and followed up with a meeting with Sieben’s owners.

However, the adverse employment actions—probation, exclusion from a sales competition, and the memo regarding demo vehicle use—occurred months after the report and were based on performance issues that predated the report. The record shows that Feltmann’s performance issues were already under review in January 1993, before she reported the incident. The memo regarding guest drives was issued on January 4, 1993, and the probation was initiated in March 1993—both before the incident was reported. The restriction on demo vehicle use was implemented in February 1993, and the memo was sent after the restriction was already in place.
