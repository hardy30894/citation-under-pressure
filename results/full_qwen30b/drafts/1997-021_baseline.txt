**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Board’s insistence that an employer may only conduct a poll of employees to test union support if it has “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status renders the poll an ineffective and impractical tool—precisely the result the Board itself has acknowledged is “potentially disruptive and unsettling.” *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061 (1989). The Board’s standard, however, is not merely impractical; it is irrational and inconsistent with the Act’s purpose of promoting stable collective bargaining relationships.

The Board’s rule creates a paradox: an employer may not poll employees unless it already has sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition, yet the very purpose of a poll is to *obtain* such evidence. As the Board concedes, polling is “potentially disruptive,” *id.* at 1061, and thus should be permitted only in limited circumstances. But if the employer must already possess the evidence it seeks to confirm through polling, the poll becomes superfluous. The Board’s standard thus undermines its own rationale for permitting polling at all.

The courts of appeals that rejected the Board’s standard did so not out of judicial overreach, but out of recognition that the Board’s rule renders polling useless. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits held that an employer may conduct a poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence falls short of justifying withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc.*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc.*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). This standard is rational: it allows employers to test union support in good faith, without risking an unfair labor practice, while preserving the union’s presumption of majority status. It balances the employer’s interest in avoiding bargaining with a minority union—prohibited under § 8(a)(2)—with the statutory goal of stable bargaining relationships.

The Board’s insistence on a higher standard for polling than for withdrawal of recognition is arbitrary. If an employer may withdraw recognition based on reasonable doubt, why may it not first test that doubt through a poll? The Board’s own reasoning in *Texas Petrochemicals* reveals the inconsistency: it acknowledges that polling “can serve an important role” in providing “more certain, precise information” than mere doubt. *Id.* at 1063. Yet it refuses to allow polling unless the employer already has that certainty. This is not a rational policy—it is a self-defeating one.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Board’s expertise in labor policy warrants deference. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 42, 107 S.Ct. 2225, 2235 (1987). But deference does not extend to rules that are “arbitrary and capricious,” *Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 463 U.S. 29, 43, 103 S.Ct. 2856, 2867 (1983), or that “fail to advance the purposes of the statute,” *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 786, 110 S.Ct. 1542, 1549 (1990). The Board’s standard fails both tests. It does not advance the Act’s goal of stable bargaining relationships; it undermines them by discouraging employers from using a tool that could prevent disputes. It is arbitrary to allow an employer to withdraw recognition based on reasonable doubt, but to bar it from polling to confirm that doubt.

II. THE BOARD’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective indications” of a loss of union support rests on a selective and erroneous interpretation of the record. The ALJ and the Board discounted the statements of seven employees who, in the months before the poll, expressed opposition to the union. The Board’s refusal to count these statements is not supported by substantial evidence.

First, the Board excluded three employees—Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack—because they were not in the bargaining unit on January 25, 1991, the date of Allentown Mack’s letter refusing recognition. But the Board’s use of that date as a cutoff is arbitrary. The company’s decision to conduct the poll was made *after* January 25, and the employees’ statements were made *before* the sale. The relevant inquiry is whether the employer had a good-faith basis for doubt *at the time it decided to poll*, not at the time it sent a letter. The Board’s rigid adherence to a single date ignores the temporal context of the employer’s decision-making.

Second, the Board rejected the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh as “equivocal” and “not indicative of opposition to union representation.” But the ALJ’s findings were not supported by substantial evidence. Ridgick’s statement—“as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union”—was made during a job interview after being told the new company would be non-union. The Board’s own precedent holds that such statements are not necessarily insincere. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978).
