# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT'S EXCLUSION OF LAMB'S CHAPEL BASED ON THE RELIGIOUS PERSPECTIVE OF ITS SPEECH VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT REGARDLESS OF THE FORUM'S CLASSIFICATION

### A. Even in a Limited Public Forum, the District May Not Discriminate on the Basis of Viewpoint

This Court has long recognized that once government opens property for expressive use by the public, it may not thereafter discriminate among speakers based on viewpoint, even though it retains discretion to limit the forum by subject matter. In *Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n*, 460 U.S. 37 (1983), this Court explained that a limited or designated public forum is subject to the same First Amendment protections as a traditional public forum for as long as the government keeps it open, and that content-based exclusions must be narrowly drawn to serve a compelling state interest. *Id.* at 45-46.

The Second Circuit correctly recognized that Center Moriches School District's facilities constitute a "limited public forum" opened to a wide range of community groups for social, civic, and recreational purposes. Pet. App. 15a. That characterization is fatal to the District's position, not supportive of it. Once a forum is created for the discussion of certain subjects—here, community and civic programs including discussions of family values, child-rearing, and educational topics—the government cannot selectively exclude speakers who wish to address those very subjects from a religious perspective while permitting secular speakers to address the same subjects. This is precisely the discrimination this Court condemned in *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788 (1985), where the Court emphasized that "the government violates the First Amendment when it denies access to a speaker solely to suppress the point of view he espouses." *Id.* at 806.

The record demonstrates that the District permitted use of its facilities for lectures on parapsychology, transpersonal psychology, and "physics and metaphysics"—the Jerry Huck lecture series—and for musical performances, yet steadfastly refused to permit Lamb's Chapel to show a film series addressing family values and child-rearing from a religious perspective. Pet. App. 22a-23a. The Huck lectures were permitted even though, as the court below acknowledged, "some incidental reference to religious matters apparently was made in the lectures." Pet. App. 23a. The District thus permitted discussion of transcendental and quasi-religious topics so long as they were not explicitly framed as religious, while categorically excluding Lamb's Chapel's film series because it was, in the District's words, "church related." Pet. App. 8a. This is viewpoint discrimination in its starkest form: the subject matter—family values and child-rearing—was welcome in the forum, but the religious viewpoint on that subject was not.

### B. The District's Rule Excluding All "Religious Purposes" Is Not Viewpoint-Neutral

The court of appeals erred in treating the exclusion of religious speech as "viewpoint-neutral" merely because the rule applies to all religious groups equally. Pet. App. 24a. This Court rejected an identical argument in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263 (1981). There, a state university opened its facilities to registered student groups but excluded a religious student group from using the same facilities for worship and religious discussion. This Court held that the exclusion constituted content discrimination subject to strict scrutiny, explaining that "the University's exclusionary policy discriminates against student groups and speakers based on their desire to use a generally open forum to engage in religious worship and discussion" and that such discrimination "is, of course, content-based." *Id.* at 269. The Court further held that an interest in achieving greater separation of church and state than is constitutionally required is not a compelling state interest sufficient to justify content-based discrimination in a public forum. *Id.* at 270-71.

The Second Circuit attempted to distinguish *Widmar* on the ground that it involved students rather than outside community groups. Pet. App. 25a. But nothing in *Widmar*'s reasoning turns on the status of the speaker seeking access; it turns on the nature of the forum and the character of the exclusion. The District here, like the university in *Widmar*, opened its property to a broad range of community organizations for expressive activities and then selectively closed the forum only to those wishing to speak from a religious viewpoint. That the speakers in *Widmar* were students and the speakers here are church members and pastors is a distinction without constitutional significance; the forum analysis focuses on the character of the property and the government's own practice in opening it, not on the identity of those seeking access. See *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 47 (forum analysis focuses on "the nature of the forum and the conflicting interests involved").

### C. Denying Access to Show a Religious-Perspective Film on Family Values Cannot Be Justified by Any Compelling Interest

Even if some content-based restriction were permissible in this forum, exclusion here cannot survive scrutiny. The District's asserted justification—avoiding Establishment Clause entanglement—is untenable. This Court has repeatedly held that permitting private religious speech on government property, on the same terms as other private speech, does not violate the Establishment Clause because such access reflects governmental neutrality rather than endorsement. In *Board of Education of Westside Community Schools v. Mergens*, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), this Court recognized that "there is a crucial difference between government speech endorsing religion, which the Establishment Clause forbids, and private speech endorsing religion, which the Free Speech and Free Exercise Clauses protect." *Id.* at 250. Allowing Lamb's Chapel to show its film series after school hours, on the same terms available to the Southern Harmonizers or the Jerry Huck lecture series, would not signal District endorsement of religion; it would simply reflect the evenhanded application of a public forum open to private speech on family and social issues.

The film series at issue—Dr. James Dobson's "Turn Your Heart Toward Home"—addressed child-rearing and family values, subjects the District's forum was already open to discuss. Pet. App. 6a-7a. The exclusion was based solely on the fact that the presentation would approach those secular subjects from a religious point of view. Under this Court's forum doctrine, such viewpoint discrimination is impermissible regardless of forum classification. See *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46 ("the State may not exclude speech where its distinction is not reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum, nor may it discriminate against speech on the basis of ... viewpoint").

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
