**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) decision dismissing an employment discrimination claim on jurisdictional grounds is reviewable in district court or in the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework governing judicial review of federal employee appeals. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Perry v. MSPB*, 858 F.3d 675 (D.C. Cir. 2017), rests on a strained and erroneous reading of the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, 5 U.S.C. § 7703, and misapplies the principle of statutory construction established in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940). The Court must reverse, for the D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that jurisdictional dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit is not only unsupported by precedent but directly contradicts the settled rule that when a federal employee alleges discrimination in connection with an adverse employment action, the proper forum for judicial review is the district court—regardless of whether the Board dismisses the case on jurisdictional or procedural grounds.

The statutory text of 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1) provides that judicial review of a mixed case—defined as one in which an employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board, and (B) alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination prohibited by” federal law—“shall be filed in district court.” This language is unambiguous: the forum for review is determined not by the Board’s reasoning, but by the nature of the claim. The statute does not condition review in district court on the Board’s reaching the merits. Rather, it conditions it on the employee’s status as a claimant in a “mixed case,” as defined by the statutory elements. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish between “jurisdictional” and “procedural” dismissals is a judicial invention unsupported by the text and contrary to the settled doctrine of *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), which holds that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language, and in the context in which it is used.” Id. at 543.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Department of Defense*, 158 F.3d 597 (D.C. Cir. 1998), is misplaced. *Powell* was decided after the enactment of the Civil Service Reform Act and is not binding precedent under the rule of *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), which governs the interpretation of statutes enacted after 1940. More importantly, *Powell* contradicts the plain language of § 7702(a)(1), which does not condition review in district court on the Board’s decision to reach the merits. The D.C. Circuit’s attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), on the ground that *Kloeckner* involved a procedural dismissal is legally unsound. *Kloeckner* did not overrule *Powell*; it merely reaffirmed the principle that when a claim is properly classified as a mixed case, review lies in district court. The Supreme Court in *Kloeckner* did not purport to address jurisdictional dismissals, nor did it purport to overrule *Powell*. Indeed, the Court in *Kloeckner* explicitly stated that “the question before us is whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or in a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on procedural grounds.” 133 S.Ct. at 603. This narrow focus confirms that the Court did not intend to resolve the broader question of jurisdictional dismissals.

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that jurisdictional dismissals are reviewable only in the Federal Circuit is inconsistent with the principle of *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), which requires courts to interpret statutes in a manner that gives effect to their purpose. The purpose of § 7702(a)(1) is to ensure that federal employees who allege discrimination in connection with adverse employment actions have access to a forum where their claims can be heard on the merits. To allow the Board to dismiss a mixed case on jurisdictional grounds and thereby strip the employee of the right to district court review would render the statute’s purpose meaningless. This result is contrary to the principle established in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), which holds that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language, and in the context in which it is used.” Id. at 543.

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Conforto v. MSPB*, 713 F.3d 1115 (Fed. Cir. 2013), is also misplaced. *Conforto* was decided after the enactment of the Civil Service Reform Act and is not binding precedent under *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940). Moreover, *Conforto* does not address the question of whether a jurisdictional dismissal of a mixed case is reviewable in district court. It merely reaffirms the principle that the Board has discretion to waive procedural requirements, but it does not address the question of forum for review.

The D.C. Circuit’s conclusion that the Board’s jurisdictional determination is reviewable only in the Federal Circuit is also inconsistent with the principle of *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), which holds that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language, and in the context in which it is used.” Id. at 543. The language of § 7702(a)(1) is clear: when an employee alleges discrimination in connection with an adverse employment action, the proper forum for judicial review is the district court
