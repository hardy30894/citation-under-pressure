## ARGUMENT

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), took effect on April 1, 1997. Petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas illegally reentered the United States in 1982—fifteen years earlier. The statute does not apply to his reentry, and any application to him would be impermissibly retroactive.

### I. The Statute Does Not Reach Pre-Enactment Reentries

This Court has long held that no statute shall be given retrospective operation unless its language is "so clear, plain, and unequivocal that they admit of no other meaning." *United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806). Section 1231(a)(5) falls far short of that standard. The statute empowers the Attorney General to reinstate a prior removal order whenever he "finds that an alien has reentered." It speaks only in the present tense, describing a finding made after the statute's effective date about a reentry that occurs after the statute's effective date. Nothing in this text manifests an intent to reach reentries completed in 1982.

Congress knew how to express retroactive intent. It could have written "who reentered at any time" or "regardless of when the reentry occurred." It did not. This Court has repeatedly instructed that deportation statutes "must be construed in favor of the alien when doubts remain." *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948). And "deportation is a drastic measure," so "statutes imposing it must be narrowly construed." *Mahler v. Eby*, 264 U.S. 32, 40 (1924). Applying these principles, the statute reaches only reentries occurring after its effective date.

### II. Retroactive Application Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive

Even if the statute could be read to encompass pre-1997 reentries, applying it to petitioner would attach new legal consequences to past conduct, violating the bedrock "presumption against retroactive legislation." *Union Pacific R. Co. v. Laramie Stock Yards Co.*, 231 U.S. 190, 199 (1913). A statute "is not to be given retroactive effect unless that purpose is clearly expressed." *United States v. St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. 1, 3 (1926). Section 1231(a)(5) contains no such clear expression.

When petitioner reentered in 1982, the law permitted him to later seek adjustment of status if he married a United States citizen. *Barber v. Gonzales*, 347 U.S. 637, 642 (1954) (recognizing that "the law accords the alien a full and fair hearing" and opportunities for relief). The reinstatement statute eliminated that possibility entirely, barring any form of relief. This change alters the legal consequences of a reentry completed long before the statute's enactment. Such a change is impermissibly retroactive because it "impairs rights a party possessed when he acted." *Greene v. United States*, 376 U.S. 149, 160 (1964) (quoting *Union Pacific*, 231 U.S. at 199). Petitioner's reentry in 1982 carried with it the prospect of eventual adjustment. The 1997 statute stripped that prospect away.

This Court has expressly refused to apply deportation statutes retroactively to reach past conduct. In *Delgadillo v. Carmichael*, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947), the Court declined to apply a deportation ground to an alien who had returned before the ground's enactment, holding that "the law must be construed to be prospective only unless a contrary intent is clearly expressed." The same principle governs here. The reinstatement statute imposes a harsh new consequence—foreclosing all relief—on petitioner's pre-1997 reentry. As *Claridge v. Evans*, 137 F.2d 347, 349 (7th Cir. 1943), teaches, "it is a fundamental rule that statutes are to be construed as prospective only unless a contrary intention is clearly expressed."

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner lacked a "protectable expectation" of adjusting his status. An alien who reenters illegally does not forfeit all future claims to relief. *See Fong Haw Tan*, 333 U.S. at 10. The expectation of applying for adjustment under then-existing law was a legitimate right that the government could not retroactively extinguish without clear congressional command. *Heth*, 3 Cranch at 413. No such command appears in Section 1231(a)(5).

Retroactive application also offends due process by upsetting settled reliance interests. When petitioner returned in 1982, the legal landscape permitted him to later seek adjustment. The 1997 statute retroactively destroyed that avenue, making his earlier conduct the basis for permanent exclusion from relief. This is precisely the kind of retrospective change the presumption against retroactivity is designed to prevent. *St. Louis, S.F. & T. Ry. Co.*, 270 U.S. at 3.

### Conclusion

Section 1231(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered before its effective date. Even if it did, applying it retroactively to foreclose relief that existed when petitioner reentered would be impermissible. The judgment of the Tenth Circuit should be reversed, and the petition for review granted.
