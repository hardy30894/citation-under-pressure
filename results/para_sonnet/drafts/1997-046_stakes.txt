# ARGUMENT

## I. RESPONDENT HAS NO LIFE OR LIBERTY INTEREST PROTECTED BY THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE IN OHIO'S CLEMENCY PROCEDURE

Clemency is not an adjudication of guilt or innocence; it is "an act of grace." *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). This Court has consistently held that pardons, commutations, and other forms of clemency lie beyond the reach of due process scrutiny because they are matters of executive grace rather than entitlement. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court squarely rejected the claim that a prisoner possesses a constitutionally protected liberty interest in a favorable commutation decision, holding that "no matter how citizens' hopes are or are not realized by the exercise of the Governor's pardoning power, a decision to commute is simply an 'act of grace.'" *Id.* at 464. As the Court explained, "the pardoning power is committed, as is our pardoning power, to the discretion of the highest state officials," and "the ordinary elements of procedural due process" do not attach. *Id.* at 464–65. This is because "pardon and commutation decisions have not traditionally been the business of courts; as such, they are rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review." *Id.* at 464 (citing *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976)).

The Sixth Circuit majority attempted to distinguish *Dumschat* by characterizing Ohio's clemency scheme as an "integral part" of the State's adjudicatory process, relying on *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), and *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956). That reasoning cannot be reconciled with *Dumschat* itself. As Judge Nelson correctly recognized in dissent below, "[b]oth strands of due process jurisprudence were unquestionably implicated by the facts presented in *Dumschat*," yet the Court still found no protected interest. Woodard's theory would require this Court to adopt the very position advanced by Justice Stevens in dissent in *Dumschat*—that the Due Process Clause applies to every step of the sentencing process, including clemency—a position the majority rejected. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 471 (Stevens, J., dissenting).

*Evitts* is inapposite. That case addressed only whether a State that creates a first appeal as of right must afford effective assistance of counsel on that appeal. 469 U.S. at 393. Clemency, unlike a statutory appeal, is not a component of the adjudicatory system determining guilt or the validity of a sentence; it operates entirely outside and after that system has run its course. Indeed, even habeas corpus proceedings—far more closely tied to the "adjudicative system" than executive clemency—do not carry a constitutional right to counsel. *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 394 (citing *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600 (1974)). If habeas does not trigger such protections, clemency, which is discretionary, non-judicial, and historically unreviewable, cannot either.

Nor can Ohio's procedural regulations, which merely describe *how* the Adult Parole Authority processes clemency applications, create a substantive liberty interest. This Court has cautioned against permitting "state law procedural devices" to transform discretionary grace into a constitutionally protected entitlement. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465. As Judge Nelson observed, expanding due process into clemency threatens to make "the camel's nose under the tent" a precursor to full constitutionalization of an area that has "not traditionally been the business of courts." *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. This Court's later decision in *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472 (1995), reinforces that state procedural regulations governing discretionary matters do not of their own force create liberty interests, and cautioned against interpretations that "create disincentives for States to codify" procedures for fear of judicializing them. *Id.* at 482.

Because clemency is not part of the criminal adjudicatory process but a wholly discretionary act of executive grace, Respondent has no life or liberty interest triggering due process protection at any stage of Ohio's clemency proceeding.

## II. THE OPTION OF A VOLUNTARY CLEMENCY INTERVIEW DOES NOT VIOLATE THE FIFTH AMENDMENT

Respondent's Fifth Amendment claim fails because Ohio's regulation does not compel him to say anything. The clemency interview is, by its own terms, available only "[i]f the prisoner requests" it, and the prisoner may decline to answer any question posed. Nothing in the regulation authorizes the Adult Parole Authority to compel an answer or to penalize silence with an adverse clemency decision.

This Court's decision in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976), controls. There, the Court held that it would be premature for a federal court to fashion relief against a hypothetical risk of self-incrimination arising from a prison disciplinary proceeding where no adverse use of compelled testimony had yet occurred. 425 U.S. at 324. The same logic applies with even greater force here: Respondent has not requested an interview, has not answered any question, and has not been penalized for silence. Any claim that his statements might someday be used against him in a future, unspecified proceeding is, in the words of *Baxter*, "premature." *Id.*

Respondent's own complaint concedes that he "does not contemplate that this action will have any effect on his state convictions and/or sentences." There is, therefore, no live controversy concerning compelled self-incrimination—only a speculative chain of contingencies. The Fifth Amendment protects against compulsion, not against the mere opportunity to speak voluntarily. *See Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 427 (1984) (privilege applies only where a person is "compelled" to give testimony). Ohio's scheme provides an option, not a mandate, and thus works no compulsion at all.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit should be reversed insofar as it recognized any due process interest in clemency proceedings and remanded for further Fifth Amendment findings.
