**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATUTE’S DISCLOSURE REQUIREMENTS ARE OVERBROAD AND UNCONSTITUTIONALLY VAGUE UNDER THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

The Florida statute’s disclosure provisions sweep far too broadly, chilling protected First Amendment activity without justification. *NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson*, 357 U.S. 449, 462 (1958) (holding that compelled disclosure of an organization’s membership lists was unconstitutional where it would “induce members to withdraw from the Association” and “deter future members from joining”). The statute requires lobbyists to disclose not only their own expenditures but also those made by their principals, *see* Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a), effectively forcing disclosure of expenditures by third parties with no direct connection to the lobbying effort. This goes beyond what this Court has permitted in prior cases. *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 245 (1957) (invalidating a statute requiring disclosure of lecture topics and associations where the inquiry was “unreasonable” and “not sufficiently limited”).

Moreover, the statute’s categorical disclosure requirements—mandating reporting of expenditures for “food and beverages, entertainment, research, communication, media advertising, publications, travel, and lodging,” *see* Fla. Stat. § 11.045(3)(a)—are unconstitutionally vague. The statute fails to provide clear standards for what constitutes a reportable expenditure, leaving lobbyists to guess whether a particular meal or communication falls within the statute’s ambit. *Lamont v. Postmaster General*, 381 U.S. 301, 307 (1965) (invalidating a statute requiring the Postmaster General to detain mail from foreign communist propaganda where the statute failed to provide clear standards for what constituted “communist political propaganda”). The lack of clarity in the statute’s terms invites arbitrary enforcement and chills protected speech. *Baggett v. Bullitt*, 377 U.S. 360, 372 (1964) (holding that a loyalty oath statute was unconstitutionally vague where it failed to provide clear notice of what conduct was prohibited).

**II. THE CONTINGENCY-FEE BAN IMPOSES AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL RESTRICTION ON LOBBYISTS’ RIGHT TO COMPENSATION.**

The statute’s prohibition on contingency-fee lobbying is an unconstitutional restriction on the right to contract and engage in lawful activity. *Adkins v. Children’s Hospital*, 261 U.S. 525, 546 (1923) (invalidating a minimum wage law that interfered with the right to contract). The ban on contingency fees is particularly problematic because it prevents lobbyists from structuring their compensation in a manner that aligns their interests with those of their clients. *Coppage v. Kansas*, 236 U.S. 1, 14 (1915) (holding that a law prohibiting employers from requiring employees to agree not to join a union was an unconstitutional restriction on the right to contract).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the right to contract is a fundamental component of liberty protected by the Fourteenth Amendment. *Allgeyer v. Louisiana*, 165 U.S. 578, 589 (1897) (holding that a state law prohibiting contracts made outside the state for insurance on Louisiana property violated the Fourteenth Amendment). The contingency-fee ban interferes with this right by preventing lobbyists from entering into agreements that are otherwise lawful and commercially reasonable. *Adair v. United States*, 208 U.S. 161, 174 (1908) (holding that a federal law prohibiting “yellow-dog contracts” was an unconstitutional restriction on the right to contract).

**III. THE STATUTE IS FACIALLY INVALID BECAUSE IT CHILLS PROTECTED SPEECH AND LACKS A SAVING CONSTRUCTION.**

Because the statute’s disclosure and contingency-fee provisions are unconstitutional, the statute must be struck down in its entirety. *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 23 (1960) (holding that a statute is facially invalid if it “covers a substantial amount of constitutionally protected conduct”). The statute’s overbreadth and vagueness deter protected speech and chill lawful activity, and there is no reasonable construction that would save it. *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 491 (1965) (holding that a statute is facially invalid if it “has an inevitable chilling effect upon protected expression”).

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to the state. The statute’s disclosure and contingency-fee provisions violate the First Amendment and are facially invalid. The judgment below should be reversed.
