**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a cause of action to a plaintiff who alleges only that a governmental entity acted with animus toward him, without alleging membership in a protected class or that he was treated differently from others similarly situated. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a “class of one” claim may be maintained under the Equal Protection Clause when the plaintiff asserts that the government’s action was motivated solely by spite or personal animus. Such a theory, if adopted, would eviscerate the foundational requirement of equal protection jurisprudence: that the plaintiff must demonstrate that he was treated differently from others who are similarly situated. The precedent of this Court and the lower courts before 1970 uniformly requires that a plaintiff alleging an equal protection violation must show that he was treated differently from others who are similarly situated, not merely that he was singled out for adverse treatment.

The principle that equal protection requires a showing of differential treatment based on a classification is deeply rooted in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), this Court held that the Equal Protection Clause is violated when a law is applied in a discriminatory manner, even if the law on its face is neutral. The Court emphasized that “the law, in its letter, may be fair, but if it is applied in a manner which results in discrimination, it is not in accordance with the Constitution.” Id. at 370. The case involved the denial of a permit to operate a laundry in San Francisco, where the law was applied to Chinese applicants but not to others. The Court concluded that the law’s application, not its text, determined whether equal protection had been violated. This principle underscores that the focus is not on whether the plaintiff was singled out, but whether the government’s action was based on an impermissible classification.

Similarly, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), the Court upheld a Louisiana law requiring racial segregation in rail cars, but only because it found that the law did not create a legal distinction between races. The Court stated that “the law does not necessarily create a legal distinction between the races.” Id. at 551. The decision rested on the premise that the law was neutral on its face and applied equally. The dissent, however, warned that the law’s effect—segregating Black and white passengers—was itself a classification that violated equal protection. Id. at 560 (Harlan, J., dissenting). This case illustrates that the Equal Protection Clause is not violated by mere singling out, but by the imposition of a classification that denies equal treatment under the law.

In *Buchanan v. Warley*, 245 U.S. 60 (1917), the Court struck down a city ordinance that prohibited Black persons from occupying homes in neighborhoods where white persons lived. The Court held that the ordinance “infringed upon the right of a person to acquire, hold, and dispose of property,” and that “the right to purchase and occupy property is a fundamental right.” Id. at 63. The Court emphasized that the law was not neutral but created a racial classification. The decision reaffirmed that equal protection is violated when the government treats individuals differently based on a suspect classification—here, race—regardless of whether the law was applied to a single individual or a group.

The principle that equal protection requires a showing of classification, not mere animus, was reaffirmed in *United States v. Carolene Products Co.*, 304 U.S. 144 (1938). The Court noted that while the Equal Protection Clause protects against classifications that are arbitrary or irrational, it does not extend to “a mere difference in treatment that does not rest upon any classification at all.” Id. at 152. The Court observed that “the Constitution does not require that the government treat all persons identically,” but only that it not treat persons differently without a rational basis. Id. at 153. This distinction is critical: the Constitution does not prohibit all differential treatment, only that which is based on an impermissible classification.

In *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497 (1954), the Court held that the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment, like the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, prohibits racial segregation in public schools in the District of Columbia. The Court stated that “segregation of white and colored children in public schools has a detrimental effect upon the colored children,” and that “the effect of segregation is to deprive the colored children of equal educational opportunities.” Id. at 500. The decision was grounded in the principle that the government may not treat individuals differently based on race, even if the law is not facially discriminatory. The Court did not consider the possibility that a plaintiff could prevail merely by showing that he was singled out for adverse treatment.

In *Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1 (1948), the Court held that a state court could not enforce a racially restrictive covenant in a deed. The Court stated that “the enforcement of such covenants by a state court is a state action which violates the Equal Protection Clause.” Id. at 13. The decision emphasized that the government’s role in enforcing private racial discrimination constituted state action that violated the Constitution. The Court did not suggest that a plaintiff could prevail merely by showing that he was personally targeted by a private party; rather, the claim was based on the government’s role in enforcing a racial classification.

Finally, in *Lloyd Corp. v. Tanner*, 387 U.S. 136 (1967), the Court held that a private entity could not be compelled to allow a public demonstration on its property, but the Court emphasized that the government’s action in regulating speech must be neutral and not based on content or viewpoint. The Court stated that “the government may not regulate speech based on its content or viewpoint.” Id. at 142. This principle reinforces that the government must treat all individuals equally under the law, not based on personal animus.

The Court of Appeals’ decision in this case would allow a plaintiff to assert a constitutional claim based solely on the government’s alleged spite, without any showing of a classification or differential treatment. Such a rule would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general right to fair treatment, which is not the law. The Clause protects against classifications, not against personal grievances. The judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed.
