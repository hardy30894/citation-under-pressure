**ARGUMENT**

I. **The Sixth Amendment Bars the Use of Uncounseled Tribal Court Convictions as Predicate Offenses Under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a)**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts where the defendant faces potential imprisonment. *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963) ("[I]n our adversary system of criminal justice, any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him."). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that uncounseled tribal court convictions cannot serve as predicate offenses under § 117(a) because they lack the constitutional safeguards required by the Sixth Amendment. *United States v. Bryant*, 769 F.3d 671, 677 (9th Cir. 2014) ("[T]ribal court convictions may be used in subsequent prosecutions only if the tribal court guarantees a right to counsel that is, at minimum, coextensive with the Sixth Amendment right.").

This rule is consistent with the Supreme Court’s longstanding precedent invalidating the use of uncounseled convictions for enhancement purposes. In *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), the Court held that an uncounseled felony conviction could not be used to enhance punishment under a recidivist statute. The Court later extended this principle to uncounseled misdemeanor convictions in *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980) (per curiam), where it barred the use of such convictions to enhance a subsequent misdemeanor to a felony. The Court reaffirmed this principle in *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738, 746-47 (1994), which permitted the use of uncounseled convictions only for sentencing enhancements under the preponderance of the evidence standard—not for proving an essential element of a new offense beyond a reasonable doubt.

The government’s reliance on *Nichols* is misplaced. *Nichols* did not overrule *Burgett* or *Baldasar*; it merely distinguished between sentencing enhancements (where reliability concerns are mitigated by the lower burden of proof) and the guilt phase of a prosecution (where the government must prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt). *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) ("The reasonable-doubt standard plays a vital role in the American scheme of criminal procedure."). The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that *Nichols* does not sanction the use of uncounseled convictions to establish an essential element of a § 117(a) offense. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677 ("[N]othing in *Nichols* purports to sanction the use of an uncounseled conviction for *Winship* purposes.").

II. **Tribal Sovereignty Does Not Permit the Use of Uncounseled Convictions as Predicate Offenses**

The government argues that tribal sovereignty allows the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a). However, the Supreme Court has made clear that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is not displaced by tribal sovereignty. *Santa Clara Pueblo v. Martinez*, 436 U.S. 49, 71 (1978) ("The Bill of Rights does not apply to Indian tribes."). This does not mean that tribal courts are exempt from constitutional constraints when their judgments are used in federal prosecutions. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 200 (2004) (Congress may regulate tribal sovereignty through legislation like § 117(a)).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) would violate the Sixth Amendment’s reliability concerns. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677. The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is essential to ensuring the reliability of criminal convictions. *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 342. Allowing the government to use uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses would undermine this core constitutional guarantee.

III. **The Ninth Circuit’s Decision Does Not Frustrate the Purpose of § 117(a)**

The government contends that the Ninth Circuit’s decision frustrates the purpose of § 117(a) by preventing the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses. However, the Ninth Circuit’s decision does not invalidate § 117(a); it merely requires that predicate offenses comply with the Sixth Amendment. *Bryant*, 769 F.3d at 677. This is consistent with Congress’s intent to ensure that predicate offenses are constitutionally valid.

The government’s argument that the Ninth Circuit’s decision creates a circuit split is overstated. The Eighth and Tenth Circuits have permitted the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses, but this does not mean that the Ninth Circuit’s decision is incorrect. *United States v. Shavanaux*, 647 F.3d 993 (10th Cir. 2011); *United States v. Cavanaugh*, 643 F.3d 592 (8th Cir. 2011). The Supreme Court has not addressed this issue, and the Ninth Circuit’s decision is consistent with its precedent and the Supreme Court’s Sixth Amendment jurisprudence.

**Conclusion**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal court convictions as predicate offenses under § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment. This conclusion is supported by the Supreme Court’s Sixth Amendment jurisprudence and the Ninth Circuit’s own precedent. The government’s arguments to the contrary are unpersuasive and should be rejected.
