**ARGUMENT**

**I. The release does not bar Bancorp’s claims because its text is limited to claims arising from the OCC’s decision to close the Bank, not to the FDIC’s later conduct as receiver.**

A release is construed according to its plain language, and it bars only those claims within its terms. *Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292, 297-98 (1959). New York courts do not extend a release beyond the “fair meaning of its language.” *Mangini v. McClurg*, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969). Here, Bancorp’s release relinquished claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” That language reaches claims challenging the closure decision itself, not distinct wrongful acts committed later in the course of the receivership.

The claims at issue are not attacks on the OCC’s insolvency determination or closure decision. Bancorp alleges that the FDIC later enriched itself by paying post-insolvency interest, charged the estate for its own legal and office expenses, sold assets below value, and failed to provide a proper accounting. Those are independent post-closing acts by the receiver. A release does not cover claims that are separate in time and character from the matter being released. *Mangini*, 24 N.Y.2d at 562-63.

The district court’s broader reading also conflicts with the principle that general release language is confined by context. Even broadly worded language cannot be read to extinguish claims that were neither known nor intended to be included, especially where the release refers to a specific event. *Centro Empresarial Cempresa S.A. v. América Móvil, S.A.B. de C.V.*, 17 N.Y.3d 269, 276-77 (2011). Bancorp’s claims concern the FDIC’s conduct as receiver after June 21, 1985, not the OCC’s decision to close the Bank. Because the release is tied to the closure decision, it does not bar claims arising from the FDIC’s later management of the receivership estate.

Nor can the FDIC invoke the release by recasting every receivership wrong as “with respect to” the closure decision. New York law requires that a release be enforced according to the parties’ manifested intent, not expanded to create a forfeiture. *Kaminsky v. Gamache*, 298 N.Y. 563, 569-70 (1949). Bancorp did not surrender claims for self-dealing, waste, or accounting failures in the administration of the receivership simply because those acts followed the closure of the Bank. The judgment should therefore be reversed on this ground.

**II. Bancorp’s claims are timely because they accrued, at the earliest, when the receivership ended on November 1, 1995.**

Under New York law, a claim for unjust enrichment accrues upon the occurrence of the wrongful enrichment, while a claim for breach of fiduciary duty seeking monetary relief is governed by the six-year limitations period of CPLR 213(1) or (7), depending on the nature of the duty and remedy. *IDT Corp. v. Morgan Stanley Dean Witter & Co.*, 12 N.Y.3d 132, 139-40 (2009); *Loengard v. Santa Fe Indus., Inc.*, 70 N.Y.2d 262, 266-67 (1987). Where a fiduciary relationship is ongoing, accrual is commonly tied to the termination of that relationship or the occurrence of a clear repudiation. *Westchester Religious Inst. v. Kamerman*, 262 A.D.2d 131, 131-32 (1st Dep’t 1999). Likewise, a claim for an accounting does not accrue until the fiduciary relationship ends or a demand for an accounting is refused. *Ackerman v. Gerlach*, 176 A.D.2d 568, 569 (1st Dep’t 1991).

The FDIC’s receivership was a continuing fiduciary administration of Bancorp’s interests. The FDIC’s alleged self-payment of interest, charging of expenses to the estate, and disposition of Bank assets were all part of an ongoing receivership, not isolated acts with immediate final effect. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995, one day before the FDIC terminated the receivership on November 1, 1995. If accrual turns on termination of the fiduciary relationship, Bancorp sued within the limitations period. *Westchester Religious Inst.*, 262 A.D.2d at 131-32; *Ackerman*, 176 A.D.2d at 569.

The district court erred in holding that the claims were time-barred from earlier dates in the 1990s. Where wrongful conduct occurs during a continuing fiduciary relationship, New York does not require the beneficiary to sue before the relationship has ended and the full scope of the wrongdoing is ascertainable. *Westchester Religious Inst.*, 262 A.D.2d at 131-32. That is especially true for an accounting claim, which necessarily depends on a complete and final reckoning of the fiduciary’s conduct. *Ackerman*, 176 A.D.2d at 569.

In any event, to the extent the FDIC urges the “continuous wrong” doctrine against Bancorp, that doctrine supports timeliness only when the wrongful conduct is ongoing and not merely the continuing effects of a single act. *Henry v. Bank of Am.*, 147 A.D.3d 599, 602 (1st Dep’t 2017). Here, the FDIC’s alleged mismanagement and self-dealing continued throughout the receivership. The suit, filed before termination, was timely.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment should be reversed. The release does not encompass Bancorp’s claims, and the claims were timely filed before the receivership ended.
