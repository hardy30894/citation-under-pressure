## Argument

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the District Court should have instructed the jury on punitive damages. The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows for punitive damages when an employer engages in discriminatory practices "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). However, this standard should be interpreted in light of the common law principles that underlie punitive damages.

At common law, punitive damages are typically reserved for cases where the defendant's conduct is not only intentional but also "egregious" or "outrageous." See, e.g., Restatement (Second) of Torts § 908(2) (1979). This principle is reflected in the legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, where Senator Dole and others noted that punitive damages should be available only in "extraordinarily egregious cases." 137 Cong. Rec. S 15473 (Oct. 30, 1991).

The Court of Appeals' decision to remand for a trial on punitive damages fails to consider this critical aspect of the law. Instead, it appears to conflate the standard for intentional discrimination with the standard for punitive damages. See Kolstad v. American Dental Ass'n, 108 F.3d 1431, 1438 (D.C. Cir. 1997). This approach is inconsistent with the principles enunciated in cases such as Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983), where the Court held that punitive damages under § 1983 require "reckless or callous disregard for the plaintiff's rights" or "evil motive or intent." Id. at 56.

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals' reliance on the "reckless indifference" language in § 1981a(b)(1) is misplaced. As Judge Williams noted in his concurrence, the legislative history suggests that Congress intended to adopt a higher standard for punitive damages, consistent with the common law trend. See Kolstad, 108 F.3d at 1441-42 (Williams, J., concurring).

In this case, while the jury found that the American Dental Association (ADA) intentionally discriminated against Carole Kolstad, the evidence does not rise to the level of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct required for punitive damages. The ADA's actions, while discriminatory, were not so extraordinary or reprehensible as to warrant punitive damages.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the District Court's decision to deny punitive damages should be reinstated. This approach is consistent with the principles of punitive damages at common law and the legislative intent behind the Civil Rights Act of 1991.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
