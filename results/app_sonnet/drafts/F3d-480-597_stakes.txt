# ARGUMENT

## I. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Establish That Nieves-Castaño Knew the Weapon Possessed the Characteristics of a Machine Gun.

A conviction must be vacated when, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, no rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979). Section 922(o) criminalizes possession of a "machine gun," a term of art defined by reference to a weapon's capacity to fire automatically. The Supreme Court has held that possession offenses involving firearms with unusual, dangerous characteristics carry a scienter requirement beyond mere knowing possession of the object itself. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 602 (1994). In *Staples*, the Court explained that the government must prove the defendant "knew of the features of his [weapon] that brought it within the scope of the Act"—that is, knowledge of the specific characteristics rendering the firearm a machine gun, not merely knowledge that the defendant possessed a firearm of a particular make. *Id.* at 619.

The government's proof here falls short of that standard. The only evidence of Nieves-Castaño's knowledge was her post-arrest statement that she looked into the golf bag, saw a rifle, and recognized it as "an AK-47." That statement establishes, at most, that she knew the make and general appearance of the weapon. It says nothing about whether she knew the rifle had been modified or manufactured to fire automatically—the very characteristic that distinguishes a lawful semi-automatic rifle from an unlawful machine gun. Because rifles bearing the AK-47 name and configuration are commonly possessed lawfully in semi-automatic form, knowledge of the make alone cannot substitute for knowledge of the automatic-fire feature that *Staples* requires the government to prove. 511 U.S. at 619. No witness testified that Nieves-Castaño ever observed the weapon fire, examined its internal mechanism, or was told by Alexis that the weapon had been altered. On this record, a rational jury could not have found beyond a reasonable doubt that she possessed the mens rea *Staples* demands. *Jackson*, 443 U.S. at 319.

Because the *Staples* mens rea element was neither proved nor, on this record, capable of proof, the district court's instructions compounded the error by permitting the jury to convict without requiring it to find that Nieves-Castaño knew of the automatic-firing characteristic specifically, rather than mere knowledge that the object was a rifle. That instructional gap is inseparable from the sufficiency defect: both stem from the government's failure to grapple with the scienter requirement *Staples* imposes. 511 U.S. at 619. The § 922(o) conviction should be reversed.

## II. Section 922(q)(2)(A) Is Unconstitutionally Vague Because It Fails to Specify How the 1000-Foot School Zone Is to Be Measured.

The Due Process Clause requires that a criminal statute "define the criminal offense with sufficient definiteness that ordinary people can understand what conduct is prohibited and in a manner that does not encourage arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement." *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983). A law is void for vagueness if it "impermissibly delegates basic policy matters to policemen, judges, and juries for resolution on an ad hoc and subjective basis, with the attendant dangers of arbitrary and discriminatory application." *Grayned v. City of Rockford*, 408 U.S. 104, 108-09 (1972). Fair warning is the touchstone: a defendant cannot be convicted under a statute that does not give her the ability to know, in advance, whether her conduct falls within the zone of criminality. *United States v. Lanier*, 520 U.S. 259, 265 (1997).

Section 922(q)(2)(A) prohibits possession of a firearm within "1000 feet" of a school, but nowhere specifies the method by which that distance is to be measured—whether by a straight line from property boundary to property boundary, by the shortest pedestrian route, by driving distance, or by some other metric. Depending on the method chosen, the same physical location may fall inside or outside the school zone. That is not a hypothetical concern here: Nieves-Castaño's third-floor apartment in Building 9 of the housing project sits at a distance from the nearest school that, on this record, was established only by law-enforcement testimony employing an unstated methodology. Neither the statute nor its legislative history instructs enforcement officers, prosecutors, juries, or citizens which measurement to use.

This ambiguity offends both aspects of the vagueness inquiry identified in *Kolender*. First, a person of ordinary intelligence residing near, but not obviously within, a school's immediate vicinity cannot determine from the statute's text whether her home falls inside the zone of criminal liability. *Grayned*, 408 U.S. at 108. Second, the absence of a specified measurement standard leaves the boundary of criminality to be fixed after the fact by whichever method the investigating officer happens to employ—precisely the kind of "ad hoc and subjective" determination the vagueness doctrine forbids. *Id.* at 108-09. Because this Court has not previously construed § 922(q)(2)(A), it should hold, consistent with *Kolender* and *Grayned*, that a criminal statute defining a geographic zone of liability by an undefined measurement standard fails to provide the fair notice the Due Process Clause requires. The § 922(q)(2)(A) conviction should accordingly be vacated.
