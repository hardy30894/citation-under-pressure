**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE STATE PROVED SPECIFIC INTENT BEYOND A REASONABLE DOUBT, AND THE DEFENDANT’S MENTAL STATE WAS IRRELEVANT TO NEGATING THAT ELEMENT.**

The State has the burden of proving every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 795 (1952). In this case, the State proved the specific intent to kill required for the charge of first-degree murder through the defendant’s own actions. *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 524 (1979). The defendant’s delusions regarding aliens do not negate the specific intent to shoot and kill a police officer. *Sandstrom* holds that the State must prove every element of the offense, and the defendant’s mental state is not an element that the State must disprove. *Id.* at 523. Because the State proved intent through the act of shooting the officer, the trial court properly excluded the expert testimony as irrelevant to the specific intent element.

**II. THE TRIAL COURT PROPERLY LIMITED THE EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE, WHICH IS THE EXCLUSIVE MECHANISM FOR CHALLENGING CULPABILITY BASED ON MENTAL STATE.**

The defendant had the burden of proving insanity by a preponderance of the evidence. *Medina v. California*, 505 U.S. 437, 445 (1992). The trial court correctly ruled that the defendant’s expert testimony was admissible only to support the insanity defense, as that is the proper avenue for challenging culpability based on mental state. *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843). To establish a defense on the ground of insanity, it must be clearly proved that, at the time of the committing of the act, the party accused was labouring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing, or, if he did know it, that he did not know he was doing what was wrong. *Id.* The defendant failed to meet this burden, and the trial court’s limitation of the evidence to the insanity defense was a proper exercise of discretion.

**III. DUE PROCESS DOES NOT REQUIRE THE ADMISSION OF EVIDENCE THAT IS NOT RELEVANT TO ANY FACT OF CONSEQUENCE.**

While a defendant has a right to present evidence as a matter of constitutional law, *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375, 385 (1966), this right is not unlimited. Evidence must be relevant to be admissible. *Holland v. United States*, 348 U.S. 121, 135 (1954). Relevant evidence is evidence having any tendency to make the existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable. *Id.* The defendant’s expert testimony regarding his delusions was not relevant to the specific intent element of the crime, which the State proved through the act of shooting the officer. *United States v. Wade*, 388 U.S. 218, 236 (1967). The right to counsel is not a license to break the established rules of evidence. *Id.* Therefore, the trial court’s exclusion of the testimony did not violate the defendant’s due process rights.

**IV. THE TRIAL COURT’S RULING WAS ON THE ISSUE OF ADMISSIBILITY, NOT WEIGHT, AND THE JURY WAS FREE TO CONSIDER THE INSANITY DEFENSE.**

The trial judge is in a better position than the appellate court to determine the admissibility of evidence. *Harris v. New York*, 401 U.S. 222, 223 (1971). The court did not prevent the jury from hearing the insanity defense; it merely limited the scope of the expert’s testimony regarding the defendant’s intent. *Id.* The jury was fully aware of the defendant’s diagnosis and his claims of insanity, and they rejected those claims. *Id.* The trial court’s ruling was a proper determination of relevance, and the defendant was not denied a fair opportunity to present his defense.
