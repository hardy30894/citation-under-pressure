**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Federal Claims erred in holding that the government’s conduct could not, as a matter of law, constitute a physical taking. The Fifth Amendment forbids the government from appropriating or authorizing a permanent physical occupation of private property without just compensation. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419 (1982); *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946). Although the modern articulation of the per se rule postdates 1970, the principle itself long predates it: where the government authorizes a physical invasion or occupation that imposes a distinct burden on private land, compensation is required. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–63; *United States v. Welch*, 217 U.S. 333, 339–41 (1910); *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. 373, 378–80 (1945).

Here, the government does not merely maintain the limited “signal lines” for which the easement was granted. It installed a vastly expanded fiber-optic system, admitted that the system contains capacity beyond current agency need, and then leased surplus fibers to private telecommunications users. That is not a mere internal upgrade; it is an unauthorized enlargement of the easement’s burden and a continuing physical use of Tuthill’s land for the benefit of third parties. An easement defines the scope of the right acquired, and use beyond that scope is an additional burden on the servient estate. *Preseault v. United States*, 100 F.3d 1525, 1530–31 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc) (recognizing that a taking occurs when government action exceeds the scope of the original easement); *United States v. Welch*, 217 U.S. at 339–41.

The government’s position improperly collapses two distinct inquiries: whether the original easement permitted the conduct, and whether the present occupation exceeds the property right previously acquired. The first question goes to liability for breach or trespass; the second goes to whether the government has imposed an additional servitude without compensation. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that when the government physically appropriates or authorizes a continued physical use of property, the fact that some prior interest existed does not immunize the new burden. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–63; *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. at 378–80. An easement holder may not transform a limited right of passage or maintenance into a commercial telecommunications platform without acquiring the additional property interest it now uses. *Welch*, 217 U.S. at 339–41.

The Court of Federal Claims’ reliance on the absence of “additional physical intrusion” was legally mistaken. A physical taking does not require a new fence, new poles, or a fresh entry every day. A permanent government occupation or authorization of use may itself be the taking. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–63; *General Motors*, 323 U.S. at 378–80. Indeed, the government’s ongoing leasing of the surplus fibers underscores that the challenged conduct is not merely preparatory or incidental to the BPA’s own transmission needs; it is an affirmative commercial occupation of space within the easement corridor for private use. The Constitution does not permit the government to repurpose private property as a revenue-generating asset while insisting that no compensable burden has been imposed. *General Motors*, 323 U.S. at 378–80.

Nor does the fact that the system may accommodate projected future governmental needs defeat Tuthill’s claim. The relevant issue is the use actually imposed on the property today. The government concedes that it currently needs only a fraction of the fibers crossing Tuthill’s land and leases the rest to third parties. That present commercial use exceeds any plausible conception of “appurtenant signal lines” necessary to the BPA’s own transmission function and therefore exceeds the easement’s scope. When the sovereign’s conduct goes beyond the property interest it acquired, compensation is due for the additional burden. *Welch*, 217 U.S. at 339–41; *Preseault*, 100 F.3d at 1530–31.

At minimum, the undisputed facts preclude summary judgment for the government. On this record, a reasonable factfinder could conclude that the BPA’s installation of excess fiber capacity for commercial leasing is a physical occupation outside the easement and thus a compensable taking. The Court of Federal Claims’ contrary holding effectively grants the government a blanket right to convert a limited easement into a broader commercial servitude without paying for the added burden. That is inconsistent with settled takings principles. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–63; *General Motors*, 323 U.S. at 378–80; *Welch*, 217 U.S. at 339–41.

The judgment should therefore be reversed.
