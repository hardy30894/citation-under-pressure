**ARGUMENT**

I. The Ohio Adult Parole Authority’s clemency process creates a constitutionally protected liberty interest under the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects “life or liberty” interests that are not merely created by state law, but are “independent of any state procedure.” *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539, 558, 94 S.Ct. 2963, 2976, 41 L.Ed.2d 935 (1974). While the Supreme Court has recognized that “a State may, in its discretion, grant clemency,” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464, 101 S.Ct. at 2464, it has never held that such discretion immunizes the state from due process obligations when the clemency process itself becomes a critical, final stage in the determination of a prisoner’s fate. Here, the Ohio clemency process is not a mere formality; it is the *last* opportunity for a death row inmate to avoid execution. The process is mandatory, triggered by statute and regulation, and conducted by a state agency with the power to recommend clemency to the Governor—whose discretion, while broad, is not unfettered. *State ex rel. Maurer v. Sheward*, 71 Ohio St.3d 513, 520, 644 N.E.2d 369, 375 (1994). When a state creates a procedure that is “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant,” *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393, 105 S.Ct. 830, 834, 83 L.Ed.2d 821 (1985), due process applies, even if the process is executive rather than judicial.

Ohio’s clemency procedure is not a mere administrative formality. It is triggered by a fixed timeline—45 days before execution—regardless of whether the prisoner has applied. *Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07*; *Death Penalty Clemency Procedure*, Ohio Dep’t of Rehab. & Corr. Reg. 5120-1-07. The process includes a mandatory pre-hearing interview, which may be requested by the prisoner. The interview is not advisory; its contents are “considered at the clemency hearing, along with all other relevant information.” This is not a passive review. The parole board’s recommendation is a *de facto* decision on life or death. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the Due Process Clause applies to each step” in a state’s adjudicatory process when that step is “a regular and critical component of the decisionmaking process.” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 471, 101 S.Ct. at 2468 (Stevens, J., dissenting). The clemency process in Ohio is such a step. It is not “rarely, if ever, appropriate for judicial review,” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464, 101 S.Ct. at 2464, because it is not a judicial function—it is a *state-created* process that determines whether a prisoner lives or dies. When a state creates such a process, it must do so in a manner consistent with due process.

The Court in *Dumschat* did not hold that no liberty interest exists in clemency; it held that the *state did not create a liberty interest* in the *denial* of clemency without explanation. 452 U.S. at 465, 101 S.Ct. at 2465. That is not the issue here. Woodard does not claim a right to clemency. He claims a right to *procedural fairness* in a process that determines whether he lives or dies. The distinction is critical. The Due Process Clause protects against arbitrary deprivation, not just against the absence of a right to a benefit. *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335, 96 S.Ct. 893, 903, 47 L.Ed.2d 18 (1976). When a state creates a process that is “an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant,” *Evitts*, 469 U.S. at 393, 105 S.Ct. at 834, it must afford due process. The clemency process in Ohio is such a system. It is not a “mere administrative formality,” *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464, 101 S.Ct. at 2464, but a final, state-sanctioned mechanism for determining life or death. The Court has never held that such a process is beyond due process scrutiny. To the contrary, *Evitts* and *Wolff* establish that due process applies to *any* stage of a state’s adjudicatory system that is “critical” to the outcome. The clemency process is that stage.

II. The clemency interview procedure imposes an unconstitutional condition by coercing a waiver of the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination.

The Fifth Amendment protects a person from being “compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself.” U.S. Const. amend. V.
