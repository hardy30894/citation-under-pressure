**ARGUMENT**

I. THE JURY’S AWARD OF TREBLE DAMAGES UNDER SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES.

The cornerstone of any recovery under § 4 of the Clayton Act is proof of *actual damages* proximately caused by a violation of § 2(a) of the Robinson-Patman Act. As this Court has repeatedly held, “the plaintiff must prove that the price discrimination caused actual injury in the form of lost profits or other measurable economic harm.” *Burlington Northern R.R. Co. v. Oklahoma City, 873 F.2d 1059, 1063 (8th Cir. 1989)*; see also *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stahl, 336 U.S. 171, 175 (1949)* (“[T]he injured party must show that he suffered actual damage as a result of the unlawful practice.”). The burden is not met by mere speculation, conjecture, or generalized assertions of harm. Rather, the plaintiff must present “specific, credible evidence” linking the alleged price discrimination to quantifiable losses. *See United States v. Borden Co., 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939)*.

Here, Stelwagon failed to carry its burden. While the jury found that Tarmac engaged in price discrimination by selling MAPs to Standard and Celotex at lower prices than those charged to Stelwagon—thereby violating § 2(a)—it did so without sufficient evidence of causation or measurable harm. The record reveals no direct evidence that Stelwagon lost sales, customers, or revenue due to Tarmac’s allegedly discriminatory pricing. Indeed, Stelwagon continued to sell Tarmac products throughout the relevant period, including after the first complaints were made in early 1989. There is no testimony, invoice data, or market analysis demonstrating that Stelwagon’s sales volume declined or that it was unable to compete effectively because of the preferential pricing to competitors.

Stelwagon’s theory of damages rests entirely on speculative inference: that if Tarmac had not sold to Standard and Celotex at lower prices, Stelwagon would have captured more business. But such speculation is insufficient. *See American Tobacco Co. v. United States, 328 U.S. 781, 815 (1946)* (“A claim for damages based upon mere possibility or conjecture is not enough.”); *Hoffman v. C.I.T. Corp., 578 F.2d 100, 104 (3d Cir. 1978)* (“Proof of damages must be more than speculative or hypothetical.”). The jury was not presented with any reliable data showing what Stelwagon’s sales would have been absent the challenged conduct. No comparative analysis of pre- and post-discrimination sales volumes was offered. No expert testimony tied the price differential to lost profits. No evidence demonstrated that Stelwagon’s market share eroded or that its ability to serve customers was impaired.

Moreover, Stelwagon’s own conduct undermines its claim of injury. It voluntarily refrained from carrying competing lines of MAPs and ceased aggressive marketing of non-Tarmac products—all in reliance on the exclusive distributorship agreement. This self-imposed limitation on competition may have contributed to its vulnerability, but it does not establish that Tarmac’s actions caused actual harm. *See United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co., 310 U.S. 150, 224 (1940)* (“Where a plaintiff has limited his own business opportunities, he cannot recover damages for loss of profits which might have resulted from expansion into areas he chose not to enter.”).

The district court’s remittitur of the antitrust damages from $2,272,000 to $450,383.60 reflects its recognition that the original award was unsupported by the evidence. Yet even this reduced figure lacks a foundation in fact. The jury awarded damages based on an unproven assumption that Stelwagon suffered lost profits attributable to Tarmac’s price discrimination—without any evidentiary support for that causal link. Such a verdict violates the fundamental principle that “damages must be reasonably certain and not speculative.” *Columbia Broadcasting Sys., Inc. v. American Tel. & Tel. Co., 316 F.2d 304, 310 (2d Cir. 1963)*.

This Court has long recognized that where a plaintiff fails to prove actual damages, treble damages under § 4 are unavailable—even if a violation of the Robinson-Patman Act is established. *See United States v. Colgate & Co., 250 U.S. 300, 307 (1919)* (“The right to recover damages depends upon proof of actual injury.”); *Fisher v. Pabst Brewing Co., 320 F.2d 115, 118 (7th Cir. 1963)* (“No recovery can be had under § 4 unless the plaintiff proves actual damages.”). Here, Stelwagon proved only that Tarmac sold to competitors at lower prices. It did not prove that those sales caused Stelwagon to suffer any measurable economic loss. Therefore, the judgment awarding treble damages must be vacated.

II. ALTERNATIVELY, THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC’S MOTIONS FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW.

Tarmac moved for judgment as a matter of law at both the close of Stelwagon’s case and at the close of all evidence. These motions should have been granted. Civ. P. 50(a)*. The standard is strict: “If the evidence is such that a reasonable jury could not find for the nonmoving party, the court must grant the motion.” *D’Amico v. City of Philadelphia, 902 F.2d 244, 247 (3d Cir. 1990)*.
