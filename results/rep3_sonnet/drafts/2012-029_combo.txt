ARGUMENT

I. A State-Law Malpractice Claim Does Not "Arise Under" the Patent Laws Merely Because It Requires Application of a Patent-Law Rule.

Federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a) is not triggered whenever a lawsuit happens to touch on a question that patent law would also answer. This Court has long held that "[a] suit arises under the law that creates the cause of action." American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co., 241 U.S. 257, 260 (1916). Minton's malpractice claim was created entirely by Texas law: it is Texas law, not the Patent Act, that defines the duty of care owed by an attorney, the standard for breach, and the elements of causation and damages. See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). That the "case within a case" happens to involve a hypothetical patent-law question—whether the experimental-use exception would have saved the '643 patent—does not convert a state tort claim into one "arising under" federal patent law.

This conclusion follows from the well-pleaded complaint rule itself. Whether a case arises under federal law "must be determined from what necessarily appears in the plaintiff's statement of his own claim . . . unaided by anything alleged in anticipation or avoidance of defenses which it is thought the defendant may interpose." Taylor v. Anderson, 234 U.S. 74, 75–76 (1914); accord Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 211 U.S. 149, 152 (1908). Minton's petition stated a garden-variety negligence claim; the patent issue arises only because Gunn raised, as a defense to causation, the argument that the experimental-use exception would not have changed the outcome of the underlying suit. A defense grounded in patent law no more confers federal jurisdiction than any other defense would.

This Court's older patent decisions confirm the point directly. In Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255 (1897), and again in New Marshall Engine Co. v. Marshall Engine Co., 223 U.S. 473 (1912), the Court held that suits to enforce contractual rights concerning patented inventions—accountings, royalty disputes, and the like—do not "arise under" the patent laws even though the validity or scope of a patent might be incidentally examined along the way. The Court reaffirmed this rule in Healy v. Sea Gull Specialty Co., 237 U.S. 479 (1915), explaining that a suit to enforce rights growing out of a contract relating to a patent remains a state-law contract action notwithstanding the presence of patent questions in the background. A legal-malpractice claim, which likewise depends on questions of duty, breach, causation, and damages defined by state law, sits in precisely the same posture: the patent question is a means to an end, not the gravamen of the suit.

Judge Friendly's oft-cited formulation for the parallel Copyright Act provision, applicable by analogy under § 1338(a), states the same limiting principle: an action "arises under" the Act only where "the complaint is for a remedy expressly granted by the Act . . . or asserts a claim requiring construction of the Act . . . or . . . a distinctive policy of the Act requires that federal principles control." T.B. Harms Co. v. Eliscu, 339 F.2d 823, 828 (2d Cir. 1964). None of those circumstances is present here. Minton seeks no patent remedy; he seeks damages for his own attorneys' negligence.

II. Recognizing Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Here Would Disturb the Historic Division of Labor Between State and Federal Courts.

Even where a patent question is embedded in a state claim, this Court has cautioned against reflexively opening "the arising-under door." Federal courts must engage in "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Gully v. First National Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). A federal issue justifies the exercise of exclusive federal jurisdiction only when it is one upon which "the result depends" in a manner that "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect" of federal law. Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912). Legal malpractice actions arising from patent litigation do not meet that standard: the patent question is hypothetical, backward-looking, and resolved only for purposes of a state tort suit between the client and his former lawyers—not to fix the scope of any patent right, which will never bind the Patent and Trademark Office, the public, or any other litigant.

Nor does state adjudication of such backward-looking patent questions threaten the uniformity concerns that animate exclusive federal patent jurisdiction. This Court has recognized that the touchstone is whether the exercise of jurisdiction would disturb "the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 671–72 (1950) (federal courts' jurisdiction is not enlarged merely because a federal question might be litigated). State courts, which possess centuries of experience adjudicating attorney malpractice, are fully competent to determine, as a matter of historical fact within a "case within a case," how a patent-law defense would have fared—without thereby altering any patent's validity or scope. Because Texas law creates Minton's cause of action, because the patent question is defensive and non-binding, and because state adjudication poses no threat to the federal patent system's uniformity, this malpractice suit does not "arise under" the patent laws, and the Texas courts possessed jurisdiction to decide it.
