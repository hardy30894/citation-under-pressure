**ARGUMENT**

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges Because They Are “Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level.”**

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act defines “employee” to exclude “any person . . . appointed . . . on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). This exemption properly encompasses appointed state judges, who exercise the quintessential policymaking function of interpreting and developing the law.

The judicial power, as exercised by state courts, is inherently a policymaking power. In *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938), this Court recognized that “law in the sense in which courts speak of it today does not exist without some definite authority behind it.” The common law, which judges author through their decisions, is “not a brooding omnipresence in the sky” but “the articulate voice of some sovereign,” *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 222 (1917) (Holmes, J., dissenting). When a state judge announces a rule of decision, that judge is making policy for the state.

Missouri judges, in particular, perform policymaking functions at the highest levels. The Missouri Supreme Court adopts rules governing the conduct of the judiciary and the bar. Mo. Const. art. V, § 5. It resolves administrative disputes within the judicial hierarchy. And its common-law decisions establish binding precedent for the entire state. Such functions are indistinguishable from the policymaking performed by high-level executive appointees.

Congress enacted the policymaking-level exemption to preserve state autonomy over essential governmental functions. In *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), this Court stated that “in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved in the judicial decision.” The tenure and qualifications of state judges are matters of “considerable importance to a state,” and traditionally “left to each state to regulate.” *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 295 (1936) (recognizing that states retain “powers not delegated to the United States” under the Constitution). Congress must speak unequivocally if it intends to intrude into this area. The ADEA’s exemption for appointees on the policymaking level reflects Congress’s recognition that such intrusion would be inappropriate.

Appellants argue that judges do not make policy but merely “decide cases between litigants involving questions of law.” That view misunderstands the judicial function. As Chief Justice Marshall observed, “It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.” *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803). When courts say what the law is—especially when resolving issues of first impression—they necessarily create law. That is policymaking.

Furthermore, reading the policymaking-level exemption to exclude judges would produce an anomalous result. The ADEA expressly excludes elected officials from the definition of “employee.” § 630(f). If appointed judges were not also excluded, then appointed judges would receive ADEA protection while elected judges would not—even though both perform identical functions. Congress could not have intended such an irrational distinction. *See United States v. Rumely*, 345 U.S. 41, 45 (1953) (courts should avoid interpretations that produce absurd results).

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not prohibit Missouri from requiring its judges to retire at age seventy. Age classifications are subject only to rational-basis review. This Court has long held that “the Fourteenth Amendment does not deny to States the power to treat different classes of persons in different ways.” *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911).

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision serves legitimate state interests. It ensures that judges retire before age-related decline impairs their performance, thereby maintaining public confidence in the judiciary. It eliminates the need for case-by-case determinations of judicial competence, avoiding the “unpleasantness of selectively removing aged judges.” *See Mitchell v. Portland Zoo Corp.*, 270 F.2d 143, 146 (8th Cir. 1959) (recognizing that administrative convenience is a legitimate governmental interest). And it promotes generational turnover, allowing new perspectives to enter the bench.

This Court has consistently upheld mandatory retirement provisions for state officials. In *Pacific States Box & Basket Co. v. White*, 296 U.S. 176, 184 (1935), the Court stated that “the classification is not arbitrary if it bears a reasonable relation to some legitimate purpose.” Age seventy is a reasonable line; it is not arbitrary to conclude that “the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.” *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (citing *Murgia*).

Appellants cannot show that the classification lacks any rational basis. Missouri’s choice to set the retirement age at seventy rather than seventy-five or sixty-five is a policy judgment that this Court should not second-guess. “It is enough that the State may rationally legislate in the area.” *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 426 (1961).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
