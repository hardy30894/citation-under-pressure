**ARGUMENT**

**I. STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES, THEREBY PRECLUDING RECOVERY UNDER THE CLAYTON ACT**

Tarmac respectfully submits that the district court erred in denying its motion for judgment as a matter of law on Stelwagon’s Robinson-Patman claim because Stelwagon presented no competent evidence of actual damages resulting from Tarmac’s alleged price discrimination. Under the Clayton Act, a plaintiff seeking treble damages must prove actual injury attributable to the antitrust violation. *J. Truett Payne Co. v. Chrysler Motors Corp.*, 451 U.S. 557, 562 (1981) (“[A] plaintiff must prove actual damages before treble damages may be awarded.”). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the antitrust laws… do not allow recovery for mere speculation or the possibility of injury.” *Id.* at 563 (quoting *Story Parchment Co. v. Paterson Parchment Paper Co.*, 282 U.S. 555, 563 (1931)).

Stelwagon’s damages model rested on a flawed methodology that assumed lost profits without accounting for market realities or alternative explanations for its financial decline. The jury was permitted to speculate that Stelwagon’s reduced profitability was solely attributable to Tarmac’s alleged price discrimination, despite evidence that the roofing market experienced broader downturns during the relevant period. This Court has repeatedly rejected damages models that fail to isolate the injury caused by the alleged antitrust violation. *See, e.g., In re Sch. Asbestos Litig.*, 977 F.2d 764, 780 (3d Cir. 1992) (“Damages must be proven with reasonable certainty; mere speculation or conjecture is insufficient.”).

Moreover, Stelwagon’s expert conceded that he could not determine whether any lost sales were directly caused by Tarmac’s pricing practices rather than other competitive factors. This failure to establish a causal nexus between the alleged discrimination and Stelwagon’s damages is fatal to its claim. *See Brunswick Corp. v. Pueblo Bowl-O-Mat, Inc.*, 429 U.S. 477, 489 (1977) (“[T]he plaintiff must prove actual damages… not just the fact of damages.”). Because Stelwagon’s evidence was insufficient to support a finding of actual damages, the district court should have granted Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the antitrust claim.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING TARMAC’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE BREACH OF CONTRACT CLAIM**

Tarmac further contends that the district court erred in denying its motion for judgment as a matter of law on Stelwagon’s breach of contract claim. The evidence at trial established that Stelwagon’s alleged damages were speculative and unsupported by the record. Under Pennsylvania law, a plaintiff seeking damages for breach of contract must prove the fact and amount of damages with reasonable certainty. *See, e.g., Barr v. Holyoke Co.*, 492 A.2d 1327, 1330 (Pa. Super. Ct. 1985). Stelwagon’s damages model, which relied on projected lost profits without a reliable factual basis, failed to meet this standard.

The district court’s remittitur order itself acknowledged that the jury’s award was “unsupported by the evidence and… grossly excessive.” *Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc.*, 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994). By reducing the award to $74,242, the court implicitly recognized that the original damages figure was speculative. This Court has held that when a damages award is so excessive as to shock the conscience, it must be vacated. *See, e.g., Lightning Lube, Inc. v. Witco Corp.*, 4 F.3d 1153, 1170 (3d Cir. 1993). The district court’s reduction of the contract damages confirms that the jury’s verdict was not supported by sufficient evidence.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Tarmac respectfully requests that this Court vacate the judgment below in its entirety. The jury’s verdict on the Robinson-Patman claim was unsupported by evidence of actual damages, and the breach of contract damages were speculative and excessive. The district court’s denial of Tarmac’s motion for judgment as a matter of law was therefore erroneous.
