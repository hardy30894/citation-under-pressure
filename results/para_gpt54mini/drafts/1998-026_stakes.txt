**Argument**

A district court’s failure to advise a defendant of the right to appeal, though contrary to Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32, does not itself warrant collateral relief where the defendant already knew of the right and knowingly declined to appeal. Habeas relief is reserved for violations that result in a cognizable injury, and this Court has repeatedly held that not every Rule-based error is grounds to disturb a final judgment. See *United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780, 783-85 (1979) (holding that a technical violation of Rule 11 is not cognizable on collateral review absent a complete miscarriage of justice); *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (same).

Rule 32’s advisement requirement is designed to ensure that a defendant who may be unaware of appellate rights is informed in time to decide whether to appeal. But where the district court’s omission causes no loss of the opportunity to appeal, there is no basis for habeas relief. The controlling inquiry on collateral review is prejudice, not formal noncompliance alone. *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784-85; *United States v. Addonizio*, 442 U.S. 178, 184-86 (1979) (collateral relief is not available for every error that might justify reversal on direct review; the error must amount to a fundamental defect resulting in a complete miscarriage of justice).

That principle disposes of this case. The District Court held, after an evidentiary hearing, that Peguero knew of his right to appeal when sentence was imposed. That finding defeats his habeas claim. If the defendant already knew of the right, the court’s failure to repeat the advisement did not deprive him of the ability to file a timely appeal, did not mislead him, and did not produce any injury cognizable under § 2255. See *Frady v. United States*, 456 U.S. 152, 166 (1982) (collateral review is not a substitute for direct appeal); *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 621-22 (1998) (same).

Peguero’s contrary position—that any violation of Rule 32 automatically vacates the sentence—would improperly convert a procedural rule into a jurisdictional command. This Court has rejected that approach in analogous settings. In *Timmreck*, the Court refused to grant collateral relief for a Rule 11 defect even though the plea colloquy was not conducted exactly as the Rule prescribed, explaining that “a formal violation” is not enough. 441 U.S. at 785. The same reasoning applies here. Rule 32 is meant to protect the right to appeal, not to supply a windfall where that right was already known and intentionally forgone.

Nor does the failure to advise Peguero constitute structural error requiring automatic vacatur. This Court has reserved automatic reversal for a narrow class of fundamental defects that infect the entire proceeding, such as the denial of counsel. *Arizona v. Fulminante*, 499 U.S. 279, 309-10 (1991); *Penson v. Ohio*, 488 U.S. 75, 88-89 (1988). An omitted appeal advisement is not comparable. Where, as here, the defendant knew of the right and chose not to exercise it, the omission did not infect the proceeding or nullify the judgment.

The Court of Appeals correctly recognized that the purpose of the rule was served. A harmless-error analysis is consistent with this Court’s treatment of procedural defaults on collateral review. *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 623 (1993) (habeas relief requires more than ordinary trial error; the error must have had a substantial and injurious effect or influence); *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 776 (1946). Because Peguero suffered no prejudice from the omission, there is no basis to set aside his sentence.

Finally, petitioner’s rule would undermine finality without advancing fairness. Habeas corpus is not designed to provide a second chance to litigate an issue that caused no real harm. *Addonizio*, 442 U.S. at 184-86; *Timmreck*, 441 U.S. at 784-85. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
