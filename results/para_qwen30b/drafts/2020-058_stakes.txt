**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a constitutes an “admission” into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255—is one of statutory interpretation, and the answer must be grounded in the text, structure, and purpose of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of TPS satisfies the “inspected and admitted or paroled” requirement of § 1255(a). That holding misreads the statutory framework, conflates distinct legal concepts, and undermines the carefully calibrated distinctions Congress drew between admission and status. We respectfully urge this Court to reverse.

### I. THE TEXT OF THE INA CLEARLY DISTINGUISHES “ADMISSION” FROM “LAWFUL STATUS,” AND A GRANT OF TPS DOES NOT CONSTITUTE THE FORMER

The INA defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A). This definition is not merely procedural—it is substantive and exclusive. The term “admission” refers to a physical, factual event: the moment an alien crosses the border under official inspection and is authorized to enter. As this Court has held, “admission” involves “physical entrance into the country,” and is “inapposite to adjustment of status in removal proceedings, a procedure that is structured to take place entirely within the United States.” *Hanif v. Att’y Gen.*, 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012); see also *Taveras v. Att’y Gen.*, 731 F.3d 281, 290 (3d Cir. 2013).

The Third Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission”—rests on a fundamental misreading of the statutory text. The court held that because § 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant,” that status equates to admission. But the statute does not say so. It says nothing about “admission,” and it does not equate “lawful status” with “admission.” Indeed, the very existence of both terms in the same statutory scheme—§ 1255(a) requiring “inspected and admitted or paroled,” and § 1254a(f)(4) conferring “lawful status”—demonstrates Congress’s intent to preserve their distinction.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Espinosa-Guillot*, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653 (BIA 2011), is misplaced. That case held that an adjustment of status “generally constitutes an admission,” but only because the adjustment process itself involves a formal, in-country determination of eligibility that includes inspection and authorization. *Id.* at 654. That is not the case with TPS. A grant of TPS does not involve inspection or authorization at the border; it is a post-entry administrative designation. As this Court has recognized, “the date of gaining a new status is not the same as the date of the physical event of entering the country.” *Hanif*, 694 F.3d at 485. To equate the two is to ignore the foundational difference between *entry* and *status*.

### II. THE STRUCTURE OF THE INA REINFORCES THAT TPS DOES NOT CONSTITUTE AN ADMISSION

Congress did not intend for TPS to serve as a backdoor to lawful permanent residence. The structure of the INA reflects a deliberate hierarchy: admission is a prerequisite for adjustment of status, and Congress provided only narrow exceptions to that rule. Section 1255(h) and (i) create exceptions for certain special immigrants and aliens eligible for visas—categories that are explicitly defined and subject to specific statutory criteria. But TPS recipients are not included in those exceptions.

The absence of a TPS-specific exception to the admission requirement is not an oversight. As this Court has held, “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.” *INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987). Congress could have said that a grant of TPS constitutes an admission. It did not. Instead, it created a separate, temporary relief mechanism—TPS—designed to protect individuals already in the country from removal due to extraordinary conditions in their home nations. See *8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)*. The fact that TPS recipients are “considered” to be in lawful status for certain purposes does not mean they are deemed admitted.

Moreover, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to satisfy § 1255(a)’s admission requirement would render § 1254a(h)(2) superfluous. That subsection requires a supermajority of the Senate to pass legislation adjusting the status of TPS recipients. If TPS recipients could adjust status under § 1255 simply by virtue of their status, Congress’s deliberate, high bar for legislative action would be circumvented. This Court has long held that “a statute should be construed so that effect is given to all its provisions, so that no part will be inoperative or superfluous.” *Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004). The Third Circuit’s interpretation violates that principle.
