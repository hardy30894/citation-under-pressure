**ARGUMENT**

I. THE GOVERNMENT’S PHYSICAL OCCUPATION OF TUTHILL’S PROPERTY CONSTITUTES A COMPENSABLE PHYSICAL TAKING UNDER THE FIFTH AMENDMENT.

The Fifth Amendment’s Takings Clause provides that private property shall not “be taken for public use, without just compensation.” U.S. Const. amend. V. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a “permanent physical occupation” of property, authorized by government, constitutes a taking per se, regardless of the public interests it may serve. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426 (1982). In *Loretto*, the Court held that even a minor physical intrusion—such as the installation of a cable box on a landlord’s building—effects a taking when it is permanent and authorized by the government. *Id.* at 435-36. The Court explained that such an occupation “is qualitatively more severe” than a regulatory restriction because it “completely exclude[s] the owner from occupying or using that space.” *Id.* at 435.

Here, the government’s installation of fiber optic cables across Tuthill’s property, coupled with its leasing of excess capacity to third-party telecommunications companies, constitutes a permanent physical occupation. The easement granted in 1953 permitted only the installation of “signal lines ... necessary and in connection with” the BPA’s power transmission lines. The government’s decision to install 36- or 72-fiber cables—far exceeding its current operational needs—does not transform the physical presence of those cables into a non-compensable use. *See United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748 (1947) (holding that the government’s physical occupation of land for a flood control project was a taking, even though the land was later returned to its original use). The fact that the government now leases unused fibers to third parties further underscores the permanent nature of the occupation, as Tuthill is deprived of the ability to use or exclude others from the space occupied by the cables. *See Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435 (noting that a physical occupation is a taking even if it is of “minimal economic impact”).

The government’s reliance on *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164 (1979), is misplaced. In *Kaiser Aetna*, the Court held that the government’s imposition of a public right of access to a private marina did not constitute a taking because the intrusion was not physical. *Id.* at 176. Here, by contrast, the government’s installation of fiber optic cables is a physical occupation, not a regulatory restriction. *See also PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins*, 447 U.S. 74, 83 (1980) (distinguishing *Loretto* and noting that physical occupations are per se takings).

II. THE GOVERNMENT’S EXPANSION OF THE EASEMENT’S SCOPE BEYOND ITS ORIGINAL TERMS DOES NOT CURE ITS LIABILITY FOR A PHYSICAL TAKING.

The government argues that its installation of fiber optic cables falls within the scope of the 1953 easement because the cables are “signal lines ... necessary and in connection with” the BPA’s power transmission lines. However, the Supreme Court has made clear that the government cannot avoid liability for a physical taking by expanding the scope of an easement beyond its original terms. In *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256 (1946), the Court held that the government’s frequent flights over a landowner’s property constituted a taking, even though the landowner had no right to exclude aircraft from the airspace. *Id.* at 266. Similarly, in *Loretto*, the Court rejected the argument that the government’s authorization of a cable installation cured the taking, noting that the government’s “consent does not obviate the fact of occupation.” *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 440.

Here, the government’s decision to install fiber optic cables far exceeding its current needs, and to lease excess capacity to third parties, constitutes an expansion of the easement’s scope beyond its original terms. The easement did not contemplate the installation of cables capable of leasing to unrelated third parties, nor did it authorize the government to occupy Tuthill’s property for commercial purposes unrelated to power transmission. *See Nollan v. California Coastal Comm’n*, 483 U.S. 825, 831 (1987) (holding that a government action that imposes a condition unrelated to the original purpose of a permit constitutes a taking).

III. THE COURT OF FEDERAL CLAIMS ERRED IN CONCLUDING THAT TUTHILL’S CLAIM SOUNDS IN TRESPASS RATHER THAN TAKING.

The Court of Federal Claims erred in dismissing Tuthill’s claim as sounding in trespass rather than taking. While it is true that the misuse of an easement may give rise to a trespass claim, *see, e.g., Restatement (Third) of Property (Servitudes) § 8.5 cmt. a (2000)*, the Supreme Court has made clear that a physical occupation authorized by the government constitutes a taking, regardless of whether it is characterized as a trespass. *See Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435-36. The Court has repeatedly held that the Takings Clause provides a remedy for physical occupations, even when the government has not exceeded the scope of an easement. *See, e.g., Causby*, 328 U.S. at 266; *Dickinson*, 331 U.S. at 748.

The government’s argument that Tuthill’s claim is merely one for trespass is unavailing. A trespass claim seeks damages for harm caused by an unauthorized intrusion, while a takings claim seeks just compensation for a government-authorized occupation of property. *See PruneYard*, 447 U.S. at 83. Here, the government’s installation of fiber optic cables and leasing of excess capacity to third parties is authorized by the government, making it a taking, not a trespass. *See Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435-36.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Tuthill respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Federal Claims and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
