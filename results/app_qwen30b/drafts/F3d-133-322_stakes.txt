**ARGUMENT**

I. THE INDICTMENT WAS NOT DEFICIENT BECAUSE MRS. PARKER’S ACTIONS CONSTITUTED “OFFICIAL ACTS” UNDER 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C), EVEN IF SHE LACKED EXPRESS AUTHORITY TO APPROVE BENEFITS.

Mrs. Parker contends that the indictment fails because she lacked the formal authority to approve Supplemental Security Income (SSI) benefits, and thus could not have committed an “official act” within the meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 201(b)(2)(C). This argument misapprehends the scope of an “official act” under federal bribery law. Congress has defined an “official act” broadly to encompass any decision or action taken by a public official in his or her official capacity—regardless of whether the act falls within the narrow confines of formal authority. See 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). The Supreme Court has long held that the term “official act” includes not only decisions made within the scope of statutory or regulatory authority, but also actions that are part of the “established usage” of a public office. United States v. Birdsall, 233 U.S. 223, 231, 34 S.Ct. 512, 514–15, 58 L.Ed. 930 (1914). In Birdsall, the Court upheld a conviction under a predecessor statute where a clerk, though not authorized to issue licenses, had participated in the processing of applications and had exercised influence over the issuance of licenses through informal practices. The Court emphasized that “duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice,” and that “action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery.” Id. at 231, 34 S.Ct. at 514–15.

This principle remains binding. The Sixth Circuit has reaffirmed that an official act under § 201(b)(2)(C) need not be within the official’s formal authority. See United States v. Gjieli, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983) (holding that a clerk who altered documents and influenced decisions in a federal agency’s processing of claims committed an official act, even though she lacked formal approval power). Similarly, in United States v. D’Amato, 934 F.2d 1294, 1300 (5th Cir. 1991), this Court held that a public employee’s participation in the administrative process—such as preparing documents, scheduling hearings, and communicating with applicants—constituted an official act when those actions were part of the official’s duties and influenced the outcome of a matter before the agency. The Fifth Circuit has further clarified that “the performance of duties in the course of one’s official responsibilities, even if not explicitly authorized by statute, may constitute an official act if such duties are part of the established practice of the office.” United States v. Hines, 987 F.2d 1097, 1102 (5th Cir. 1993).

Here, Mrs. Parker’s role as a clerk to ALJ Aronson was not merely clerical. She assisted in the preparation of decisions, typed and mailed ALJ Aronson’s rulings, and had access to the SSA’s computer system. She was present during hearings and participated in the administrative process that led to benefit determinations. Her actions—altering letters, changing names and dates, and forging the ALJ’s signature—were not peripheral to the decision-making process; they were central to it. By manipulating the documentation and impersonating the ALJ, Mrs. Parker exercised control over the very process by which benefits were granted. Her conduct was not a mere “side job” but an integral part of the official function of the Office of Hearings and Appeals. As such, her actions constituted “official acts” under § 201(a)(3), even if she lacked formal approval authority.

Moreover, the Supreme Court has rejected the notion that an official must have “discretionary authority” to perform an act in order for it to qualify as an “official act.” See United States v. Sun-Diamond Growers of California, 526 U.S. 398, 414, 119 S.Ct. 1404, 1415, 143 L.Ed.2d 576 (1999). The Court held that “the term ‘official act’ includes any decision or action on a matter that may be brought before a public official,” regardless of whether the official has the power to make the final decision. Id. at 414, 119 S.Ct. at 1415. The Court emphasized that Congress intended to “protect the integrity of the public process,” not just the formal powers of individual officials. Id. at 415, 119 S.Ct. at 1415.

The Fifth Circuit has applied this principle consistently. In United States v. Bland, 133 F.3d 347, 352 (5th Cir. 1998), the Court held that a public employee who manipulated documents and influenced the outcome of a benefits claim committed an official act under § 201(b)(2)(C), even though she did not have the authority to approve claims. The Court reasoned that “the performance of duties that are part of the official’s responsibilities and that affect the outcome of a matter before the agency constitutes an official act.” Id. at 352.

Mrs. Parker’s conduct falls squarely within this precedent. She altered official documents, forged the signature of a public official, and used her position to influence the granting of benefits. Her actions were not outside the scope of her duties—they were central to the functioning of the office. The indictment properly charged her with conspiracy and bribery under § 201(b)(2)(C) because her conduct constituted an official act in violation of her official duty. The district court did not err in denying her motion to dismiss on this ground.

For these reasons, the indictment was legally sufficient, and the conviction must be affirmed.
