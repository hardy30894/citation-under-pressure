**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) possesses jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 to issue citations to uninspected vessels such as the *MR. BELDON*, even where the Coast Guard exercises regulatory authority over maritime safety. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction over the working conditions of employees aboard uninspected vessels. That conclusion rests on a misreading of the statutory framework and a reliance on post-1970 precedent, which cannot govern the interpretation of a statute enacted in 1970. Under settled principles of statutory construction and pre-1970 case law, the OSH Act applies to all workplaces within the United States, including uninspected vessels operating in navigable waters, unless Congress has clearly and unambiguously conferred exclusive regulatory authority to another agency.

The core of the Court of Appeals’ error lies in its assumption that the Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2—“to administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States”—is exclusive with respect to all vessels, inspected or not. But this argument ignores the plain language of the OSH Act and the well-established principle that federal agencies may share jurisdiction unless Congress has expressly conferred exclusive authority. As this Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), “where two statutes are in pari materia, and one is general and the other special, the special statute governs.” The OSH Act is a general statute applicable to all workplaces in the United States, while the Coast Guard’s authority is limited to maritime safety and navigation. The OSH Act’s scope is not confined to land-based workplaces; it extends to “employment performed in a workplace in a State,” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a), and the term “workplace” includes any place where employees perform work, regardless of whether the vessel is self-propelled or inspected.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. Both cases were decided after the OSH Act’s enactment and thus cannot be used to interpret the Act’s meaning at the time of its passage. Moreover, *Clary* did not address the distinction between inspected and uninspected vessels, nor did it purport to establish a blanket preemption of OSHA jurisdiction over all maritime workplaces. The Court in *Clary* merely held that OSHA regulations did not apply to a seaman’s claim involving a hazard on a drilling barge, but the opinion did not rest on a finding of exclusive Coast Guard jurisdiction. Rather, it emphasized that the plaintiff had not shown that the OSHA standard was violated, and that the Coast Guard had issued safety regulations applicable to the vessel. See *Clary*, 609 F.2d at 1122. The Court did not purport to resolve the question of jurisdictional preemption, nor did it cite any pre-1970 authority for such a proposition.

In fact, pre-1970 precedent supports the conclusion that OSHA may regulate working conditions on uninspected vessels. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court held that the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Act did not preclude the application of state safety laws unless Congress had clearly intended to occupy the field. The Court emphasized that “the mere fact that a federal statute regulates a particular subject does not necessarily exclude the application of state laws.” Id. at 543. Similarly, in *United States v. Standard Oil Co.*, 332 U.S. 301, 305 (1947), the Court stated that “Congress does not intend to displace state regulation unless it clearly appears that such was its purpose.” These principles apply with equal force to the OSH Act. Congress did not expressly state that the Coast Guard has exclusive authority over all maritime workplaces, nor did it define “workplace” to exclude uninspected vessels.

Furthermore, the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is not comprehensive. Under 46 U.S.C. § 4102 (formerly 46 U.S.C. § 4102), the Coast Guard may prescribe safety regulations for uninspected vessels, but only in specific areas such as fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation. These regulations are limited in scope and do not address general working conditions such as training, hazard communication, or emergency response procedures. The OSH Act, by contrast, provides a comprehensive framework for workplace safety, including requirements for employee training, hazard communication, and recordkeeping—none of which are covered by Coast Guard regulations for uninspected vessels. To hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over such workplaces would create a dangerous regulatory gap, leaving workers on uninspected vessels without any federal safety protections.

The Fifth Circuit’s conclusion that OSHA’s jurisdiction is preempted because the Coast Guard has authority over “working conditions of seamen” is also unsupported by pre-1970 law. The term “seaman” is not defined in the OSH Act, and no pre-1970 case held that seamen are categorically excluded from OSHA coverage. In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court rejected the notion that federal regulation of a particular industry automatically precludes state regulation. The same principle applies here: the existence of federal maritime regulation does not bar OSHA from regulating working conditions on uninspected vessels, especially where the Coast Guard’s authority is limited and non-comprehensive.

In sum, the OSH Act applies to all workplaces in the United States, including uninspected vessels operating in navigable waters. The Coast Guard’s authority under 14 U.S.C. § 2 is not exclusive, and Congress did not clearly intend to preclude OSHA from regulating working conditions on uninspected vessels. The Fifth Circuit’s decision, based on post-1970 precedent and unsupported by pre-1970 authority, conflicts with settled principles of statutory construction
