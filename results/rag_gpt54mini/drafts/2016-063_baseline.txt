**Argument**

The Civil Service Reform Act channels review of Merit Systems Protection Board decisions in “mixed cases” to district court, not the Federal Circuit. A mixed case is one in which an employee was affected by an action appealable to the MSPB and alleges discrimination prohibited by federal law. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41, 50-56 (2012). The Board’s own disposition here fits that description: Perry challenged a suspension and retirement he alleges were coerced by discrimination and retaliation. The only dispute is whether the Board’s dismissal for lack of jurisdiction changes the forum. It does not.

In *Kloeckner*, this Court held that district court review is required when the MSPB dismisses a mixed case on a “procedural ground[]” rather than on the merits. 568 U.S. at 56-60. The Court explained that the statute directs “mixed cases—those appealable to the MSPB and alleging discrimination” to district court. Id. at 56. Nothing in the text of § 7703(b)(2) turns on whether the Board reached the merits or instead disposed of the case on a threshold ground. Id. at 54-56. The same statutory command governs here.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with *Kloeckner*’s reasoning. The statute asks whether the employee “has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board” and alleges discrimination. 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1); *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 56-57. Perry was affected by removal-related discipline and alleges discrimination. That makes his case a mixed case at the outset, before the Board decides whether it has jurisdiction. *Kloeckner* did not make district-court review depend on the Board’s self-characterization of its ruling. To the contrary, the Court rejected a forum rule that would “complicate” the statutory scheme and “create[] a strange world” in which the proper forum would vary depending on the Board’s disposition. Id. at 54-55, 60.

This Court’s decision in *Elgin v. Department of the Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1 (2012), confirms that the CSRA’s review provisions are meant to channel covered personnel disputes through the prescribed judicial path, while preserving the statutory exception for discrimination cases. *Elgin* explained that after the MSPB renders a decision, the Federal Circuit has “exclusive jurisdiction on appeal,” but only “[a]s already” provided by the CSRA’s review provisions. 567 U.S. at 5-6. The Court emphasized the statute’s carefully structured review scheme, not a mechanical rule that every jurisdictional dismissal goes to the Federal Circuit. Id. at 5-11. And *Gregory* likewise recognized the Board’s role in independently reviewing disciplinary actions while applying the governing statutory framework. *United States Postal Service v. Gregory*, 534 U.S. 1, 6-10 (2001).

The government and Federal Circuit cannot avoid *Kloeckner* by labeling Perry’s dismissal “jurisdictional.” The distinction is not dispositive under the statute. What matters is that Perry’s claim is a discrimination-based challenge to an action that is the subject of MSPB review. In *Kloeckner*, the Court was explicit that the “proper forum for judicial review” of a mixed case is district court whether the Board rules on the merits or disposes of the case on a non-merits ground. 568 U.S. at 50-56. A jurisdictional dismissal does not erase the mixed nature of the employee’s claim; it merely reflects the Board’s conclusion that the employee has not established MSPB jurisdiction. But the forum question is one for § 7703, not for the Board’s own threshold ruling.

The contrary view would also create the very uncertainty this Court sought to avoid in *Kloeckner*. If the proper forum depended on whether the Board’s threshold ruling is labeled “procedural” or “jurisdictional,” employees would be forced to litigate forum questions before they can litigate their rights. That is inconsistent with the straightforward statutory command that mixed cases go to district court. *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 50-56. It is also inconsistent with Congress’s decision to route discrimination claims to a court equipped to adjudicate them in full. See *University of Tennessee v. Elliott*, 478 U.S. 788, 796-99 (1986) (recognizing the special federal interest in discrimination claims and independent judicial adjudication of federal civil-rights issues).

Finally, this Court should not allow the Federal Circuit’s pre-*Kloeckner* line to control a question that *Kloeckner* answered in substance. The Court there rejected a forum regime that would send mixed cases to the Federal Circuit whenever the MSPB failed to reach the merits. 568 U.S. at 50-56. A jurisdictional dismissal is no different in principle. Perry alleged discrimination in a matter otherwise appealable to the MSPB; under § 7703(b)(2), district court is the proper forum.

The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to transfer the petition to the district court.
