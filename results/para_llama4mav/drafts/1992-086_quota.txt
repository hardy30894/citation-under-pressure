The Center Moriches Union Free School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request to use school facilities for a religious-oriented film series on family values and child rearing constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a limited public forum, violating the First Amendment's freedom of speech.

The Supreme Court has established that the extent of permissible governmental regulation of expressive activity on publicly owned property depends on the character of the public property in question (Perry Education Ass'n v. Perry Local Educators' Ass'n, 460 U.S. 37, 44 (1983)). The Court has identified three categories of publicly owned property: traditional public forums, limited or designated public forums, and nonpublic forums (Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense and Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985)).

The Center Moriches School District facilities are a limited public forum, as they have been opened for use by the general public for certain purposes (Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260, 267 (1988)). In a limited public forum, the government may not exclude speech based on its content unless the exclusion is reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum and is viewpoint-neutral (Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of the Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995)).

The School District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's film series is not viewpoint-neutral, as it is based on the religious content of the speech (Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist., 959 F.2d 381, 386 (2d Cir. 1992) (Miner, J., dissenting)). The District's policy of excluding religious groups from using school facilities is a content-based exclusion that is subject to strict scrutiny (Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 269-70 (1981)).

The Supreme Court has held that a state university's denial of access to facilities to a student religious group was unconstitutional, as the university had created a limited public forum by allowing other student groups to use the facilities (Widmar, 454 U.S. at 273). Similarly, in Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226 (1990), the Court held that a high school's denial of a request to form a Christian club was a denial of equal access under the Equal Access Act, as the school had created a limited open forum by allowing noncurriculum-related student groups to use the facilities.

The School District's attempt to distinguish Widmar and Mergens is unpersuasive, as the same principles apply to the use of school facilities by outside organizations (Lamb's Chapel, 959 F.2d at 388 (Miner, J., dissenting)). The fact that Lamb's Chapel is an outside organization does not justify the exclusion of its speech, as the School District has opened its facilities to other outside organizations for similar purposes (see Gregoire v. Centennial School Dist., 907 F.2d 1366, 1374 (3d Cir. 1990)).

Furthermore, the School District's exclusion of Lamb's Chapel's speech is not justified by a compelling state interest (Perry, 460 U.S. at 45). The District's concern about avoiding an Establishment Clause violation is not a sufficient justification, as the Supreme Court has held that the Establishment Clause does not require the exclusion of religious speech from a limited public forum (Widmar, 454 U.S. at 271-73).

In fact, allowing Lamb's Chapel to use the school facilities would not violate the Establishment Clause, as the use would be for a secular purpose, would not have the primary effect of advancing religion, and would not foster excessive government entanglement with religion (Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971)). The School District's denial of Lamb's Chapel's request is therefore a content-based exclusion of speech that violates the First Amendment's freedom of speech.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the principles of the First Amendment.
