**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit’s Precedent Correctly Recognizes Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims That Require Resolution of Substantial Patent Issues**

The Supreme Court has long held that federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over cases “arising under” federal patent law. *See Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988) (holding that § 1338(a) grants federal courts jurisdiction over state-law claims that “necessarily depend on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law”). This principle applies with equal force to state legal malpractice claims that hinge on the proper application of patent law. *See Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262, 1271–73 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (finding exclusive federal jurisdiction where the plaintiff’s malpractice claim required resolution of a substantial patent issue).

The Federal Circuit’s reasoning in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP*, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007), is consistent with Supreme Court precedent. As the Court explained in *Christianson*, federal jurisdiction exists where a state-law claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue, actually disputed and substantial.” *Id.* at 813 (quoting *Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng’g & Mfg.*, 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005)). The Federal Circuit correctly applied this test in *Air Measurement*, where it held that the plaintiff’s malpractice claim required resolution of a substantial patent issue—whether the defendant’s conduct in prior patent litigation constituted inequitable conduct. *Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1271–73. The same analysis applies here, where Minton’s malpractice claim turns on whether his attorneys should have raised the experimental use exception under § 102(b) of the Patent Act. *See* 35 U.S.C. § 102(b).

**II. The Grable Factors Are Satisfied Here, and the Texas Supreme Court Erred in Concluding Otherwise**

The Texas Supreme Court’s decision conflicts with the Supreme Court’s framework in *Grable*, which requires that a federal issue be (1) necessarily raised, (2) actually disputed, (3) substantial, and (4) capable of resolution without disrupting the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. The Federal Circuit has correctly applied these factors in patent malpractice cases. *See Air Measurement*, 504 F.3d at 1271–73; *Immunocept*, 504 F.3d at 1281.

Here, the experimental use exception is a necessary element of Minton’s malpractice claim. To prevail, he must prove that his attorneys’ failure to raise the exception proximately caused his injury. *See Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs.*, 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). The exception is also disputed—Gunn argues it was not viable under the facts, while Minton contends it would have saved his patent. Moreover, the issue is substantial, as it implicates the federal patent laws governing patent validity. *See Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09. Finally, federal resolution of the issue will not disrupt the balance of judicial responsibilities, as the patent issue is embedded in a state malpractice claim. *See Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314.

The Texas Supreme Court’s contrary conclusion misapplies *Grable* by focusing on the state-law nature of the malpractice claim rather than the embedded federal issue. *See* Slip Op. at 12–13. As the Supreme Court has made clear, federal jurisdiction exists where a state-law claim “necessarily raise[s] a stated federal issue.” *Grable*, 545 U.S. at 314. The experimental use exception is such an issue here.

**III. The Federal Circuit’s Precedent Is Binding and Consistent with Supreme Court Jurisprudence**

The Texas Supreme Court’s refusal to follow the Federal Circuit’s well-reasoned decisions in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* is erroneous. The Federal Circuit is the nation’s specialized court for patent appeals, and its interpretations of patent law are entitled to deference. *See U.S. v. Carlo Biado*, 778 F.3d 1251, 1255 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (noting that the Federal Circuit’s patent jurisdiction is exclusive and its decisions are authoritative on patent issues). The Supreme Court has recognized that the Federal Circuit’s expertise in patent law justifies its role in shaping patent jurisprudence. *See Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. 370, 390 (1996).

The dissent’s reliance on non-patent cases to cabin federal jurisdiction is misplaced. *See* Slip Op. at 1 (Green, J., dissenting). The Supreme Court has expressly held that the “arising under” language in § 1338(a) is construed consistently with § 1331. *Christianson*, 486 U.S. at 808–09. The Federal Circuit’s application of *Grable* in patent malpractice cases is thus consistent with Supreme Court precedent.

**IV. Other Courts Have Recognized Exclusive Federal Jurisdiction in Similar Cases**

Numerous courts have followed the Federal Circuit’s lead in recognizing exclusive federal jurisdiction over state malpractice claims that require resolution of substantial patent issues. *See, e.g., USPPS, LLC v. Avery Dennison Corp.*, 647 F.3d 274, 278–81 (5th Cir. 2011) (applying *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept*); *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 702 N.W.2d 336, 342 (Neb. 2005) (finding federal jurisdiction where patent law was a necessary element of the malpractice claim); *E-Pass Techs., Inc. v. Moses & Singer, LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 1152 (2010) (same). These decisions confirm that the Federal Circuit’s approach is correct and should be followed.

**CONCLUSION**

The Texas Supreme Court erred in concluding that it possessed jurisdiction over Minton’s malpractice claim. The Federal Circuit’s decisions in *Air Measurement* and *Immunocept* correctly recognize exclusive federal jurisdiction where, as here, a state malpractice claim requires resolution of a substantial patent issue. This Court should reverse the judgment below and remand with instructions to dismiss the case for lack of jurisdiction.
