**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s ADA and MHRA claims because a genuine dispute exists as to whether Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled.**

The ADA prohibits discrimination against a qualified individual “on the basis of disability.” 42 U.S.C. § 12112(a). A plaintiff establishes a “regarded as” disability by showing that the employer mistakenly believed he had an impairment that substantially limits a major life activity. *Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc.*, 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). The major life activity of working is substantially limited when the employee is “significantly restricted in the ability to perform either a class of jobs or a broad range of jobs in various classes.” *Fjellestad v. Pizza Hut of Am., Inc.*, 188 F.3d 944, 949 (8th Cir. 1999) (quoting 29 C.F.R. § 1630.2(j)(3)(i)). An employer’s belief that an employee cannot perform a single, particular job does not suffice. *Toyota Motor Mfg., Ky., Inc. v. Williams*, 534 U.S. 184, 200 (2002).

Here, Chief Hardy recommended termination not because Epps could not be a police officer for a particular reason, but because Dr. Mirkin opined that Epps’s degenerative disk disease would “very likely need to seek a different type of occupation.” Hardy’s second memorandum concluded that Epps “will never be able to function as a regular officer” and noted that no light-duty positions existed. This evidence demonstrates that Pine Lawn perceived Epps as precluded from an entire class of jobs—those requiring bending, stooping, squatting, or carrying equipment—which is precisely the class of jobs encompassed by “regular officer” duties. *Cf. Webb v. Garelick Mfg. Co.*, 94 F.3d 484, 488 (8th Cir. 1996) (finding genuine issue where employer believed employee’s back condition prevented him from performing “a broad range of jobs” requiring heavy lifting). The district court incorrectly required Epps to prove he could not perform any job, when the “regarded as” inquiry focuses on the employer’s perception. *Cooper v. Olin Corp.*, 246 F.3d 1083, 1089 (8th Cir. 2001). Because a jury could find that Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in the major life activity of working, summary judgment was improper.

**II. The district court erred in granting summary judgment on Epps’s workers’ compensation retaliation claim because sovereign immunity does not bar a statutory claim under Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780.**

Section 287.780 prohibits an employer from discriminating against an employee for exercising rights under the workers’ compensation law. Missouri’s sovereign immunity doctrine does not bar this claim because it arises from a statute, not from common-law tort. *State ex rel. Bd. of Trustees v. Russell*, 843 S.W.2d 353, 358 (Mo. 1992) (en banc) (holding that immunity is not a defense to claims “based upon a statute that expressly creates a cause of action”). The Missouri Court of Appeals has repeatedly applied § 287.780 against public entities without immunity. *Henson v. City of St. Louis*, 60 S.W.3d 654, 657 (Mo. Ct. App. 2001) (city not immune from retaliation claim because it “arises from a statutory right that exists independently of common law”). *See also Keeton v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 920 S.W.2d 555, 560 (Mo. Ct. App. 1996) (explaining that § 287.780 provides a statutory cause of action, not a tort). The district court’s holding that sovereign immunity bars this claim directly conflicts with these precedents.

Moreover, even if immunity could apply, Pine Lawn waived it by purchasing workers’ compensation insurance. *Weiler v. City of St. Louis*, 5 S.W.3d 545, 549 (Mo. Ct. App. 1999) (holding that purchase of liability insurance waives sovereign immunity to the extent of coverage). The record shows that Pine Lawn’s carrier initially covered Epps’s injuries, confirming the existence of insurance. Summary judgment was therefore erroneous.

**III. The district court likewise erred in barring Epps’s intentional infliction of emotional distress claim under sovereign immunity, as genuine issues of fact remain regarding waiver.**

The same analysis applies: Pine Lawn’s procurement of liability insurance, including for workers’ compensation matters, creates a factual dispute as to waiver. *See Weiler*, 5 S.W.3d at 549. Additionally, Hardy’s actions in repeatedly recommending termination based on Epps’s protected workers’ compensation history may fall outside discretionary immunity. This Court should reverse summary judgment on this claim as well.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for further proceedings.
