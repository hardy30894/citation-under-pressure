Respondent respectfully submits that the judgment should be affirmed. ANILCA § 103(c) does not bar the National Park Service from enforcing generally applicable regulations within the geographic boundaries of an Alaska park unit where those regulations are directed to federal lands and park administration, rather than to nonfederal land as such. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the hovercraft ban applies in Yukon-Charley notwithstanding the State’s ownership of submerged lands beneath the Nation River. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 872 F.3d 927, 931-41 (9th Cir. 2017).

ANILCA’s text supports that result. Section 103(c) states that “No lands which, before, on, or after December 2, 1980, are conveyed to the State, Native Corporation, or any private party . . . shall be subject to the regulations applicable solely to public lands within such units.” 16 U.S.C. § 3102(c). By its terms, the provision limits only regulations “applicable solely to public lands”; it does not strip the National Park Service of all authority to regulate conduct occurring on nonfederal lands or waters within Alaska park unit boundaries. The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that statutory interpretation begins with the text and structure Congress enacted. *Robinson v. Shell Oil Co.*, 519 U.S. 337, 340-41 (1997); *United States v. Gonzales*, 520 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1997).

That reading fits ANILCA as a whole. Congress made clear that Alaska park units were not ordinary park areas and that, in many respects, federal authority would remain operative. Section 1110(a) broadly guarantees access within park units, while § 103(c) specifically protects conveyed lands from regulations “applicable solely to public lands.” 16 U.S.C. §§ 3102(c), 3170(a). The Supreme Court has recognized that “statutory construction is a holistic endeavor.” *United Sav. Ass’n of Tex. v. Timbers of Inwood Forest Assocs., Ltd.*, 484 U.S. 365, 371 (1988). Reading § 103(c) to disable the Park Service from enforcing generally applicable park regulations everywhere within a park unit would create a rule Congress did not write.

The Ninth Circuit’s own cases confirm that the federal government retains significant authority in Alaska park waters and over park administration. In *John v. United States (Katie John III)*, 720 F.3d 1214, 1216-20 (9th Cir. 2013), the court explained that ANILCA must be read against preexisting federal authority and that the Act did not displace all federal regulation in Alaska. Earlier, in *John v. United States (Katie John II)*, 247 F.3d 1032, 1034-39 (9th Cir. 2001) (en banc) (per curiam), the court rejected a cramped reading of ANILCA that would have needlessly cut back federal power. And in *State of Alaska v. Babbitt (Katie John I)*, 72 F.3d 698, 701-06 (9th Cir. 1995), the court recognized the continuing force of federal authority in navigable waters within conservation system units. Those cases do not turn § 103(c) into a blanket prohibition on park regulations affecting state or private lands and waters.

Petitioner’s contrary reading also collides with settled administrative-law principles. Where Congress has not directly forbidden the agency’s construction, a court asks whether the interpretation is reasonable. *Chevron U.S.A. Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). The Park Service’s longstanding view that its park-wide safety and resource-protection regulations may apply within Alaska park boundaries is, at minimum, a permissible interpretation of ANILCA’s text. Agency inconsistency does not defeat deference. *Nat’l Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981 (2005).

Nor does petitioner’s reliance on state title to submerged lands change the analysis. Federal power under the Property Clause extends to lands and waters within federal jurisdiction, and the Supreme Court has long held that Congress may regulate conduct on nonfederal lands when necessary to protect federal property. *Kleppe v. New Mexico*, 426 U.S. 529, 539-40 (1976); *Camfield v. United States*, 167 U.S. 518, 525-29 (1897). The hovercraft ban is a general park regulation enacted to govern activity in a national preserve and to protect visitors and resources. Nothing in § 103(c) withdraws that authority.

Finally, petitioner’s theory would produce anomalous results. It would require the Park Service to police a park unit’s boundaries as though state and private inholdings were regulatory islands, even where Congress created the unit to be managed as an integrated whole. The better reading, and the one adopted below, is that § 103(c) bars only regulations aimed solely at conveyed lands, not generally applicable park regulations that incidentally reach conduct on those lands. Because the hovercraft prohibition is not a regulation “applicable solely to public lands,” the judgment should be affirmed. *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 872 F.3d 927, 939-41 (9th Cir. 2017).
