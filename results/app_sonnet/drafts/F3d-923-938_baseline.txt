ARGUMENT

I. The Transfers Through Merco's Affiliates Constituted Bona Fide Indebtedness Running Directly to Meruelo.

Treasury Regulation § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i) requires only that a shareholder's claimed indebtedness be "bona fide" and "run directly to the shareholder." The regulation does not require that the funds travel in an unbroken straight line from the shareholder's own checking account to the corporation's; it requires that the economic substance of the arrangement establish the shareholder, and not some other party, as the true creditor. Federal tax law has long recognized that the label placed on a transaction by its participants does not control its tax consequences; substance governs over form. See Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465, 469-70 (1935) (disregarding the form of a corporate reorganization because "the whole undertaking, though conducted according to the terms of subdivision (B), was in fact an elaborate and devious form of conveyance masquerading as a corporate reorganization"). That same principle animates the conduit doctrine applied throughout federal tax law: an intermediary entity cannot be used to convert, for tax purposes, a transaction that is in substance direct into one that is indirect. See Commissioner v. Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945) ("A sale by one person cannot be transformed for tax purposes into a sale by another by using the latter as a conduit through which to pass title.").

This Court applied precisely that substance-based inquiry to the shareholder-basis context in Selfe v. United States, 778 F.2d 769 (11th Cir. 1985). There, a shareholder had guaranteed a bank loan made nominally to her S corporation, and this Court held that the form of a guarantee does not foreclose a finding of direct indebtedness; rather, the court must examine "the facts and circumstances surrounding" the transaction to determine "whether the shareholder is the true obligor." Id. at 772-73. Selfe teaches that the corporate form interposed between a shareholder and the funds actually attributable to him does not defeat basis where the shareholder is, in economic reality, the source of the credit extended to the S corporation.

The same reasoning governs here. Meruelo personally borrowed $10 million to fund Merco's acquisition deposit and routed a portion of those proceeds through Akoya, an affiliate he substantially owned and controlled, directly into Merco's escrow account. The remaining net transfers among the Merco affiliates arose from the same integrated cash-management system that Meruelo himself created to fund Merco's operations, and Merco's own books recorded the resulting net payable as a "shareholder loan" to Meruelo, memorialized by the $10 million promissory note that Carreras drafted at Meruelo's direction. Under Selfe's economic-substance approach, and consistent with the conduit principle of Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. at 334, the affiliated entities functioned as intermediaries carrying out Meruelo's own credit extension to Merco, not as independent creditors in their own right. The Tax Court's contrary conclusion—that any indebtedness ran only from Merco to the affiliates themselves—elevates bookkeeping form over the economic reality that Meruelo, not the affiliates, was the ultimate and true source of the funds.

II. Meruelo Made the Requisite Actual Economic Outlay.

Even accepting that a shareholder must demonstrate an "actual economic outlay" to support basis in indebtedness, Meruelo satisfied that standard. This Court in Selfe recognized that a shareholder's personal exposure to loss—there, a guarantee—can itself constitute the kind of outlay that supports basis where the shareholder, and not a third party, ultimately bears the economic burden of the debt. 778 F.2d at 772. Here, Meruelo's outlay was more direct still: he personally incurred a $10 million loan obligation, a real and immediate economic detriment, and channeled a substantial portion of those proceeds into Merco's acquisition of the condominium property. The remaining net transfers reflected in Merco's accounts payable were likewise attributable to entities in which Meruelo held direct ownership interests and whose funds, in economic substance, were his own. The promissory note that Carreras prepared did not create a fiction; it formalized an existing economic reality—that Meruelo, having supplied the initial capital that made Merco's operations possible, continued to stand behind Merco's shortfalls through the network of affiliates he controlled.

The purpose of Subchapter S's pass-through regime confirms this conclusion. As the Supreme Court has explained, Subchapter S establishes "a pass-through system under which corporate income, losses, deductions, and credits are attributed to individual shareholders in a manner akin to the tax treatment of partnerships." Bufferd v. Commissioner, 506 U.S. 523, 525 (1993). That pass-through design is frustrated, not honored, by a rule that denies basis to a shareholder who actually bore the corporation's losses simply because the funds moved through affiliated entities he controlled rather than through his personal bank account. The Tax Court's rigid, form-driven approach cannot be reconciled with the substance-over-form principles of Gregory, 293 U.S. at 469-70, the conduit doctrine of Court Holding Co., 324 U.S. at 334, or this Court's own economic-substance analysis in Selfe, 778 F.2d at 772-73.

For these reasons, the Tax Court's judgment should be reversed.
