The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff's claims "arise out of or relate to" the defendant's activities within the state. In this case, Ford Motor Company's activities in Montana, including its advertising and sale of parts within the state, are sufficient to establish specific jurisdiction over the company in Montana state court, even though the particular vehicle involved in the accident was not sold or manufactured in Montana.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a state may exercise jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant that has "purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State, thus invoking the benefits and protections of its laws." Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (quoting Hanson v. Denckla, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958)). In this case, Ford has purposefully availed itself of the Montana market by advertising and selling parts within the state.

The Court has also held that specific jurisdiction is not limited to cases where the defendant's in-state activities directly caused the plaintiff's claims. See, e.g., Hess v. Pawloski, 274 U.S. 352 (1927) (upholding jurisdiction over nonresident motorist who caused an accident in the state). Rather, the Court has emphasized that the critical inquiry is whether the defendant's contacts with the forum state are sufficient to make it "reasonable... to require the corporation to defend the particular suit" brought. International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310, 317 (1945).

In Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S. A. v. Brown, 564 U.S. 915 (2011), the Court distinguished between general and specific jurisdiction, noting that specific jurisdiction depends on an "affiliatio[n] between the forum and the underlying controversy." Id. at 919 (quoting von Mehren & Trautman, Jurisdiction To Adjudicate: A Suggested Analysis, 79 Harv. L. Rev. 1121, 1136 (1966)). Here, Ford's activities in Montana, including its advertising and sale of parts, establish a sufficient affiliation between the forum and the controversy to support specific jurisdiction.

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this principle in affirming the district court's denial of Ford's motion to dismiss for lack of personal jurisdiction. By advertising and selling parts within Montana, Ford has availed itself of the privilege of doing business in that state and is therefore subject to specific jurisdiction there.

In contrast, Ford's reliance on cases such as Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court, 582 U.S. 255 (2017), is misplaced. While Bristol-Myers Squibb held that specific jurisdiction requires a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue, it did not disturb the long-standing principle that a defendant's purposeful availment of a state's market can support specific jurisdiction.

In conclusion, the Due Process Clause permits the Montana state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over Ford Motor Company, despite the fact that the particular vehicle involved in the accident was not sold or manufactured in Montana. Ford's activities in Montana, including its advertising and sale of parts, establish a sufficient connection between the forum and the controversy to support specific jurisdiction. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
