The district court lacked authority to assign this transferred action to itself for trial. Section 1407(a) commands that civil actions transferred for “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings” “shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). That language is mandatory, and a court may not rewrite a statute to reach a more convenient result. See Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 188 (1824); Texas & Pacific Ry. Co. v. Rigsby, 241 U.S. 33, 39 (1916) (“A thing which is within the letter of the statute is not necessarily within the statute, unless within the intention of the makers.”); Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

Nor can § 1404(a) be used to defeat § 1407(a)’s remand command. Section 1404(a) authorizes transfer “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but it presupposes a valid transferee court-to-transferee court transfer. 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). It does not authorize a transferee judge, sitting only because of an MDL assignment for pretrial proceedings, to keep the case for trial in derogation of the statutory remand requirement. Statutes addressing the same subject must be read harmoniously if possible, but the specific controls the general. Rodgers v. United States, 185 U.S. 83, 87-88 (1902); Townsend v. Little, 109 U.S. 504, 512 (1883); MacEvoy Co. v. United States, 322 U.S. 102, 107 (1944).

The settled pre-1970 venue cases confirm that transfer is a creature of statute, not inherent judicial power. In Ex parte Collett, 337 U.S. 55, 58-59 (1949), the Court held that venue transfer exists only because Congress created it. Norwood v. Kirkpatrick, 349 U.S. 29, 31-32 (1955), likewise recognized that § 1404(a) broadened transfer discretion only within the bounds Congress set. But even broad discretion cannot override a statute’s express limitation. See United States v. Missouri Pac. R.R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929) (“There is no power of the courts to modify the statute.”).

The history of § 1407 confirms the point. Congress enacted multidistrict transfer to centralize common pretrial matters, not to reassign trial forum. Where Congress has authorized a limited transfer for a limited purpose, courts may not extend it by judicial convenience. See FTC v. Eastman Kodak Co., 274 U.S. 619, 623-24 (1927); Louisville & Nashville R.R. Co. v. Mottley, 219 U.S. 467, 482 (1911). The district court’s self-transfer converts a temporary pretrial assignment into a de facto permanent venue selection, contradicting the statute’s structure and depriving the remand command of independent force.

The decision below also conflicts with the settled rule that jurisdiction and venue must rest on authorization from Congress, not on a court’s assessment of efficiency. See Hepburn & Dundas v. Ellzey, 6 U.S. (2 Cranch) 445, 452 (1805); Robertson v. Cease, 97 U.S. 646, 648-49 (1878). If the transferee court may retain the case for trial under § 1404(a), then the mandatory remand provision becomes surplusage, contrary to the presumption that every word in a statute has effect. See Montclair v. Ramsdell, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883); In re Pacific Ry. Com’n, 32 F. 241, 255 (C.C.N.D. Cal. 1887).

Finally, any ambiguity must be resolved in favor of the statutory forum Congress selected. The Court has repeatedly refused to permit procedural devices to defeat explicit venue limitations. See United States v. Stone, 69 U.S. (2 Wall.) 525, 535 (1865); Mechanical Appliance Co. v. Castleman, 215 U.S. 437, 444 (1910). Because § 1407(a) requires remand to the transferor district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings,” the district court’s self-assignment for trial was ultra vires and must be reversed.
