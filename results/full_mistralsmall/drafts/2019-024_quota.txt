**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT § 1113(2)’S “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” REQUIREMENT IS NOT SATISFIED BY DISCLOSURES THAT A PLAINTIFF CHOOSES NOT TO READ OR CANNOT RECALL READING.

A. Section 1113(2) requires “actual knowledge” of the “breach or violation,” not merely access to information about it. *See generally Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523, 530 (2015) (explaining that § 1113(2) “requires actual knowledge of the breach”). The Supreme Court has cautioned that ERISA’s limitations provisions are not “to be given a grudging application.” *Id.* at 531 (quoting *United States v. Kubrick*, 444 U.S. 111, 117 (1979)). This Court has likewise recognized that § 1113(2) “is triggered by [a plaintiff’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988) (quoting *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985)).

B. The Ninth Circuit’s construction is consistent with this Court’s precedent holding that “actual knowledge” requires subjective awareness of the underlying facts constituting the breach. *See Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Rest. Emps. Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509, 520–21 (9th Cir. 1991) (holding that § 1113(2) bars claims where plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach more than three years before filing suit, even if plaintiff did not know the conduct was unlawful). The Ninth Circuit correctly declined to impute knowledge from disclosures that Sulyma did not read or recall reading. *See id.* (rejecting argument that constructive knowledge suffices under § 1113(2)).

C. The Ninth Circuit’s holding aligns with the Supreme Court’s interpretation of analogous limitations provisions. *See Merck & Co. v. Reynolds*, 559 U.S. 633, 648 (2010) (holding that “actual knowledge” under the Securities Exchange Act requires “a subjective awareness of the relevant facts,” not merely access to information). The Court reasoned that “[t]o equate ‘inquiry notice’ with ‘actual knowledge’ would effectively eliminate the distinction between § 10(b)’s two limitations periods.” *Id.* Similarly, equating disclosure with “actual knowledge” under § 1113(2) would render the three-year limitations period meaningless.

II. THE NINTH CIRCUIT PROPERLY REJECTED INTEL’S ATTEMPT TO EXPAND § 1113(2) TO INCLUDE KNOWLEDGE OF THE LEGAL CONSEQUENCES OF A BREACH.

A. The Ninth Circuit correctly distinguished between knowledge of the underlying transaction and knowledge of its legal consequences. *See Blanton*, 760 F.2d at 991–92 (holding that actual knowledge of the transaction, not knowledge of its legal impropriety, triggers the limitations period). This distinction is consistent with the Supreme Court’s admonition that “[t]he statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff knows or has reason to know of the injury that is the basis of the action.” *Rotella v. Wood*, 528 U.S. 549, 555 (2000) (quoting *Hunt v. HUD*, 414 U.S. 522, 531 (1974)).

B. The Ninth Circuit’s holding prevents fiduciaries from insulating themselves from liability by inundating participants with disclosures that go unread. *See Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990) (holding that actual knowledge requires more than general awareness of plan documents; plaintiff must have known of the specific breach). This interpretation ensures that § 1113(2) serves its purpose of barring stale claims while protecting participants who lack subjective awareness of a breach.

III. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S DECISION DOES NOT CONFLICT WITH *WALLER V. BLUE CROSS OF CALIFORNIA*, AS INTEL ARGUES.

A. *Waller* does not stand for the proposition that disclosure alone satisfies § 1113(2). *See Waller v. Blue Cross of Cal.*, 32 F.3d 1337, 1342 (9th Cir. 1994) (holding that plaintiffs had actual knowledge where they were “fully informed” of the challenged transactions and their legal consequences). The Ninth Circuit distinguished *Waller* on the ground that the plaintiffs in that case had actual knowledge of both the transactions and their legal implications. *See id.* at 1342–43.

B. The Ninth Circuit’s decision in this case is consistent with *Waller* because it requires actual knowledge of the breach itself, not merely access to information about it. *See id.* at 1342 (emphasizing that plaintiffs had “actual knowledge” of the challenged conduct and its legal consequences). The Ninth Circuit’s holding here does not expand or contract *Waller*; it merely applies the same standard to a different set of facts.

IV. THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S CONSTRUCTION OF § 1113(2) PROMOTES CONSISTENCY WITH OTHER ERISA LIMITATIONS PROVISIONS.

A. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation harmonizes § 1113(2) with § 1113(1), which provides a six-year limitations period for “the date of the last action which constituted a part of the breach or violation.” *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1113(1). The three-year period under § 1113(2) is triggered only when a plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach, ensuring that participants are not unfairly barred from bringing claims before they are aware of a violation. *See Tibble*, 575 U.S. at 530.

B. This interpretation also aligns with ERISA’s remedial purpose. *See Massachusetts Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Russell*, 473 U.S. 134, 147 (1985) (holding that ERISA is “remedial legislation” to be construed liberally). The Ninth Circuit’s decision ensures that participants are not penalized for failing to read disclosures they did not recall or understand, while still protecting fiduciaries from stale claims where participants had actual knowledge of a breach.

**CONCLUSION**
The Ninth Circuit correctly held that § 1113(2)’s “actual knowledge” requirement is not satisfied by disclosures that a plaintiff chooses not to read or cannot recall reading. This interpretation is consistent with this Court’s precedent and the Supreme Court’s guidance on analogous limitations provisions. The petition for certiorari should be denied.
