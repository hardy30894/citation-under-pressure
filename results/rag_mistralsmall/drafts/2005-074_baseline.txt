**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Re-Entered Before Its Effective Date**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5) (2000) authorizes reinstatement of prior deportation orders only for aliens who “reenter[] the United States illegally after having been removed.” *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 187 (1958) (construing predecessor statute). The statute contains no express retroactive provision, and this Court has repeatedly refused to infer retroactivity absent clear congressional intent. *Landgraf v. USI Film Prods.*, 511 U.S. 244, 260 (1994). Here, Congress enacted § 1231(a)(5) in 1996, effective April 1, 1997, but Fernandez-Vargas re-entered in 1982—nearly fifteen years before the statute’s enactment. The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that the statute applies to pre-enactment re-entries. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316 (2001) (rejecting retroactive application of new deportation rules to pre-enactment conduct).

The government’s contrary position—that the statute applies to all illegal re-entries, regardless of timing—would render the statute impermissibly retroactive. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. This Court has long held that statutes altering the legal consequences of past conduct are retroactive unless Congress clearly expresses otherwise. *Id.* at 270. Section 1231(a)(5) does not do so. *Compare Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 692 (1957) (finding retroactive effect where statute expressly applied to pre-enactment conduct). Thus, the statute cannot be applied to Fernandez-Vargas’ 1982 re-entry.

**II. The Reinstatement Statute’s Application to Pre-Enactment Re-Entries Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if § 1231(a)(5) could be construed to reach pre-enactment re-entries, its application here is retroactive in violation of due process. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269. The statute strips aliens of eligibility for discretionary relief—including adjustment of status—based on pre-enactment conduct. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). This is precisely the type of retroactive application this Court has condemned. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 321 (holding that retroactive application of new deportation rules violates due process).

Fernandez-Vargas’ reliance on his pre-enactment re-entry was entirely reasonable. *See INS v. Phinpathya*, 464 U.S. 183, 189 (1984) (noting that aliens have no vested right to discretionary relief). The government’s attempt to retroactively impose new legal consequences for past conduct violates due process. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 270. The Tenth Circuit’s holding to the contrary is erroneous.

**III. The Reinstatement Statute Does Not Bar Adjustment of Status for Aliens Who Re-Entered Before Its Effective Date**

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that § 1231(a)(5) bars adjustment of status for aliens who re-entered before its enactment. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). The statute’s text and structure make clear that it applies only to aliens who re-entered *after* its effective date. *See id.* The government’s contrary interpretation would render the statute unconstitutionally retroactive. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. Thus, Fernandez-Vargas remains eligible for adjustment of status.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Tenth Circuit’s decision and remand for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
