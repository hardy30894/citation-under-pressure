**ARGUMENT**

I. **Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Fernandez-Vargas Because Congress Did Not Clearly Impose Reinstatement on Pre-1997 Reentrants**

This Court should begin with the statutory presumption against retroactivity. A statute will not be construed to attach “new legal consequences to events completed before its enactment” absent clear congressional intent. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244, 269-70 (1994). Immigration statutes are no exception. *I.N.S. v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 316-20 (2001). And where a statute’s text is ambiguous, courts do not infer retroactive reach lightly. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280; *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 320.

Section 241(a)(5) provides that if the Attorney General finds that an alien “has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed,” the prior order “is reinstated” and the alien “is not eligible and may not apply for any relief.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). The question is whether Congress clearly intended that language to reach aliens who, like Fernandez-Vargas, reentered before the provision became effective in 1997. It did not.

The operative conduct here was Fernandez-Vargas’s illegal reentry in 1982. At that time, the INA did not strip him of all avenues to seek adjustment of status if he later became eligible through marriage to a U.S. citizen. Before IIRIRA, aliens in similar circumstances could pursue discretionary relief, including suspension-related forms of relief and adjustment mechanisms preserved by the immigration laws. See *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 293-94, 326; *Vartelas v. Holder*, 566 U.S. 257, 273-74 (2012). Congress knows how to speak with clarity when it intends to eliminate such reliance interests. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 316-17.

The Tenth Circuit’s approach treated the statute as though any post-1997 enforcement is automatically prospective because the reinstatement decision occurs after enactment. That reasoning conflicts with *Landgraf* and *St. Cyr*. The proper inquiry is whether the statute attaches new legal consequences to pre-enactment conduct. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70. Here it does: Fernandez-Vargas reentered when the law did not foreclose his later pursuit of lawful status. Section 241(a)(5) now makes that prior reentry itself the trigger for a complete bar on relief. That is the very kind of statutory change the retroactivity presumption guards against. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 325-26.

II. **Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Fernandez-Vargas Would Be Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if Section 241(a)(5) reaches pre-1997 reentrants as a matter of text, applying it to Fernandez-Vargas is impermissibly retroactive under *Landgraf*. A statute is retroactive when it “impair[s] rights a party possessed when he acted” or “increase[s] a party’s liability for past conduct.” *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 280. The Court applied that principle in immigration in *St. Cyr*, holding that repeal of discretionary relief could not be applied to aliens who pleaded guilty before the repeal because they had relied on the availability of relief. 533 U.S. at 325-26.

Fernandez-Vargas’s reliance is not speculative. He reentered in 1982 and remained in the United States for nearly two decades. During that period he married a U.S. citizen and filed an application to adjust status. Under the pre-IIRIRA regime, his marriage created a legally significant opportunity to seek lawful status. Section 241(a)(5) extinguishes that opportunity based on his earlier reentry and prior deportation order. That is a retroactive attachment of adverse legal consequences to completed conduct. *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 269-70, 280.

The government will likely rely on *Fernandez-Vargas v. Gonzales*, 548 U.S. 30 (2006), but that case is distinguishable in the critical respect that the Court emphasized the alien’s continued unlawful presence after IIRIRA and his failure to take steps to legalize his status before the statute’s effective date. Id. at 44-46. Here, petitioner did precisely what the law once allowed: he remained, married a citizen, and applied for adjustment when he became eligible. Moreover, *Vartelas* confirms that courts should not treat post-enactment enforcement as nonretroactive when the statute “attaches a new disability” to pre-enactment conduct. 566 U.S. at 273-75.

At minimum, the statute is ambiguous as applied to pre-1997 reentrants, and that ambiguity must be resolved against retroactivity. *St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. at 320; *Landgraf*, 511 U.S. at 265. Because Fernandez-Vargas reentered before Section 241(a)(5) existed, the reinstatement bar should not apply to him.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment below should be reversed. Section 241(a)(5) does not clearly reach aliens who reentered before its effective date, and applying it to Fernandez-Vargas would impermissibly impose new legal consequences on past conduct. *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994); *I.N.S. v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289 (2001).
