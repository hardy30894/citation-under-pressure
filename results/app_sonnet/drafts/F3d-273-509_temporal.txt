**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE RELEASE DOES NOT BAR BANCORP’S CLAIMS**

A release is a species of contract, and its scope is fixed by the intention of the parties as reflected in the language they chose, read against the dispute the release was executed to resolve. See *Lucio v. Curran*, 2 N.Y.2d 157, 161 (1956) (a release, however broadly worded, is confined to the controversy the parties had in mind when it was signed). This Court’s predecessor rule was reaffirmed in *Cahill v. Regan*, 5 N.Y.2d 292, 299 (1959), where the New York Court of Appeals held that the coverage of a general release “depend[s] upon the controversy being settled and upon the purpose for which the release was actually given,” and that a release will not be construed to extend to claims outside that controversy merely because its language, read in isolation, might be broad enough to reach them.

The release Bancorp signed on June 6, 1988 arose out of the settlement of the *May 1986 action*—Joseph Chuang’s suit against the FDIC concerning the seizure of his law offices—and the related *September 1985 action* concerning recovery of loans and guarantees. By its own terms, the release runs only to claims “arising from or with respect to the decision of the [OCC] to close the Bank on June 21, 1985.” None of Bancorp’s four claims here challenges the OCC’s closure decision. Rather, each concerns the FDIC’s post-closure conduct as receiver: paying itself post-insolvency interest, charging the estate for its own legal and office expenses, mismanaging the sale of Bank assets, and failing to account. These are claims about how the receivership was administered, not about whether the Bank should have been closed at all.

Where, as here, the release’s operative clause is susceptible of a narrower reading tied to the underlying litigation it settled, that narrower reading controls, and any ambiguity is resolved against the party that drafted the instrument. *Rentways, Inc. v. O’Neill Milk & Cream Co.*, 308 N.Y. 342, 348 (1955) (ambiguous contract language construed against the drafter). Because the FDIC drafted the release as part of settling litigation to which Bancorp was not even a party, and because the release addresses only the closure decision, the district court erred in reading it to reach claims about the FDIC’s subsequent conduct as receiver—conduct not remotely in the parties’ contemplation in 1988, since the FDIC did not even begin paying itself post-insolvency interest until March 15, 1991, nearly three years after the release was signed.

**II. THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS DOES NOT BAR BANCORP’S CLAIMS**

Even apart from the release, the district court erred in holding Bancorp’s claims untimely. A receiver, like a trustee, stands in a fiduciary relationship to those with an interest in the estate, and the settled rule governing such relationships is that the statute of limitations does not begin to run against the beneficiary while the fiduciary relationship continues. *Toplitz v. Bauer*, 161 N.Y. 325, 332 (1899) (limitations period for a beneficiary’s claim against a trustee does not commence until the trust is repudiated or otherwise terminated, because until that point the beneficiary is entitled to rely on the fiduciary’s continuing duty to account). The rationale is straightforward: a beneficiary cannot be charged with a duty to sue over conduct that the fiduciary is still obligated to disclose and correct through the ordinary course of administering the estate.

That rationale applies with full force here. The FDIC remained receiver of the Bank—and thus a continuing fiduciary to Bancorp as residual claimant to any surplus—from June 21, 1985 until it formally ended the receivership on November 1, 1995. Bancorp’s claims for unjust enrichment, breach of fiduciary duty, and corporate waste all concern conduct undertaken by the FDIC in that fiduciary capacity: the charging of expenses to the estate, the payment of interest to itself, and the disposition of estate assets. Under *Toplitz*, limitations on those claims did not begin to run until the fiduciary relationship terminated. Bancorp filed suit on October 31, 1995—one day before the receivership closed. The action was therefore timely no matter which six-year period is measured, since the claims had not yet even accrued.

To the extent the district court instead treated Bancorp’s claims as accruing at the moment of the challenged conduct itself, that approach ignores the discovery-based accrual analysis this Court’s predecessor court applied to comparable claims sounding in breach of duty. *Mangini v. McClurg*, 24 N.Y.2d 556, 562 (1969) (accrual for limitations purposes turns on when the plaintiff knew or should have known of the facts underlying the claim, not merely on the date of the underlying act). Bancorp, a minority participant excluded from any role in administering the receivership, could not reasonably have discovered the FDIC’s self-interested accounting practices—including its 1991 decision to begin paying itself interest—until the FDIC’s conduct as receiver was disclosed in the course of winding up the estate. Under either *Toplitz*’s termination rule or *Mangini*’s discovery rule, Bancorp’s 1995 filing was timely.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
