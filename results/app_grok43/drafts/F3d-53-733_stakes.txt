I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Non-Expert Opinion Testimony.

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 permits opinion testimony only when it rests on “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” that will assist the trier of fact. Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 592 (1993). The defendant’s witness offered no such knowledge; he merely recounted the condition of the line after examining it and concluded that heat from Cook’s torch caused the parting. That conclusion required no expertise beyond ordinary observation and was therefore inadmissible. See United States v. Johnson, 488 F.2d 1206, 1209 (6th Cir. 1973) (testimony “not beyond the ken of the average juror” is not expert). Admission of the testimony was prejudicial because it supplied the only evidence attributing the line’s failure to Cook’s conduct.

II. The District Court Erred in Denying Judgment as a Matter of Law on Unseaworthiness.

A shipowner’s duty to furnish a seaworthy vessel is absolute and independent of negligence. Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 549 (1960). The evidence established as a matter of law that the BUFFALO was unseaworthy: the bearings contained summer grease that froze in sub-zero temperatures, preventing the boom from swinging; the crew had only fifteen to twenty minutes to de-ice and rig the gear; and the line, stored in a heated compartment, was then exposed to the same conditions. These facts left no reasonable basis for a jury to find the vessel reasonably fit for its intended service. See Mahnich v. Southern S.S. Co., 321 U.S. 96, 99 (1944). The motion for judgment as a matter of law should have been granted.

Because the erroneous admission of lay opinion and the denial of judgment on unseaworthiness each require a new trial, the judgment must be vacated and the case remanded.
