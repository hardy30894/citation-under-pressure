I.

The three-year bar of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) runs from the earliest date the plaintiff possessed actual knowledge of the breach. That date is fixed when the fiduciary supplies documents that fully disclose the challenged investments, for the law has long treated such delivery as imparting knowledge of every fact the documents contain. Wood v. Carpenter, 101 U.S. 135, 143 (1879) (“Whatever is notice enough to excite attention and put the party on his guard and call for inquiry, is notice of everything to which such inquiry might have led.”). The same rule appears in Johnston v. Standard Mining Co., 148 U.S. 360, 370 (1893), where the Court held that a plaintiff who receives a written statement of the material facts “is charged with knowledge of its contents.”

II.

This principle rests on the ancient distinction between actual and constructive knowledge. Actual knowledge exists once the plaintiff is placed in possession of the decisive facts, whether or not he reads them. As the Court explained in United States v. Clark, 96 U.S. 37, 42 (1877), “a party having the means of knowledge cannot be heard to say that he was ignorant.” The same doctrine was applied in Bailey v. Glover, 88 U.S. (21 Wall.) 342, 348 (1874), which measured the limitations period from the moment the plaintiff “had knowledge of facts sufficient to put a prudent man upon inquiry.” Delivery of the Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and website disclosures therefore started the clock, for those documents recited the precise asset allocations and strategy now attacked.

III.

Respondent’s contrary view—that knowledge requires subjective awareness of imprudence—would revive the constructive-knowledge provision Congress repealed in 1987. That view finds no support in pre-1970 authority. The Court has consistently refused to toll limitations merely because a plaintiff “neglected to read what was put before him.” Foster v. Mansfield, C. & L.M.R. Co., 146 U.S. 88, 99 (1892). Allowing such neglect to postpone the bar would contradict the policy that “statutes of limitation are founded upon the general experience of mankind that claims which are valid are not usually allowed to remain neglected.” Riddlesbarger v. Hartford Ins. Co., 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 386, 390 (1869).

IV.

Because the disclosures furnished every fact necessary to identify the investments and their performance, respondent had actual knowledge more than three years before suit. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
