# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT SHAPIRO WAS ENTITLED TO IMMEDIATE, PREFERENTIAL PARKING WITHOUT REGARD TO THE COOPERATIVE'S NEUTRAL WAITING-LIST POLICY

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 makes it unlawful to discriminate against a handicapped person by "a refusal to make reasonable accommodations in rules, policies, practices, or services, when such accommodations may be necessary to afford such person equal opportunity to use and enjoy a dwelling." 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f)(3)(B). The statute requires only "reasonable" accommodation, not the wholesale abandonment of neutral policies that serve legitimate purposes for other residents. See *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 405 (1979) (an accommodation is not required where it would require "a fundamental alteration in the nature of a program").

Cadman Towers' first-come/first-served waiting-list policy is not itself discriminatory; it applies equally to all 400 apartment units competing for only 66 available spaces at 101 Clark Street. Courts have long recognized that a facially neutral policy allocating a genuinely scarce resource does not become unlawful discrimination merely because a handicapped individual finds herself, like everyone else, waiting in line. Cf. *Alexander v. Choate*, 469 U.S. 287, 301 (1985) (noting that "[t]he balance between statutory purpose and the desire to keep § 504 within manageable bounds" requires courts to avoid "fundamental alteration[s]" to programs, and that the antidiscrimination statutes do not guarantee "the achievement of equality of results").

The district court's injunction did not merely require Cadman Towers to consider Shapiro's disability in the ordinary course of business; it required Cadman Towers to jump Shapiro to the front of a list of tenants who have, in many cases, waited for years for a coveted parking space. This is not "reasonable accommodation"—it is preferential treatment that comes at the direct expense of other tenants' legitimate expectations. The Fair Housing Act was not intended to convert a fair, transparent allocation system into an all-purpose vehicle for jumping to the head of every scarce-resource queue in a residential building. See *School Board of Nassau County v. Arline*, 480 U.S. 273, 287 n.17 (1987) (accommodation obligations do not require actions that "impose an undue burden" on the operation of the program).

## II. THE COOPERATIVE'S PROPOSED ALTERNATIVE—REASONABLE ACCOMMODATION ONCE A SPACE BECAME AVAILABLE THROUGH THE NORMAL WAITING PROCESS—SATISFIED ITS STATUTORY OBLIGATION

Cadman Towers did not refuse Shapiro any accommodation whatsoever. It offered to accommodate her disability once she reached the normal course of the waiting list, at which point it committed to assigning her a space near her apartment entrance, consistent with her medical needs. This is precisely the sort of interactive, good-faith engagement with a disabled tenant that the FHAA contemplates. See *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Management Co.*, 29 F.3d 1413, 1416 (9th Cir. 1994) (accommodation requirement satisfied where landlord engages in reasonable efforts to address disability-related needs without fundamentally altering existing practices).

An accommodation is not unreasonable merely because it requires the disabled individual to wait her turn, particularly where—as here—the underlying scarcity of the resource means that immediate accommodation necessarily disadvantages other tenants, several of whom may have equally compelling personal circumstances that led them to seek parking in the first place. The FHAA "does not require [a landlord] to provide an ideal accommodation, or the accommodation of the plaintiff's choosing," but only a reasonable one. *United States v. California Mobile Home Park Management Co.*, 29 F.3d at 1416. Cadman Towers' proposal—first-in-line accommodation followed by tailored assistance—falls comfortably within this standard.

## III. THE PRELIMINARY INJUNCTION IMPOSES AN UNDUE BURDEN ON THE COOPERATIVE AND THE OTHER TENANTS WAITING FOR PARKING

The district court's order effectively displaces another waiting tenant from the position that tenant would otherwise have occupied. This "cost" is directly borne not by Cadman Towers as an institution, but by an identifiable third party: whichever tenant was next in line for the 101 Clark Street garage. The Supreme Court has cautioned that reasonable-accommodation analysis must account for the accommodation's impact on others, not simply the benefit to the individual seeking it. *Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. at 412 (accommodation not required where it would impose "unreasonable burdens" upon the institution and, implicitly, upon those it serves).

Moreover, the preliminary injunction here was granted based on a record showing that Shapiro's medical difficulties, while genuine, could be substantially ameliorated through less drastic means—for example, expedited processing of her position on the waiting list, priority in her building's grandfather exceptions, or use of the existing car-service and public-transit alternatives she had used successfully for two years before acquiring her own vehicle. The extraordinary remedy of preliminary injunctive relief requires a showing that no adequate remedy at law exists and that the balance of hardships tips decidedly in the moving party's favor. See *Jackson Dairy, Inc. v. H.P. Hood & Sons, Inc.*, 596 F.2d 70, 72 (2d Cir. 1979) (setting forth the standard for preliminary injunctive relief in this Circuit, requiring irreparable harm and either a likelihood of success on the merits or sufficiently serious questions going to the merits combined with a balance of hardships tipping decidedly in the movant's favor). The district court's order does not reflect the careful balancing this standard demands.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the preliminary injunction should be vacated, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with the standards for reasonable accommodation set forth above.
