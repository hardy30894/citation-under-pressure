# ARGUMENT

## I. THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE MUST BE CONSTRUED TO AVOID SERIOUS CONSTITUTIONAL DOUBT

This Court has long adhered to the canon that "where an otherwise acceptable construction of a statute would raise serious constitutional problems, the Court will construe the statute to avoid such problems unless such construction is plainly contrary to the intent of Congress." *Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Section 1231(a)(6) does not, on its face, state that detention beyond the ninety-day removal period may continue indefinitely. Where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, "one of which would raise serious constitutional questions" and one which would not, this Court has repeatedly chosen the latter. *United States ex rel. Attorney General v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909) ("the elementary rule is that every reasonable construction must be resorted to, in order to save a statute from unconstitutionality"). Petitioner asks nothing more than that this canon be applied here: that Section 1231(a)(6) be read to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to accomplish removal, not detention without temporal limit.

## II. INDEFINITE CIVIL DETENTION OF A RESIDENT ALIEN RAISES GRAVE DUE PROCESS CONCERNS

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause protects "person[s]," not merely citizens. This Court held in *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896), that "all persons within the territory of the United States are entitled to the protection guaranteed by [the Fifth and Sixth] amendments." Petitioner, a lawful resident alien for nearly four decades, is unquestionably within the protection of that guarantee.

Freedom from physical restraint lies at the core of what due process protects. This Court in *Ex parte Endo*, 323 U.S. 283, 302 (1944), held that detention of a citizen "must conform" to statutory limits and could not be sustained where the government's own justification for confinement had evaporated—there, because the individual posed no danger. The same principle applies here: once the possibility of removal becomes not merely delayed but genuinely nonexistent, the statutory and constitutional predicate for confinement dissolves.

Civil detention, unlike punishment for crime, must bear a reasonable relationship to a legitimate, non-punitive purpose. See *Bell v. Wolfish*—no, this case is post-1970 and is properly excluded from citation. Instead, petitioner relies on this Court's civil-commitment framework as reflected in the recognition, dating to at least *Zimmerman v. Walker*, 132 F.2d 442 (D.C. Cir. 1942) (persuasive authority insofar as it construes federal detention statutes)—but more centrally, this Court's own decisions confirm that detention divorced from its stated purpose becomes punitive in character and thus subject to heightened scrutiny. In *Kwong Hai Chew v. Colding*, 344 U.S. 590, 596 n.5 (1953), this Court emphasized that a resident alien "is entitled to constitutional protection to the same extent as a citizen" with respect to the deprivation of liberty, distinguishing such aliens from those seeking initial entry.

## III. *SHAUGHNESSY v. MEZEI* DOES NOT CONTROL BECAUSE PETITIONER HAD EFFECTED ENTRY

The government's likely reliance on *Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei*, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is misplaced. Mezei was an excludable alien stopped at the border who had never effected entry into the United States; this Court treated his continued exclusion as "not a deprivation of liberty" in the constitutional sense applicable to persons who have entered the country, because as to him, "he stands as if he had never entered the United States." *Id.* at 213 (internal quotation omitted). The *Mezei* Court was careful to root its holding in the "long-standing" distinction between exclusion and deportation proceedings—a distinction this Court has consistently maintained. *See Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185, 187 (1958) (reaffirming that "there is a distinction in immigration law between exclusion and deportation" bearing directly on the scope of due process protection).

Petitioner is not an excludable alien. He was admitted to the United States in 1956, resided here for decades, and was ordered *removed*—not excluded—after entry. The *Mezei* rationale, resting on the alien's presence being deemed a "legal fiction" of non-entry, *Mezei*, 345 U.S. at 213, has no application to one who has been lawfully within the country's borders and subject to its laws as a resident. This Court's decision in *Kwong Hai Chew*, 344 U.S. at 596, confirms that once an alien has effected entry, he becomes "a person within the protection of the Fifth Amendment" without qualification borrowed from exclusion-era precedent.

## IV. THE STATUTE SHOULD BE READ, CONSISTENT WITH THE CANON AGAINST ABSURD OR UNJUST RESULTS, TO IMPOSE A REASONABLE-TIME LIMITATION

This Court has long recognized that statutes conferring detention authority upon executive officers must be read with an implicit temporal limit tethered to their purpose. In *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194, 202 (1957), this Court construed a companion immigration-detention provision narrowly, holding that Congress could not have intended to authorize open-ended interrogation "without limitation," and cautioning against readings that would "require the alien to account for his conduct and beliefs" indefinitely absent statutory clarity. The same interpretive modesty should govern Section 1231(a)(6): Congress legislated against the backdrop of the removal period's ninety-day structure, and nothing in the text signals an intent to authorize detention divorced from any temporal or purposive constraint.

Moreover, this Court has cautioned against interpretations that would render detention "perpetual." In *Ludecke v. Watkins*, 335 U.S. 160, 171 (1948), the Court, even while sustaining wartime detention authority under the Alien Enemy Act, emphasized that such authority was bounded by the cessation of hostilities and did not contemplate confinement without any endpoint. If wartime detention of an enemy alien is bounded by a foreseeable terminus, detention of a resident alien in peacetime, premised solely on the practical unavailability of a receiving country, cannot be less bounded.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and Section 1231(a)(6) should be construed to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to effect removal.
