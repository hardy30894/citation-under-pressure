The United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim against the United States is time-barred under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA). The plain language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) provides that a party that has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from any person who is not a party to the settlement. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA constitutes such a settlement, as it required Guam to take specific remedial actions, including closing the Ordot Dump and designing a cover system.

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree did not "resolve" its liability is unpersuasive. The Decree's substantive provisions, including the requirement that Guam complete construction of a cover system, demonstrate that Guam assumed obligations consistent with a finding of liability. See Asarco LLC v. Atl. Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017) (holding that a settlement agreement can trigger section 113(f)(3)(B) even if it does not contain an admission of liability). The Decree's disclaimer of liability does not overcome its substantive provisions, which effectively resolved Guam's liability for the response action.

Furthermore, Guam's contention that the Consent Decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it addressed Clean Water Act violations is unfounded. The Complaint demanded that Guam complete construction of a cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate, which is a "remedial action" under CERCLA § 101(24). 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24).

The Court of Appeals correctly applied the plain language of CERCLA and relevant case law to conclude that Guam's claim is time-barred. Guam's failure to bring its contribution claim within the three-year statute of limitations under CERCLA § 113(g)(3) bars its action. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals.

In Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157 (2004), this Court held that a party must satisfy the requirements of § 113(f) to bring a contribution claim. Id. at 166. Guam's attempt to circumvent this requirement by bringing a § 107 claim is unavailing. The Court of Appeals correctly held that Guam's settlement with the EPA triggered its right to pursue a contribution claim under § 113(f)(3)(B), precluding it from pursuing a claim under § 107.

The decision below is consistent with the principles of statutory interpretation and the case law interpreting CERCLA. See, e.g., United States v. Bestfoods, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (noting that CERCLA is a "complex and highly detailed statute"). The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that Guam's claim is time-barred.
