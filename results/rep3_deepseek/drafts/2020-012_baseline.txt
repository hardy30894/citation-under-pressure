**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s Decision Denying Reopening Is a “Final Decision” Subject to Judicial Review Under 45 U.S.C. § 355(f)**

The Railroad Retirement Act provides that “[a]ny final decision of the Board” may be reviewed by the courts of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Board’s denial of Salinas’s request to reopen his 2006 claim is a “final decision” because it conclusively resolved the reopening issue, leaving no further administrative recourse. The D.C. Circuit correctly held in *Stovic v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 826 F.3d 500, 502 (D.C. Cir. 2016), that “the Railroad Retirement Act grants the [circuit] Court jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying requests to reopen initial benefits determinations.” The Second Circuit similarly recognized over fifty years ago that such denials are reviewable. *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966).

The text of § 355(f) does not exclude reopening decisions. The statute broadly covers “any final decision,” and the Board’s refusal to reopen is a definitive agency action that terminates the administrative process. *See Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 503 (noting that the Board’s reopening regulations “create a right to have a prior decision reopened under certain circumstances,” making denial of that right a final decision). Because Salinas exhausted his administrative remedies on the reopening issue, the Fifth Circuit had jurisdiction to review the Board’s order.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s Reliance on *Roberts* and *Califano v. Sanders* Is Misplaced**

The Fifth Circuit dismissed Salinas’s petition based on *Roberts v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 346 F.3d 139 (5th Cir. 2003), which held that § 355(f) does not confer jurisdiction to review reopening denials. *Roberts* relied on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), which held that denials of reopening under the Social Security Act are not judicially reviewable. But *Sanders* is inapposite. The Social Security Act’s judicial review provision, 42 U.S.C. § 405(g), expressly limits review to “final decision of the Secretary made after a hearing,” and the Court in *Sanders* interpreted that language against the backdrop of the Act’s administrative scheme. 430 U.S. at 109. The Railroad Retirement Act has no such limitation. Section 355(f) contains no hearing requirement and does not incorporate the Social Security Act’s restrictive language. *Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 503–04 (distinguishing *Sanders* because the Railroad Retirement Act “does not incorporate the same limitations” as the Social Security Act).

The Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits followed *Sanders* in cases like *Harris v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 198 F.3d 139 (4th Cir. 1999); *Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 918 F.2d 567 (6th Cir. 1990); *Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250 (7th Cir. 1983); and *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972 (10th Cir. 1995). But these decisions improperly imported a Social Security principle into a different statutory regime. *See Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 504 (declining to follow the majority because “the Railroad Retirement Act . . . is not the Social Security Act”). The Board’s own regulations, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, create a substantive right to reopening based on new evidence or error, and the denial of that right is a final agency action reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act. *See* 5 U.S.C. § 704.

**III. The Circuit Split Requires This Court’s Resolution**

The question presented has divided the circuits. The Second, Eighth, and D.C. Circuits have held that reopening denials are reviewable, *see Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 502; *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991); *Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 254–55, while the Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Seventh, and Tenth Circuits have held otherwise. This split creates uncertainty for claimants like Salinas, who are denied any forum to challenge erroneous reopening decisions. The Fifth Circuit’s rule of orderliness prevented it from reconsidering *Roberts*, but this Court is not bound by that precedent. The Board’s decision here—interpreting its own regulations and denying Salinas’s request on the merits—is a final decision that Congress intended to be reviewable. The Court should reverse and hold that the courts of appeals have jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying reopening of prior benefits claims.
