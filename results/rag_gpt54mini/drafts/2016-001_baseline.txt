**Argument**

The judgment below should be reversed because it permits a design-patent owner to recover an infringer’s *entire profits* even when the patented design covers only a component of a larger product. That result conflicts with the Supreme Court’s settled rule that profits and damages must be apportioned when the patented feature creates only part of the value of the accused article. See *Westinghouse Elec. & Mfg. Co. v. Wagner Elec. & Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614–15 (1912); *Seymour v. McCormick*, 57 U.S. 480, 490–91 (1854). The rule applies with special force here, because Apple’s asserted design patents cover selected ornamental aspects of Samsung phones—not the phones as a whole.

1. **Patent remedies are compensatory, not windfalls.**

This Court has long held that a patentee may recover profits only to the extent the patented invention actually contributes to them. In *Westinghouse*, the Court explained that where a patent is “only a part of the machine and creates only a part of the profits,” the patentee “is only entitled to recover that part of the net gains.” 225 U.S. at 614–15. The infringer is liable for all profits only when the patent is shown to embody the article’s “entire value.” Id. at 614. Earlier, in *Seymour*, the Court rejected a damages award that treated infringement of an improvement patent “as if the defendant had pirated the whole machine,” holding that the patentee could not recover as though the improvement were the entire invention. 57 U.S. at 490–91.

The same principle appears in the design-patent cases. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 444–45 (1885), the Court rejected an award of the entire profit from carpet sales where the patented design contributed only one element of the product’s market appeal. The Court reiterated that no rule permits recovery of “the entire profit from the manufacture and sale” of the article merely because it contains the design. *Dobson v. Dornan*, 118 U.S. 10, 17–18 (1886). Those decisions confirm that design-patent remedies must be tied to the value created by the design itself.

2. **Section 289 does not displace apportionment when the design is only part of the product.**

Apple and the court below rely on the statutory phrase allowing recovery of “the total profit” from the “article of manufacture.” But statutes are construed against background principles, and this Court has repeatedly required apportionment where a patent covers only a portion of the accused product. *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614–15. Nothing in the cited design-patent cases authorizes a patent owner to capture profits attributable to unpatented features, manufacturing, software, brand, and market demand unrelated to the ornamental design. To the contrary, *Dobson* and *Westinghouse* teach that a patentee must recover only the profits “properly and legally attributable to the patented feature.” *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614–15.

That principle is especially important in the smartphone context. A phone’s consumer demand reflects numerous features: hardware, software, carrier compatibility, ecosystem, processing speed, camera quality, and price. Awarding all profits because of a design applied to only one component would contravene the apportionment requirement recognized in *Westinghouse* and the design-patent decisions. See *Dobson*, 114 U.S. at 444–45; *Dobson*, 118 U.S. at 17–18.

3. **The contrary rule would create an unjustified penalty.**

This Court has cautioned that patent remedies should compensate for actual injury, not impose punitive awards absent clear authorization. See *General Motors Corp. v. Devex Corp.*, 461 U.S. 648, 653–54 (1983). Likewise, where damages are difficult to quantify, the law still requires a rational basis for the award rather than speculation. *Douglas v. Cunningham*, 294 U.S. 207, 209–10 (1935). Here, allowing recovery of all profits from an entire smartphone when the patent covers only a component yields a windfall untethered to actual contribution.

The Federal Circuit’s en banc decision below relied on the premise that the statutory phrase “article of manufacture” permits total-profit recovery even for a component design. But the Supreme Court’s precedents do not permit ignoring the role of the patented feature within the larger product. *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614–15; *Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 490–91; *Dobson*, 114 U.S. at 444–45. Where, as here, the design is only one contributor to the product’s value, damages must be limited to the profits attributable to that component.

4. **The Court should adopt an apportionment rule consistent with longstanding precedent.**

The correct rule is straightforward: when a design patent covers only a component of a multi-component product, recovery under the design-patent profits remedy must be limited to the infringer’s profits attributable to that component. That rule follows directly from *Westinghouse*’s apportionment principle, *Seymour*’s rejection of treating an improvement as the whole invention, and *Dobson*’s refusal to award entire product profits for a design that contributed only part of the value. See *Westinghouse*, 225 U.S. at 614–15; *Seymour*, 57 U.S. at 490–91; *Dobson*, 114 U.S. at 444–45.

The judgment should therefore be reversed.
