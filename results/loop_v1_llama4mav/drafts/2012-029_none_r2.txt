The federal courts possess exclusive jurisdiction over Vernon Minton's state-based legal malpractice claim because it necessarily involves a substantial question of federal patent law. The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal jurisdiction extends to cases where a well-pleaded complaint establishes that the plaintiff's right to relief depends on the resolution of a substantial federal question. As established in Osborn v. Bank of the United States, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), federal jurisdiction is proper when a case involves a federal question, even if it is not the sole issue in the case. Id. at 822.

Minton's malpractice claim against his attorneys turns on the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar, a critical component of federal patent law. The validity of Minton's patent, and thus his malpractice claim, depends on the resolution of this federal issue. The Court has consistently held that cases "arising under" federal law involve a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of a federal law, as seen in Shulthis v. McDougal, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912), and Wilson v. Sandford, 51 U.S. 99, 102 (1850). Minton's claim meets this standard, as the outcome depends on the interpretation of federal patent law.

The importance of uniformity in the application of federal patent law is also a crucial consideration. The federal patent laws are intended to provide a uniform system of patent regulation, as noted in Wheaton v. Peters, 33 U.S. 591, 658 (1834). Allowing state courts to adjudicate patent issues in the context of malpractice claims could undermine this uniformity by permitting multiple interpretations of federal patent law. This concern is echoed in Pratt v. Paris Gas Light & Coke Co., 168 U.S. 255, 259 (1897), where the Court held that the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases involving the validity and infringement of patents.

Furthermore, the Court has cautioned against broadly defining "arising under" jurisdiction. In Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian, 299 U.S. 109 (1936), the Court adopted a "selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." Id. at 117-18. Applying this principle, it is clear that Minton's claim involves a substantial question of federal patent law necessary to the resolution of his state-based malpractice claim.

The Court's decision in Bloomer v. McQuewan, 55 U.S. 539 (1852), also supports the proposition that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over patent law cases. The Court has consistently recognized the supremacy of federal law in matters involving patent law, and Minton's claim is no exception.

In light of these precedents, it is evident that Minton's malpractice claim falls within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal courts. The experimental use exception is a critical aspect of federal patent law, and its interpretation is essential to resolving Minton's claim. Therefore, the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over this case, and the state court lacks subject-matter jurisdiction to consider Minton's appeal.
