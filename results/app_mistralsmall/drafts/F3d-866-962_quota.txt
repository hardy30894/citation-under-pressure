**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Appellants Presented Sufficient Evidence to Establish a Prima Facie Case of Retaliation Under the Federal Railroad Safety Act (FRSA).**

To establish a prima facie case of retaliation under the FRSA, 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C), a plaintiff must show: (1) he engaged in protected activity; (2) the railroad knew of the protected activity; (3) the railroad subjected him to an adverse employment action; and (4) there was a causal connection between the protected activity and the adverse action. *See generally Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53, 69 (2006); *Haddle v. Garrison*, 525 U.S. 121, 124 (1998). The burden then shifts to the railroad to articulate a legitimate, non-retaliatory reason for the action, and if it does so, the plaintiff must show that the proffered reason is pretextual. *See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973); *Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine*, 450 U.S. 248, 252-56 (1981).

Here, Appellants engaged in protected activity by reporting unsafe working conditions at the Creston bridge—specifically, the lack of side rails and lighting—and by participating in BNSF’s subsequent investigation. *See* 49 U.S.C. § 20109(a)(1)(C) (prohibiting retaliation against employees who "provide[] information relating to any Federal Railroad Safety Act violation or gross fraud, waste, or abuse"). BNSF undisputedly knew of this activity, as Appellants provided written and recorded statements to company officials. The adverse actions—Foster’s termination, Kline’s suspension, and Snyder’s reprimand—followed closely in time, creating an inference of causation. *See Clark Cty. Sch. Dist. v. Breeden*, 532 U.S. 268, 273 (2001) (noting that temporal proximity alone may suffice to show causation in some cases).

BNSF argues that the discipline was justified by rule infractions, but this proffered reason is pretextual. The record shows that the crew-change location was never reduced to writing, and Appellants’ testimony contradicted BNSF’s witnesses at the hearing. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986) (credibility determinations are for the jury). Moreover, the discipline was disproportionate: Foster, who had a clean record, was fired, while others received lesser penalties. *See Trans World Airlines, Inc. v. Thurston*, 469 U.S. 111, 122 (1985) (disparate treatment may evidence pretext).

**II. The District Court Erred in Concluding That Appellants Failed to Show That BNSF’s Post-Investigation Actions Were Adverse.**

The FRSA’s anti-retaliation provision protects against actions that "dissuade[] a reasonable worker from making or supporting a charge of discrimination." *Burlington Northern*, 548 U.S. at 68 (quoting *Robinson v. Shell Oil Co.*, 519 U.S. 337, 348 (1997)). The district court erred in holding that increased operations testing and reassignment to less lucrative work were not adverse. *See id.* at 67-69 (finding that reassignment to less desirable duties could be adverse). Appellants testified that they feared further discipline if they did not avoid certain coworkers, and Kline and Snyder signed up for less lucrative work as a result. This evidence, if credited, suffices to show adverse action. *See id.* at 68-69.

**III. The District Court Misapplied the FRSA’s Causation Standard by Requiring Direct Evidence of Retaliatory Motive.**

The FRSA incorporates the *McDonnell Douglas* burden-shifting framework, which permits circumstantial evidence of retaliation. *See White*, 548 U.S. at 69; *Burdine*, 450 U.S. at 253-56. The district court erred in requiring Appellants to present direct evidence of retaliatory motive, as the FRSA does not impose such a heightened standard. *See Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Prods., Inc.*, 530 U.S. 133, 142 (2000) (permitting circumstantial evidence to show pretext). The temporal proximity between Appellants’ protected activity and the adverse actions, combined with the weak proffered reasons for discipline, creates a genuine dispute of material fact. *See id.* at 146-48.

**IV. The District Court Improperly Deferred to BNSF’s Internal Investigation Findings.**

The district court accorded excessive deference to BNSF’s investigative findings, but such findings are not entitled to judicial deference under the FRSA. *See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 490 (1951) (holding that courts must independently assess the evidence). Here, the record shows that BNSF’s investigation was flawed: Appellants were not permitted to fully present their version of events, and the discipline imposed was inconsistent with prior practice. *See id.* at 488-91 (courts must examine the entire record).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this brief.
