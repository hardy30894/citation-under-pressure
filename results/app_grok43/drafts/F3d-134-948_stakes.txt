I. Selam Was Denied His Right to Compulsory Process.

The Indian Civil Rights Act guarantees that “[n]o Indian tribe in exercising powers of self-government shall . . . deny to any person accused of an offense punishable by imprisonment the right . . . to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses in his favor.” 25 U.S.C. § 1302(6). This provision incorporates the Sixth Amendment guarantee recognized in Washington v. Texas, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967), that the accused must be afforded “a meaningful opportunity to present a complete defense.” The tribal court failed to afford Selam that opportunity. Although Selam supplied his lay spokesperson with the names of five character witnesses, the spokesperson’s limited efforts produced only two witnesses, both of whom refused to testify favorably. Selam was never advised that he could obtain judicial process to secure their attendance, nor did the court issue subpoenas sua sponte. The resulting absence of any defense testimony other than Selam’s own denied him the compulsory-process right secured by the ICRA.

II. Selam’s Confrontation Claim Was Not Waived.

The district court held that Selam waived his Confrontation Clause claim by failing to present it to the Warm Springs Tribal Court of Appeals. That holding rests on an erroneous view of exhaustion under the ICRA. Federal habeas review of tribal convictions is governed by the same principles that require a petitioner to “fairly present” federal claims to the state courts before seeking relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2254. See Picard v. Connor, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971). Selam’s tribal appeal squarely challenged the denial of compulsory process; the same facts that supported that claim—introduction of the five-year-old victim’s out-of-court statements through four witnesses without any opportunity for cross-examination—also established a violation of the confrontation guarantee incorporated in § 1302(6). See Pointer v. Texas, 380 U.S. 400, 405 (1965) (holding that the right of confrontation is “a fundamental right”). Because the tribal appellate court had before it the identical factual predicate, the confrontation claim was fairly presented and therefore exhausted.

Even if the claim were viewed as unexhausted, the district court should have reached its merits. The introduction of the child’s statements through Dr. Beamer, the parents, and Investigator Clements without any showing of unavailability or particularized guarantees of trustworthiness denied Selam the “opportunity for effective cross-examination” required by the Confrontation Clause. See Ohio v. Roberts, 448 U.S. 56, 66 (1980). The error is of constitutional magnitude and warrants relief on the habeas petition.
