## Argument

The district court erred in denying Soares's motion to suppress the handgun found on his person because the police pat-frisked him in violation of the Fourth Amendment.

The Fourth Amendment guarantees individuals the right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures. U.S. Const. amend. IV. A traffic stop is a seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment, and it must be reasonable. Whren v. United States, 517 U.S. 806, 809-810 (1996). While a traffic stop is generally considered a Terry stop, see Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1 (1968), the police may conduct a pat-frisk of a passenger during a traffic stop if they have a reasonable suspicion that the passenger is armed and dangerous. Arizona v. Johnson, 555 U.S. 323, 327 (2009).

The Supreme Court has held that a pat-frisk is justified if the officer has a reasonable suspicion that the individual is armed and poses a threat to the officer or others. Terry, 392 U.S. at 27. The test is an objective one, and the officer's subjective fear or unease is not sufficient to justify a pat-frisk. Id. at 27. The court must consider the totality of the circumstances to determine whether the officer had a reasonable suspicion. United States v. Cortez, 449 U.S. 411, 417 (1981).

In this case, the police stopped the car in which Soares was a passenger for a minor traffic infraction - driving without headlights. The stop occurred in a high-crime area, late at night. While these factors may contribute to a reasonable suspicion, they are not sufficient on their own to justify a pat-frisk. See United States v. McKoy, 428 F.3d 38, 40 (1st Cir. 2005) (presence in a high-crime area is a relevant factor, but it is not enough to justify a pat-frisk).

The police observed the passengers in the car make movements down and to their left before the car stopped. Reid testified that this movement concerned him, and he wanted to "keep a good eye on everyone's movement from that point on." However, the movement observed by the police was not sufficient to create a reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous. See United States v. Am, 564 F.3d 25, 31 (1st Cir. 2009) (furtive movements alone are not enough to justify a pat-frisk).

Soares's behavior after the stop, including becoming agitated, verbally abusive, and moving his hands around, did not provide a sufficient basis for a reasonable suspicion that he was armed and dangerous. While Reid testified that he "got a little uneasy" due to Soares's behavior, this subjective feeling is not enough to justify a pat-frisk. Terry, 392 U.S. at 27.

The government cannot rely on Soares's agitation and verbal abuse to justify the pat-frisk, as this behavior is protected by the First Amendment. See Houston v. Hill, 482 U.S. 451, 461 (1987) (First Amendment protects verbal criticism and challenge directed at police officers). The police must have an objective basis for their suspicion, and Soares's behavior, while uncooperative, did not provide a sufficient basis for a reasonable suspicion that he was armed.

The pat-frisk of Soares was not justified under the Fourth Amendment, and the handgun found on his person should have been suppressed. The district court's denial of Soares's motion to suppress was therefore in error.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
