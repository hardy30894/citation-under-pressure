**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act, 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A), adopts state law as surrogate federal law only to fill a genuine gap in federal law—not whenever state law is merely “not inconsistent” with federal law. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary reading misreads the statute, ignores this Court’s precedent, and would displace the comprehensive federal scheme Congress designed for the Outer Continental Shelf.

**I. The OCSLA’s Text and Purpose Require a Gap-Filling Approach**

The OCSLA declares that federal law extends to the OCS “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). State law applies only “[t]o the extent that [it is] applicable and not inconsistent with this subchapter or with other Federal laws.” § 1333(a)(2)(A). This language mirrors the traditional federal-enclave rule: state law supplies the rule of decision only when federal law is silent. *See Chicago, R.I. & P. Ry. v. McGlinn*, 114 U.S. 542, 546 (1885) (upon cession of exclusive jurisdiction, “the laws of the State in force at the time of the cession” remain “until Congress shall otherwise provide”); *James v. Dravo Contracting Co.*, 302 U.S. 134, 142 (1937) (state law applies on federal enclaves only “in so far as such laws are not inconsistent with Federal law”). The OCSLA’s legislative history confirms that Congress feared federal law “was never designed to be a complete body of law in and of itself,” 99 Cong. Rec. 6963 (1953), and therefore borrowed state law to fill inevitable gaps. *See United States v. Sharpnack*, 355 U.S. 286, 293–94 (1958) (discussing similar purpose of Assimilative Crimes Act). The “applicable and not inconsistent” standard thus operates as a gap-filling mechanism, not an open-ended invitation to apply state law whenever it does not conflict with federal law.

**II. This Court’s OCSLA Precedent Confirms the Gap-Filling Rule**

In *Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co.*, 395 U.S. 352 (1969), this Court applied the OCSLA to fixed drilling platforms. The Court held that state wrongful-death law applied because the Death on the High Seas Act did not cover accidents on fixed platforms—there was a “void” in federal law. *Id.* at 366. The Court emphasized that the OCSLA “remove[d] any obstacle to the application of state law” only because federal admiralty law was “inapplicable.” *Id.* at 366. *Rodrigue* did not hold that state law applies whenever it is not inconsistent; it held that state law fills the gap left by federal law’s inapplicability. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Continental Oil Co. v. London S.S. Owners’ Mut. Ins. Ass’n*, 417 F.2d 1030, 1036 (5th Cir. 1969), correctly distilled this principle: state law is adopted only when “necessary to fill a significant void or gap” in federal law. That test respects the OCSLA’s structure and this Court’s teaching.

**III. The FLSA Is a Comprehensive Federal Scheme That Leaves No Gap**

The Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. §§ 201–219, establishes a “comprehensive” and “uniform” national policy on wages and hours. *Powell v. United States Cartridge Co.*, 339 U.S. 497, 516 (1950). The FLSA applies to employees on the OCS by virtue of § 1333(a)(1). It sets minimum wage, overtime, and recordkeeping requirements, and it provides a federal remedy for violations. The FLSA’s savings clause, 29 U.S.C. § 218(a), preserves state laws that are “more favorable” to employees, but that clause does not incorporate state law into federal law on the OCS. It merely prevents preemption of state law where state law independently applies. On the OCS, state law does not independently apply; it applies only as surrogate federal law under the OCSLA. Because the FLSA supplies a complete set of wage-and-hour rules, there is no “void or gap” for state law to fill. *See Pacific Coast Dairy v. Department of Agriculture*, 318 U.S. 285, 294 (1943) (on a federal enclave, state law “cannot be applied” where federal law “covers the field”); *Collins v. Yosemite Park & Curry Co.*, 304 U.S. 518, 530 (1938) (state law displaced where federal law “occupies the field”).

**IV. The Ninth Circuit’s “Not Inconsistent” Test Would Undermine Federal Uniformity**

The Ninth Circuit held that state law applies whenever it is “not inconsistent” with federal law, regardless of whether federal law already covers the subject. That reading would allow state wage-and-hour laws to supplant the FLSA’s carefully calibrated standards on the OCS, creating a patchwork of state regulations that Congress sought to avoid. The OCSLA’s purpose was to provide a “stable and predictable” legal regime for offshore development. *Rodrigue*, 395 U.S. at 355. The “gap” requirement ensures that federal law remains the primary source of regulation, with state law stepping in only when federal law is silent. The Ninth Circuit’s approach would turn the OCSLA into a vehicle for importing state law wholesale, contrary to the statute’s text and this Court’s interpretation in *Rodrigue*.

**Conclusion**

The OCSLA permits application of state law only when there is a gap in federal law. Because the FLSA comprehensively regulates wages and hours on the OCS, no such gap exists. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
