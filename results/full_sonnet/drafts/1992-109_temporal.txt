**ARGUMENT**

**I. Due process demands only the single competency standard set out in Dusky v. United States, and that standard governs a defendant's decision to waive counsel or plead guilty.**

This Court has never required more than one constitutional floor of mental competence for a defendant to participate in his own criminal proceeding. In *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), the Court held that the test for competency is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding -- and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." *Id.* at 402. That standard was not confined to a defendant's capacity to sit through testimony; it defines the minimum rational capacity the Constitution requires before the State may proceed against him at all, and before he may make any of the decisions--including whether to plead guilty or forgo counsel--that a competent defendant is entitled to make for himself. *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), confirmed that this floor is itself a matter of due process, holding that a State denies due process when it fails to provide adequate procedures to protect against trying a defendant who lacks the capacity described in *Dusky*. Nothing in *Pate* suggests that a different, higher threshold of sanity attaches once a defendant chooses to represent himself or to admit guilt rather than contest the charges. The competency inquiry approved in *Pate* and *Dusky* concerns the defendant's rational faculties, not the wisdom or complexity of the particular choice he makes with those faculties.

**II. This Court's waiver decisions ask only whether the defendant's choice was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary--not whether he possesses some heightened capacity for "reasoned choice."**

The decisions to waive counsel and to plead guilty are governed by a distinct but equally settled body of law asking whether the waiver was knowing and voluntary given the particular defendant's own understanding--not whether he possesses some elevated quantum of mental acuity. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court explained that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," *id.* at 464, and that whether an accused has validly waived counsel "must depend, in each case, upon the particular facts and circumstances surrounding that case, including the background, experience, and conduct of the accused." *Id.* That inquiry is factual and contextual; it is not a psychiatric threshold set above the rational understanding described in *Dusky*. *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), reaffirmed that courts must not presume waiver of counsel from a silent record, but it imposed no standard of mental competence beyond the capacity to understand and intentionally relinquish the right at issue. And in *Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann*, 317 U.S. 269 (1942), the Court held that a defendant may waive counsel and conduct his own defense, explaining that the Constitution "does not force a lawyer upon a defendant" who "knowingly and intelligently" elects to proceed without one. *Id.* at 279. The competence required for that election was never treated as distinct from, or higher than, the competence required to stand trial at all.

The same is true of guilty pleas. In *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), this Court held that because a guilty plea itself waives the constitutional rights to trial, confrontation, and against self-incrimination, the record must affirmatively show the plea was entered voluntarily and understandingly. *Id.* at 242-244. *Boykin*'s concern was the adequacy of the record demonstrating a knowing and voluntary choice--not a separate mental-capacity requirement beyond that already guaranteed by *Dusky* and *Pate*. Nothing in *Boykin* turns on the defendant's psychiatric capacity for "reasoned choice"; it turns on whether the colloquy establishes that the defendant understood the rights he surrendered and the consequences of his plea.

**III. A heightened "reasoned choice" standard has no doctrinal foundation and conflates two distinct due process inquiries that this Court has always kept separate.**

These decisions demonstrate that this Court has always analyzed two separate questions when a defendant elects to plead guilty or dispense with counsel: first, whether he is mentally competent under *Dusky*; second, whether his particular waiver was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary under *Johnson v. Zerbst*, *Carnley*, and *Boykin*. Neither inquiry has ever required proof that a defendant's mental capacity rises above the *Dusky* floor merely because the choice at hand is momentous. The seriousness of a defendant's decision bears on how searching the waiver colloquy must be under *Johnson v. Zerbst* and *Boykin*--not on whether some undefined, higher psychiatric standard of "reasoned choice" must first be satisfied. Collapsing these two settled inquiries into a single heightened competency test finds no support in *Dusky*, *Pate*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, *Carnley*, *Adams*, or *Boykin*. The Ninth Circuit's imposition of such a standard rests on no authority of this Court and should be rejected.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
