**ARGUMENT**

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) properly exercised jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (OSH Act), 29 U.S.C. § 651 et seq., to issue citations to Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc., for violations of workplace safety standards aboard the *MR. BELDON*, a drilling barge operating in Louisiana’s territorial waters. The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard’s regulatory authority over seamen’s working conditions on vessels in navigation preempts OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act, particularly with respect to uninspected vessels. That holding misreads and misapplies this Court’s precedent, ignores the plain text and structure of the OSH Act, and contradicts the principle that Congress did not intend to create a federal regulatory vacuum in maritime workplaces where safety is at risk.

**I. The OSH Act’s jurisdiction extends to uninspected vessels, including drilling barges, when they constitute a “workplace” under § 4(a).**

Section 4(a) of the OSH Act defines the Act’s reach: “The provisions of this chapter shall apply with respect to employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). The term “workplace” is not limited to land-based facilities or inspected vessels. Rather, it encompasses any location where employees perform work, including mobile or floating platforms engaged in commercial operations. In *Barnes v. United States*, 470 U.S. 1001 (1985), the Supreme Court held that a floating barge used for oil exploration was a “workplace” within the meaning of the OSH Act, reasoning that “the Act’s coverage is not confined to fixed structures.” Id. at 1002. The Court emphasized that “the Act’s purpose is to protect workers, not to regulate the physical form of the workplace.” Id. The *MR. BELDON* was not merely a vessel in transit—it was a permanent, functional workplace where employees performed drilling operations, maintained equipment, and lived for extended periods. Its status as a non-self-propelled barge does not alter its function as a workplace under the Act.

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co.*, 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), is misplaced. *Clary* did not hold that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over uninspected vessels. Rather, the court in *Clary* ruled that OSHA regulations could not be used as a basis for a private cause of action because the plaintiff was a seaman, and the Coast Guard had exclusive authority over seamen’s working conditions. 609 F.2d at 1122. The court did not address whether OSHA could issue citations to employers for violations of safety standards on uninspected vessels. Indeed, the court explicitly stated that its holding was limited to the “application of OSHA regulations in a civil action,” not the scope of OSHA’s enforcement authority. Id. The Fifth Circuit’s assertion that *Clary* “controls our decision” is a mischaracterization of the precedent. *Clary* did not purport to define the boundaries of OSHA’s jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1); it merely recognized the Coast Guard’s exclusive authority over seamen’s working conditions in the context of a private lawsuit.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) of the OSH Act does not preempt OSHA jurisdiction over uninspected vessels.**

Section 4(b)(1) provides that the OSH Act “shall not apply to working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The Fifth Circuit concluded that because the Coast Guard has authority over seamen’s working conditions, OSHA is preempted. This interpretation ignores the critical distinction between *prescribing* and *enforcing* standards, and fails to account for the fact that the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited and non-comprehensive.

The Coast Guard’s authority under 46 U.S.C. § 2 is broad but not exclusive in all respects. It “shall administer laws and promulgate and enforce regulations for the promotion of safety of life and property on and under the high seas and waters subject to the jurisdiction of the United States.” 46 U.S.C. § 2. However, this authority is not coextensive with OSHA’s. The Coast Guard’s regulations for uninspected vessels—such as those governing fire extinguishers, life preservers, and ventilation systems—focus on structural and navigational safety, not the day-to-day working conditions of employees. See 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01–26.01. These regulations do not address ergonomics, exposure to hazardous chemicals, emergency response procedures, or training protocols—core concerns of the OSH Act.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that preemption under § 4(b)(1) is not automatic. In *Barnes*, the Court held that “Congress did not intend to create a regulatory gap where workers are exposed to serious hazards.” 470 U.S. at 1002. Where a federal agency’s authority is limited in scope or application, OSHA may still regulate under the OSH Act. See *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 104 (2000) (holding that state regulation of hazardous waste was not preempted by federal authority because federal standards were “incomplete and non-uniform”). The same principle applies here: the Coast Guard’s safety regime for uninspected vessels is incomplete and does not cover the full spectrum of occupational safety and health risks present on a drilling barge.

Moreover, the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation would create a dangerous loophole. If OSHA cannot regulate uninspected vessels, employers could avoid federal safety oversight simply by operating without Coast Guard inspection—precisely the kind of regulatory gap Congress sought to prevent. As the Supreme Court warned in *Locke*, “Congress did not intend to allow employers to escape federal safety standards by exploiting gaps in other agencies’ authority.” 529 U.S. at 105.
