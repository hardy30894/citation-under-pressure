**Argument**

I. Guam’s 2004 Clean Water Act consent decree triggered CERCLA section 113(f)(3)(B), so Guam’s only federal remedy is contribution, not cost recovery.

CERCLA’s structure makes the distinction between sections 107 and 113 central. Section 107 permits recovery of cleanup costs incurred in the first instance, while section 113 supplies the contribution remedy for parties that have resolved liability to the United States or a State. See **Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161-68 (2004)**; **United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 132-39 (2007)**. This Court has recognized that “cost recovery” under section 107 and “contribution” under section 113 are distinct causes of action with different triggers and limitations periods. **Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. at 139**; **AmeriPride Services Inc. v. Texas Eastern Overseas, Inc., 782 F.3d 474, 479-82 (9th Cir. 2015)**.

The 2004 decree here resolved Guam’s liability for a response action. CERCLA provides that a party “who has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action” in a judicially approved settlement “may seek contribution.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). The decree required Guam to close the Ordot Dump, pay a civil penalty, and design and install a dump cover system to eliminate leachate discharges. Those obligations were not merely prospective regulatory housekeeping; they were the means by which Guam compromised the very environmental liability that EPA had sued to enforce. A settlement need not use the word “CERCLA” to trigger section 113(f)(3)(B). See **ASARCO LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co., 866 F.3d 1108, 1122-24 (9th Cir. 2017)**; **Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1001-03 (6th Cir. 2015)**; **Niagara Mohawk Power Corp. v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc., 596 F.3d 112, 126-28 (2d Cir. 2010)**. What matters is substance: the decree imposed cleanup obligations in exchange for judicial approval and the cessation of enforcement litigation. **ASARCO LLC, 866 F.3d at 1123-24**.

The court of appeals erred by treating the decree’s disclaimer of liability as dispositive. This Court has repeatedly looked to the operative terms of an agreement, not labels or boilerplate, in determining its legal effect. **United States v. ITT Continental Baking Co., 420 U.S. 223, 236-38 (1975)**; **United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673, 681-82 (1971)**. A settlement may resolve liability even when it contains no admission of fault. **ASARCO LLC, 866 F.3d at 1123**; **Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries Inc., 937 F.3d 928, 931-33 (7th Cir. 2019)**. The decree here did exactly that by fixing Guam’s cleanup obligations and ending the EPA enforcement action. **Florida Power Corp., 810 F.3d at 1002-03**.

II. Because Guam’s settlement triggered section 113(f)(3)(B), Guam cannot circumvent CERCLA’s three-year limitations period by recasting its claim as section 107 cost recovery.

Once section 113(f)(3)(B) is available, it is the exclusive avenue for recouping those response costs. **Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. at 139**; **Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 163-67**; **Jersey City Redevelopment Authority v. PPG Industries, Inc., 655 F. App’x 857, 862-64 (3d Cir. 2016)**. Section 113(g)(3) imposes a three-year limitations period that begins when the settlement is entered. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3); see **Niagara Mohawk Power Corp., 596 F.3d at 128-29**. That period expired long before Guam filed suit in 2017.

Allowing Guam to proceed under section 107 would invert CERCLA’s carefully calibrated scheme. The Court has rejected efforts to use section 107 as an end-run around section 113’s contribution regime. **Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. at 139**; **Agere Systems, Inc. v. Advanced Environmental Technology Corp., 602 F.3d 204, 224-25 (3d Cir. 2010)**; **Bernstein v. Bankert, 733 F.3d 190, 201-05 (7th Cir. 2013)**. Guam seeks exactly that end-run: a six-year limitations period for a claim that, by statute, became a contribution claim upon settlement. That theory is irreconcilable with CERCLA’s text and structure. **Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 167-68**; **Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. at 137-39**.

III. Guam’s contrary arguments fail under settled principles of statutory interpretation.

Guam relies on the absence of an express CERCLA citation in the 2004 decree, but statutory rights turn on what the parties actually settled, not on magic words. **Armour & Co., 402 U.S. at 681-82**; **ITT Continental Baking Co., 420 U.S. at 236-38**. Guam also invokes its continued ownership of the dump, but CERCLA’s trigger is not ownership; it is resolution of liability to the United States for some or all of a response action. 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B); **Niagara Mohawk Power Corp., 596 F.3d at 127-28**. Finally, Guam’s appeal to fairness cannot overcome the statute Congress enacted. As this Court has instructed, when statutory text is clear, courts must apply it as written. **Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004)**; **Connecticut National Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992)**.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
