**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Circuit Erred in Affirming Damages Based on Samsung’s Entire Profit Where the Patented Design Applied Only to a Component of the Accused Products.**

The Patent Act provides that a design patent infringer “shall be liable to the owner to the extent of his total profit.” 35 U.S.C. § 289. However, this Court has long recognized that the statute’s plain text does not foreclose apportionment where the patented design covers only a component of a multicomponent product. *See* *Apple Inc. v. Samsung Elec. Co.*, 786 F.3d 983, 996 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (en banc) (recognizing that § 289 “does not categorically preclude[] the award of less than an infringer’s entire profits”), *aff’d in part, rev’d in part sub nom. Samsung Elecs. Co. v. Apple Inc.*, 137 S. Ct. 429 (2016). This principle comports with the general rule in patent law that damages must be apportioned to the patented feature. *See* *Garretson v. Clark*, 111 U.S. 120, 121 (1884) (“[T]he patentee . . . must in every case give evidence tending to separate or apportion the defendant’s profits and the patentee’s damages between the patented feature and the unpatented features.”).

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach—permitting damages based on the entire profit of a multicomponent product—ignores this foundational principle. In *Apple v. Samsung*, the Federal Circuit affirmed a damages award based on Samsung’s entire profits from smartphones embodying Apple’s patented designs, even though those designs covered only the front face, bezel, and graphical user interface. *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996. The court reasoned that § 289’s “total profit” language precluded apportionment. *Id.* at 995. This was error. As this Court later recognized in *Samsung*, the Federal Circuit’s interpretation “c[ould] not be squared with § 289’s text.” 137 S. Ct. at 435.

This Court’s decision in *Samsung* makes clear that apportionment is required where the patented design covers only a component of the accused product. The Court explained that § 289’s “total profit” provision “does not categorically preclude[] the award of less than an infringer’s entire profits,” and that “the damages awarded should be limited to the infringing component’s contribution to the value of the accused product.” *Id.* at 435 (internal quotation marks omitted). The Court remanded for the Federal Circuit to consider whether the jury’s damages award could be sustained under this standard. *Id.*

The Federal Circuit’s subsequent decision in *Apple* failed to apply this principle. The court acknowledged that apportionment may be appropriate but held that the jury’s damages award was supported by substantial evidence. *Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996. This was error. The Federal Circuit improperly deferred to the jury’s award without requiring evidence that the patented design drove consumer demand for the entire product. *See id.* at 997 (noting that the jury could have reasonably found that the patented designs were “the predominant basis” for consumer demand). The court’s approach conflates causation with apportionment, permitting damages based on the entire profit even where the patented design is only one of many features driving demand.

**II. The District Court Erred in Failing to Instruct the Jury on Apportionment.**

Samsung requested a jury instruction requiring apportionment of damages to the patented design. The district court refused, instead instructing the jury that it could award damages based on Samsung’s entire profits. This was error. The Supreme Court has long held that damages must be apportioned to the patented feature. *See Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121. The Federal Circuit has likewise recognized that apportionment is required in design patent cases where the patented design covers only a component of the accused product. *See Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996; *see also* *Nordock, Inc. v. Systems Inc.*, 801 F.3d 1365, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (requiring apportionment in design patent cases).

The district court’s refusal to instruct the jury on apportionment deprived Samsung of a fair trial. The jury was left to speculate about the extent to which the patented design contributed to the value of Samsung’s products. This violated the fundamental principle that damages must be tied to the patented invention. *See* *Panduit Corp. v. Stahlin Bros. Fibre Works, Inc.*, 575 F.2d 1152, 1156 (6th Cir. 1978) (requiring damages to be “reasonably related to the infringer’s wrong”).

**III. The Damages Award Must Be Reversed and Remanded for a New Trial on Damages.**

Because the district court erred in failing to instruct the jury on apportionment, the damages award cannot stand. The jury’s award was based on Samsung’s entire profits, not the portion attributable to the patented design. This was prejudicial error. *See* *Uniloc USA, Inc. v. Microsoft Corp.*, 632 F.3d 1292, 1315 (Fed. Cir. 2011) (reversing damages award where jury was not properly instructed on apportionment).

The Federal Circuit’s decision affirming the damages award is likewise erroneous. The court improperly deferred to the jury’s award without requiring evidence of apportionment. *See Apple*, 786 F.3d at 996. This approach ignores the Supreme Court’s holding in *Samsung* and the longstanding principle that damages must be apportioned to the patented feature. *See* 137 S. Ct. at 435; *Garretson*, 111 U.S. at 121.

**Conclusion**

The Federal Circuit erred in affirming a damages award based on Samsung’s entire profits where the patented design covered only a component of the accused products. The district court’s failure to instruct the jury on apportionment compounded this error. The damages award must be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial on damages consistent with this Court’s holding in *Samsung*.
