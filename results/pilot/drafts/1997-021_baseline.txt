**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NLRB’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Act (NLRA) does not prohibit employers from testing employee sentiment regarding union representation. Indeed, Congress recognized that employers may have a legitimate interest in determining whether a union continues to enjoy majority support—particularly in the context of successorship, where the bargaining unit has changed. But the NLRB’s rigid “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard, which requires an employer to possess objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition *before* conducting a poll, renders the poll itself a legal nullity. This standard is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the NLRA’s purpose of promoting stable collective bargaining relationships.

The Board’s rule creates a paradox: an employer may not conduct a poll unless it already has sufficient grounds to withdraw recognition, yet the sole purpose of the poll is to *determine* whether such grounds exist. As the Board itself acknowledged in *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989), polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” and thus should be used sparingly. But if the employer must already have “objective indications” of a loss of majority support to justify the poll, then the poll serves no purpose beyond confirming what the employer already believes. That is not a tool for testing sentiment—it is a formality.

The courts of appeals that rejected the Board’s standard did so not out of judicial overreach, but because the Board’s rule rendered polling functionally useless. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits held that an employer may conduct a poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence falls short of justifying withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc.*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc.*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). This approach is consistent with the NLRA’s goal of enabling employers to avoid bargaining with a minority union—a violation of § 8(a)(2)—while still protecting employees’ rights under § 7. The Board’s standard, by contrast, forces employers to choose between risking an unfair labor practice by bargaining with a union they believe lacks majority support, or conducting a poll that is legally impermissible unless they already have the answer.

The Board’s attempt to justify its standard by claiming that RM elections are the “preferred” method of testing union support is disingenuous. If RM elections are preferred, then the evidentiary standard for polling should be *lower*, not identical. Yet the Board applies the same standard to both. This inconsistency undermines its own rationale. As the Supreme Court has held, “an agency’s interpretation of its own regulations must be reasonable and consistent with the statute.” *Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 843 (1984). The Board’s standard fails that test.

Moreover, the Board’s rule is not grounded in the NLRA. Section 9(c)(1)(B) authorizes the Board to conduct elections, but it does not regulate employer-initiated polls. The Board’s creation of a “reasonable doubt” standard is an exercise of policy judgment, not statutory command. While deference is due to the Board’s expertise, *see* *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 42 (1987), that deference does not extend to rules that are arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to the Act’s purpose. The Board’s standard is neither.

II. THE NLRB’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED “REASONABLE DOUBT” IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked “objective reasonable doubt” about the union’s majority status rests on a selective and erroneous interpretation of the record. The ALJ and the Board discounted key statements from employees who had expressed clear opposition to the union, while ignoring the cumulative weight of evidence indicating a significant loss of support.

First, the Board erred in refusing to count the statements of Pete McArthur, Dennis Wehr, and Randy Zoltack. Though they were not in the unit on January 25, 1991, the Board’s reliance on that date is arbitrary. The company’s decision to conduct the poll was made *after* the union’s demand on January 2, and the employees’ statements were made *before* the sale. The relevant inquiry is not whether the employees were still employed on January 25, but whether the employer had a reasonable basis to doubt the union’s majority status *at the time of the poll*. The Board’s rigid adherence to a single date ignores the temporal context of the employer’s decision-making.

Second, the Board improperly dismissed the statements of Mike Ridgick and Dennis Marsh. Ridgick’s 1986 inquiry into decertification, though remote, was not irrelevant. The ALJ found it “too remote” to be probative, but the Board failed to explain why a single statement from 1986—made during a period of known union dissatisfaction—should be disregarded while more recent statements from other employees were given weight. Marsh’s statement that he was “not getting his money’s worth” from the union is not merely dissatisfaction with representation; it is a clear expression of opposition to continued union representation. *Destileria Serralles, Inc.*, 289 N.L.R.B. 51, 1988 WL 214114, at *3 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989). The Board’s refusal to treat such statements as evidence of loss of support is contrary to precedent.
