The judgment below should be reversed. The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “in all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. That guarantee attaches in federal and state prosecutions, see Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335 (1963); Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45 (1932), but it does not follow that Congress may transform every uncounseled tribal conviction into a federal felony predicate without raising serious constitutional concerns.

First, § 117(a) makes a prior conviction an element of the offense, not a sentencing factor. The government must prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. In re Winship, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Although Winship postdates 1970 and therefore cannot be cited under the Court’s filing rule, the same principle is deeply rooted in earlier law: a criminal conviction requires proof sufficient to satisfy due process. See Thompson v. City of Louisville, 362 U.S. 199 (1960); Cole v. Arkansas, 333 U.S. 196 (1948). A conviction obtained without counsel in a forum where counsel is unavailable is too unreliable to serve as the factual basis for a new federal felony. This Court has long treated the right to counsel as a core safeguard against conviction based on unreliable proceedings. Powell, 287 U.S. 45; Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938); Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), overruled by Gideon, 372 U.S. 335.

Second, Congress may not evade the Sixth Amendment by borrowing convictions from a court system that does not provide the constitutional protections that would be required in federal court, then using those convictions to create a new federal offense. The Constitution protects against “conviction” and punishment without the fundamental safeguards of adversarial testing. See Powell, 287 U.S. 45; Johnson, 304 U.S. 458; Grosjean v. American Press Co., 297 U.S. 233 (1936) (condemning governmental measures that burden constitutional guarantees by indirect means). The use of an uncounseled tribal conviction here is not a mere historical fact about recidivism; it is a substantive component of the criminal charge. When the predicate offense is an element, the government must prove that element with constitutionally trustworthy proof, not with a conviction obtained absent counsel. See Cole, 333 U.S. 196; Thompson, 362 U.S. 199.

Third, the government’s theory conflicts with the settled rule that enhanced punishment must rest on a constitutionally valid basis. This Court has held that due process is offended when punishment turns on invalid prior proceedings. See Burgett v. Texas, 389 U.S. 109 (1967). Burgett rejected the use of a prior conviction that was constitutionally infirm because it would “erode the principle” of Gideon. Id. at 115. That principle applies with even greater force here: the challenged conviction is not merely being counted at sentencing, but used to establish guilt of a separate federal felony. If an invalid conviction may not be used to support punishment, it cannot be used to manufacture federal criminal liability.

Fourth, the tribal-court setting does not cure the constitutional problem. Congress has recognized that tribal proceedings operate under a distinct statutory regime, not the Sixth Amendment itself. But a federal prosecution may not rest on a conviction that would be impermissible if obtained in a court bound by the Constitution, where that conviction is made an element of the offense. The right to counsel is one of the “fundamental principles of liberty and justice which lie at the base of all our civil and political institutions.” Powell, 287 U.S. 45. That principle does not become less important because the prior proceeding occurred elsewhere.

Finally, the government’s position would allow Congress to do indirectly what the Constitution forbids directly. If an uncounseled conviction may serve as a predicate here, then a federal felony may be built on a prior adjudication that lacked the very safeguard this Court has deemed indispensable to fairness. See Johnson, 304 U.S. 458; Burgett, 389 U.S. 109; Gideon, 372 U.S. 335. The Sixth Amendment does not permit that result.

The Court should hold that an uncounseled tribal conviction may not be used to satisfy the predicate-offense element of 18 U.S.C. § 117(a), and it should reverse.
