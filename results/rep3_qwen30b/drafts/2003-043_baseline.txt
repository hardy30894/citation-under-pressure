**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Government must assess individual partners for a tax debt in order to collect from them after the partnership has been assessed—requires a straightforward application of the Internal Revenue Code and settled principles of statutory construction. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the Government may not collect a partnership’s unpaid taxes from individual partners without first making individualized assessments against those partners. The Government’s failure to do so, and the resulting expiration of the three-year statute of limitations under § 6501(a), bars any collection attempt against the partners, even if the partnership was timely assessed. This conclusion is compelled by the text, structure, and precedent of the Internal Revenue Code.

**I. THE GOVERNMENT MUST INDIVIDUALLY ASSESS PARTNERS TO COLLECT FROM THEM; THE PARTNERSHIP ASSESSMENT DOES NOT EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS FOR INDIVIDUALS**

The Internal Revenue Code treats partnerships and individual partners as distinct taxpayers. Section 7701(a)(1) defines “person” to include “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation,” and § 7701(a)(14) defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax.” Because each partner is a separate “person” and “taxpayer,” the Government must assess each partner individually to trigger the ten-year collection period under § 6502(a). The statute does not permit the Government to extend the limitations period for individual partners merely by assessing the partnership.

This interpretation is not merely logical—it is required by the text. Section 6502(a) provides that a tax “may be collected by levy or by a proceeding in court, but only if the levy is made or the proceeding begun—within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” The phrase “the assessment of the tax” refers to the assessment of the taxpayer against whom collection is sought. As the Ninth Circuit correctly observed, “the individual or entity assessed must be a separately identified ‘taxpayer.’” *United States v. Galletti*, 298 F.3d 1107, 1155 (9th Cir. 2002) (amended opinion). The IRS’s assessment against the partnership did not constitute an assessment against the individual partners, who remain separate taxpayers under the Code.

The Government’s contrary argument—that a partnership assessment automatically extends the limitations period for partners—has no basis in the statute. The Code does not say that an assessment against a partnership “extends” the limitations period for partners. It says the period is extended “after the assessment of the tax,” and the tax must be assessed against the taxpayer. *See* 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). The Government’s theory would render the word “taxpayer” in § 6502(a) meaningless, as it would allow collection from individuals without any assessment against them. That cannot be the law. *See* *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561, 563 (7th Cir. 1995) (noting that the statute “does not permit the Government to collect from a partner without first assessing the partner”).

**II. THE GOVERNMENT’S ALTERNATIVE THEORY—STATE-LAW JOINT AND SEVERAL LIABILITY—FAILS BECAUSE IT DOES NOT CREATE A RIGHT TO COLLECT WITHOUT A JUDGMENT**

The Government alternatively argues that California law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, and that this allows the Government to collect from partners without individual assessments. This argument fails for the same reason: under California law, a judgment against the partnership is not a judgment against the partner. *Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c)* provides that “a judgment against a partnership is not by itself a judgment against a partner.” To reach a partner’s assets, the Government must obtain a separate judgment against the partner. *See also* 9 B.E. Witkin, *Summary of California Law* § 60V (9th ed. Supp. 2001) (“[A] partner must be individually named and served in the action or in a later suit, and judgment entered against that partner, in order to reach the partner’s personal assets.”).

The Government has not obtained such a judgment. It has not even attempted to do so. The three-year limitations period under § 6501(a) has long since expired. *See* *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that “the Government does not have any lien” against a partner’s property unless the partner was individually assessed). The Government’s claim is not merely unenforceable—it is legally impossible to enforce, because the statute of limitations has run.

**III. THE GOVERNMENT’S PRECEDENTS ARE DISTINGUISHABLE AND DO NOT SUPPORT ITS POSITION**

The Government cites *United States v. Wright*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), but that case is inapposite. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners. *Id.* at 562. Because the partners were individually assessed within the three-year period, the ten-year collection period applied to them as well. The Seventh Circuit did not address whether a partnership assessment alone could extend the limitations period for partners. *Id.* at 563. The Government’s reliance on *Wright* is therefore misplaced.

Similarly, *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), does not support the Government’s position. There, the partner had already paid his share of the tax and sought a refund. The court held that he was not entitled to a refund because he was liable under state law. *Id.* at 910. The court did not hold that the Government could collect from the partner without an individual assessment.
