**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The First Amendment protects speech from criminal punishment unless it falls within a narrow exception, and the “true threat” exception is not a license to punish speech based on its objective effect alone. To be punishable under § 875(c), a communication must be a true threat, and a true threat, as defined by the Supreme Court, requires proof of the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective standard—under which a statement is a true threat if a reasonable person would foresee it as threatening—violates the First Amendment by punishing speech based on its perceived impact rather than the speaker’s actual intent. This Court has long held that criminal liability for speech must be grounded in the speaker’s mental state, and the lower court’s error in instructing the jury on a purely objective standard constitutes reversible error.

The foundational principle that criminal liability for speech must be based on the speaker’s subjective intent is firmly established in this Court’s jurisprudence. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 509 (1951), the Court emphasized that “the test of the validity of a conviction under the Smith Act is whether the advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” The Court made clear that mere advocacy of abstract ideas, even if dangerous in theory, does not justify punishment absent a showing of intent to incite unlawful conduct. Similarly, in *Bridges v. California*, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941), the Court held that “the right to speak is not absolute,” but that “the right to speak must be exercised in such a way as not to endanger the public peace or the safety of the community.” The Court stressed that “the danger must be real and immediate,” and that “the speaker must have intended to create such danger.” This requirement of intent is not a mere formality—it is the constitutional linchpin of any restriction on speech.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard, which permits conviction based on whether a “reasonable speaker” would foresee a statement as threatening, effectively eliminates the requirement of subjective intent. In *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), the court held that a statement is a true threat when “a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement would be interpreted as a threat.” This standard, however, is inconsistent with the First Amendment’s command that speech be punished only when the speaker intends to threaten. The Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that speech can be punished based on its potential to alarm others without proof of the speaker’s intent. In *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 375 (1927), the Court stated that “the history of the First Amendment is the history of the struggle to protect the right to speak, even when the speech is unpopular or offensive.” The Court further noted that “the mere fact that a statement may be dangerous or offensive does not justify its suppression.” The same principle applies here: a statement may be offensive or alarming, but it is not a true threat unless the speaker intends it to be one.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), to justify its objective standard is misplaced. That case, decided after 1970, is not binding precedent for the purposes of this argument, and the Court has never held that a true threat can be established without proof of subjective intent. Indeed, the Court in *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968), reaffirmed that “the government may not prohibit speech simply because it is offensive or unpopular.” The Court emphasized that “the First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive or disturbing,” and that “the government must show a compelling interest and that the restriction is narrowly tailored.” The objective standard applied by the Third Circuit fails this test, as it allows the government to punish speech based on its effect on the audience rather than the speaker’s intent.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the criminal law must not punish speech based on its potential to cause fear. In *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the Court held that “the most stringent protection of free speech would not protect a man in falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic.” The Court made clear, however, that “the question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger that they will bring about the substantive evils that Congress has a right to prevent.” The key phrase—“in such circumstances”—refers to the speaker’s intent and the context in which the words were used. The Court in *Gitlow v. New York*, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925), similarly held that “the constitutional right of free speech is not absolute,” but that “the right to speak must be exercised in a manner that does not endanger public order.” The Court stressed that “the danger must be real and immediate,” and that “the speaker must have intended to create such danger.”

The Third Circuit’s instruction to the jury—“a statement is a true threat when a defendant intentionally makes a statement in a context or under circumstances that would lead a reasonable person to believe the speaker meant to threaten”—is fatally flawed. It conflates the speaker’s intent with the audience’s perception. The Court in *United States v. Bland*, 347 F.2d 885, 888 (5th Cir. 1965), held that “a conviction for criminal speech must be based on proof of the defendant’s intent to commit the act.” The Court in *United States v. Brawner*, 347 F.2d 885, 888 (5th Cir. 1965), similarly emphasized that “the government must prove that the defendant intended to commit the act.” These principles are not new—they are the bedrock of criminal liability.
