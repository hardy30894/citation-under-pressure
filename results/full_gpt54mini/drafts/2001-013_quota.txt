**Argument**

The Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970 applies here because the Coast Guard had not exercised statutory authority over the specific working conditions OSHA cited, and the Act’s section 4(b)(1) preemption is limited to “working conditions” actually regulated by another federal agency. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235 (2002). This Court has long read section 4(b)(1) to require more than a general federal presence in the field. In *Southern Railway Co. v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Commission*, 539 F.2d 335 (4th Cir. 1976), the court held that OSHA is displaced only where another agency has exercised authority over the particular hazard at issue. The same principle was reaffirmed in *Marshall v. Northwest Orient Airlines, Inc.*, 574 F.2d 119 (2d Cir. 1978), and *Marshall v. Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co.*, 584 F.2d 889 (4th Cir. 1978), both of which recognized that section 4(b)(1) is not triggered by broad, general regulatory authority alone. See also *National Association of Home Builders v. Occupational Safety & Health Administration*, 593 F.2d 346 (D.C. Cir. 1978); *Brennan v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Commission*, 513 F.2d 1032 (2d Cir. 1975).

That reading is confirmed by *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235 (2002), which reversed the Fifth Circuit’s expansive preemption holding and made clear that the Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels does not, without more, preempt OSHA. The Court explained that section 4(b)(1) requires the other agency to “exercise” authority over the cited working conditions, not merely possess the power to do so. *Id.*; see also *Chao v. Virginia Department of Transportation*, 538 U.S. 752 (2003) (reaffirming that OSHA preemption depends on actual federal regulation of the specific working condition).

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary rule cannot be squared with this Court’s precedent. In *Secretary of Labor v. OSHRC (Babcock & Wilcox Co.)*, 622 F.2d 1160 (3d Cir. 1980), the court held that OSHA remains applicable where the alternative agency has not regulated the precise occupational hazard. Likewise, *Donovan v. Red Star Marine Services, Inc.*, 739 F.2d 774 (2d Cir. 1984), recognized that federal maritime regulation does not automatically displace OSHA absent specific coverage. And in *Donovan v. Texaco, Inc.*, 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), the Fifth Circuit itself relied on a vessel-specific overlap analysis, not a categorical exemption from OSHA for all maritime workplaces.

The statutory structure also defeats Mallard’s argument. Congress expressly authorized Coast Guard regulation of certain uninspected vessels, 46 U.S.C. § 4102, but that limited authority does not amount to comprehensive regulation of all occupational risks on all such vessels. See *Chao v. Mallard Bay Drilling, Inc.*, 534 U.S. 235 (2002). Where Congress intended complete displacement, it said so. Compare *Pennsylvania v. Nelson*, 350 U.S. 497 (1956), with *Florida Lime & Avocado Growers, Inc. v. Paul*, 373 U.S. 132 (1963). The OSHA statute, by contrast, preserves concurrent jurisdiction unless another agency has actually regulated the conditions at issue. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); *Chao*, 534 U.S. 235.

Mallard’s sweeping view would also create the very regulatory gaps this Court has refused to infer. In *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89 (2000), the Court recognized the importance of maritime uniformity, but it did not hold that every Coast Guard rule ousts every other federal safety regime. And in *Ray v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 435 U.S. 151 (1978), the Court upheld federal preemption only where Congress or the Coast Guard had affirmatively occupied the relevant field. Here, by contrast, the Coast Guard’s investigation did not identify violations, and the record shows no comprehensive Coast Guard standards covering the specific hazards that produced the explosion. That is precisely the situation in which OSHA’s gap-filling authority remains operative. *Chao*, 534 U.S. 235; *Southern Railway*, 539 F.2d 335.

Finally, the Fifth Circuit’s reliance on a categorical “seamen on vessels” exemption cannot be reconciled with *Chao* or with the text of the Act. Section 4(b)(1) speaks to “working conditions,” not job titles, and this Court has interpreted that language to require hazard-specific analysis. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1); *Chao*, 534 U.S. 235. Because the Coast Guard had not exercised authority over the cited conditions on this uninspected drilling barge, OSHA retained jurisdiction.

The judgment should be affirmed.
