**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN FINDING THAT APPELLANT CONSENTED TO THE REMOVAL AND RETENTION OF DANELSY UNDER ARTICLE 13(A) OF THE HAGUE CONVENTION.**

The Hague Convention on the Civil Aspects of International Child Abduction, incorporated into U.S. law by the International Child Abduction Remedies Act, 42 U.S.C. §§ 11601–11610, requires the prompt return of a child wrongfully removed or retained from his or her habitual residence, unless the respondent establishes one of the Convention’s narrow exceptions. *See* Hague Convention, art. 12; *Mozes v. Mozes*, 239 F.3d 1067, 1070 (9th Cir. 2001). One such exception is where the petitioner “consented to or subsequently acquiesced in the removal or retention.” *Id.* at 1071 (citing Hague Convention, art. 13(a)). Consent must be “clear and unequivocal,” and courts must examine the totality of the circumstances to determine whether such consent existed. *Id.* (quoting *Friedrich v. Friedrich*, 983 F.2d 1396, 1401 (6th Cir. 1993)).

Here, the district court erred in finding that Appellant consented to Danelsy’s removal and retention. The record does not support a finding of clear and unequivocal consent. Appellant’s testimony was inconsistent regarding the nature of any agreement with Mena—at times claiming she agreed to a two-week visit, at others asserting an agreement contingent on her eventual immigration to the United States. *See* App. 123–25. Such ambiguity cannot constitute the “clear and unequivocal” consent required under the Convention. *See Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1071.

Moreover, even if Appellant’s statements could be construed as consent, they were not voluntary. Mena’s testimony reveals that he used his financial leverage—his advance for work in Honduras—to purchase Danelsy’s one-way ticket to the United States. *See* App. 89. This coercive context undermines any claim of voluntary consent. *See In re Application of Adan*, 437 F. Supp. 2d 1178, 1185 (W.D. Wash. 2006) (finding consent involuntary where respondent was pressured by petitioner’s financial control).

Finally, the district court improperly relied on Appellant’s provision of Danelsy’s legal documents as evidence of consent. *See* App. 120. While such conduct may be relevant, it is not dispositive. *See Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1071. The Convention requires a showing of consent to the *removal and retention*, not merely cooperation in travel logistics. *Id.* Here, Appellant’s actions were consistent with facilitating a temporary visit, not an indefinite retention. *See id.*

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT’S FINDING OF FACT WAS IN CLEAR ERROR.**

Under the Hague Convention, factual findings are reviewed for clear error. *See United States v. State of Washington*, 157 F.3d 630, 644 (9th Cir. 1998). The district court’s conclusion that Appellant consented to Danelsy’s removal and retention was clearly erroneous. The record reflects no clear agreement, no voluntariness, and no intent to relinquish custody permanently. *See Mozes*, 239 F.3d at 1071.

Appellant’s subsequent actions further demonstrate her lack of consent. Upon realizing Danelsy had not been returned as promised, Appellant immediately sought assistance from Panamanian authorities and the U.S. military. *See* App. 126–28. This conduct is inconsistent with consent to retention. *See Friedrich*, 983 F.2d at 1401 (noting that prompt action to secure a child’s return weighs against a finding of consent).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the district court erred in finding that Appellant consented to Danelsy’s removal and retention under Article 13(a) of the Hague Convention. The factual findings underlying this conclusion were clearly erroneous. This Court should reverse the district court’s denial of Appellant’s petition and remand with instructions to order Danelsy’s return to Panama.
