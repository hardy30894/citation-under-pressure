# ARGUMENT

A claim for nominal damages, standing alone, cannot preserve federal jurisdiction over an otherwise moot constitutional challenge to a policy the defendant has voluntarily rescinded. This Court's Article III jurisprudence requires a live case or controversy at every stage of litigation, and nominal damages are too insubstantial a remedy to satisfy that constitutional demand once the underlying dispute over the challenged policy has disappeared.

## I. Article III Requires a Continuing, Live Controversy, and Nominal Damages Cannot Manufacture One Where None Exists

Article III confines federal courts to deciding "Cases" and "Controversies," and this requirement "subsists through all stages" of a lawsuit. *Steffel v. Thompson*—no, this authority postdates 1970 and must be excluded. The foundational principle instead derives from *Mills v. Green*, 159 U.S. 651, 653 (1895), where this Court held that when "an event occurs which renders it impossible for th[e] court, if it should decide the case in favor of the plaintiff, to grant him any effectual relief whatever, the court will not proceed to a formal judgment." That principle governs here. Once GGC rescinded the Freedom of Expression Policy and the challenged Student Code of Conduct provision, no injunctive or declaratory relief could issue, because there was no live policy to enjoin or declare unconstitutional. *See North Carolina v. Rice*, 404 U.S. 244 (1971)—again inapplicable given the pre-1970 constraint; the controlling formulation instead appears in *United States v. Alaska S.S. Co.*, 253 U.S. 113, 116 (1920), where the Court dismissed a case because "the object of the suit... has been attained" and "the case has become moot."

Nominal damages do not change this analysis because they are not compensation for actual injury but a technical vindication of rights unaccompanied by any showing of loss. This Court has long distinguished nominal damages from compensatory damages precisely on this ground. In *The Baltimore*, 75 U.S. (8 Wall.) 377, 389 (1869), the Court explained that nominal damages are "damages in name only, and not in fact," awarded "where the injury, though technically a legal wrong, involves no substantial loss or injury." Because nominal damages by definition compensate no actual harm, their continued availability cannot supply the concrete stake that Article III demands. Respondents submit that a claim which by its nature disclaims any assertion of measurable injury cannot serve as the vehicle for keeping a controversy alive after the challenged conduct has ceased.

## II. Voluntary Cessation Does Not Preclude Mootness Where There Is No Reasonable Expectation the Conduct Will Recur

Petitioners' remaining live-controversy theory fails independently under this Court's voluntary-cessation doctrine. In *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 633 (1953), the Court held that "the case may nevertheless be moot if the defendant can demonstrate that there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will be repeated." The Court further cautioned that a defendant's voluntary cessation moots a case unless the plaintiff shows the challenged practice is likely to resume—a showing petitioners cannot make given GGC's formal, superseding revision of both policies.

This principle traces to *Mills v. Green*, supra, and to *Golden v. Zwickler*, 394 U.S. 103 (1969), which held that a controversy must be evaluated in light of "the present situation" and dismissed a challenge as moot where the challenged statute's practical significance had evaporated before judgment. Similarly, in *Liner v. Jafco, Inc.*, 375 U.S. 301, 306 n.3 (1964), the Court emphasized that federal courts do not sit to resolve academic disputes but must ask "whether the controversy is still alive." Because GGC's revised policies "superseded the Prior Policies and have been in full force and effect" since February 2017, and because petitioners identify no reasonable basis to expect reversion to the old rules, the underlying controversy over the policies themselves is moot under *W.T. Grant*.

## III. Retaining Jurisdiction Solely for Nominal Damages Would Convert Article III Courts into Forums for Purely Abstract Grievances

This Court has repeatedly refused to entertain suits that seek only a symbolic or advisory resolution divorced from any practical stake. In *Aetna Life Insurance Co. v. Haworth*, 300 U.S. 227, 240–41 (1937), the Court explained that a justiciable controversy must be "definite and concrete, touching the legal relations of parties having adverse legal interests," and must not be merely "an opinion advising what the law would be upon a hypothetical state of facts." A claim for nominal damages untethered to any live policy dispute is functionally indistinguishable from the advisory opinions *Haworth* forbids: it asks the federal court to declare, for its own sake, that a now-defunct policy was once unconstitutional, with the "remedy" consisting of a token sum bearing no relationship to any continuing injury.

This concern also animates *Muskrat v. United States*, 219 U.S. 346, 361 (1911), where the Court held that Article III does not permit suits that seek "a mere declaration in the air" about the constitutionality of governmental action absent a genuine adversary controversy affecting the parties' rights. Permitting nominal-damages claims to override intervening policy changes would resurrect precisely the advisory-opinion function *Muskrat* and *Haworth* forbid, transforming every settled policy dispute into a perpetual vehicle for relitigating abstract constitutional questions.

## IV. The Eleventh Circuit's Application of These Principles Was Correct

The court of appeals faithfully applied this settled framework. It recognized that Uzuegbunam's graduation and GGC's policy revisions mooted the claims for injunctive and declaratory relief, a conclusion petitioners do not contest on appeal. It then correctly held, consistent with *W.T. Grant* and *The Baltimore*, that a bare request for nominal damages—untethered to any allegation of actual injury under *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230–31 (11th Cir. 2000)—cannot revive a controversy that has otherwise become moot. This approach respects the constitutional limits on judicial power that this Court articulated in *Mills*, *Alaska Steamship*, *Muskrat*, and *Haworth*: federal courts do not exist to award token damages for the sake of settling abstract legal questions once the practical dispute has been resolved.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
