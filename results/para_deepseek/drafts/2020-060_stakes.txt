## ARGUMENT

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that the 2004 Consent Decree triggered CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B), thereby foreclosing Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery action. The decree did not “resolve[] [Guam’s] liability to the United States . . . for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Because the decree only resolved Clean Water Act violations—not CERCLA liability—Guam retains its right to seek full cost recovery under § 107.

### I. The Consent Decree Did Not Resolve CERCLA Liability

Section 113(f)(3)(B) requires a settlement that “resolves” a party’s “liability” for a “response action” or its costs. The 2004 Consent Decree did none of these things.

**First**, the decree addressed only Clean Water Act violations. The EPA’s complaint alleged that Guam discharged untreated leachate into waters of the United States without a permit—a Clean Water Act violation, not a CERCLA claim. The decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the dump, and install a cover system. Critically, the decree never mentioned CERCLA, never identified Guam as a “potentially responsible party,” and never allocated response costs under CERCLA. As the Sixth Circuit has recognized, “a settlement that resolves only non-CERCLA liability does not trigger section 113(f)(3)(B).” *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1003 (6th Cir. 2015).

**Second**, the decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for a “response action” as defined by CERCLA. CERCLA defines “response” as “remove, removal, remedy, and remedial action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25). “Remedial action” includes “the collection of leachate and runoff.” *Id.* § 9601(24). But the decree’s requirement to install a cover system was aimed at eliminating *ongoing* discharges of untreated leachate—not at remediating *past* contamination from hazardous substances. The decree did not require Guam to address the decades of contamination from munitions, DDT, or Agent Orange. A settlement that does not resolve liability for CERCLA response costs cannot trigger § 113(f)(3)(B). *See Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 168 (2004) (§ 113(f) requires a settlement that “resolves liability” for response costs).

**Third**, the decree’s express disclaimer of liability confirms that it did not resolve Guam’s liability. The decree states it is “without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. While a disclaimer does not automatically defeat a finding of resolved liability, *see Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017), it is a significant factor. Here, the decree’s terms—a civil penalty and a closure plan—bore no relationship to CERCLA response costs. The decree did not adjudicate Guam’s liability for the dump’s contamination; it merely required Guam to comply with the Clean Water Act going forward. Such an agreement does not “resolve” liability for past hazardous substance releases.

### II. Section 107 Remains Available Absent a CERCLA Settlement

In *Cooper Industries*, this Court held that a party may bring a § 107(a) cost-recovery action even if it is a PRP, so long as it has not been subject to a § 113(f) action or settlement. 543 U.S. at 163 n.3. The D.C. Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that a non-CERCLA settlement triggers § 113(f) and precludes § 107—contradicts *Cooper* and the text of CERCLA.

Section 107(a)(4)(B) allows “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person” to be recovered from any PRP. This provision is not limited to innocent parties. *See United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 138 (2007) (a PRP that incurs response costs may sue under § 107(a)(4)(B)). Guam incurred response costs—closing the dump, designing a cover system, and eventually remediating the site—that were necessary to address contamination caused by the Navy. The Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for those costs; it simply compelled Guam to take action. Guam remains liable for the full cost of cleanup, and the Navy remains a PRP. Under *Atlantic Research*, Guam may seek contribution under § 107.

The D.C. Circuit relied on the principle that a settling PRP must use § 113, not § 107. *See, e.g., Solutia Inc. v. McWane, Inc.*, 672 F.3d 1230, 1238 (11th Cir. 2012). But that principle applies only when the settlement resolved CERCLA liability. Here, the decree did not. Guam’s § 107 claim is timely because the statute of limitations for remedial actions is six years, 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B), and Guam filed within six years of completing the closure in 2011.

### III. The D.C. Circuit’s Interpretation Frustrates CERCLA’s Purpose

Congress enacted CERCLA to ensure prompt cleanup and to make responsible parties pay. *General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). If a non-CERCLA settlement can trigger § 113(f)(3)(B) and bar § 107, then a PRP like Guam—forced to act by the EPA under a different statute—loses its ability to recover from the true responsible party. That result undermines CERCLA’s goal of holding polluters accountable. The Navy used the Ordot Dump for decades, depositing hazardous substances. Guam, as the current owner, was compelled to clean up. The D.C. Circuit’s decision leaves Guam with no remedy—a result Congress could not have intended.

### CONCLUSION

The 2004 Consent Decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for a CERCLA response action. Guam may therefore proceed under § 107. The judgment of the D.C. Circuit should be reversed.
