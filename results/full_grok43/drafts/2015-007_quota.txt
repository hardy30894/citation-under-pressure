The Florida capital sentencing scheme satisfies the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584, 609 (2002), held only that aggravating circumstances “operate as the functional equivalent of an element of a greater offense” and therefore must be found by a jury. Florida’s statute assigns that role to the jury through its advisory verdict, while reserving final sentencing to the trial judge. This division of labor was expressly approved in Spaziano v. Florida, 468 U.S. 447, 465 (1984), and Hildwin v. Florida, 490 U.S. 638, 640-41 (1989) (per curiam), both of which remain undisturbed on the question of advisory recommendations.

Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000), and its progeny require that any fact increasing the maximum penalty be submitted to the jury; they do not mandate that the jury’s determination be unanimous or that it constitute the final sentence. Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 358 (2004), confirmed that Ring announced a procedural rule whose retroactive application is not compelled. Florida’s requirement of a majority jury recommendation therefore supplies the jury finding Ring demands without violating the Sixth Amendment.

The same framework governs mental retardation. Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002), left “to the States the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” Florida’s three-prong statutory test, administered by the court after an evidentiary hearing, is one such procedure. Nothing in Ring or Atkins requires that the factual determination of intellectual disability be made by a jury, let alone unanimously.

Florida’s scheme also accords with the Eighth Amendment. Proffitt v. Florida, 428 U.S. 242, 252 (1976), upheld the identical statutory structure as providing “specific and detailed guidance” that avoids arbitrary imposition of death. Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153, 195 (1976), likewise approved bifurcated proceedings in which the sentencer weighs aggravating and mitigating circumstances. The absence of a unanimity requirement does not render the process cruel or unusual; the Court has never imposed such a mandate on capital sentencing.

Because the jury found the existence of aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt and the trial court independently weighed those circumstances against mitigation, Hurst’s sentence rests on constitutionally sufficient jury fact-finding. The judgment of the Florida Supreme Court should be affirmed.
