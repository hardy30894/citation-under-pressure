**ARGUMENT**

**I. PLAIN ERROR REVIEW IS LIMITED TO THE TRIAL RECORD**

A federal appellate court may correct plain error only where the error is evident from the record and affects substantial rights. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936). The plain-error doctrine does not authorize courts to consider matters outside the trial record when assessing prejudice or the fairness of a proceeding. *Id.* at 160–61 ("If the error did not affect the substantial rights of the accused, it will not be noticed.").

This Court has consistently confined plain-error review to the record below. In *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632 (1896), the Court refused to consider evidence outside the trial transcript when evaluating whether an error was prejudicial. *Id.* at 658 ("The error must be one which appears upon the record, and which is so substantial as to affect the rights of the accused."). Similarly, in *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150 (1940), the Court emphasized that appellate review is confined to the record, stating that "[t]he power of the appellate court to correct errors is limited to those appearing in the record." *Id.* at 253.

The Eleventh Circuit’s consideration of matters outside the trial record—such as Greer’s prior convictions—to determine prejudice was erroneous. *See United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993) (applying plain-error review strictly to the record). The court’s reliance on extra-record evidence to assess the fairness of the trial contravenes the well-established principle that appellate review is confined to the trial transcript. *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658.

**II. PREJUDICE MUST BE DEMONSTRATED FROM THE RECORD**

To establish prejudice under plain-error review, a defendant must show that the error "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 63 (2002) (quoting *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993)). This requires a showing that the error "seriously affect[ed] the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings." *Id.* (quoting *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985)).

The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis improperly expanded this standard by considering Greer’s prior convictions to assess prejudice. *See United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152 (1982) (requiring prejudice to be shown from the record). The court’s reliance on extra-record evidence to determine whether the error undermined the fairness of the trial was inconsistent with this Court’s precedent. *See Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658.

**III. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT’S APPROACH UNDERMINES FINALITY AND JUDICIAL ECONOMY**

Permitting appellate courts to consider extra-record evidence in plain-error review would disrupt the finality of judgments and encourage collateral attacks on convictions. *See United States v. Timmreck*, 441 U.S. 780 (1979) (emphasizing the importance of finality in criminal judgments). The Eleventh Circuit’s approach risks transforming plain-error review into a broad inquiry into a defendant’s character rather than a focused assessment of trial errors. *See United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. at 253 (limiting review to the record).

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit erred by considering matters outside the trial record to assess prejudice under plain-error review. This Court should reaffirm that plain-error review is confined to the record and that prejudice must be demonstrated from the trial transcript. *Wiborg*, 163 U.S. at 658. The judgment below should be affirmed.
