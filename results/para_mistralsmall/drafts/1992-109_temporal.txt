**ARGUMENT**

I. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to ensure that a defendant who seeks to waive counsel and plead guilty is competent to make a “reasoned choice” among the available alternatives. *Godinez v. Moran*, 509 U.S. 389, 399 (1993) (citing *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987)). This standard is higher than the competency to stand trial standard articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), which requires only a “rational understanding” of the proceedings and “sufficient present ability to consult with [one’s] lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding.” *Id.* at 402. The “reasoned choice” standard ensures that a defendant’s waiver of fundamental constitutional rights is the product of a competent and voluntary decision. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (holding that waiver of constitutional rights must be “an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege”).

II. The trial court in this case failed to apply the heightened “reasoned choice” standard when Moran sought to discharge his counsel and plead guilty. The record demonstrates substantial evidence calling Moran’s competency into question, including his recent suicide attempt, his use of multiple medications, and his monosyllabic responses during the plea colloquy. *See Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 268 (9th Cir. 1992). Under *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), a trial court has an affirmative duty to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte whenever it has a “bona fide doubt” about a defendant’s competency. *Id.* at 385. The Supreme Court in *Pate* emphasized that due process requires such an inquiry to protect the integrity of the judicial process and the rights of the accused. *Id.* at 384.

III. The Nevada courts erred by relying on psychiatric evaluations that assessed Moran’s competency to stand trial rather than his ability to make a reasoned choice to waive counsel and plead guilty. *See Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975) (distinguishing between competency to stand trial and competency to waive constitutional rights). The evaluations conducted by Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky were limited to Moran’s capacity to understand the charges and assist counsel—criteria relevant only to trial competency. *See Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. They did not address whether Moran possessed the higher level of mental functioning necessary to make a voluntary, knowing, and intelligent waiver of his constitutional rights. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464.

IV. The trial court’s failure to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte violated due process. In *Pate*, the Supreme Court held that a state’s failure to inquire into a defendant’s competency when there is substantial evidence of incompetence deprives the defendant of due process. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 385. The Court reasoned that the state’s “failure to observe procedures adequate to protect [the defendant’s] rights” rendered the proceedings fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 385-86. Here, the trial court’s acceptance of Moran’s waiver without ensuring his competency to make a reasoned choice was similarly deficient. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464 (requiring that waivers of constitutional rights be knowing and voluntary).

V. The post-conviction proceedings did not cure the error. The Nevada courts applied the wrong legal standard by conflating competency to stand trial with competency to waive constitutional rights. *See Drope*, 420 U.S. at 172. Moreover, the psychiatric evaluations relied upon were not designed to assess Moran’s ability to make a reasoned choice. *See Dusky*, 362 U.S. at 402. Due process requires that competency determinations be made under the correct legal standard. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 385.

VI. The Ninth Circuit correctly concluded that the record did not support a finding that Moran was capable of making a reasoned choice when he waived counsel and pleaded guilty. *Moran*, 972 F.2d at 268. The trial court’s failure to conduct a competency hearing sua sponte violated due process, and the state courts’ reliance on an incorrect legal standard further undermined the fairness of the proceedings. *Pate*, 383 U.S. at 385; *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. at 464.

**CONCLUSION**
For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
