# ARGUMENT

## I. The Final-Decision Requirement Must Be Construed to Favor, Not Foreclose, Judicial Review

Section 5 of the Railroad Retirement Act, 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), permits a claimant aggrieved by "a final decision" of the Board to obtain review in the courts of appeals. That provision must be read against this Court's longstanding instruction that statutory review provisions are construed generously rather than grudgingly. In *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48 (1955), the Court held that a statutory grant of judicial review extends to agency action unless Congress has clearly withdrawn it, refusing to infer a repeal of review rights from silence or ambiguity. That principle matured into the settled rule that "only upon a showing of 'clear and convincing evidence' of a contrary legislative intent should the courts restrict access to judicial review." *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 141 (1967); *accord Rusk v. Cort*, 369 U.S. 367, 379-80 (1962) (review will be denied only where there is persuasive reason to believe Congress meant to foreclose it). Nothing in § 355(f), which speaks broadly of "any final decision," supplies the requisite clear and convincing evidence that Congress meant to exclude decisions denying reopening from review.

## II. Finality Is a Practical, Not a Formal, Concept, Turning on Whether the Agency Has in Fact Passed on the Merits

This Court has long held that whether agency action is "final" does not depend on the label the agency affixes to its own order, but on the order's practical effect. In *Frozen Food Express v. United States*, 351 U.S. 40 (1956), the Court held reviewable an ICC order that carried immediate practical consequences for regulated parties, regardless of its formal characterization. In *Federal Communications Commission v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co.*, 309 U.S. 134 (1940), the Court emphasized that Congress committed to the courts, not to rigid procedural labels, the task of policing the line between reviewable and unreviewable agency action. And in *Interstate Commerce Commission v. Jersey City*, 322 U.S. 503 (1944), the Court held that when an agency grants rehearing and reconsiders its earlier order on the merits, the resulting order is itself a new, independent decision subject to judicial review, because the agency has, as a practical matter, redetermined the rights of the parties.

These principles govern here. When the Board convenes a hearing, receives evidence, and evaluates whether "new and material evidence" or "administrative error" justifies revisiting a prior claim, 20 C.F.R. § 261.2, it does not merely decline to act—it adjudicates the merits of the claimant's continuing entitlement to benefits. That is exactly what occurred: the Board's hearing officer conducted an oral hearing and made findings on the timeliness and substance of Salinas's claim of administrative error. Under *Jersey City*, such reconsideration on the merits yields a new, reviewable decision, regardless of the "reopening" label attached to the proceeding.

## III. The Second Circuit's Reviewability Rule, and This Court's Analogous Social Security Precedent, Confirm That Reopening Denials Are Reviewable

Confronting this precise question, the Second Circuit held in *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966), that § 355(f) confers jurisdiction to review Board decisions denying a petitioner's request to reopen a prior benefits determination. The court found no basis in the statute for distinguishing between a decision granting or denying benefits in the first instance and a decision declining to disturb an earlier denial after the agency has actually examined the claim anew.

That conclusion draws further support from this Court's construction of the closely analogous Social Security Act, whose review provision likewise limits review to a "final decision . . . made after a hearing." 42 U.S.C. § 405(g). Addressing the identical reopening question, the Second Circuit held in *Cappadora v. Celebrezze*, 356 F.2d 1 (2d Cir. 1966), that although a bare refusal to reopen is ordinarily discretionary and unreviewable, a decision that in fact reconsiders the merits—rather than summarily rejecting the request—is reviewable as a final decision, because the agency's own conduct transforms the reopening request into a fresh adjudication. The Board's disposition of Salinas's petition fits that description precisely: rather than summarily rejecting his request, the Board convened a hearing, took evidence, and issued reasoned findings on the merits of his claim.

## Conclusion

The Fifth Circuit's categorical rule that reopening denials are unreviewable cannot be reconciled with the presumption of reviewability applied in *Shaughnessy*, *Abbott Laboratories*, and *Rusk v. Cort*; with the practical, functional finality test of *Pottsville Broadcasting*, *Frozen Food Express*, and *Jersey City*; or with the persuasive reasoning of *Szostak* and *Cappadora*. The Board's decision denying Salinas's request to reopen his 2006 application was a "final decision" within the meaning of § 355(f), and the judgment below should be reversed.
