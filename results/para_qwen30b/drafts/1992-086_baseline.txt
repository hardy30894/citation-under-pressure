**ARGUMENT**

The First Amendment guarantees that “Congress shall make no law…abridging the freedom of speech.” U.S. Const. amend. I. This protection extends to religious speech, and when a government creates a limited public forum—by policy or practice—it may not exclude speech based on its religious content. The Second Circuit erred in concluding that the Center Moriches School District’s facilities were not a limited public forum open to religious groups, and in holding that the District’s blanket exclusion of religious use violated no constitutional principle. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to show a religious film series on family values constitutes a content-based exclusion of speech in a forum that, by its own conduct, has been opened to non-religious expressive activity. Such exclusion is unconstitutional under *Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983), and *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 273 (1981), and violates the Free Speech Clause of the First Amendment.

The District’s facilities are a limited public forum. The Supreme Court has held that a “limited public forum” is created when a government “by policy or practice” opens a non-traditional public space for expressive activity. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 46. The Second Circuit acknowledged this standard but incorrectly concluded that no such forum existed here. The record, however, demonstrates that the District has repeatedly permitted non-religious groups to use its facilities for expressive purposes. The District allowed the Southern Harmonizers, a secular musical group, to perform in the high school auditorium. It also permitted the Center Moriches Free Public Library to sponsor a four-night lecture series on parapsychology, transpersonal psychology, and metaphysics—topics that, while esoteric, were presented in a non-religious context. The speaker, Jerry Huck, testified that his lectures were grounded in science and that any references to religion were “a fascinating sideline” and not the purpose of the series. These uses were not incidental or isolated; they were deliberate, repeated, and publicly announced. The District’s own policy of allowing such non-religious, non-curricular groups to use its facilities establishes a limited public forum.

Once a limited public forum is created, the government may not exclude speech based on its religious content. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 273. The Court in *Widmar* held that a state university could not deny access to student religious groups when it had opened its campus to other student organizations. The Court emphasized that “the University has created a forum generally open for use by student groups,” and that “the First Amendment protects the right of students to use the forum for religious expression.” Id. at 267, 273. The same principle applies here. The District has opened its facilities to non-religious groups for expressive purposes—groups that, like Lamb’s Chapel, are outside the school system. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use the auditorium for a film series on family values, which is religious in perspective but otherwise identical in purpose and format to the non-religious programs permitted, constitutes a content-based exclusion that violates the First Amendment.

The District’s argument that its policy of excluding religious use is viewpoint-neutral is unavailing. The District claims that its rule is “neutral” because it applies to all religious groups. But the First Amendment does not permit content-based discrimination, even if it is applied uniformly. *Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995). A rule that bars all religious speech in a forum open to non-religious speech is not viewpoint-neutral—it is content-based. The District’s policy treats religious speech differently from non-religious speech, even when the subject matter is identical. The film series on family values, though religious in perspective, is no different in substance from the lectures on psychology or the musical performances allowed by the District. The District’s refusal to allow religious expression while permitting secular expression on the same subject is a classic example of impermissible content discrimination.

The Second Circuit’s reliance on *Deeper Life* cases is misplaced. Those cases involved outside groups seeking access to facilities that had never been opened to any non-curricular use. Here, the District has repeatedly permitted non-religious groups to use its facilities for expressive purposes. The distinction is critical. In *Mergens*, the Court held that a high school that allowed non-curricular student groups to meet must also allow a religious student group to meet, because the school had created a limited public forum. 496 U.S. at 247. The same logic applies here: the District has created a limited public forum by allowing non-religious groups to use its facilities. It cannot now exclude religious speech simply because it is religious.

Finally, the District’s claim that the exclusion is necessary to avoid violating the Establishment Clause is without merit. The Establishment Clause does not permit the government to suppress religious speech in order to avoid the appearance of endorsement. *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free School Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 395 (1993). The government may not “suppress religious expression simply because it is religious.” *Texas v. Johnson*, 491 U.S. 397, 414 (1989). The District’s policy is not neutral; it is a blanket ban on religious speech in a forum that is otherwise open. That is not neutrality—it is discrimination.

The judgment of the Second Circuit must be reversed. The District’s refusal to allow Lamb’s Chapel to use its facilities for a religious film series violates the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause. The District created a limited public forum and may not exclude speech based on its religious content. The judgment below is contrary to settled precedent and must be vacated.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Second Circuit and remand for entry of judgment in favor of petitioner.
