**ARGUMENT**

**I. Ohio’s clemency proceedings create no protected life or liberty interest under the Due Process Clause.**

The Sixth Circuit correctly held that Woodard has no constitutionally protected life or liberty interest in the clemency process itself. That conclusion is compelled by a long line of this Court’s decisions recognizing that executive clemency is a matter of grace, not right. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. 150, 160 (1833) (“A pardon is an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws.”). Because clemency is “a private, though official act,” *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. 333, 380 (1866), it “cannot be claimed as a right.” *Id.* at 381. The power to grant clemency “is a power of the executive, and is not subject to judicial control.” *Knote v. United States*, 95 U.S. 149, 154 (1877). Indeed, “the whole of the pardoning power is committed to the executive.” *Ex parte Wells*, 59 U.S. 307, 310 (1855). Thus, “a pardon is an act of grace,” *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927), and “the decision to grant or withhold it is a matter of executive discretion that ‘has not traditionally been the business of courts.’” *Connecticut Bd. of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981) (quoting *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 225 (1976)). 

Woodard’s argument that the Due Process Clause attaches to clemency because it is “an integral part of the … system for finally adjudicating guilt or innocence” misapprehends the nature of clemency. *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387, 393 (1985), dealt with a first appeal as of right, which is a “defendant’s one and only appeal.” *Id.* at 394. Clemency, by contrast, occurs long after adjudication is complete. It is a “wholly separate” executive act, not a component of the judicial process. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. The great distance between trial and clemency, *Woodard v. Ohio Adult Parole Auth.*, 107 F.3d 1178, 1188 (6th Cir. 1997) (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part), means that any due process protections—if they existed at all—would be “minimal, perhaps even barely perceptible.” *Id.* But the Court has never imposed such protections, and doing so would “create disincentives for States to offer clemency petitioners an opportunity to have a personal interview.” *Id.* at 1193 n.2. 

**II. The voluntary interview option does not violate Woodard’s Fifth Amendment rights.**

Woodard’s Fifth Amendment claim is premature. The challenged regulation gives him the option to request a personal interview; it does not compel him to answer any questions. *Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07; Ohio Admin. Code § 5120-1-01*. “The privilege against self-incrimination is a personal privilege,” *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598 (1896), and “may be waived by the witness.” *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 429 (1943). If Woodard chooses to participate, he “remains free to say as much or as little … as he wishes.” *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1193 (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). Nothing in the record suggests that the parole board will compel him to divulge incriminating information. *Cf. Hoffa v. United States*, 385 U.S. 293, 304 (1966) (“No claim that the petitioner was a victim of coercion.”). 

The Sixth Circuit’s remand to assess “the nature of the APA’s clemency procedures” and “whether the clemency interview procedure might impose an unconstitutional condition” is speculative. *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1188. As in *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 324 (1976), such a risk is “premature” when “the facts of which can only be imagined at this point.” *Woodard*, 107 F.3d at 1194 (Nelson, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). This Court should not constitutionalize a hypothetical future proceeding. *See Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78, 114 (1908) (the privilege “is not a fundamental right”). 

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s dismissal of Woodard’s complaint should be affirmed.
