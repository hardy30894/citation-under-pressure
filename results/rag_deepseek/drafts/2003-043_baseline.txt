**ARGUMENT**

The Internal Revenue Code unambiguously requires that the government must assess a tax against each individual taxpayer before it may collect that tax from the taxpayer’s assets beyond the ordinary three-year limitations period. Because the IRS never assessed the Gallettis or the Briguglios, the statute of limitations has expired, and the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the government’s claims.

**I. The Code Requires an Assessment Against Each Separate Taxpayer**

Section 6203 provides that an “assessment shall be made by recording the liability of the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual, a trust, estate, partnership, association, company or corporation.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). An individual partner is a “person” and thus a separate “taxpayer” from the partnership itself. The implementing regulation confirms that a valid assessment “shall provide identification of the taxpayer.” 26 C.F.R. § 301.6203-1.

The collection statute, § 6502(a), likewise presumes that the taxpayer against whom a deficiency was assessed is the same taxpayer for whom the limitations period is extended to ten years. The assessment against the partnership therefore extended the limitations period only for the partnership, not for the individual partners. The government’s failure to assess the partners within the three-year period of § 6501(a) bars it from collecting from them now.

**II. The Supreme Court Has Consistently Distinguished Partnership and Partner Entities**

This Court has long recognized that a partnership is a separate entity from its individual partners for bankruptcy and tax purposes. In *Francis v. McNeal*, 228 U.S. 695, 700 (1913), the Court observed that the Bankruptcy Act assumed “that the partnership and individual estates both were to be administered,” and that it would be “an anomaly to allow proceedings in bankruptcy against joint debtors from some of whom … the debt could be collected in full.” Similarly, *Meek v. Centre County Banking Co.*, 264 U.S. 499 (1924), and *Liberty Nat. Bank v. Bear*, 276 U.S. 215, 220 (1928), confirm that partnership property and individual property are separate estates, with partnership debts paid first from partnership assets and individual debts from individual assets. The tax code follows the same principle: a partnership is a distinct taxpayer.

In *Guaranty Trust Co. v. Commissioner*, 303 U.S. 493, 494 (1938), the Court treated a partnership’s fiscal year separately from a partner’s individual tax year, recognizing the partnership as an independent accounting entity. *Lusthaus v. Commissioner*, 327 U.S. 293 (1946), and *Commissioner v. Tower*, 327 U.S. 280 (1946), further illustrate that the tax consequences of partnership arrangements depend on the reality of the partnership and the separate status of the partners. And in *Commissioner v. Culbertson*, 337 U.S. 733, 740 (1949), the Court reiterated that a wife may become a partner for tax purposes only if she contributes capital or services, underscoring that partnership status is not automatic and that partners are separate taxpayers.

**III. The Government’s State-Law Theory Is Unavailing**

The government argues that California partnership law makes partners jointly and severally liable for partnership debts, so it need not assess them individually. But California law itself requires a creditor first to obtain a judgment against the partner before satisfying a debt from the partner’s assets. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c). The IRS obtained no such judgment. And the time for doing so has expired because the assessment against the partnership did not extend the limitations period for the partners.

The government’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. That case merely held that a partner who voluntarily paid a partnership tax could not obtain a refund. As the Ninth Circuit explained, the district court in *Young* acknowledged that to collect from a non-paying partner, the IRS would need either a separate assessment or a court action. *United States v. Galletti*, 299 F.3d 913, 920 (9th Cir. 2002) (discussing *Young*). The refund context does not support the government’s collection theory.

**IV. The Bankruptcy Code Requires an Enforceable Claim**

Under 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1), a bankruptcy court shall disallow any claim that is “unenforceable against the debtor and property of the debtor” under applicable law. Because the IRS never assessed the individual partners and never obtained a judgment against them, its claims are unenforceable. The statute of limitations has run, and the bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the claims.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
