**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel, even if itself procedurally defaulted, may serve as “cause” to excuse the default of a separate, underlying claim. This conclusion contravenes settled principles of federal habeas law, undermines the finality of state court judgments, and creates a dangerous precedent that would allow federal courts to circumvent procedural rules with impunity.

The doctrine of procedural default is a cornerstone of federal habeas corpus jurisprudence. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, “a state prisoner must exhaust his state remedies before seeking federal habeas relief,” and “a federal court may not consider a claim that has been procedurally defaulted in state court unless the petitioner can demonstrate cause for the default and actual prejudice resulting from the alleged constitutional violation.” *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). The cause requirement is strict: “a procedural default will not be excused unless the petitioner shows that some external impediment prevented him from raising the claim in state court.” *Id.* at 752. The petitioner must demonstrate that the default was not the result of his own neglect or strategic decision.

Here, Robert Carpenter’s claim that his appellate counsel was ineffective in failing to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge on direct appeal was not raised in state post-conviction proceedings. The Ohio courts rejected his application to reopen his direct appeal as untimely, and the Ohio Supreme Court affirmed. That decision was final and binding under *Estelle v. McGuire*, 502 U.S. 626, 632 (1992), which holds that “a state court’s decision that a claim is procedurally barred is a final judgment on the merits for purposes of federal habeas review.” Thus, Carpenter’s sufficiency claim is procedurally defaulted.

The Court of Appeals held that the ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim could serve as “cause” to excuse the default, even though that claim itself was not properly raised in state court. This is a fundamental error. The cause must be “a circumstance external to the petitioner,” not a claim that itself depends on the very default the petitioner seeks to excuse. *Id.* at 752. To allow a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse the default of another claim is to permit a petitioner to use a procedural default as a tool to overcome another procedural default—a circular and self-justifying process that undermines the entire structure of procedural rules.

This Court has consistently rejected such “double-default” reasoning. In *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993), the Court held that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot be used to excuse a procedural default unless the claim itself is not procedurally defaulted.” The Court explained that “the cause must be something beyond the petitioner’s control,” and that “a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel is not a valid cause for a procedural default unless it is itself timely raised.” *Id.* at 637–38. The Court reiterated in *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977), that “a procedural default will not be excused unless the petitioner shows cause for the default and actual prejudice from the alleged constitutional violation.” The cause must be “something external to the petitioner,” not a claim that itself requires federal habeas review.

The Court of Appeals’ reasoning—that the ineffective-assistance claim “could excuse the default regardless of whether it was itself defaulted”—is legally untenable. It would allow a petitioner to bypass the exhaustion requirement and the procedural bar simply by asserting a claim that is itself barred. This would render the procedural default doctrine meaningless. As this Court stated in *Coleman*, “the procedural default doctrine is not a mere technicality; it is a necessary component of the federal habeas system.” 501 U.S. at 750. To permit such a loophole would invite abuse and undermine the finality of state court judgments.

Moreover, the Court of Appeals’ conclusion that prejudice was established is unsupported. The Court of Appeals found that Carpenter’s appellate counsel failed to raise a sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim, and concluded that this failure prejudiced Carpenter. But prejudice under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 694 (1984), requires a showing that “there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different.” Here, the Ohio courts had already rejected the sufficiency claim on the merits. The state court’s decision was not based on procedural grounds alone, but on the actual record. The evidence of Carpenter’s guilt was overwhelming: he pleaded guilty to aggravated murder and robbery, and the trial court imposed a life sentence with parole after 30 years. The sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was not meritorious, and thus no prejudice can be shown.

In sum, the Court of Appeals erred in allowing a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the default of another claim. The cause must be external, not self-generated. The doctrine of procedural default exists to preserve the integrity of the state court system and to prevent federal courts from becoming a “second appeal.” *See* *Wainwright*, 433 U.S. at 87. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.

**For the foregoing reasons, the petition for a writ of certiorari should be granted, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.**
