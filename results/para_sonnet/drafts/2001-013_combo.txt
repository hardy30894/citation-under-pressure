**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 4(b)(1) Exempts Working Conditions Only Where Another Federal Agency Has Actually Exercised Authority Over Those Conditions**

Section 4(b)(1) withdraws OSHA coverage only where "other Federal agencies... exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The statute asks not whether another agency *could* regulate, but whether it *has* regulated. This Court has long distinguished latent regulatory power from its exercise in resolving overlapping federal and state authority over transportation and vessels. In *Maurer v. Hamilton*, 309 U.S. 598 (1940), the Court sustained a state's truck-height regulation against a preemption challenge because the Interstate Commerce Commission, though empowered to regulate the same subject, had not yet done so; the mere existence of federal authority did not oust the state rule until the agency acted.

The same principle controls when the question is whether the Coast Guard's authority over vessels ousts OSHA. In *Kelly v. Washington*, 302 U.S. 1 (1937), shipowners argued that comprehensive federal navigation and vessel-inspection statutes precluded a state license requirement for hull inspectors. This Court disagreed, holding that Congress's general regulatory scheme for vessels did not preempt state regulation of a subject "which the federal government has not attempted to regulate." *Id.* at 15. Congress's broad grant of authority over vessel safety did not itself displace regulation of matters the responsible federal agency had left untouched.

*Huron Portland Cement Co. v. City of Detroit*, 362 U.S. 440 (1960), confirms the point in the precise context of vessel safety. There, a shipowner argued that the federal Boiler Inspection Act's comprehensive inspection scheme preempted a municipal smoke-abatement ordinance applied to its vessels. This Court rejected the argument, explaining that the federal scheme addressed the structural and mechanical safety of vessels, not the subject the local ordinance regulated, and that preemption could not be inferred merely from the breadth of the federal agency's overall jurisdiction. *Id.* at 446-48. The lesson of *Kelly* and *Huron* is the same one *Maurer* teaches for interstate carriers: a general grant of regulatory authority to an agency does not, by itself, extinguish another body's power to regulate a subject the first agency has not actually addressed.

That principle answers this case. The Coast Guard's investigatory and equipment-inspection authority over uninspected vessels, see 46 U.S.C. § 4102, is not the same as the "exercise" of authority over the occupational-safety conditions—drilling procedures, well-control practices, workplace hazard abatement—that caused the explosion aboard Rig 52. Because the Coast Guard has not promulgated standards addressing those conditions on uninspected vessels, Section 4(b)(1) does not withdraw OSHA's jurisdiction.

**II. The Occupational Safety and Health Act Is Remedial Legislation Whose Coverage Must Be Construed Broadly and Whose Exemptions Must Be Construed Narrowly**

Even apart from the "exercise" requirement, ordinary canons of construction confirm OSHA's jurisdiction. This Court has repeatedly held that exemptions from remedial worker-protection statutes must be construed narrowly against the party invoking them. In *Arnold v. Ben Kanowsky, Inc.*, 361 U.S. 388 (1960), the Court explained that "[a]ny exemption from such humanitarian and remedial legislation must therefore be narrowly construed, giving due regard to the plain meaning of statutory language and the intent of Congress," *id.* at 392, and that such exemptions are "limited to those [situations] plainly and unmistakably within their terms and spirit." The same rule applies with equal force to the OSH Act's exemption provision, and it forecloses reading Section 4(b)(1) to cover working conditions the Coast Guard has never regulated.

This Court has likewise resisted attempts to infer broad ousters of general federal labor and safety law over maritime workers absent a clear, specific, and actually operative competing federal scheme. In *Southern S.S. Co. v. NLRB*, 316 U.S. 31 (1942), the Court held that the National Labor Relations Act's general jurisdiction over "employees" could not require reinstatement of seamen discharged for mutiny, because a specific and long-established body of maritime law governed that precise conduct. But the Court's holding was narrow and conduct-specific—it did not hold that general labor statutes categorically yield to the Coast Guard's or any maritime authority's mere potential jurisdiction. Where, as here, no specific competing regulation exists, general remedial statutes protecting workers retain full force. See also *International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty*, 272 U.S. 50, 52 (1926) (statutes enacted "for the benefit and protection of seamen" are entitled to "liberal construction"); *Seas Shipping Co. v. Sieracki*, 328 U.S. 85, 94-95 (1946) (same).

Finally, the presumption against implied ouster of one federal scheme by another parallels this Court's federalism preemption jurisprudence. In *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947), the Court explained that courts must "start with the assumption that" historic regulatory authority is "not to be superseded... unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress." Congress's manifest purpose here was to fill regulatory gaps, not to create them. Reading Section 4(b)(1) to bar OSHA wherever the Coast Guard has *any* latent authority—rather than where it has actually regulated—would leave uninspected vessels like Rig 52 in a regulatory void that neither *Kelly*, *Huron*, *Maurer*, nor *Rice* countenances.

The Fifth Circuit's judgment should be reversed.
