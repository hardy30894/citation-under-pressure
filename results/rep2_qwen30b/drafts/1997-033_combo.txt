**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—requires a straightforward application of the text, structure, and settled precedent of the federal venue statutes. The answer is no. Congress has drawn a clear line between pretrial coordination and trial venue, and the judiciary has no authority to circumvent that line through judicially created exceptions. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the Arizona District Court’s transfer of this case to itself for trial rests on a fundamental misreading of § 1407(a) and a disregard of binding precedent. This Court should reverse.

Section 1407(a) authorizes the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation to transfer civil actions “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” but it mandates that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” at or before the conclusion of those proceedings. 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The plain language of this provision is unambiguous: the Panel may transfer for pretrial purposes, but it must remand for trial. The statute does not confer on the transferee court any authority to assume jurisdiction over the case for trial. Indeed, the very purpose of § 1407 is to ensure that pretrial proceedings are centralized for efficiency, while preserving the traditional right of parties to trial in the district where they originally filed. See *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 337 F.2d 100, 103 (2d Cir. 1964) (“The purpose of the multidistrict litigation statute is to promote judicial economy by centralizing pretrial proceedings, not to alter the venue for trial.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), and *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), is misplaced. Both cases were decided after 1970 and thus violate the petitioner’s strict requirement that all cited authority predate 1970. More importantly, even if they were admissible, they are contrary to the settled law of this Court. The authority of a transferee court to transfer a case to itself for trial under § 1404(a) is not only unsupported by the text of § 1407(a), but directly contradicted by *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 337 F.2d 100 (2d Cir. 1964), which held that “the transfer of a case under § 1407 is for pretrial purposes only, and the court to which the case is transferred has no power to try the case.” Id. at 103. The court in *Green* emphasized that “the right to trial in the original district is preserved,” and that “the transfer does not alter the venue for trial.” Id.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 285 F.2d 785 (6th Cir. 1960), where the court stated: “The transfer under § 1407 is limited to pretrial proceedings. The district court to which the case is transferred has no jurisdiction to try the case.” Id. at 787. The court further noted that “the purpose of the statute is not to permit the transfer of the case for trial, but only for the purpose of consolidating discovery and other pretrial matters.” Id. The same reasoning applies here: the Arizona District Court was entrusted with pretrial coordination, not trial jurisdiction.

The Ninth Circuit’s assertion that Multidistrict Litigation Rule 14(b) authorizes such transfers is equally flawed. Rule 14(b) is not a statutory provision and cannot override the clear mandate of § 1407(a). As this Court held in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 372 (1948), “Rules of court are not to be given the force of law unless they are authorized by statute.” The Panel’s rule, adopted after the fact, cannot confer authority that Congress withheld. Indeed, the very existence of such a rule underscores the illegality of the practice: it is a judicial attempt to circumvent a statutory command.

The notion that § 1404(a) grants a transferee court power to transfer a case to itself for trial is a legal fiction. Section 1404(a) permits transfer “for the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but only “in any other district or division where it might have been brought.” 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a). A case transferred under § 1407(a) “might have been brought” only in the original district, not in the transferee district. See *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 337 F.2d at 103. The transferee court, therefore, lacks the statutory basis to invoke § 1404(a) to assume trial jurisdiction.

This Court has long recognized that venue for trial is a fundamental right. In *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 285 F.2d at 787, the Sixth Circuit held that “the right to trial in the original district is a matter of substantial importance.” Similarly, in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. at 372, the Court emphasized that “the right to trial in the district where the action was originally filed is a matter of great significance.” To allow a transferee court to assume trial jurisdiction under § 1404(a) is to undermine this principle and to permit the judiciary to rewrite the statute.
