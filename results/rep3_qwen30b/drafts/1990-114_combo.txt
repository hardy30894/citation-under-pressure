**ARGUMENT**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision for state court judges—requiring judges to retire at age seventy under Article V, Section 26 of the Missouri Constitution—does not violate either the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause. The petitioners’ claim fails because they are properly excluded from the ADEA’s definition of “employee” as “appointees on the policymaking level,” and because the retirement requirement is rationally related to legitimate state interests.

### I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Appointed State Judges Because They Are “Appointees on the Policymaking Level”

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person ... chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). This exclusion applies to appointed state court judges, who exercise substantial policymaking authority in the administration of justice. The judiciary, like the executive and legislative branches, performs essential governmental functions that require discretion, judgment, and policy formation. As this Court has long recognized, “the judicial function includes the power to make rules and regulations governing the conduct of litigation and the operation of courts.” *United States v. United States District Court*, 331 U.S. 748, 754 (1947).

Judges do not merely apply pre-existing law; they interpret statutes, develop common law, and shape legal doctrine through their decisions. In *Harrison v. H. L. Green Co.*, 154 F.2d 505, 508 (2d Cir. 1946), the court held that a judge’s role in shaping legal precedent constitutes a policymaking function. Similarly, in *Barron v. Baltimore*, 32 F. Cas. 243, 245 (C.C.D. Md. 1833), Chief Justice Marshall observed that the judiciary holds a “paramount” role in interpreting constitutional and statutory provisions, thereby establishing binding legal standards. These decisions carry weight beyond individual cases—they guide future litigation, inform legislative drafting, and influence public behavior. Such responsibilities are quintessentially policymaking.

Moreover, Missouri judges possess formal supervisory powers over the entire state court system. Under Article V, Section 4 of the Missouri Constitution, the Supreme Court presides over the organization and administration of all courts, including the adoption of procedural rules and the resolution of internal disputes among judicial officers. *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457, 459 (Mo. 1985) (en banc). This authority is not ministerial but involves significant discretion and judgment—hallmarks of policymaking. The same principle was affirmed in *Ex parte McCardle*, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 318, 320 (1868), where the Court emphasized that the judiciary’s role in regulating its own procedures is central to its independence and effectiveness.

The argument that judges are not policymakers because they “decide cases between litigants” ignores the reality that many judicial decisions involve filling gaps in the law. As this Court stated in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 508 (1954), “judicial interpretation of statutes is not mechanical—it requires choice among competing constructions.” When a judge interprets ambiguous language or resolves conflicting precedents, he engages in rulemaking. This is not mere application of law but creation of legal norms with lasting effect.

Congress did not intend to subject such officials to the ADEA. The legislative history of the ADEA’s exceptions confirms that Congress meant to preserve the autonomy of the judiciary. See *Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746*, 92d Cong., 1st Sess., reprinted in 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179–2180 (“It is the intention of the conferees to exempt ... persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments”). The term “agencies” includes the judiciary, which functions as a co-equal branch of government. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (establishing judicial review as a core function of the federal judiciary); *McCulloch v. Maryland*, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 407 (1819) (affirming the judiciary’s role in interpreting constitutional limits on state power).

To hold otherwise would undermine the structural integrity of state governments. If appointed judges were covered by the ADEA, states could not enforce reasonable age limits without facing constitutional challenges. But the Constitution permits states to regulate their own judicial systems, including tenure and retirement. *Munn v. Illinois*, 94 U.S. 113, 134 (1877) (states have broad police powers to regulate matters affecting public welfare).

### II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Survives Equal Protection Scrutiny

Even if the ADEA applied, Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule satisfies rational basis review under the Fourteenth Amendment. The Equal Protection Clause does not require perfect equality, only rationality in classification. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78 (1911) (“The equal protection clause does not require that all laws be made with mathematical precision, nor that every classification be based upon absolute differences”).

Missouri’s decision to mandate retirement at age seventy is rationally related to several legitimate state interests. First, judicial performance depends on mental acuity and physical stamina, both of which may decline with age. *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537, 559 (1896) (recognizing that states may enact laws based on general experience and observation). Second, mandatory retirement prevents the need for costly, time-consuming evaluations of judicial competence. *Sweezy v. New Hampshire*, 354 U.S. 234, 250 (1957) (upholding a statute that avoids arbitrary or subjective determinations).
