## ARGUMENT

The district court erred in granting summary judgment of invalidity because it misconstrued the claim term “customer interface” and then incorrectly held that the original application failed to provide written description for that construction. A proper claim construction and written description analysis would have entitled PowerOasis to the February 1997 priority date, antedating the MobileStar prior art.

### I. The District Court’s Claim Construction Was Erroneous

The district court adopted PowerOasis’s own expansive construction that the “customer interface” may be located on a customer’s laptop. *See PowerOasis, Inc. v. T-Mobile USA, Inc.*, No. 05-cv-42-PB, 2007 WL 962937, at *3 (D.N.H. Mar. 30, 2007). But that construction is inconsistent with the intrinsic evidence and contravenes the principle that claims must be construed in light of the specification. *Phillips v. AWH Corp.*, 415 F.3d 1303, 1316 (Fed. Cir. 2005) (en banc). The original specification—the only relevant disclosure for priority purposes—describes the customer interface as part of the vending machine itself, not as a separate laptop interface. For example, the original application states that the vending machine includes “a customer interface for indicating the status of said vending machine.” The term “customer interface” in context refers to a display or indicator *on the machine*. The 2000 CIP added entirely new language about a “graphical user interface on the customer’s laptop,” which the district court improperly imported into its construction of the original claims. *See* *Vitronics Corp. v. Conceptronic, Inc.*, 90 F.3d 1576, 1582 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (claims must be read in view of the specification, not new matter).

Thus, the correct construction limits “customer interface” to a component located on the vending machine. Under that construction, the original application clearly provides written description: the specification describes a “display” or “indicator” on the machine that “indicates the status of said vending machine.” That disclosure is sufficient to satisfy § 112. *See Ariad Pharms., Inc. v. Eli Lilly & Co.*, 598 F.3d 1336, 1351 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (en banc) (written description requires that the patentee “convey with reasonable clarity to those skilled in the art that, as of the filing date, he or she was in possession of the invention”).

### II. The Original Application Provides Written Description for the Claimed Invention

Even under the district court’s construction, the original application still supports the claimed “customer interface” because the specification inherently describes a customer interface that could be located on a laptop. The original application discloses that the vending machine “enables a customer to connect a laptop to a telecommunications channel.” A person of ordinary skill would understand that the laptop itself would function as a user interface for selecting channels, viewing status, or inputting payment information. *In re Smith*, 481 F.3d 1347, 1350 (Fed. Cir. 2007) (written description can be inherent if “the missing descriptive matter is necessarily present”). The district court erred by requiring explicit, verbatim disclosure of a laptop-based interface, contrary to *Falkner v. Inglis*, 448 F.3d 1357, 1366 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (written description may be satisfied by “implicit disclosure” where the specification “contains information sufficient to inform a person skilled in the art that the applicant was in possession of the claimed invention”).

### III. The District Court Improperly Relied on New Matter to Deny Priority

The district court based its written description analysis entirely on language added in the 2000 CIP application. This was legal error. To determine whether a later-filed claim is entitled to priority under § 120, a court must compare the claimed invention to the *original* disclosure, not to a later-added specification. *Tronzo v. Biomet, Inc.*, 156 F.3d 1154, 1158 (Fed. Cir. 1998). The new matter in the 2000 CIP cannot serve as evidence that the original application lacked written description. The district court’s reliance on that new matter “improperly conflated the analysis of whether the later claims are supported by the original disclosure.” *Purdue Pharma L.P. v. Faulding Inc.*, 230 F.3d 1320, 1326 (Fed. Cir. 2000).

### IV. Conclusion

Because the district court erred in claim construction and in its written description analysis, the grant of summary judgment must be reversed. PowerOasis’s asserted claims are entitled to the February 1997 priority date, which antedates the MobileStar prior art. Accordingly, the asserted claims cannot be invalid under § 102(b).
