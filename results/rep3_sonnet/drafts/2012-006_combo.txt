# ARGUMENT

## I. The Presumption of Innocence Requires the Government to Prove Every Element of a Charged Offense, Including the Defendant's Membership in a Conspiracy During the Limitations Period, Beyond a Reasonable Doubt.

The presumption of innocence is not a mere procedural nicety; it is, as this Court declared in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432 (1895), "the undoubted law, axiomatic and elementary," and its enforcement "lies at the foundation of the administration of our criminal law." *Id.* at 453. That presumption does not evaporate simply because a defendant offers proof tending to negate an element of the charge. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court held that once evidence bearing on an element of the offense is introduced, the government may not obtain a conviction unless the jury is satisfied of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt notwithstanding that evidence; the burden of persuasion does not shift to the accused merely because he has come forward with proof. *Id.* at 484-88. This Court reaffirmed the principle in *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), holding that an element the government must establish—there, criminal intent—cannot be presumed against the defendant or removed from the government's burden by judicial shortcut. *Id.* at 274-76.

Membership in a conspiracy during the period covered by the statute of limitations is such an element. A conspiracy prosecution brought outside the limitations period is barred; consequently, the government must prove, as part of its case, that the charged conspiracy—and the defendant's participation in it—persisted into the limitations window. Nothing in *Coffin*, *Davis*, or *Morissette* permits that element to be proved by anything less than the same quantum of proof, and borne by the same party, as any other element of the crime.

## II. Withdrawal Terminates a Defendant's Membership in the Conspiratorial Partnership, and the Government Must Prove the Partnership—and the Defendant's Place in It—Continued Into the Limitations Period.

This Court has long described conspiracy as "a partnership in criminal purposes." *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910). A partnership, by its nature, can end. In *Kissel*, the Court recognized that a conspiracy is a continuing offense that persists "as long as the partnership in crime continues," *Pinkerton v. United States*, 328 U.S. 640, 646 (1946), but the corollary is equally true: once a conspirator's participation in that partnership ceases, he is no longer answerable, as a member, for what the remaining partners do thereafter. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369-70 (1912). *Hyde* confirms that continued liability turns on continued membership—not on the defendant's status at some earlier point in the conspiracy's life.

That principle has direct force in the limitations context. In *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391 (1957), this Court refused to allow the government to extend a conspiracy artificially past its natural termination merely by pointing to later acts of concealment that were not shown to further the conspiracy's central criminal objectives. *Id.* at 401-05. The Court insisted that the government must identify an act "done in furtherance of the main criminal objectives of the conspiracy" occurring within the limitations period; absent such proof, the conspiracy—and the defendant's exposure for it—had already run its course. *Id.* at 405. *Grunewald* thus places the burden squarely on the prosecution to prove that the conspiracy, as to the particular defendant, was a live and ongoing enterprise within the statutory period. This Court's caution in *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440 (1949), about permitting the "elastic, sprawling and pervasive" doctrines of conspiracy to expand a defendant's liability beyond what the evidence actually shows, *id.* at 445-46 (Jackson, J., concurring), counsels the same result: courts must not relax the government's evidentiary burden simply because the charge is conspiracy rather than a discrete substantive crime.

## III. Once a Defendant Produces Evidence of Withdrawal, the Government—Not the Defendant—Bears the Burden of Persuasion That Membership Continued Into the Limitations Period.

Lower courts applying these principles have recognized that a defendant's obligation with respect to withdrawal is one of production, not persuasion. In *United States v. Borelli*, 336 F.2d 376 (2d Cir. 1964), Judge Friendly explained that although a defendant claiming withdrawal must come forward with some evidence of an affirmative act inconsistent with the conspiracy's purpose, the ultimate question—whether the conspiracy, and the defendant's membership in it, extended into the period charged—remains one the government must establish to the jury's satisfaction beyond a reasonable doubt. That allocation follows ineluctably from *Coffin*, *Davis*, and *Morissette*: the presumption of innocence attaches to every element of the offense, and the element of continued membership is no exception merely because the defendant, rather than the government, first raised the issue by evidence of separation from his former confederates.

## Conclusion

Consistent with *Coffin*, *Davis*, *Morissette*, *Kissel*, *Hyde*, *Pinkerton*, *Grunewald*, *Krulewitch*, and *Borelli*, this Court should hold that once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the limitations period, the burden of persuasion remains on the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The judgment below should be reversed.
