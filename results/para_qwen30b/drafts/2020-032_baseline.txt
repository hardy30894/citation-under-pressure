**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy that seeks nominal damages is rendered moot when the unconstitutional policy is revised during litigation—is answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that the claims for declaratory and injunctive relief were rendered moot by Georgia Gwinnett College’s (GGC) voluntary, permanent revision of its Freedom of Expression Policy and Student Code of Conduct, and that the claim for nominal damages alone cannot revive a case otherwise devoid of a live controversy. This Court should affirm.

### I. A Voluntary, Permanent Change in the Challenged Policy Renders the Case Moot

The doctrine of mootness ensures that federal courts resolve actual controversies, not hypothetical or abstract disputes. *See* *United States v. Raines*, 362 U.S. 17, 21 (1960). When a party voluntarily abandons the challenged conduct, “the case is no longer ‘live’” and the court lacks jurisdiction to decide it. *See* *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1255 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), cert. denied, 138 S. Ct. 2655 (2018). Here, GGC unambiguously eliminated the very policies at issue: it removed all permit requirements for expressive activity on campus, abolished the two designated speech zones, and deleted the provision authorizing disciplinary action for “disorderly conduct” related to speech. These changes were not temporary or conditional; they were permanent and fully implemented as of February 28, 2017, and remain in effect today.

This Court has long held that “a defendant’s voluntary cessation of allegedly illegal conduct does not automatically moot a case.” *See* *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982). But where the change is “clear, unequivocal, and permanent,” and there is “no reasonable expectation that the wrong will recur,” the case becomes moot. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255–56. The district court applied this standard correctly, finding that GGC had terminated the Prior Policies and that there was “no reasonable basis to expect that it will return to them.” That conclusion is supported by the record: GGC did not merely suspend enforcement—it replaced the entire framework with one that affords students greater freedom of expression than the First Amendment requires. As such, any future harm from the Prior Policies is speculative and insufficient to sustain standing.

Appellants argue that their desire to speak again on campus creates a live controversy. But *Uzuegbunam* and *Bradford* have not attempted to engage in expressive activity since the incidents in 2016, and neither has expressed an intent to do so under the current policy. *See* *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 570 U.S. 693, 705 (2013) (“[A]n injury that is purely speculative or conjectural does not satisfy Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement.”). Their mere assertion of a future desire to speak, without more, is not enough. *See* *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560 (1992) (“[T]he plaintiff must show that he personally suffered some actual or threatened injury as a result of the putatively illegal conduct.”).

### II. Nominal Damages Do Not Save a Moot Claim

Even if Appellants had alleged a concrete injury, the fact remains that they seek only nominal damages. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (“A request for nominal damages alone does not save a case from being moot.”). Nominal damages are awarded “not to compensate for loss but to recognize the violation of a legal right.” *See* *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Fed. Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388, 397 (1971). They are not a substitute for compensatory damages, which require proof of actual injury. *See* *Slicker v. Jackson*, 215 F.3d 1225, 1230 (11th Cir. 2000).

Here, the First Amended Complaint contains no allegations of financial loss, emotional distress, physical harm, or other tangible injury. Instead, Appellants rely solely on the “abstract value” of their constitutional rights. *Id.* at 1231. They allege only that they “suffered, and continue to suffer, irreparable harm,” but never specify what that harm is. Such conclusory allegations are insufficient to support a claim for compensatory damages—and thus cannot transform a nominal damages claim into a live controversy. *See* *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1256 (“The mere existence of a constitutional violation, without accompanying actual injury, does not create a justiciable dispute.”)

Appellants attempt to distinguish *Flanigan’s* on the ground that their challenge was “as-applied,” not facial. But this distinction is irrelevant. *Flanigan’s* explicitly recognized that even as-applied challenges may be mooted by a voluntary policy change. *Id.* at 1256 n.10. And the Court has repeatedly held that “the mere possibility of future violations” is not enough to prevent mootness. *See* *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner*, 387 U.S. 136, 149 (1967); *DaimlerChrysler Corp. v. Cuno*, 547 U.S. 332, 342 (2006). The absence of a present threat of harm renders the case nonjusticiable.
