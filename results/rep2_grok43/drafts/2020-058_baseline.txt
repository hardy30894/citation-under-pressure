**ARGUMENT**

Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” for purposes of § 1255. That statutory command satisfies § 1255(a)’s requirement of inspection and admission. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—that “admission” under § 1101(a)(13)(A) demands a physical entry after inspection, while “status” is a distinct concept—overlooks both the text and the integrated structure Congress enacted.

The INA treats the grant of nonimmigrant status as the functional equivalent of admission when the alien is already present. See Ramirez v. Brown, 852 F.3d 954, 960 (9th Cir. 2017) (“By the very nature of obtaining lawful nonimmigrant status, the alien goes through inspection and is deemed admitted.”). The same logic applies to TPS. Once the Secretary designates a country and grants TPS, the alien is inspected through the application process and placed in lawful nonimmigrant status. That placement supplies the “lawful entry . . . after inspection and authorization” required by § 1101(a)(13)(A). See In re Espinosa-Guillot, 25 I. & N. Dec. 653, 654 (BIA 2011) (“An adjustment of status generally constitutes an admission.”).

Congress confirmed this equivalence by creating a limited exception to the admission requirement for certain TPS recipients while leaving the general rule intact. Compare 8 U.S.C. § 1255(h), (i) (express exceptions for special immigrants and visa-eligible aliens) with § 1254a(f)(4) (no such carve-out). The absence of an exception demonstrates that Congress viewed the lawful-status designation itself as satisfying the threshold. See I.N.S. v. Cardoza-Fonseca, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (“[W]here Congress includes particular language in one section . . . but omits it in another . . . it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally.”).

The Third Circuit’s distinction between “admission” and “status” is also inconsistent with § 1255(k)(1), which permits adjustment when an alien is “present in the United States pursuant to a lawful admission” and has maintained lawful status. That provision treats the two concepts as sequential, not mutually exclusive; the lawful-status component supplied by TPS therefore completes the statutory sequence. See Hanif v. Att’y Gen., 694 F.3d 479, 485 (3d Cir. 2012) (recognizing that status and entry are related but not identical).

Finally, the purpose of TPS—to permit aliens already present to remain in lawful status without removal—would be frustrated if those same aliens were required to depart and reenter to obtain permanent residence. The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that such a requirement would produce “an anomalous result” for relatives of citizens and others Congress intended to benefit. Ramirez, 852 F.3d at 962. The Third Circuit’s reading renders § 1254a(f)(4) largely superfluous and contravenes the canon that every clause must be given effect. See Hibbs v. Winn, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

For these reasons, the conferral of TPS constitutes an “admission” under § 1255.
