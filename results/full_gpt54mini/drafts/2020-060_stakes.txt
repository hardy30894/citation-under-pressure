**Argument**

CERCLA gives Guam one avenue to recover from the United States for the Ordot Dump costs it incurred: contribution under § 113(f), not cost recovery under § 107(a). In **Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc., 543 U.S. 157, 161, 167 (2004)**, this Court explained that § 113 added “two express avenues for contribution” and that Congress filled the original statute’s gap by creating a contribution remedy. The Court later held that when a party has been sued or has settled its liability, it proceeds under § 113 rather than § 107. See **United States v. Atlantic Research Corp., 551 U.S. 128, 139-40 (2007)**. Here, Guam entered a judicially approved consent decree resolving the EPA action about the Ordot Dump, so its claim falls within § 113(f)(3)(B).

Section 113(f)(3)(B) applies when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State for some or all of a response action or for some or all of the costs of such action in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” **42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B)**. The 2004 consent decree did exactly that. It required Guam to close the dump, design and install a cover system, and pay a civil penalty. Those obligations were not mere housekeeping; they were the relief EPA sought to address leachate and contamination from the dump. A settlement need not use talismanic words to “resolve” liability. Courts of appeals have repeatedly held that substance controls over form. See **ASARCO LLC v. Goodwin, 756 F.3d 191, 206-08 (2d Cir. 2014)**; **Niagara Mohawk Power Corp. v. Chevron U.S.A., Inc., 596 F.3d 112, 128-29 (2d Cir. 2010)**; **Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp., 810 F.3d 996, 1002-03 (6th Cir. 2015)**; **Refined Metals Corp. v. NL Industries Inc., 937 F.3d 928, 931-33 (7th Cir. 2019)**.

Guam’s argument that the decree did not “resolve” liability because it included no admission of fault fails for the same reason. A party may settle without conceding liability while still resolving it. As the Seventh Circuit explained, “parties often expressly refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability.” **Refined Metals Corp., 937 F.3d at 931**. Likewise, the Sixth Circuit has held that a disclaimer of liability does not prevent a settlement from triggering § 113(f)(3)(B). **Florida Power Corp., 810 F.3d at 1002**. The consent decree here imposed concrete cleanup obligations and therefore resolved Guam’s liability for at least some of the response action.

Once § 113(f)(3)(B) was triggered, Guam’s § 107(a) claim was unavailable. In **Atlantic Research, 551 U.S. at 139**, this Court explained that §§ 107 and 113 “provide two potentially overlapping remedies” but that the contribution remedy governs where a party seeks to recover expenses after it has become subject to liability. This Court has also cautioned that a PRP may not use § 107 to “circumvent” the limits Congress imposed on contribution actions. **Cooper Industries, 543 U.S. at 169**. Allowing Guam to proceed under § 107 would nullify that structure by converting a time-barred contribution claim into a six-year cost-recovery claim.

That result would also undermine CERCLA’s statute of limitations. Congress chose a three-year limitations period for contribution actions, running from the entry of a qualifying settlement. **42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(3)(B)**. Guam signed the 2004 decree, but it did not sue until 2017, long after the three-year period expired. Courts do not extend CERCLA’s deadlines by recharacterizing the claim. See **ASARCO, 756 F.3d at 205-06**; **Refined Metals Corp., 937 F.3d at 933**.

Finally, Guam’s attempt to recast the Clean Water Act settlement as unrelated to CERCLA response costs fails. CERCLA defines “remedial action” broadly to include “the collection of leachate and runoff.” **42 U.S.C. § 9601(24)**. EPA’s complaint sought a cover system and closure to stop pollutant discharges from the dump, relief squarely within CERCLA’s response framework. A settlement addressing those cleanup obligations is a settlement for response action within § 113(f)(3)(B).

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
