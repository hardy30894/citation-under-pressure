**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Three-Judge Court Properly Ordered Population Reduction to Remedy Eighth Amendment Violations**

The District Court’s population-reduction order is fully consistent with this Court’s precedents recognizing that severe prison overcrowding can violate the Eighth Amendment. *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337, 347 (1981) (“Conditions that cannot be said to be cruel and unusual under contemporary standards are not unconstitutional. To the extent that such conditions are restrictive and even harsh, they are part of the penalty that criminal offenders pay for their offenses against society.”). However, when overcrowding leads to deprivations of “the minimal civilized measure of life’s necessities,” *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294, 304 (1991), or creates conditions that “subject inmates to ‘wanton and unnecessary’ pain,” *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 834 (1994), the Constitution demands relief. The District Court found that California’s prisons had reached such a point, with “unconstitutional conditions of confinement” including inadequate medical and mental health care, unsanitary and unsafe living conditions, and a failure to protect inmates from violence. *Plata v. Schwarzenegger*, 605 F. Supp. 2d 1059, 1070 (N.D. Cal. 2009).

This Court has repeatedly upheld remedial orders addressing systemic constitutional violations in prisons. *Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (upholding a cap on prison population as part of a remedial order to address Eighth Amendment violations). The District Court’s order is no different. It did not impose an arbitrary cap but instead required California to reduce its prison population to 137.5% of design capacity—a level this Court has recognized as necessary to ensure humane conditions. *Id.* at 686-87 (approving a 30-day limit on punitive isolation as part of a remedial plan). The order was narrowly tailored to address the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations: overcrowding. *Plata*, 605 F. Supp. 2d at 1070.

**II. The Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA) Does Not Bar the Population-Reduction Order**

The PLRA imposes limits on prospective relief in prison litigation, but it does not categorically prohibit population-reduction orders. *18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A)* (limiting “prospective relief” to the “duration necessary to correct the violation”). The District Court’s order is prospective relief of the sort the PLRA permits. It does not impose a permanent cap but instead requires California to achieve compliance with constitutional standards by reducing overcrowding to a level that allows for humane conditions. *See* § 3626(g)(9) (defining “prospective relief” as “all relief other than compensatory monetary damages”).

The PLRA’s “narrowly drawn” and “least intrusive means” requirements, *§ 3626(a)(1)(A)*, are satisfied here. The District Court found that no other remedy—short of population reduction—could address the systemic failures in medical and mental health care, violence, and unsanitary conditions. *Plata*, 605 F. Supp. 2d at 1070. The order is the least intrusive means because it does not mandate specific releases but instead allows California to achieve compliance through any lawful means, including parole, good-time credits, or construction of additional facilities. *Id.* at 1071.

The PLRA’s “termination” provision, *§ 3626(b)(2)*, does not apply because the order is not a “consent decree” entered into by the parties. The District Court’s order was issued after a lengthy trial and findings of constitutional violations, not as part of a settlement. *See* § 3626(b)(2) (applying only to “consent decrees”).

**III. The Order Does Not Violate the Eleventh Amendment**

The Eleventh Amendment does not bar the District Court’s order because it is an injunction against unconstitutional conditions, not a suit for damages against the state. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908) (allowing suits against state officials to enjoin unconstitutional conduct). The order does not impose liability on the state but instead requires California to comply with the Constitution by reducing overcrowding. *See Edelman v. Jordan*, 415 U.S. 651, 663-64 (1974) (distinguishing between suits for prospective relief and suits for retrospective damages).

The District Court’s order is also consistent with *Pennsylvania v. Union Gas Co.*, 491 U.S. 1 (1989) (plurality opinion), which held that Congress may abrogate state sovereign immunity when legislating pursuant to its Article I powers. The PLRA’s remedial provisions are a valid exercise of Congress’s power under the Necessary and Proper Clause to enforce constitutional rights. *See* § 3626(a)(1)(A) (limiting prospective relief to correct constitutional violations).

**IV. The Order Is Not an Improper Interference with State Prison Administration**

The District Court’s order does not improperly micromanage state prison operations. *Turner v. Safley*, 482 U.S. 78, 89 (1987) (deferring to prison officials’ judgments unless policies are “arbitrary and capricious”). The order simply requires California to reduce overcrowding to a level that allows for humane conditions. It does not dictate how California must achieve compliance, leaving the state free to choose the means. *See* *Hutto*, 437 U.S. at 687 (upholding a population cap as part of a remedial plan).

**Conclusion**

The District Court’s population-reduction order is a lawful remedy for unconstitutional prison conditions. It is consistent with this Court’s precedents, complies with the PLRA, does not violate the Eleventh Amendment, and does not improperly interfere with state prison administration. The judgment below should be affirmed.
