**ARGUMENT**

Both questions presented turn on the same historical understanding of executive clemency: it is an act of grace administered outside the judicial process, not a further stage of adjudication to which the safeguards of trial attach. Applying that understanding, Woodard can show neither a protected liberty interest in the clemency process itself nor any compulsion violating his Fifth Amendment privilege.

**I. Woodard Has No Life or Liberty Interest in Clemency Proceedings Protected by the Due Process Clause.**

The Due Process Clause protects only interests created by the Constitution or by positive law. Executive clemency is neither. Since the founding, this Court has treated clemency as an act of sovereign grace lying outside the adjudicatory process that due process governs. In *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), Chief Justice Marshall described a pardon as "an act of grace, proceeding from the power entrusted with the execution of the laws, which exempts the individual on whom it is bestowed from the punishment the law inflicts for a crime he has committed." *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380 (1866), confirmed that this power "is unlimited" and may be exercised "at any time after [the offense's] commission, either before legal proceedings are taken, or during their pendency, or after conviction and judgment"—language that places clemency conceptually apart from the sequence of trial and appeal.

*Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486 (1927), reaffirmed this understanding. Justice Holmes explained that a pardon "is not a private act of grace from an individual happening to possess power" but "a part of the Constitutional scheme," reflecting "the determination of the ultimate authority that the public welfare will be better served" by mercy than by the sentence as imposed. Nothing in that description treats clemency as an extension of adjudication demanding the procedural protections that attend trial or direct appeal.

The court below erred in borrowing due process principles from the appellate context, because even direct appeal—far closer to the adjudicatory core than clemency—is not a matter of constitutional entitlement. *McKane v. Durston*, 153 U.S. 684, 687 (1894), held that "[a]n appeal from a judgment of conviction is not a matter of absolute right, independently of constitutional or statutory provisions allowing such appeal," and that a State "may . . . make it dependent upon conditions." If a State may condition or withhold appellate review itself without offending due process, a State may likewise structure executive clemency—an act of grace lying still further from trial—without triggering comparable scrutiny. *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), addressed only whether a State that voluntarily creates a right of appeal may condition it on ability to pay; it has no bearing on clemency, which creates no comparable statutory entitlement in the first instance.

Due process protections attach only where government action threatens an interest to which the individual is already entitled. *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895 (1961) (the process due depends on "the precise nature of the government function involved as well as . . . the private interest that has been affected by governmental action"). A capital prisoner's hope for clemency is not such an entitlement; it depends wholly on executive grace, as *Wilson*, *Garland*, and *Biddle* establish. Because clemency does not redetermine guilt or reopen a lawfully imposed sentence, but merely asks whether mercy should mitigate a judgment due process has already validated, no protected liberty interest arises from the clemency process itself. The court of appeals therefore erred in extending due process scrutiny—however "minimal" or "barely perceptible"—to a proceeding this Court has consistently placed outside the adjudicatory system.

**II. Because Participation in the Clemency Interview Is Voluntary, It Does Not Compel Self-Incrimination in Violation of the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment forbids only *compelled* self-incrimination. *Malloy v. Hogan*, 378 U.S. 1, 8 (1964), held that the privilege "is fulfilled only when the person is guaranteed the right 'to remain silent unless he chooses to speak in the unfettered exercise of his own will, and to suffer no penalty . . . for such silence.'" Ohio's regulation satisfies precisely that standard. A death-sentenced inmate may request a personal interview or decline one; nothing compels him to answer any particular question, and no penalty attaches to silence at any stage.

Where disclosure is voluntary, no Fifth Amendment violation occurs. *Rogers v. United States*, 340 U.S. 367, 371 (1951) (the privilege protects against compulsion, not voluntary disclosure); *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951) (the privilege applies only where compelled answers would themselves support conviction or "furnish a link in the chain of evidence"). The privilege belongs to the witness, who must invoke it as to particular questions rather than refuse to appear altogether. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 594–97 (1896); *United States v. Sullivan*, 274 U.S. 259, 263–64 (1927). Ohio's procedure preserves exactly that choice: an inmate who requests an interview may answer as much or as little as he wishes, and may assert the privilege as to any question without consequence.

Nor may Woodard manufacture a constitutional violation from speculation about hypothetical future prosecutions. This Court has cautioned against extending the privilege to remote, contingent harms. *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 430–31 (1956) (the privilege does not shield against speculative future risk absent a realistic threat of prosecution). Woodard's own complaint disclaims any effect on his existing convictions or sentence, and no adverse consequence follows from declining the interview or limiting his answers. Any risk of self-incrimination is thus wholly theoretical—precisely the sort of abstract contingency the privilege does not reach.

Because the clemency interview is optional and carries no penalty for silence, Ohio's regulation does not compel Woodard to be a witness against himself. The judgment below should be reversed.
