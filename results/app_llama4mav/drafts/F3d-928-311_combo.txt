## Argument

The district court erred in designating Furlow as an "armed career criminal" under the ACCA and a "career offender" under the Sentencing Guidelines. Furlow's prior convictions for distribution of crack cocaine in South Carolina and first-degree arson in Georgia do not qualify as predicate offenses under the ACCA and the Guidelines.

### I. Furlow's Conviction for Distribution of Crack Cocaine is Not a Predicate "Serious Drug Offense" or "Controlled Substance Offense"

The ACCA defines a "serious drug offense" as an offense under state law involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute a controlled substance, for which a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The South Carolina statute under which Furlow was convicted in 2016 does not necessarily prescribe a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co., 255 U.S. 81 (1921), a statute that does not clearly define the offense is void for vagueness. Similarly, in International Harvester Co. v. Kentucky, 234 U.S. 216 (1914), the Court struck down a statute that was overly broad and failed to provide clear guidance. Here, the South Carolina statute's maximum penalty is not clearly defined, rendering it uncertain whether Furlow's conviction qualifies as a "serious drug offense." See also Cline v. Frink Dairy Co., 274 U.S. 445 (1927) (striking down a statute that was overly broad and vague).

Furthermore, the Guidelines define a "controlled substance offense" as an offense under federal or state law punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year. See USSG § 4B1.2(b). As the Court held in Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451 (1939), a statute that is vague and uncertain is void. The South Carolina statute's penalty provisions are similarly unclear, making it uncertain whether Furlow's conviction qualifies as a "controlled substance offense." See also Connally v. General Construction Co., 269 U.S. 385 (1926) (striking down a statute that was vague and uncertain).

### II. Furlow's Conviction for First-Degree Arson is Not a Predicate "Violent Felony" or "Crime of Violence"

The ACCA and the Guidelines enumerate "arson" as a qualifying predicate offense. See 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). However, the Georgia statute under which Furlow was convicted in 2008 is broader than the generic definition of arson. As the Court held in United States v. Reese, 92 U.S. 214 (1876), a statute that is overly broad is not limited to its plain meaning. Similarly, in United States v. Brewer, 139 U.S. 108 (1891), the Court held that a statute that is not limited to its plain meaning is void for vagueness. Here, the Georgia statute's definition of first-degree arson is not limited to its plain meaning, rendering it uncertain whether Furlow's conviction qualifies as a "violent felony" or "crime of violence." See also Baldwin v. Franks, 120 U.S. 678 (1887) (striking down a statute that was overly broad).

Moreover, as the Court held in Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), a statute that is vague and uncertain is void. The Georgia statute's definition of first-degree arson is similarly unclear, making it uncertain whether Furlow's conviction qualifies as a predicate offense. See also Herndon v. Lowry, 301 U.S. 242 (1937) (striking down a statute that was vague and uncertain).

The district court's designation of Furlow as an "armed career criminal" and a "career offender" was therefore erroneous. Furlow's prior convictions do not qualify as predicate offenses under the ACCA and the Guidelines. We urge the Court to vacate Furlow's sentence and remand for resentencing.
