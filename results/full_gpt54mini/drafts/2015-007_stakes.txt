Florida’s capital sentencing scheme cannot survive Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), because it permits a judge, not a jury, to make the factual findings necessary to impose death. Ring held that “the Sixth Amendment requires more than a judge’s finding of the existence of an aggravating circumstance” when that finding exposes the defendant to a greater punishment. 536 U.S. at 609. That rule applies here. Under Florida law as applied to Hurst, the jury returned only a nonbinding recommendation, by a seven-to-five vote, and the trial judge alone made the critical findings that the murder was especially heinous, atrocious, or cruel and committed during a robbery. As the Florida Supreme Court acknowledged, those findings were the basis for the death sentence. Hurst v. State, 147 So. 3d 1040, 1047-48 (Fla. 2014), rev’d, 136 S. Ct. 616 (2016). Ring makes clear that such facts, because they increase punishment, must be found by a jury. 536 U.S. at 609; Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 U.S. 466, 490 (2000).

The Florida scheme also fails because it allows a defendant to be condemned to death without a unanimous jury determination. The Sixth Amendment jury trial guarantee does not permit the State to treat a bare, divided advisory vote as the functional equivalent of a jury verdict on the facts that authorize death. The Court in Ring emphasized that the jury, not the judge, must find the aggravating circumstance necessary for death eligibility. 536 U.S. at 589, 609. Florida’s procedure strips the jury of that constitutionally required role by making its recommendation nonbinding and allowing the judge to impose death even when the jury’s vote is 7-5. Hurst, 147 So. 3d at 1048. That is incompatible with Ring’s command that the jury, not the court, make the finding necessary to expose the defendant to capital punishment. 536 U.S. at 609.

Nor does the Eighth Amendment save Florida’s scheme. The Court has repeatedly recognized that “death is different” and that capital sentencing requires heightened reliability. Woodson v. North Carolina, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976); Gardner v. Florida, 430 U.S. 349, 357-58 (1977); Sumner v. Shuman, 483 U.S. 66, 72 (1987). A procedure that permits death without a unanimous jury determination of the facts authorizing that sentence falls short of that heightened reliability. Florida’s nonunanimous recommendation procedure is especially troubling because it is an outlier among death-penalty jurisdictions. The Florida Supreme Court itself recognized that Florida stood apart in not requiring unanimity for the aggravating facts necessary for death. Hurst, 147 So. 3d at 1081-82 (Pariente, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). The Eighth Amendment’s demand for reliability is not satisfied by a scheme that places such extraordinary weight on a nonunanimous, nonbinding vote. See Caldwell v. Mississippi, 472 U.S. 320, 329-30 (1985); Beck v. Alabama, 447 U.S. 625, 638 (1980).

The State cannot avoid this result by pointing to Florida’s treatment of mental retardation. Atkins v. Virginia, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), held that the Eighth Amendment bars execution of the mentally retarded, but it left to the States “the task of developing appropriate ways to enforce the constitutional restriction.” 536 U.S. at 317. That issue is distinct from the Ring question. The constitutional defect here is not merely the allocation of the mental-retardation determination, but the broader structure by which Florida permits a judge to make the factual findings necessary for death and permits death without a unanimous jury verdict. Ring and Apprendi govern those facts. 536 U.S. at 609; 530 U.S. at 490.

Accordingly, the judgment below should be reversed. The Constitution requires that any fact necessary to impose death be found by a jury, and Florida’s sentencing scheme—allowing a judge to find those facts and to impose death on a nonunanimous advisory recommendation—violates both the Sixth Amendment and the Eighth Amendment. Ring, 536 U.S. at 609; Apprendi, 530 U.S. at 490; Hurst v. State, 147 So. 3d at 1047-48.
