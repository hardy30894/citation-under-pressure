# ARGUMENT

The three-judge court's population order rests on a foundation of settled equitable authority, established remedial precedent, and a factual record showing an entrenched constitutional violation that lesser measures failed to cure. The order easily satisfies the Prison Litigation Reform Act's requirement that relief be narrowly drawn and represent the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation, because that requirement simply codifies principles this Court has long applied to structural remedies.

## I. Federal Courts Possess Broad Equitable Authority to Remedy Systemic Constitutional Violations Through Structural Relief.

A court sitting in equity has traditionally possessed expansive authority to fashion whatever relief the violation before it demands. As this Court explained in *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), "the essence of equity jurisdiction has been the power of the Chancellor to do equity and to mould each decree to the necessities of the particular case." *Id.* at 329. That flexible authority extends to remedies that reach beyond individual relief when the violation itself is systemic in origin and scope. *See In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 593 (1895) (recognizing the broad power of a federal court to enter relief adequate to secure compliance with federal law). The population order is a straightforward application of this principle: having found, after years of litigation and extensive fact-finding, that overcrowding was the "primary cause" of continuing Eighth Amendment violations, the three-judge court was entitled to order the remedy the record showed would actually work.

Nor does the PLRA's narrow-tailoring requirement displace this framework. Courts have always been obliged to calibrate relief to the scope of the violation, but calibration has never meant powerlessness. In *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106 (1932), this Court confirmed that a court retains continuing authority to supervise and adjust its decree "in adaptation to changed conditions." *Id.* at 114. That is precisely what occurred below: the court considered less intrusive alternatives, made detailed findings on why they had failed, and retained jurisdiction to modify the order as circumstances evolved.

## II. This Court's Structural-Remedy Precedents Confirm That District Courts May Order Specific, Quantifiable Relief to Eliminate Entrenched Constitutional Violations.

The State's assertion that a numerical population cap is inherently too intrusive cannot be reconciled with this Court's repeated approval of specific, quantifiable remedial orders where state officials failed for years to cure a proven constitutional violation. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294 (1955) ("*Brown II*"), this Court directed the district courts to enter "such orders and decrees consistent with this opinion as are necessary and proper to admit to public schools on a racially nondiscriminatory basis," *id.* at 301, thereby entrusting district courts with broad discretion to design remedies commensurate with the violation, notwithstanding the significant reordering of state institutions such remedies required.

That discretion is not merely nominal. In *Griffin v. County School Board of Prince Edward County*, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), this Court sustained an order compelling a local government to levy taxes and reopen public schools, explaining that "the time for mere 'deliberate speed' has run out," *id.* at 234, and that courts possess the power to require officials to exercise the authority necessary to eliminate the violation. In *Green v. County School Board of New Kent County*, 391 U.S. 430 (1968), this Court reaffirmed that once a constitutional violation is established, officials and courts alike bear an "affirmative duty to take whatever steps might be necessary" to eliminate it "root and branch." *Id.* at 437-38 (quoting *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145, 154 (1965)). And in *Louisiana v. United States*, 380 U.S. 145 (1965), this Court approved a remedy restructuring an entire state voter-registration system, holding that a court confronting a violation of that magnitude "has not merely the power but the duty to render a decree which will so far as possible eliminate the discriminatory effects of the past." *Id.* at 154.

These decisions establish a clear rule: when a coordinate branch of state government has, over years, failed to remedy an established constitutional violation, a federal court does not exceed its authority by ordering specific, quantifiable relief—whether framed as pupil ratios, tax levies, or, as here, a population ceiling tied to design capacity. The order below is not meaningfully distinguishable from the remedial decrees this Court has consistently sustained.

## III. The Order Is a Proportionate Response to an Ongoing Violation of the Eighth Amendment.

The gravity of the underlying violation confirms the propriety of the remedy chosen. The Eighth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962), draws its meaning from "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society." *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). This Court has long held that punishment inflicting needless suffering offends the Constitution regardless of its formal legality. *See Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 366-67 (1910). A prison system so overcrowded that inmates are systematically denied adequate medical and mental health care—as the district court found after extensive evidentiary hearings—inflicts exactly the kind of needless suffering the Eighth Amendment forbids. And where, as in *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 4 (1958), state officials' own conduct perpetuated the violation over many years despite repeated remedial opportunities, the obligation to ensure compliance falls squarely on the courts. The State cannot now claim that a remedy carefully tailored to the violation it allowed to persist exceeds the PLRA's bounds.

## CONCLUSION

The population order is narrowly drawn, represents the least intrusive means of correcting a violation the State itself permitted to fester for years, and is fully consistent with this Court's equitable and Eighth Amendment precedents. The judgment below should be affirmed.
