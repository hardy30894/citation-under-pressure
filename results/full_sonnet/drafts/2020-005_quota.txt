# ARGUMENT

## I. The Due Process Clause Permits Jurisdiction Where Claims "Relate To" a Defendant's Forum Contacts, Without Requiring Strict Causation

The touchstone of specific jurisdiction has always been whether a defendant has "certain minimum contacts with [the forum] such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945) (internal quotation marks omitted). This Court has consistently framed the jurisdictional inquiry disjunctively: a suit may proceed where the claim "arise[s] out of or relate[s] to the defendant's contacts with the forum." *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court of California*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017) (quoting *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117, 127 (2014)). The word "or" is not surplusage. It reflects that jurisdiction may rest on a relationship between the forum, the defendant, and the litigation even when the defendant's specific forum conduct is not the mechanical, but-for cause of the plaintiff's injury. *See Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014) (jurisdiction depends on "the relationship among the defendant, the forum, and the litigation").

Ford's proposed causation requirement would collapse "relate to" into "arise out of," reading the first phrase out of this Court's settled formulation. Nothing in *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462 (1985), supports that result. There, the Court asked only whether the defendant "purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State," id. at 475, and whether the resulting litigation was one that the defendant should "reasonably anticipate being haled into court" there, *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980). A manufacturer that cultivates a market in a State, and whose product of the same type and design causes injury there, has anticipated exactly this kind of suit.

## II. Ford's Extensive, Purposeful Contacts With Montana Relate Directly to Gullett's Claims

Ford is not a stranger to Montana. It has systematically and continuously "avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities" there, *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 475, by advertising, marketing, distributing, and selling Explorers—the very model at issue—through Montana dealerships, and by facilitating the ongoing sale, maintenance, and repair of those vehicles through Ford-authorized dealers and parts networks within the State. These are not "random, isolated, or fortuitous" contacts, *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984), but a deliberate, sustained course of business aimed at Montana consumers, of the sort this Court has treated as satisfying purposeful availment. *See World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297-98 (jurisdiction proper where a corporation "delivers its products into the stream of commerce with the expectation that they will be purchased by consumers in the forum State").

This case is far removed from *Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408 (1984), where the defendant's contacts with Texas—a single purchasing trip and related negotiations—had no relationship whatsoever to the type of product or activity that caused the plaintiffs' injuries in Peru. Nor is it comparable to *Bristol-Myers*, where the nonresident plaintiffs' claims concerned a drug that was neither developed, manufactured, labeled, nor sold to them in California. 137 S. Ct. at 1781-82. Here, by contrast, the product line that injured a Montana resident, on a Montana highway, is the same product line Ford marketed and sold to Montana residents for years. The relationship Due Process demands between the forum, the defendant's conduct, and the litigation, *Walden*, 571 U.S. at 284, is squarely present.

Ford's argument that jurisdiction fails because this particular vehicle was first sold in Washington misapprehends the constitutional inquiry. Specific jurisdiction does not require that the defendant's forum contacts produce the individual chattel that injured the plaintiff; it requires only that the litigation "relate to" activity the defendant purposefully directed at the forum. *Bristol-Myers*, 137 S. Ct. at 1780. Ford's decision to serve the Montana market for Explorers—accepting the accompanying benefits of that market, including resale, service revenue, and goodwill—cannot be disaggregated, for jurisdictional purposes, from the risk that one of those Explorers would cause injury on a Montana road. *Cf. Goodyear Dunlop Tires Operations, S.A. v. Brown*, 564 U.S. 915, 927 (2011) (distinguishing "the regular flow or regular course of sales" in a forum, which can support specific jurisdiction, from the kind of episodic contact that cannot support general jurisdiction).

## III. Exercising Jurisdiction Comports With Fair Play and Substantial Justice

Once minimum contacts are shown, jurisdiction must also be reasonable, considering "the burden on the defendant, the forum State's interest in adjudicating the dispute, the plaintiff's interest in obtaining convenient and effective relief," and the interstate judicial system's interest in efficient resolution. *Burger King*, 471 U.S. at 476-77 (quoting *World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 292). Ford, a global manufacturer that litigates routinely across the country, faces no unfair burden defending a products-liability suit in the State where its product caused a fatal injury. Montana, meanwhile, has a substantial interest in "providing a forum" for its own injured citizens, *Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court of California*, 480 U.S. 102, 114 (1987) (quoting *Keeton*, 465 U.S. at 776), and in regulating the safety of vehicles operated on its roads. Because "modern transportation and communications have made it much less burdensome for a party sued to defend himself," *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957), and because Ford purposefully cultivated the Montana market from which this suit arises, the exercise of jurisdiction here offends no "traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice." *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316.
