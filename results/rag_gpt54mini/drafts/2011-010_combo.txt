Respondent’s question is answered by the statute’s text and this Court’s OCSLA decisions. Section 1333(b) extends LHWCA benefits for disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress could have limited coverage to injuries “upon” the Shelf, as it did in the original LHWCA, but it did not. See Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act of 1927, ch. 509, § 3(a), 44 Stat. 1424; Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford Railroad, 281 U.S. 128, 136-37 (1930); South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 256-57 (1940). The proper inquiry is whether the injury resulted from Shelf operations, not whether it happened on a particular geographic situs.

This Court has long recognized that workers’ compensation statutes are not controlled by formal labels when Congress chose broader functional coverage. In Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford Railroad, 281 U.S. 128 (1930), the Court explained that the Act’s coverage is “manifestly broad enough” to reach employers with employees engaged in maritime employment, and that the statute looks to the relationship of the work and injury, not a crabbed territorial distinction. Id. at 136-37. Likewise, in Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917), the Court emphasized that maritime compensation questions turn on the nature of the work and the location of the maritime connection, not wooden formalism. See also Northern Coal & Dock Co. v. Strand, 278 U.S. 142, 145-46 (1928); Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244, 246-47 (1942).

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” approach is consistent with that text and history. Section 1333(b) ties coverage to injuries “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” The phrase “as the result of” naturally denotes causation, not situs. A situs-of-injury limitation would add words Congress omitted. See Pennsylvania Railroad v. O’Rourke, 344 U.S. 334, 336-37 (1953); Hahn v. Ross Island Sand & Gravel Co., 358 U.S. 272, 273-74 (1959). It would also create arbitrary gaps: a worker injured onshore while performing a task integral to offshore extraction would be excluded, while an identical injury on a platform would be covered. Congress enacted OCSLA to create a comprehensive federal regime for offshore resource development. See United States v. Louisiana, 363 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1960); Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969).

Petitioners rely on Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), but that case does not control. The Court there said only that § 1333(b) “has no bearing on this case.” Id. at 219 n.2. That statement was made in a case about the § 1333(a)(2)(A) choice-of-law provision, not the workers’ compensation provision at issue here. Id. at 209, 219 n.2. And where a prior remark is not grounded in the question presented, it should not be stretched beyond its setting. See Humphrey’s Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935); Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821).

Nor does the original LHWCA compel petitioners’ rule. This Court held in Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson, 396 U.S. 212 (1969), that Congress used a geographic line to define the Act’s situs requirement for piers, but that was because the statute expressly required injury “upon the navigable waters of the United States.” Id. at 214-16. OCSLA uses different language. Courts must respect that choice. See Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co., 375 U.S. 34, 36-37 (1963) (recognizing that OCSLA provided a separate compensation question); Rodrigue, 395 U.S. at 355-56.

The better reading is the one adopted by the Third Circuit in Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988): if the injury flows from Shelf operations, coverage follows. That approach gives effect to Congress’s remedial design and avoids inserting a situs restriction nowhere found in § 1333(b). The contrary Fifth Circuit view in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), is textually unsupported and inconsistent with this Court’s instruction that compensation statutes should be construed according to their functional purpose. See Parker, 314 U.S. at 246-47; Hahn, 358 U.S. at 273-74.

Accordingly, the judgment should be affirmed on the OCSLA claim.
