The district court erred in denying Winbush's Motion for Fingerprint Identification Expert and Motion to Continue, thereby prejudicing his defense.

The denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint expert was an abuse of discretion. The government's expert testimony regarding the absence of latent fingerprints on the physical evidence was a crucial aspect of their case. By stipulating to Shay's testimony, Winbush did not waive his right to challenge the government's evidence. The Supreme Court has held that a defendant's stipulation to certain facts does not preclude them from challenging the sufficiency of the evidence. See Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942) ("The fact that [the defendant] stipulated to certain facts does not mean that he admitted the sufficiency of the evidence to convict him.").

Furthermore, the denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint expert deprived him of the opportunity to present a complete defense. As the Court held in Jencks v. United States, 353 U.S. 657, 668 (1957), "the defense is entitled to have the government's witnesses produce all relevant and competent evidence touching the events in issue." By denying Winbush's motion, the court prevented him from obtaining potentially exculpatory evidence.

The timing of Winbush's motion is not dispositive. The Court has recognized that a defendant's right to a fair trial takes precedence over concerns about delay. See Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) ("[T]he right to have counsel assigned becomes so imperative that it can be denied only when it is denied with an appreciation of the consequences that will follow."). The magistrate judge's denial of Winbush's motion was not accompanied by a consideration of the potential prejudice to Winbush's defense.

The denial of Winbush's Motion to Continue was also in error. As the Court held in Avery v. Alabama, 308 U.S. 444, 446 (1940), "the denial of opportunity for appointed counsel to confer, to consult with the accused and to prepare his defense, could convert the appointment of counsel into a sham and nothing more than a formal compliance with the Constitution's requirement that an accused be given the assistance of counsel." Similarly, the denial of Winbush's Motion to Continue prevented him from adequately preparing his defense.

In light of these errors, Winbush's conviction should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial.
