ARGUMENT

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage only to disability or death resulting from injury occurring on the outer Continental Shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (1964). The text limits benefits to injuries arising from “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf,” confirming that both the operations and the resulting injury must occur there. See Rodrigue v. Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co., 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969) (describing the Act’s extension of federal law “to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and to all artificial islands and fixed structures which may be erected thereon”).

This geographic limitation is consistent with the Act’s structure. Congress extended “the Constitution and laws and civil and political jurisdiction of the United States” to the Shelf “to the same extent as if the outer Continental Shelf were an area of exclusive Federal jurisdiction located within a State.” Id. at 355 (quoting 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1)). The same territorial principle governs compensation. See United States v. Louisiana, 363 U.S. 1, 4-5 (1960) (recognizing federal paramount rights in submerged lands beyond three miles).

Precedent confirms that situs controls. In Northern Coal & Dock Co. v. Strand, 278 U.S. 142, 144 (1928), the Court held that when employment “pertained to” work on navigable waters, “the rights of the parties must be ascertained upon a consideration of the maritime law.” Similarly, Nacirema Operating Co. v. Johnson, 396 U.S. 212, 214-15 (1969), and Pennsylvania R.R. v. O’Rourke, 344 U.S. 334, 337 (1953), tied coverage to the place of injury. Nogueira v. New York, New Haven & Hartford R.R., 281 U.S. 128, 131-32 (1930), applied the same rule to injuries on moored vessels.

The Longshore Act itself requires “an injury occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251, 254 (1940) (quoting 33 U.S.C. § 903(a)). OCSLA incorporates that requirement without enlarging the geographic reach. See Parker v. Motor Boat Sales, Inc., 314 U.S. 244, 246-47 (1942); Hahn v. Ross Island Sand & Gravel Co., 358 U.S. 272, 273 (1959). Tipton v. Socony Mobil Oil Co., 375 U.S. 34, 35 (1963), likewise treated the Shelf’s compensation provisions as tied to the location of the injury.

Because Juan Valladolid’s death occurred at an onshore facility in California, the Benefits Review Board correctly denied benefits. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
