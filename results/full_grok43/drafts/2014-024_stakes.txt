ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires only an objective showing that a reasonable speaker would foresee the statements as threatening. Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), establishes that the First Amendment true-threats exception demands proof the speaker subjectively intended to threaten.

In Black the Court defined true threats as “those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” Id. at 359. The verb “means” imports a subjective inquiry into the speaker’s purpose. An objective test that ignores whether the speaker actually intended the communication as a threat therefore sweeps in protected speech.

Section 875(c) is silent on mens rea. When a criminal statute is silent, courts must “imply the mental state that separates wrongful from innocent conduct.” See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The objective standard applied below fails that requirement; it criminalizes statements the speaker did not intend as threats, including artistic, satirical, or hyperbolic expression.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), is misplaced. Kosma predates Black and cannot survive its holding that the speaker must “mean[] to communicate” a threat. 538 U.S. at 359. Because the jury was instructed only on the objective standard, the convictions rest on an unconstitutional mens-rea requirement and must be reversed.
