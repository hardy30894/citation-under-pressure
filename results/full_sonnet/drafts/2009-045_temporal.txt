**ARGUMENT**

**I. The century-old equitable practice from which Congress borrowed the "reasonable fee" standard has always permitted courts to account for the quality of representation and the results counsel obtained.**

Long before Congress began enacting fee-shifting statutes, this Court recognized that courts sitting in equity possess inherent authority to compensate counsel according to the value of the services actually rendered, not merely according to a mechanical measure of time expended. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881), the Court approved compensation to counsel who had preserved a trust fund for numerous beneficiaries, reasoning that equity would not permit those who benefited from counsel's labor to escape paying for the value that labor produced. That principle was reaffirmed in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), where the Court again looked past a simple time-based calculation to the substance of what counsel had accomplished for the fund and its beneficiaries.

This Court applied the same equitable logic in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), sustaining an award that reflected the benefit counsel's efforts conferred rather than a fee derived from hours alone. And in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court explained that such fee awards rest on "the historic power of equity to permit fees," id. at 166, a power exercised according to the equities of the particular case rather than by rigid formula. Nothing in these decisions treats time and rate as the exclusive measure of a "reasonable" fee; to the contrary, each recognizes that the results secured and the skill with which counsel secured them bear directly on what a reasonable fee should be.

When Congress subsequently enacted statutes shifting the ordinary American Rule and authorizing prevailing parties to recover a "reasonable attorney's fee," it legislated against this settled backdrop. This Court has long recognized that fee-shifting statutes represent a deliberate departure from the common-law rule that each party bears its own costs, adopted precisely so that meritorious claims—often brought by parties without resources to pay market rates—could attract capable counsel. *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717 (1967) (discussing the American Rule and the statutory exceptions Congress has carved from it). Congress's choice of the word "reasonable," rather than some narrower formula tied strictly to hours and rates, should be read as an invitation to the courts to apply the same equitable judgment historically exercised in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, and *Sprague*—judgment that has never excluded quality of performance and results obtained from the calculus.

**II. Because a fee calculated from hours and a standard rate does not always capture exceptional performance or exceptional results, courts retain equitable discretion, in the rare case, to enhance the award.**

The hourly-rate-times-hours method is a useful proxy for the value of an attorney's work in the ordinary case, but it is only a proxy. The equitable principle underlying *Greenough* and *Pettus* was never that time records alone measure a reasonable fee; it was that the fee should reflect the actual value conferred through counsel's skill and the outcome achieved. A fee set solely by multiplying a standard hourly rate by hours billed may, in the unusual case, fail to reflect that value—just as the fee arrangements approved in *Newton* and *Sprague* looked beyond time sheets to what counsel's efforts were actually worth to those who benefited from them.

Nothing in this history suggests that such considerations become irrelevant merely because Congress, rather than a chancellor administering a common fund, now directs the fee award. The statutory word "reasonable" imported the same flexible, results-sensitive inquiry that this Court's own equity precedents had already developed. A rule categorically foreclosing enhancement for extraordinary performance and extraordinary results would sever the statutory fee standard from the very body of law that gave the word "reasonable" its content in this context.

That does not mean enhancements should be routine. Consistent with the equitable tradition reflected in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, and *Sprague*, an enhancement is warranted only where the district court, which observes counsel's performance and the litigation's course firsthand, finds that the case is the rare one in which the lodestar—calculated by ordinary hours and ordinary rates—does not adequately capture the value of services whose quality and result exceeded what those rates were designed to compensate. Respondents submit that such a finding, resting on the equitable discretion this Court has recognized for well over a century, is precisely what the district court made here, and that nothing in the fee-shifting statute's text or purpose forecloses it.

For these reasons, the "reasonable attorney's fee" that Congress authorized may, in the exceptional case, be enhanced based on the quality of performance and the results obtained.
