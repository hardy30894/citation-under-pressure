**ARGUMENT**

The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not afford a cause of action to a plaintiff who alleges only that a governmental entity acted out of personal spite or animus toward him, without alleging membership in a protected class or discriminatory treatment of a similarly situated group. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a “class of one” claim may be maintained under the Equal Protection Clause when the plaintiff asserts that the government’s action was motivated solely by a desire to “get” him for reasons unrelated to any legitimate state objective. Such a theory, if accepted, would eviscerate the foundational requirement of equal protection jurisprudence: that the plaintiff must demonstrate that he was treated differently from others similarly situated, not merely that he was singled out for adverse treatment.

The principle that equal protection requires a showing of differential treatment based on classification is firmly established in this Court’s precedent. In *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), the Court held that the Equal Protection Clause protects individuals from arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement of laws, but only where the law is applied in a manner that treats similarly situated persons differently. The Court emphasized that “the law, in its nakedness, is colorless and impartial,” but its application may be tainted by discriminatory intent. Id. at 370. The case involved a San Francisco ordinance that, on its face, applied equally to all laundry operators, but was enforced in a way that targeted Chinese residents. The Court concluded that the law’s discriminatory application violated the Equal Protection Clause, not because of the law’s text, but because of its discriminatory enforcement. Id. at 373. This case stands for the proposition that equal protection is violated only when there is a pattern of unequal treatment based on a cognizable classification—here, race—and not when a single individual is treated differently without a comparable group being similarly situated.

Similarly, in *Plessy v. Ferguson*, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), the Court upheld the constitutionality of state laws requiring racial segregation in public accommodations, reasoning that the Equal Protection Clause did not prohibit laws that treated all persons equally under the law, even if the treatment was unequal in effect. The Court stated: “The object of the amendment was undoubtedly to enforce the absolute equality of the two races before the law.” Id. at 559. The Court made clear that the Clause was not designed to eliminate all distinctions, but only those based on arbitrary or invidious classifications. Id. at 560. Thus, the Clause protects against classifications based on race, sex, or other suspect categories, but does not create a right to equal treatment in every instance of governmental action.

The Court has long recognized that the Equal Protection Clause does not require the government to treat all individuals identically in every circumstance. In *Baldwin v. Franks*, 208 U.S. 371 (1908), the Court upheld a state law that required certain individuals to obtain licenses before engaging in business, noting that “the state may regulate the exercise of rights in the interest of the public welfare.” Id. at 375. The Court rejected the argument that the law violated equal protection because it applied to some but not all individuals, stating that “the law may be applied to those who are engaged in a particular business, and not to others, without violating the Constitution.” Id. at 376. This principle underscores that differential treatment is not per se unconstitutional; it is only when such treatment is based on an impermissible classification that the Equal Protection Clause is offended.

The Court of Appeals’ decision in this case would permit a plaintiff to assert a constitutional claim based solely on the government’s subjective animus toward him, without any showing that others similarly situated were treated differently. This would transform the Equal Protection Clause into a general due process right against arbitrary government action—a role the Clause was never intended to fulfill. In *Meyer v. Nebraska*, 262 U.S. 390 (1923), the Court held that the Due Process Clause protects certain fundamental liberties, such as the right to teach and learn. Id. at 399. But the Court made clear that the Due Process Clause, not the Equal Protection Clause, was the source of that protection. Id. at 400. The Equal Protection Clause, by contrast, is a mechanism for ensuring that the government does not treat individuals differently based on impermissible classifications.

To allow a “class of one” claim under the Equal Protection Clause would create a new and undefined category of constitutional protection, one that has no basis in the text or history of the Fourteenth Amendment. The Clause was designed to protect against discrimination based on race, national origin, and other suspect classifications, not to serve as a remedy for personal grievances against government officials. The Court has consistently held that the Constitution does not provide a remedy for every instance of governmental unfairness. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542 (1875), the Court stated that “the Constitution does not create rights in individuals against the government in every case where the government acts unfairly.” Id. at 553. The Court emphasized that the Constitution protects only those rights that are specifically enumerated or protected by the Bill of Rights or the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Court of Appeals’ decision, by permitting a claim based solely on personal spite, would open the door to an unlimited number of constitutional challenges based on subjective grievances. It would allow any individual to claim that a government decision was made out of personal animus, regardless of whether others were treated similarly. This would undermine the principle that equal protection requires a showing of differential treatment based on classification, not mere individualized hostility.

For these reasons, the Equal Protection Clause does not give rise to a cause of action on behalf of a “class of one” where the plaintiff does not allege membership in a protected class or that similarly situated individuals were treated differently. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
