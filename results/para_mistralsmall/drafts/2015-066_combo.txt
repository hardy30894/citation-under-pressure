**ARGUMENT**

Petitioner respectfully submits that the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts whose judgments are later used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that uncounseled tribal convictions cannot serve as predicate offenses because they violate the Sixth Amendment’s core guarantee of counsel. This conclusion is compelled by longstanding precedent recognizing that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel attaches to any criminal prosecution that may result in imprisonment, regardless of the forum in which the conviction is obtained.

The Sixth Amendment provides that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right . . . to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the right to counsel is fundamental to a fair trial and applies to all criminal prosecutions, not merely those in federal courts. In *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 53 (1932), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is “a fundamental right, essential to a fair trial,” and that its protections extend to state prosecutions through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Powell* made clear that the right to counsel is not limited to federal courts but is a bedrock principle of due process applicable to all criminal prosecutions. *Id.* at 60-61.

Similarly, in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), this Court held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is “a fundamental right . . . of the utmost importance to a defendant charged with crime,” and that it applies to all federal criminal prosecutions. *Id.* at 462-63. The Court explained that the right to counsel is essential to ensure a fair trial, particularly where the defendant may face imprisonment. *Id.* at 463. These principles were later extended to state prosecutions in *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), which held that the Sixth Amendment right to counsel is incorporated against the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Id.* at 342-45.

The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to tribal court prosecutions where the defendant may be imprisoned. In *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), the court held that tribal court convictions used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions must comply with the Sixth Amendment. The court explained that the Sixth Amendment’s protections are not limited to federal or state courts but apply to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. *Id.* at 1394-95. The court further held that the use of uncounseled tribal convictions as predicate offenses would violate the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel. *Id.* at 1395.

This Court’s decision in *Baldasar v. Illinois*, 446 U.S. 222 (1980) (per curiam), reinforces the principle that uncounseled convictions cannot be used as predicate offenses in subsequent prosecutions. In *Baldasar*, the Court held that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction could not be used to enhance a sentence under a recidivist statute. *Id.* at 224. Although *Baldasar* did not explicitly address tribal courts, its reasoning applies with equal force to tribal convictions used as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions. The Court’s concern in *Baldasar* was that the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses would undermine the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel. *Id.* at 224-25.

The Ninth Circuit’s decision in *Ant* is consistent with this Court’s precedents recognizing that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. The court correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal convictions as predicate offenses would violate the Sixth Amendment. This conclusion is compelled by *Powell*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, and *Baldasar*, which establish that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel is fundamental to a fair trial and applies to all criminal prosecutions, regardless of the forum.

The dissent’s reliance on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994), is misplaced. *Nichols* involved the use of an uncounseled conviction in sentencing, not as a predicate offense in a subsequent prosecution. The Court in *Nichols* explicitly distinguished between the use of uncounseled convictions in sentencing and their use as predicate offenses. *Id.* at 747-48. The Court held that the use of uncounseled convictions in sentencing does not violate the Sixth Amendment because sentencing is not a separate criminal prosecution. *Id.* at 747. However, the Court did not address the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses in subsequent prosecutions, leaving that question open. *Id.* at 748.

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the use of uncounseled tribal convictions as predicate offenses in federal prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) violates the Sixth Amendment. This conclusion is compelled by *Powell*, *Johnson v. Zerbst*, and *Baldasar*, which establish that the Sixth Amendment’s right to counsel applies to all criminal prosecutions, including those in tribal courts. The dissent’s reliance on *Nichols* is misplaced because *Nichols* did not address the use of uncounseled convictions as predicate offenses in subsequent prosecutions.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
