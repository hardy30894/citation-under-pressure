## Argument

The Equal Protection Clause does not transform every claim of arbitrary governmental treatment into a constitutional cause of action. It forbids states from denying any person the equal protection of the laws, which has long meant that the Constitution is concerned with discrimination as compared with others similarly situated. See *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373-74 (1886); *Bell’s Gap R.R. Co. v. Pennsylvania*, 134 U.S. 232, 237 (1890); *Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949). Because respondent alleges no membership in a class or group, her claim fails absent a showing that the Village drew a classification line and treated her differently from other similarly situated owners for a constitutionally impermissible reason. *Snowden v. Hughes*, 321 U.S. 1, 8 (1944); *McLaughlin v. Florida*, 379 U.S. 184, 191-92 (1964).

A. The Equal Protection Clause requires a classification and discriminatory treatment, not merely alleged unfairness.

This Court has repeatedly held that equal protection is implicated when the government classifies persons and denies a benefit to one class while granting it to another similarly situated class. *Yick Wo*, 118 U.S. at 373-74; *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry. Co. v. Ellis*, 165 U.S. 150, 155-56 (1897); *Tigner v. Texas*, 310 U.S. 141, 147 (1940). The Clause is not a “general guarantee against arbitrary governmental action.” *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 11; *Ohio ex rel. Clarke v. Deckebach*, 274 U.S. 392, 397 (1927). Where there is no classification, there is no equal protection problem. *Gulf, Colorado & Santa Fe Ry.*, 165 U.S. at 155-56.

Respondent’s complaint alleges only that the Village demanded a larger easement from her than from other applicants. Even taking that allegation as true, it does not establish that the Village acted pursuant to a classification that singles out a constitutionally cognizable group. Equal protection doctrine has never required that the government’s line-drawing be wise, kind, or even consistent; it requires only that similarly situated persons be treated alike. *McGowan v. Maryland*, 366 U.S. 420, 425-26 (1961); *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911). Respondent’s pleading thus sounds, at most, in state-law arbitrariness, not federal equal protection.

B. This Court’s decisions recognizing irrational discrimination have involved class-based classifications, not a freestanding “class of one” claim.

The cases respondent invokes involve the denial of rights based on race, alienage, or other discriminatory classifications. In *Yick Wo*, the city administered a facially neutral ordinance in a manner that discriminated against Chinese laundry operators. 118 U.S. at 373-74. In *Bolling v. Sharpe*, 347 U.S. 497, 499-500 (1954), the Court addressed segregation by race in the public schools. In *McLaughlin*, the Court invalidated a statute criminalizing interracial cohabitation. 379 U.S. at 191-92. In *Morey v. Doud*, 354 U.S. 457, 463-64 (1957), the Court struck down an irrational statutory distinction among money-order sellers. And in *Allegheny Pittsburgh Coal Co. v. County Commission*, 488 U.S. 336, 344-46 (1989), the Court addressed grossly unequal property-tax assessments under a state system.

Those decisions do not establish a constitutional cause of action whenever a single individual believes government officials treated her unfavorably. To the contrary, this Court has cautioned that “every selection of subjects for differential treatment” is not unconstitutional merely because it is imperfect. *Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 110. The Equal Protection Clause permits government to address problems one step at a time and to draw distinctions that are not mathematically exact. *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955); *Tigner*, 310 U.S. at 147. That principle is inconsistent with a rule that would constitutionalize every dispute over a municipality’s discretionary demands in a permit or utility-connection negotiation.

C. Respondent’s “spite” theory would improperly federalize ordinary local disputes and erase the requirement of a legal classification.

The Court has never held that alleged ill will alone creates an equal protection claim. In *Snowden*, the Court explained that unequal administration violates the Constitution only when it is intentional discrimination “upon an unjustifiable standard,” not when officials simply err or act irregularly. 321 U.S. at 8. Likewise, *Oyler v. Boles*, 368 U.S. 448, 456 (1962), held that selective enforcement is not unconstitutional absent a showing of “intentional or purposeful discrimination.” Those decisions require more than allegations of annoyance, animus, or retaliation divorced from a recognized class-based distinction.

Respondent asks the Court to convert any arbitrary or hostile governmental act into a constitutional violation. But that approach would collapse the line between the Constitution and ordinary tort or administrative law. This Court has consistently resisted such expansion. *Snowden*, 321 U.S. at 11; *Lindsley*, 220 U.S. at 78-79; *Railway Express Agency*, 336 U.S. at 110. The better rule is the one reflected in the Court’s prior cases: where no class, group, or similarly situated comparator is alleged, and where no suspect or quasi-suspect classification is claimed, the Equal Protection Clause is not implicated.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
