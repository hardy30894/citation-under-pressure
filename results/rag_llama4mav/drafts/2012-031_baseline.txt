The District Court's certification of the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis was an abuse of discretion. Under Rule 23(b)(3) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, a class may be certified only if "the court finds that the questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members, and that a class action is superior to other available methods for fairly and efficiently adjudicating the controversy." The requirement that common questions predominate "tests whether proposed classes are sufficiently cohesive to warrant adjudication by representation." Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor, 521 U.S. 591, 623 (1997).

To satisfy the predominance requirement, the plaintiffs must show that they can prove their claims through common evidence. See Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 311-312. In this case, the District Court certified the class based on the theory that Comcast's clustering conduct deterred overbuilder competition, causing antitrust impact. However, the court failed to ensure that the plaintiffs had presented a reliable methodology for measuring damages on a class-wide basis.

The Supreme Court has held that a district court must conduct a "rigorous analysis" of the evidence and arguments in making the class certification decision. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes, 131 S.Ct. 2541, 2551 (2011). This analysis requires "a thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations" and "may include a preliminary inquiry into the merits." Hydrogen Peroxide, 552 F.3d at 317. The District Court's failure to rigorously analyze the evidence and ensure that the plaintiffs had a reliable damages model was an error of law.

The Third Circuit's decision in Hydrogen Peroxide emphasizes that a district court must "resolve all factual or legal disputes relevant to class certification, even if they overlap with the merits." 552 F.3d at 307. In this case, the District Court failed to do so, certifying the class without adequate evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit erred in affirming this decision.
