# ARGUMENT

## I. States Possess Broad Authority to Define the Insanity Defense and to Channel Mental-State Evidence Accordingly

This Court has long recognized that the definition of criminal responsibility, including the role that mental illness plays in it, is a matter primarily for the States. In *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's rule requiring a defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt and its rejection of the "irresistible impulse" test, holding that the State was free to choose among competing formulations of criminal responsibility because "knowledge of right and wrong is the exclusive test of criminal responsibility" is a policy choice belonging to the States. *Id.* at 800-01. The Court expressly declined to constitutionalize any single test of insanity, recognizing that such judgments rest on medical and moral premises about which reasonable jurisdictions may differ.

That same principle governs the closely related question here—whether a State may confine expert psychiatric evidence to the insanity inquiry rather than allow it to negate the mens rea element directly. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), this Court sustained a trial court's refusal to admit psychiatric evidence offered to show that the defendant's mental condition reduced murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, even though such evidence was admissible on an insanity defense. The Court held that the decision whether to recognize a doctrine of "partial responsibility" distinct from full insanity was one for the jurisdiction's own law to make, not one compelled by the Constitution. *Id.* at 476. Arizona's rule, which channels mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense while excluding it from the mens rea determination, follows directly from the framework *Fisher* approved.

The allocation of the burden of proving mental incapacity has likewise long rested with the States. In *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), this Court described the insanity inquiry as one governed by rules that federal and state courts could develop according to their own traditions, and *Leland* confirmed that the Constitution does not freeze those rules at any particular common-law formulation. 343 U.S. at 800-01.

## II. Due Process Requires Only Fundamental Fairness, Not a Particular Procedural or Evidentiary Regime

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not impose a uniform code of criminal procedure on the States; it forbids only proceedings that offend fundamental fairness. In *Snyder v. Massachusetts*, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court explained that due process is violated only where a state procedure is "so acute and shocking that our polity will not endure it," and that the Constitution leaves States wide latitude to shape the mechanics of criminal trials. *Id.* at 122. Similarly, in *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), the Court held that due process does not incorporate the particular procedural guarantees found in the Bill of Rights but instead protects only those principles "of justice which are of the very essence of a scheme of ordered liberty." *Id.* at 106.

This Court applied that same restraint to a state evidentiary rule in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), rejecting a due process challenge to Texas's rule permitting evidence of prior convictions in sentencing proceedings despite the risk that jurors might misuse the evidence on the question of guilt. The Court emphasized that "the process of trial necessarily demand[s] that some limits be placed on the free play of relevancy" and that state legislatures and courts are entitled to strike that balance themselves. *Id.* at 562-64. Arizona's rule confining mental-disease evidence to the insanity defense is precisely the sort of considered limitation on the scope of admissible evidence that *Spencer* held States may adopt without violating due process.

## III. Arizona's Channeling Rule Is a Rational Structuring of the Criminal Responsibility Inquiry, Not a Denial of a Fundamental Right

In *Powell v. Texas*, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), a plurality of this Court cautioned against using the Constitution to "adopt a single, static, and modish theory of criminal responsibility" and confirmed that the States retain "wide latitude" to decide how, and to what extent, considerations of mental abnormality bear upon guilt. *Id.* at 536-37. That latitude encompasses the choice Arizona has made: to treat the insanity defense as the exclusive vehicle for psychiatric evidence bearing on moral culpability, while leaving the mens rea element to be judged by objective evidence of conduct and circumstance. This structuring does not eliminate any element the State must prove; it simply determines the procedural avenue through which a particular category of evidence may be presented—precisely the kind of judgment *Fisher* and *Spencer* leave to the States.

Nor does *Robinson v. California*, 370 U.S. 660 (1962), assist petitioner. *Robinson* held that punishing the mere "status" of narcotics addiction, absent any culpable act, violated the Eighth Amendment. *Id.* at 666-67. Arizona's rule punishes only the completed criminal act, proved through the State's evidence of conduct and intent; it does not punish status, and *Robinson*'s narrow holding has no application to an evidentiary channeling rule governing the mens rea inquiry.

## CONCLUSION

Because the Constitution leaves to the States the authority to define the insanity defense, to allocate its burden of proof, and to determine the evidentiary channels through which psychiatric evidence may be presented, Arizona's rule confining Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim violates no principle of fundamental fairness under the Fourteenth Amendment. The judgment below should be affirmed.
