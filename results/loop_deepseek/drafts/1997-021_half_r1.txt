**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR POLLING IS IRRATIONAL AND INCONSISTENT WITH THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.**

The Board requires an employer to possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” before polling employees. That standard demands the same quantum of evidence needed to withdraw recognition—a far more drastic step—thereby rendering the polling mechanism illusory. The Act is intended to protect the free flow of commerce from the substantial obstructions of labor disputes. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 41 (1937). A rule that forces an employer to choose between recognizing a union that may lack majority support or facing an unfair-labor-practice charge for withdrawing recognition frustrates that objective.

The presumption of an incumbent union’s majority status is a rebuttable one. *Brooks v. NLRB*, 348 U.S. 96, 103 (1954). When an employer possesses a good-faith doubt, polling provides a lawful, non-coercive means of resolving that doubt. The Board’s requirement that the employer first satisfy the strict standard for withdrawal of recognition makes polling a dead letter: if the employer met that threshold, it could simply cease bargaining. While this Court accords the Board deference in interpreting the Act, that deference does not extend to constructions that are arbitrary or inconsistent with the statutory scheme. *NLRB v. Hearst Publications, Inc.*, 322 U.S. 111, 130–31 (1944).

The Board’s uniform evidentiary standard is also internally incoherent. It applies the same test to employer polls, Board-conducted RM elections, and outright withdrawal of recognition, despite each having vastly different procedural safeguards. The Board cannot rationally impose a standard that makes an unsupervised poll as difficult to justify as a supervised election while simultaneously making a unilateral withdrawal—the least regulated option—equally easy. This inconsistency is the hallmark of arbitrary agency action, frustrating rather than effectuating the basic policies of the Act. *NLRB v. Insurance Agents’ Int’l Union*, 361 U.S. 477, 497–98 (1960). The Board’s approach disregards the careful tailoring required to balance employer interests and statutory goals. *See NLRB v. Exchange Parts Co.*, 375 U.S. 405, 409 (1964). The Board must act within the boundaries of the Act, and its present standard exceeds them. *NLRB v. Wooster Div. of Borg-Warner Corp.*, 356 U.S. 342, 350 (1958).

**II. EVEN UNDER THE BOARD’S STANDARD, ITS FACTUAL FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN LACKED A REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.**

An unfair labor practice finding must be supported by substantial evidence considering the record as a whole. *NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co.*, 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939). The record here contains overwhelming, unrefuted evidence that Allentown reasonably doubted the union’s majority support. At least seven employees made direct statements repudiating the union. Additional reports from employees Bloch and Mohr—that the night shift of five or six employees and the entire service department opposed the union—provided objective corroboration. The Board selectively credited only direct statements and ignored this circumstantial evidence, applying rigid rules that distort the totality-of-circumstances inquiry.

The Board’s rejection of employee Ridgick’s statement was particularly arbitrary. The ALJ found it equivocal because, during a job interview after being told the company would be non-union, Ridgick said “as long as the new company would treat them right, there was no need for a union.” The Board’s rule that such interview statements are inherently insincere ignores common sense: an employee seeking a job has every incentive to express satisfaction with the employer’s stated position. Likewise, employee Marsh’s complaint about paying $35 monthly in dues without receiving value was dismissed as mere dissatisfaction. But dissatisfaction with the cost of representation, linked to a desire to escape that cost, is a clear expression of anti-union sentiment. The Board’s hypertechnical parsing of such language finds no support in the Act.

The Board also excluded employees hired after January 25, 1991, yet the employer’s doubt was based on information available at the time of its decision. The Board may not penalize an employer for acting on the evidence then before it. *NLRB v. Mackay Radio & Telegraph Co.*, 304 U.S. 333, 345–46 (1938). The statutory presumption of majority status is rebuttable—*Brooks*, 348 U.S. at 103—and the record demonstrates that rebuttal was more than adequate. The Board’s factual finding is arbitrary and unsupported.

**III. THE BOARD’S STANDARD UNDERMINES THE ACT’S POLICY OF EMPLOYEE FREE CHOICE AND INDUSTRIAL PEACE.**

The Act guarantees employees the right to freely choose or reject a bargaining representative. The Board’s rule discourages employers from testing employee sentiment, forcing them into a Catch-22 that destabilizes labor relations. By refusing to permit a fair poll absent the strictest evidence, the Board prevents the peaceful resolution of representation questions and invites protracted disputes. This Court has long recognized that the Act’s central purpose is to promote industrial peace through orderly processes. *Jones & Laughlin*, 301 U.S. at 41. The Board’s standard is a departure from the flexibility inherent in the Act and should be rejected.
