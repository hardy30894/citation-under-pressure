ARGUMENT

Section 1407(a) provides that actions transferred for pretrial proceedings “shall be remanded by the Panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). The word “shall” creates a mandatory obligation. Anderson v. Yungkau, 329 U.S. 482, 485 (1947) (“The word ‘shall’ is ordinarily the language of command.”). Once pretrial proceedings end, the statute leaves no discretion to retain the case for trial.

Section 1404(a) supplies no authority to circumvent this command. That provision permits transfer “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but it is a general venue statute. A specific statutory mandate controls over a general one. Radzanower v. Touche Ross & Co., 426 U.S. 148, 153 (1976) (“[A] specific statute controls over a general one.”). Section 1407(a) expressly limits the transferee court’s role to “coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings” and requires remand thereafter. Allowing self-transfer under § 1404(a) would nullify the remand command and the Panel’s exclusive authority to assign cases for trial.

The structure of § 1407 confirms the limitation. Congress authorized transfer only for pretrial purposes and preserved the plaintiff’s choice of forum for trial. See Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947) (plaintiff’s choice of forum is “a privilege of venue” not lightly disturbed). The statute’s text reflects a deliberate policy choice that transferee judges may not override by invoking a general transfer provision.

The decisions permitting self-transfer, such as Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord, 447 F.2d 122, 125 (2d Cir. 1971), and In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982), rest on policy considerations rather than statutory text. They cannot displace the plain language of § 1407(a). Because the District of Arizona lacked authority to retain the case for trial, the judgment should be reversed.
