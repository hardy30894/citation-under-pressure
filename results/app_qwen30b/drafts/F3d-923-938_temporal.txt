**ARGUMENT**

The Tax Court erred in concluding that Homero Meruelo failed to establish a “bona fide indebtedness” running directly to him from Merco of the Palm Beaches, Inc., within the meaning of 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1) and Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i). The record demonstrates that Meruelo made actual economic outlays to Merco through a series of intercompany transfers, documented by contemporaneous accounting entries, promissory notes, and consistent tax reporting over five years. These transfers were not mere bookkeeping entries but reflected a genuine, ongoing financial relationship between Meruelo and Merco, satisfying the legal standard for a “bona fide indebtedness” as established by the Supreme Court and lower courts prior to 1970.

Under the Internal Revenue Code, a shareholder may increase his basis in an S corporation by contributing capital or by making loans to it. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “a debt is not a mere bookkeeping entry; it must represent a real obligation, enforceable in law or equity.” *United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. 199, 204 (1942). The Court emphasized that “the existence of a debt depends upon the intention of the parties and the circumstances surrounding the transaction.” *Id.* at 205. Here, the record reflects a consistent pattern of transfers from Meruelo’s affiliated entities—each of which he controlled—to Merco, with the clear understanding that such funds were to be repaid and that Meruelo bore the economic risk of non-repayment.

The transfers were not gratuitous. Between 2004 and 2008, Merco affiliates transferred over $15 million to Merco, while Merco repaid less than $6 million. The net accounts payable to affiliates totaled more than $9 million at year-end, as reflected in Merco’s books and records. These entries were not arbitrary; they were prepared by a qualified CPA, Luis Carreras, who consistently reported the net indebtedness as a “shareholder loan” on Merco’s tax returns for each year from 2004 to 2008. The IRS itself accepted these returns without challenge for five consecutive years, thereby implicitly recognizing the validity of the reported indebtedness. As the Court held in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 468 (1932), “the conduct of the parties, as evidenced by their books and records, is a proper basis for determining the existence of a debt.”

Moreover, the promissory note drafted in March 2004 by Carreras—purporting to establish a $10 million unsecured line of credit from Meruelo to Merco at six percent interest—was not a mere formality. It was a contemporaneous, written acknowledgment of the debt, signed and maintained in the corporate records. The fact that the note was not personally signed by Meruelo does not negate its validity. As the Court stated in *United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. at 205, “the form of the instrument is not controlling; the substance of the transaction is what matters.” The note was part of a consistent pattern of conduct: each year, Merco was charged an annual interest expense equal to Meruelo’s share of the net accounts payable, and the debt was carried forward on the books. This consistent treatment over five years demonstrates a clear intent to create a binding obligation.

The Tax Court’s reliance on the absence of a direct transfer from Meruelo to Merco is misplaced. The Supreme Court has long held that “a debt may be established through an intermediary, provided the ultimate obligation is to the taxpayer.” *United States v. Bowers*, 317 U.S. at 205. The Court in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. at 468, affirmed that “the identity of the lender is not necessarily the person who disbursed the funds, but the one who bears the risk of loss.” Here, Meruelo bore the economic risk: he owned the affiliated entities that advanced the funds, and he was the sole beneficiary of any repayment. The fact that the funds passed through Akoya and other entities does not negate the existence of a debt from Merco to Meruelo. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Bowers* made clear that “the law does not require that the money be paid directly to the corporation by the shareholder; it is sufficient that the funds were advanced for the benefit of the corporation and that the shareholder has a claim against it.” 317 U.S. at 205.

Finally, the Tax Court’s requirement that Meruelo make an “actual economic outlay” directly to Merco misapplies the law. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. at 468, rejected the notion that a debt must be evidenced by a direct cash transfer: “The fact that the money was not paid directly to the corporation by the taxpayer does not destroy the debt if the funds were used for the corporation’s benefit and the taxpayer has a right to repayment.” Meruelo’s capital contribution of $4,985,035 was used to secure the deposit on the condominium complex, and the subsequent $9 million in intercompany transfers were used to fund Merco’s operations. These funds were not lost; they were invested in the corporation, and Meruelo’s interest in repayment was real and enforceable.

The Tax Court’s conclusion that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness is contrary to settled precedent. The record shows a consistent, documented, and economically meaningful relationship between Meruelo and Merco, supported by contemporaneous books, a promissory note, and years of consistent tax reporting. The debt ran directly to Meruelo, not to the intermediate entities. The judgment of the Tax Court must be reversed.

**WHEREFORE**, appellant Homero F. Meruelo respectfully requests that this Court reverse the decision of the Tax Court and remand with instructions to allow Meruelo to deduct his share of Merco’s loss up to the amount of his basis in the corporation, including the $9 million in indebtedness established through the intercompany transfers.
