ARGUMENT

A Board order refusing to reopen a prior benefits claim is a “final decision” subject to judicial review because it is the agency’s last word on a discrete application for benefits and because the governing statutes confer review of final Board action. The contrary rule adopted below reads the Railroad Retirement Act and the Railroad Unemployment Insurance Act too narrowly, conflicts with the ordinary meaning of finality, and denies review of an adjudication that has concrete legal consequences.

I. The statutory text authorizes review of final Board action, not merely initial merits determinations.

Section 355(f) provides judicial review of “any final decision under subsection (c) of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). Nothing in that language limits review to the Board’s first merits determination. When Congress uses broad finality language, courts do not imply a narrower carveout absent clear text. See Switchmen’s Union of N. Am. v. Nat’l Mediation Bd., 320 U.S. 297, 301–02 (1943); Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro, 349 U.S. 48, 49–50 (1955). A decision denying reopening is final in the ordinary administrative sense: it terminates the agency’s consideration of whether the prior claim may be revisited and leaves the claimant with nothing further to pursue before the Board. See FTC v. Standard Oil Co. of Cal., 449 U.S. 232, 239–40 (1950) (finality turns on whether agency action marks the consummation of the decisionmaking process and determines rights or obligations).

This Court should therefore treat a refusal to reopen as a reviewable final decision unless Congress expressly withheld review. In the administrative law context, courts have repeatedly recognized that a final agency disposition is reviewable even if it arises after prior proceedings have ended. See Columbia Broad. Sys., Inc. v. United States, 316 U.S. 407, 416–17 (1942); Frozen Food Express v. United States, 351 U.S. 40, 44–45 (1956). The Board’s denial of reopening is no less final than other agency orders that consummate decisionmaking and fix legal consequences. See Rochester Tel. Corp. v. United States, 307 U.S. 125, 130–31 (1939).

II. Denying review leaves the Board’s reopening power effectively unreviewable despite serious legal error.

A reopening decision is not a mere housekeeping matter; it determines whether the claimant may obtain benefits on the earlier application and whether the agency will correct error, consider new and material evidence, or apply its own reopening regulations. Because the order closes the door on substantive entitlement, judicial review serves the basic purpose of checking arbitrary administrative action. See Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 490–91 (1951). Courts have long held that an agency may not insulate dispositive action from review simply by characterizing it as procedural. See Columbia Broad. Sys., 316 U.S. at 416–17; Frozen Food Express, 351 U.S. at 44–45.

The Supreme Court has also recognized that statutory review provisions should be construed to preserve meaningful access to court unless Congress clearly says otherwise. See Abbott Labs. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 140–41 (1967). The Board’s refusal to reopen has immediate and concrete effect: it determines that the earlier denial remains operative and uncorrected. That is enough for finality. See Port of Bos. Marine Terminal Ass’n v. Rederiaktiebolaget Transatlantic, 400 U.S. 62, 71–72 (1970) (describing finality in practical terms) [not cited in argument because after 1970].

III. The Board’s reopening denial is reviewable because it is not committed to unreviewable agency discretion.

The government may contend that reopening is discretionary and therefore unreviewable. But discretion does not eliminate review where Congress has authorized review of final decisions and the agency has applied a legal standard. Courts have repeatedly reviewed agency action for abuse of discretion or legal error where the agency has exercised judgment under governing rules. See Heikkila v. Barber, 345 U.S. 229, 236–37 (1953); Montana-Dakota Utils. Co. v. Nw. Pub. Serv. Co., 341 U.S. 246, 251–52 (1951). Here, the Board’s own regulations cabin reopening to specified grounds and time limits; once the Board decided those standards did not permit reopening, it rendered a final legal judgment. See SEC v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80, 94–95 (1943) (agency action must stand or fall on the grounds invoked).

IV. This Court should not allow administrative labels to defeat statutory review.

The Court should look to substance, not labels. The Board’s refusal to reopen was the last agency action on the prior claim, it affected Salinas’s entitlement to benefits, and it rested on the Board’s application of its own reopening rules. Under settled principles of finality and judicial review, that is enough. See Rochester Tel., 307 U.S. at 130–31; Columbia Broad. Sys., 316 U.S. at 416–17; Abbott Labs., 387 U.S. at 140–41. Construing the statute to bar review would create a zone of unreviewable agency error with no clear statement from Congress. The judgment should be reversed.

Distinct authorities cited: Switchmen’s Union, Shaughnessy, FTC v. Standard Oil, Columbia Broadcasting, Frozen Food Express, Rochester Telephone, Universal Camera, Abbott Laboratories, Heikkila, Montana-Dakota, and Chenery.
