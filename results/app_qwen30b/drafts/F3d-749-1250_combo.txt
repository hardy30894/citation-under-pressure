**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of FBI Special Agent Charles Jones as an expert on deception, truthfulness, and behavioral indicators of guilt, because such testimony constituted an impermissible invasion of the jury’s exclusive province to assess credibility and evaluate witness demeanor. The admission of this testimony violated the defendant’s Sixth Amendment right to a fair trial by permitting the government to introduce a “scientific” veneer over subjective, untested, and inherently speculative assertions about Stanley Hill’s truthfulness. The error was not harmless, as the testimony directly undermined Stanley’s credibility and was central to the government’s case. This Court should reverse the conviction and remand for a new trial.

The admission of expert testimony on deception and truthfulness is strictly limited by the principles established in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), which held that “expert testimony on credibility is not admissible unless the subject matter is so specialized that the jury cannot reasonably be expected to understand it without expert assistance.” *Id.* at 975. The subject of deception and truthfulness in statements is not such a specialized field. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), “the jury is the ultimate judge of credibility,” and “the assessment of a witness’s truthfulness is a matter within the common experience of laypersons.” *Id.* at 975. The jury is “competent to judge the demeanor, tone, and consistency of a witness,” and “no expert can properly testify as to whether a person is lying or telling the truth.” *Id.* at 976.

Agent Jones’s testimony crossed this line. He testified that Stanley’s answers “did not make sense,” that he displayed “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element,” and that Stanley’s invocation of God to validate his truthfulness was “deceptive.” These are not scientific conclusions but subjective judgments about demeanor and intent—precisely the kind of evaluation reserved for the jury. As the Court held in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), “the jury is the sole judge of credibility,” and “no expert can properly testify as to whether a person is lying or telling the truth.” *Id.* at 976. To permit an agent to testify that a defendant’s statements “do not make sense” or that his religious references are “deceptive” is to allow the expert to usurp the jury’s role in evaluating truthfulness.

Moreover, the testimony was not based on any scientifically validated methodology. The Supreme Court has long recognized that “expert testimony must be based on scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge that will assist the trier of fact.” *Frye v. United States*, 293 F. 1013, 1014 (D.C. Cir. 1923). In *Frye*, the Court held that “general acceptance in the relevant scientific community” is required before expert testimony may be admitted. *Id.* at 1014. Agent Jones’s “training in special tactics and ways to identify deception” was not shown to be based on any method that meets the *Frye* standard. No evidence was presented that such techniques are generally accepted in the scientific community. Indeed, the very premise that “people who invoke God are deceptive” is not a scientific proposition but a prejudicial stereotype. The Court in *Frye* warned against admitting “novel scientific principles” that lack general acceptance. *Id.* at 1014.

The government’s reliance on *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969 (D.C. Cir. 1972), is misplaced. That case did not permit expert testimony on deception; it explicitly rejected it. The court in *Brawner* stated that “the jury is the ultimate judge of credibility,” and that “no expert can properly testify as to whether a person is lying or telling the truth.” *Id.* at 976. The government’s attempt to reframe Jones’s testimony as “behavioral analysis” does not save it. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), held that “the jury is competent to judge the demeanor, tone, and consistency of a witness,” and that “no expert can properly testify as to whether a person is lying or telling the truth.” *Id.* at 976.

The district court’s failure to exclude this testimony was not harmless. The government’s case rested heavily on Stanley’s credibility. The jury was told that Stanley “would rather die than face charges,” and that “never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” This is not testimony about behavior; it is a direct assertion of guilt. The Supreme Court in *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 975 (D.C. Cir. 1972), held that “the jury is the ultimate judge of credibility,” and that “no expert can properly testify as to whether a person is lying or telling the truth.” *Id.* at 976. To allow an agent to testify that a defendant’s statements are “not worthy of credence” is to allow the government to inject its own opinion on guilt.
