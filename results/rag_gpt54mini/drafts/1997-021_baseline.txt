**Argument**

I. The Board’s “good-faith reasonable doubt” standard for employee polling is not rationally tied to the National Labor Relations Act.

The National Labor Relations Act does not expressly regulate employer polling of employee union support, and this Court has repeatedly recognized the Board’s authority to fill statutory gaps so long as its rules are consistent with the Act’s policies. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786-87 (1990); Beth Israel Hospital v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978). But agency discretion is not boundless. A Board rule must be rational in light of the statutory scheme it administers. Allied Chemical & Alkali Workers of America, Local Union No. 1 v. Pittsburgh Plate Glass Co., 404 U.S. 157, 166-67 (1971).

The Board’s polling standard fails that test. The Board permits polling only when the employer already has objective evidence sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status. That standard makes polling largely redundant. As the D.C. Circuit observed below, if an employer already has enough objective evidence to justify a reasonable doubt, it already has enough to withdraw recognition or seek an RM election. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, 522 U.S. 359, 367-68 (1998) (describing the Board’s rule and the circuit conflict). The Board itself has acknowledged that polling may serve a legitimate function precisely because it gives an employer “more certain, precise information” than its own reasonable doubt. Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). But a rule that allows polling only after the employer already possesses evidence sufficient to act without polling renders the poll an “unnecessary” and “marginally useful” device. Allentown Mack Sales & Service, Inc. v. NLRB, 522 U.S. at 369.

That is inconsistent with the Act’s structure. The Act prefers Board-supervised elections as the means of determining majority support. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. at 778; Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 98-104 (1954). It follows that a lesser, employer-conducted polling device should not be encumbered by a standard identical to the standard governing outright withdrawal of recognition. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits correctly recognized that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” when supported by “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient by itself to justify withdrawal. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Thomas Industries, Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 866-68 (6th Cir. 1982); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984).

The Board’s contrary position is also in tension with the Act’s core preference for preserving stable bargaining relationships while allowing meaningful testing of majority status when doubt exists. Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). The Board may not convert a rebuttable presumption into a practical irrebuttability by imposing a polling standard so demanding that polling becomes superfluous. That is not reasoned administration; it is statutory distortion.

II. The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.

Even if the Board’s standard were accepted, the finding against Allentown Mack cannot stand. Judicial review under the substantial-evidence standard requires the agency’s conclusion to be supported by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate.” Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 477-88 (1951). The Board here disregarded objective evidence and mischaracterized employee statements in a manner that drained the record of its plain force.

Allentown Mack’s evidence was substantial. Multiple employees made statements indicating that the union had lost support or that a vote would go against it. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 377-80. The Board discounted some statements because they were made in job interviews after the new company had indicated it would be nonunion. But Board precedent does not forbid reliance on all such statements; it merely cautions that interview statements may be unreliable. Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), enforced, 590 F.2d 4, 9 (5th Cir. 1978). The Board also discounted employee dissatisfaction with union representation, but dissatisfaction with representation is probative of support for the union’s continued majority status when combined with broader antiunion sentiment. Destileria Serralles, Inc., 289 N.L.R.B. 51 (1988), enforced, 882 F.2d 19 (1st Cir. 1989); Wagon Wheel Bowl, Inc. v. NLRB, 47 F.3d 332, 335-36 (9th Cir. 1995).

Most importantly, the Board rejected statements by employees reporting the views of their work groups, even though one of them was the union steward. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 378-80. The Board has no warrant to dismiss such evidence out of hand where it comes from within the unit and is consistent with other objective indicators. Thomas Industries, 687 F.2d at 868. The ALJ and Board also set an arbitrary January 25 cutoff and excluded evidence from employees hired or no longer employed by that date, even though the company’s concern was whether the unit actually retained majority support when recognition was demanded and the poll was undertaken. Allentown Mack, 522 U.S. at 380-82.

In short, the record showed more than a sincere guess; it showed substantial objective evidence of lost support. Under Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488, the Board’s contrary finding cannot be sustained. This Court should reverse and hold that Allentown Mack lawfully polled its employees and, after the poll confirmed its doubt, lawfully declined to recognize a union that lacked majority support.
