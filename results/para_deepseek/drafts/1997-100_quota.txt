**ARGUMENT**

The Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969), precludes retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding when the evidence at the initial proceeding was legally insufficient. The California Supreme Court erred in holding otherwise.

**I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Applies to Noncapital Sentencing Proceedings That Function Like Trials on Guilt or Innocence**

The Double Jeopardy Clause protects against a second prosecution for the same offense after an acquittal. *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 446 (1981), this Court held that the Clause applies to a capital sentencing proceeding because the proceeding “resemble[d] a trial on the issue of guilt.” The critical inquiry is whether the sentencing proceeding “had the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 439. This analysis is not limited to capital cases. *See Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 210-11 (1984) (applying *Bullington* analysis outside the guilt phase); *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994) (noting that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases but recognizing that the question remains open for noncapital proceedings).

Here, California’s procedure for trying prior conviction allegations mirrors a criminal trial. Under California Penal Code § 1025, the defendant is entitled to a jury trial, the prosecution must prove the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993), the rules of evidence apply, *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224 (1996), and the defendant enjoys the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses, *id.* at 228 n.6. These features indisputably “resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 209-10.

**II. Insufficient Evidence at a Prior Conviction Prohibition Bars Retrial**

When a factfinder concludes that the prosecution has failed to prove an element of a prior conviction allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, that determination is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes. *See Smalis v. Pennsylvania*, 476 U.S. 140, 144 (1986) (holding that a trial court’s grant of a demurrer due to insufficient evidence constituted an acquittal). The Court of Appeal found the evidence insufficient to support the allegation that Monge personally used a deadly weapon. That finding—essentially a resolution of the facts in Monge’s favor—bars retrial under the Double Jeopardy Clause. *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 18 (1978) (holding that the Double Jeopardle Clause “forbids a second trial” after an appellate reversal based on insufficient evidence).

**III. The State Court’s Distinction Between Capital and Noncapital Cases Is Unsupported**

The California Supreme Court erred in concluding that *Bullington* and *Rumsey* are limited to capital cases. The reasoning of those cases turns on the nature of the proceeding, not the potential punishment. *Ross v. Oklahoma*, 487 U.S. 81, 90 (1988) (noting that “the purpose of the Double Jeopardy Clause is to prevent repeated attempts to convict an individual for a single offense”). Moreover, the suggestion in *Caspari*, 510 U.S. at 392, that *Bullington’s* reasoning was “based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding” was dictum made in the context of a *Teague* analysis, not a holding on the merits.

**IV. Williams v. New York Does Not Control**

The state court’s reliance on *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), is misplaced. *Williams* addressed due process, not double jeopardy. It held that a sentencing judge may consider out-of-court information. *Williams* did not address whether, if a state elects to provide a trial-like sentencing proceeding, it may retry that proceeding after an appellate finding of insufficient evidence. *Bullington* squarely answered that question: a state that provides such a proceeding must respect the double jeopardy bar. *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 445-46.

**Conclusion**

Because California’s prior conviction allegation proceeding bears all the hallmarks of a trial on guilt or innocence, the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial after an appellate finding of insufficient evidence. The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
