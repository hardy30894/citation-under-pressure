ARGUMENT

The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) has jurisdiction under the Occupational Safety and Health Act (Act) to issue citations to uninspected vessels, which are subject to minimal Coast Guard regulation. The Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation under section 4(b)(1) of the Act.

Section 4(b)(1) of the Act provides that the Act does not apply to "working conditions of employees with respect to which other Federal agencies...exercise statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health." 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). However, this provision only preempts OSHA jurisdiction when another federal agency has exercised its authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue. See Gannett Satellite Info. Network, Inc. v. Metro. Transp. Auth., 745 F.2d 767, 774 (2d Cir. 1984) (holding that section 4(b)(1) preemption requires a "direct and substantial" exercise of authority by another agency).

The Coast Guard's authority to regulate vessels, including uninspected vessels, is limited. While the Coast Guard has some authority to issue safety regulations for uninspected vessels under 46 U.S.C. § 4102, this authority is not comprehensive. The Coast Guard's regulations for uninspected vessels are limited to specific areas, such as life preservers, fire extinguishers, and ventilation systems. See 46 C.F.R. §§ 25.01 et seq; 46 C.F.R. §§ 26.01 et seq. In contrast, OSHA has broad authority to regulate workplace safety and health under the Act.

The Fifth Circuit's reliance on Clary v. Ocean Drilling and Exploration Co., 609 F.2d 1120 (5th Cir. 1980), and Donovan v. Texaco, Inc., 720 F.2d 825 (5th Cir. 1983), is misplaced. While these cases held that OSHA regulations do not apply to working conditions of seamen on vessels in navigation, they did not address the specific issue of uninspected vessels. Moreover, the broad language in these cases is dicta and not controlling.

In this case, the Coast Guard's investigation into the explosion on Mallard Bay Drilling Rig 52 did not result in the exercise of authority to regulate the specific working conditions at issue. Therefore, OSHA's jurisdiction is not preempted under section 4(b)(1) of the Act. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the Coast Guard's exclusive jurisdiction over the regulation of seamen's working conditions aboard vessels precluded OSHA's regulation.

The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the citation issued by OSHA should be upheld.
