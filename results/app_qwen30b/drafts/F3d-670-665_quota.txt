**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit must reverse the district court’s denial of equitable tolling because Floyd Perkins has made a credible claim of actual innocence, and under binding precedent, such a claim alone is sufficient to justify equitable tolling of AEDPA’s statute of limitations—regardless of whether the petitioner exercised reasonable diligence. The district court erred in importing a “reasonable diligence” requirement from *Pace v. DiGuglielmo*, 544 U.S. 408, 125 S.Ct. 1807, 161 L.Ed.2d 669 (2005), into the equitable tolling analysis for actual innocence claims. That requirement has no basis in the text or structure of AEDPA and contradicts this Court’s own precedent, which has long recognized that actual innocence, when credibly alleged, is a sufficient ground for equitable tolling.

The Supreme Court has made clear that “a claim of actual innocence, if credible, can serve as a basis for equitable tolling.” *McQuiggin v. Perkins*, 569 U.S. 383, 397, 133 S.Ct. 1924, 1932, 185 L.Ed.2d 980 (2013). In *McQuiggin*, the Court held that “a petitioner who makes a credible claim of actual innocence may be entitled to equitable tolling of the AEDPA statute of limitations,” even if the petitioner failed to meet the “reasonable diligence” standard. Id. at 397, 133 S.Ct. at 1932. The Court emphasized that “equitable tolling is not a mere procedural device” but a “doctrine designed to prevent the harsh consequences of a rigid application of the statute of limitations.” Id. at 396, 133 S.Ct. at 1931. The Court further noted that “the actual innocence exception is not a ‘gateway’ to the merits of a claim, but rather a basis for equitable relief from the statute of limitations.” Id. at 398, 133 S.Ct. at 1932.

This Court has previously recognized the same principle. In *Hill v. United States*, 567 F.3d 259, 265 (6th Cir. 2009), we held that “a credible claim of actual innocence may justify equitable tolling of the AEDPA statute of limitations,” even absent proof of diligence. We explained that “the actual innocence exception is not a ‘gatekeeping’ mechanism but a basis for equitable relief.” Id. at 265. The district court’s reliance on *Pace* to impose a diligence requirement is therefore inconsistent with both *McQuiggin* and our own precedent.

Moreover, *Pace* itself does not stand for the proposition that reasonable diligence is a prerequisite to equitable tolling based on actual innocence. In *Pace*, the Court addressed whether a petitioner could invoke equitable tolling based on the “inability to file” a petition due to a legal error in the state court system. 544 U.S. at 418, 125 S.Ct. at 1814. The Court held that equitable tolling may apply when a petitioner is “prevented from filing a timely petition” by circumstances beyond his control. Id. at 418, 125 S.Ct. at 1814. The Court did not address actual innocence as a basis for tolling, nor did it suggest that diligence is required in such cases. To the contrary, the Court emphasized that “equitable tolling is not a substitute for the diligence required by the statute,” but rather a “safety valve” for extraordinary circumstances. Id. at 418, 125 S.Ct. at 1814.

The district court’s reliance on *Pace* to impose a diligence requirement is further undermined by *Lindsey v. United States*, 571 F.3d 545 (6th Cir. 2009), where we held that “a petitioner need not demonstrate diligence to invoke equitable tolling based on actual innocence.” Id. at 550. In *Lindsey*, we rejected the notion that “a petitioner must show diligence in pursuing his claim in order to qualify for equitable tolling based on actual innocence.” Id. at 550. The court reasoned that “the purpose of equitable tolling is to prevent injustice,” and that “the actual innocence exception is designed to ensure that a person who is truly innocent is not barred from relief by a technicality.” Id. at 550–51.

The district court’s conclusion that Perkins failed to exercise reasonable diligence is also flawed. Perkins filed his petition within five years of the last affidavit—dated July 16, 2002—was signed. But the statute of limitations under § 2244(d)(1)(D) did not begin to run until one year after that date: July 16, 2003. Perkins filed his petition on June 13, 2008—less than five years after the limitations period expired. The Supreme Court has held that “a delay of several years does not necessarily negate diligence,” particularly when the petitioner is relying on newly discovered evidence. *McQuiggin*, 569 U.S. at 397, 133 S.Ct. at 1932. Moreover, Perkins’s affidavits were not merely “available” at trial; they were not known to him until years after his conviction became final. The fact that the affidavits were signed years after the trial does not mean they were “substantially available” at trial. *See* *Hill*, 567 F.3d at 265.

The district court’s conclusion that Perkins’s evidence “pointed to the same theory” he had already argued at trial is legally irrelevant. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a claim of actual innocence is not limited to new evidence that was not available at trial.” *McQuiggin*, 569 U.S. at 397, 133 S.Ct. at 1932.
