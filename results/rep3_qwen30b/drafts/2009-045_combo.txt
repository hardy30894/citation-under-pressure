**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained—must be answered in the negative. The settled law of this Court, grounded in principles of statutory construction, equitable fairness, and judicial restraint, firmly establishes that the lodestar method, as refined by the Supreme Court in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), is the exclusive and exclusive measure of a “reasonable” fee under federal fee-shifting statutes. No enhancement based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” may be awarded unless such factors are already reflected in the lodestar calculation. To permit such an enhancement would violate the foundational principle that a fee award must be grounded in objective, record-based evidence, not judicial subjectivity or personal evaluation.

The Supreme Court has long held that the lodestar—calculated by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—is the presumptively correct fee under federal fee-shifting statutes. In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 433 (1983), the Court stated: “The starting point for determining a reasonable fee is the number of hours reasonably expended on the litigation multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. at 897, where the Court declared that “the lodestar is presumed to be the reasonable fee.” The Court did not, as the dissent suggests, leave open a broad exception for “exceptional” results or “superior” performance. Rather, it explicitly rejected the notion that such factors could justify an upward adjustment *in the absence of specific evidence* that the lodestar itself failed to reflect the quality of representation. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899.

This principle finds its roots in earlier decisions that established the necessity of objective, record-based determinations in fee awards. In *United States v. Hensley*, 354 U.S. 104, 107 (1957), the Court emphasized that “the amount of a fee must be determined by the facts of the case and not by the judge’s personal opinion.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 195 (1939), the Court held that “a fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and not upon the judge’s estimate of their value.” These cases make clear that judicial discretion in fee awards is not unfettered; it must be anchored in the record and not in the judge’s personal assessment of counsel’s “excellence.”

The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on *NAACP v. City of Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332 (11th Cir. 1987) (per curiam), and *Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292 (11th Cir. 1988), to justify the enhancement in this case is misplaced. Neither case stands for the proposition that a district court may enhance a fee based on subjective evaluations of counsel’s performance or the results achieved. In *Evergreen*, the court vacated the district court’s denial of a fee enhancement because the lower court failed to address the plaintiffs’ argument that they had achieved “exceptional success.” 812 F.2d at 1336–37. The court did not hold that such an enhancement was *required* or even permissible; it merely remanded for proper consideration. In *Norman*, the court reversed because the district court applied an incorrect standard in evaluating the results. 836 F.2d at 1306. Neither case approved of enhancements based on “quality of performance” or “results obtained” as independent bases for upward adjustment.

Indeed, the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 372 (1948), is dispositive: “The fee must be based upon the actual services rendered and not upon the judge’s estimate of their value.” The Court in *Gypsum* rejected the idea that a judge could “enhance” a fee based on the “importance” of the case or the “excellence” of counsel. The same principle applies here. The district court’s reliance on its own “58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge” to assess the “stellar performance” of counsel—without any supporting evidence in the record—violates this foundational rule. *United States v. United States Fidelity & Guaranty Co.*, 309 U.S. 506, 510 (1940), held that “a judge cannot base a fee award on his own personal opinion of the value of the services rendered.” The district court’s self-justified “testimony” in its order is not evidence; it is judicial self-evaluation, which is impermissible.

Moreover, the principle that a fee must be based on objective, record-supported facts is reinforced by *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 328 U.S. 781, 791 (1946), where the Court stated: “The amount of a fee must be determined by the facts of the case and not by the judge’s personal opinion.” The Court in *Tobacco* rejected the notion that a judge could award a higher fee simply because the case was “important” or “difficult.” The same logic applies here: the difficulty of the case or the quality of counsel’s performance must be reflected in the hours worked and the hourly rate, not in a separate enhancement.

The dissent’s claim that *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* permit enhancements in “rare” and “exceptional” cases is a misreading of those decisions. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899, did not hold that enhancements are ever permissible; it held that they are not permissible *unless* there is specific evidence that the lodestar does not reflect the quality of representation. The Court in *Blum* reversed the fee award precisely because the district court had not provided such evidence. The dissent’s citation to *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), is equally flawed.
