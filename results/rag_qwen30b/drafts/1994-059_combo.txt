**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for the United States Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution’s framers deliberately established a uniform national standard for congressional eligibility, and the States may not impose additional or conflicting qualifications. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by barring individuals who have served three terms in the House or two in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, adds a qualification not found in the Constitution and thereby violates Article I, Section 2 and Section 3. This Court has long held that the qualifications for federal office are exclusive and non-delegable to the States.

The Constitution explicitly sets forth the qualifications for members of Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for seven Years, and who shall have been an Inhabitant of the State in which he is chosen.” Article I, Section 3, Clause 1 provides that a Senator must be “at least thirty Years of Age, a Citizen of the United States for nine Years, and who shall have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall represent.” These are the *only* qualifications for federal office. As this Court held in *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. 58, 65 (1900), “the office, if it be properly called an office, is created by that Constitution and by that alone.” The Constitution does not permit the States to add to or subtract from these qualifications. To allow such additions would undermine the uniformity and national character of federal representation.

The Arkansas Amendment attempts to do precisely that. By declaring that a person who has served three terms in the House is “not eligible to have his/her name placed on the ballot,” the State imposes a qualification not found in the Constitution. This is not a mere procedural rule about ballot access; it is a substantive disqualification based on past service. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that States may regulate federal elections in ways that conflict with constitutional standards. In *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. 221, 235 (1920), the Court held that “the power to amend the Constitution is not a power to be exercised by the people of a State in the manner of a legislative enactment,” and that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land.” The Court emphasized that “the people of a State cannot, by their own action, alter the provisions of the Federal Constitution.” The Arkansas Amendment, by attempting to impose a term limit on federal officeholders, seeks to alter a constitutional provision through state initiative—a power the Constitution does not grant.

The argument that the amendment merely regulates ballot access is unavailing. The Supreme Court has long recognized that a law that effectively bars a candidate from running is not a mere procedural rule but a substantive restriction on eligibility. In *Barry v. United States Ex Rel. Cunningham*, 279 U.S. 597, 605 (1929), the Court stated that “the Senate acts as a judicial tribunal” in judging the qualifications of its members, and that “the authority to judge of the qualifications of its members is a power inherent in the Senate.” This power is not delegated to the States. The Court further held that “the effect of the refusal of the Senate to seat Vare pending investigation was to deprive the state of its equal representation in the Senate” only if the Senate’s action were arbitrary or capricious. But the point remains: the Senate alone has the authority to determine who qualifies. The States may not preempt that authority.

The Arkansas Amendment also violates the principle of uniformity in federal elections. The Constitution requires that “the electors in each State shall have the qualifications requisite for electors of the most numerous branch of the State legislature.” *Wiley v. Sinkler*, 179 U.S. at 65. This provision ensures that federal elections are conducted under a uniform standard. If each State could impose its own term limits, the result would be a patchwork of qualifications—some States allowing three terms, others two, others none. Such a system would “engender a hodge-podge of qualifications,” as the Arkansas Supreme Court observed, and would “undermine the national character of Congress.” The Court has consistently rejected such fragmentation. In *Newberry v. United States*, 256 U.S. 232, 257 (1921), the Court stated that “the power to regulate elections for the Senate is a power of the United States, not of the States.” The Court emphasized that “the people of the United States, not the people of the several States, are the ultimate source of authority in the election of Senators.” The States may not, therefore, impose additional qualifications.

The argument that the amendment is severable from the state term limits is also flawed. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly recognized that the amendment was presented as an “all or nothing” package. The ballot title referred to “elected officials” without distinction, and the preamble spoke of “elected officials who remain in office too long.” The voters were not given a choice between state and federal term limits. As the Court held in *Hawke v. Smith (No. 1)*, 253 U.S. at 235, “the people of a State cannot, by their own action, alter the provisions of the Federal Constitution.” The attempt to couple a valid state initiative with an unconstitutional federal restriction is not a proper use of a severability clause. The Court has long rejected such “ballot packaging” that masks constitutional defects. In *Brockington v. Rhodes*, 396 U.S. 41, 42 (1969), the Court held that “a State may not impose a requirement that is so burdensome as to effectively deny a candidate access to the ballot.” The Arkansas amendment does precisely that by barring candidates based on past service, a qualification not found in the Constitution.

Finally, the argument that the amendment is a valid exercise of state power under the initiative process fails. The initiative power is not unlimited. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that the amendment violated the U.S. Constitution, and that the Constitution is supreme. As the Court stated in *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 480 (1917), “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land.” No state action may contravene it.
