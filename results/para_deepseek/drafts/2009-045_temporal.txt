**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes, but only in the rare and exceptional case where specific evidence in the record demonstrates that the lodestar—hours reasonably expended multiplied by a reasonable hourly rate—does not adequately compensate counsel for superior performance and exceptional success. This Court’s precedents, including *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), and *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), establish that such enhancements are permissible, though not routine.

**I. The Lodestar Presumption Does Not Foreclose Enhancements for Superior Performance and Exceptional Results**

In *Hensley*, this Court held that the lodestar is the “starting point” for determining a reasonable fee, but it did not erect an absolute bar against upward adjustments. 461 U.S. at 433. The Court expressly recognized that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* at 435. This language is not dicta; it is a deliberate acknowledgment that the lodestar, while presumptively reasonable, may undercompensate counsel in extraordinary circumstances.

*Blum* reaffirmed this principle. The Court rejected the argument that “an upward adjustment to an attorney’s fee is never appropriate under § 1988.” 465 U.S. at 901. Instead, the Court held that an enhancement may be justified in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was exceptional.” *Id.* at 899. The Court’s reversal of the enhancement in *Blum* was based on the absence of such evidence, not on a categorical prohibition. *Id.* at 900-01.

**II. The District Court’s Enhancement Was Supported by Specific Evidence and Detailed Findings**

The district court in this case did not rely on vague or conclusory assertions. It made detailed findings, based on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 454 F. Supp. 2d 1260, 1286 (N.D. Ga. 2006). The court noted that counsel’s representation “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee.” *Id.* at 1290. These findings are precisely the kind of “specific evidence” that *Blum* requires. 465 U.S. at 899.

The district court’s reliance on its own observations is not a due process violation. A trial judge is uniquely positioned to evaluate the quality of advocacy, and this Court has long recognized that “the district court has discretion in determining the amount of a fee award.” *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437. The judge’s findings here were not ex parte testimony; they were reasoned explanations for the enhancement, grounded in the record and the court’s professional judgment.

**III. The Eleventh Circuit’s Decision Is Consistent with This Court’s Precedents**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the district court did not abuse its discretion. The court of appeals recognized that *Blum* and *Hensley* permit enhancements in “rare” and “exceptional” cases, and it found that this case met that standard. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008). The court also noted that every other circuit to consider the issue agrees that such enhancements are permissible. *Id.* at 1242-43 (collecting cases).

The dissent’s argument that the enhancement is unreviewable or violates due process is without merit. The district court’s findings are part of the record and subject to appellate review for abuse of discretion. The court did not “testify”; it explained its reasoning. And the defendants had ample opportunity to challenge the enhancement on appeal.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The district court properly exercised its discretion to enhance the lodestar based on specific evidence of superior performance and exceptional results, consistent with this Court’s precedents in *Hensley* and *Blum*.
