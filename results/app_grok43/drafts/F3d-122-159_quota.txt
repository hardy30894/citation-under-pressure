The BOP’s denial of early release rests on an impermissible expansion of the term “crime of violence.” Section 3621(e)(2)(B) authorizes reduction only for prisoners convicted of a “nonviolent offense.” The governing regulation, 28 C.F.R. § 550.58, incorporates the definition in 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which requires that the offense either have as an element the use of force or, “by its nature, involve[] a substantial risk that physical force … may be used in the course of committing the offense.”  

Under the categorical approach mandated by Taylor v. United States, 495 U.S. 575, 600 (1990), a court must “look only to the statutory definitions of the prior offense, and not to the particular facts underlying those convictions.” Conspiracy to distribute a controlled substance, 21 U.S.C. § 846, contains no force element and does not, by its nature, present the requisite risk. The sentencing enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 therefore cannot convert the offense into a crime of violence. See Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36, 42 (1993) (guidelines commentary cannot alter the statutory elements of an offense).  

The BOP Program Statement 5162.02, which treats the enhancement as dispositive, is inconsistent with both the statute and the regulation. When an agency interpretation conflicts with the plain language of a regulation that itself tracks a statute, it receives no deference. Martin v. Occupational Safety & Health Review Comm’n, 499 U.S. 144, 152 (1991); see also Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837, 842–43 (1984) (interpretation must be consistent with congressional intent). Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944), further instructs that an agency’s position is entitled only to the weight its reasoning merits; here the Program Statement substitutes sentencing facts for the offense’s statutory nature, contrary to the categorical command of § 924(c)(3).  

Babbitt v. Sweet Home Chapter of Communities for a Great Oregon, 515 U.S. 687, 704 (1995), confirms that an agency may not “alter the meaning of a statute” through interpretive rules. The Third Circuit has likewise rejected attempts to redefine offenses by reference to enhancements rather than elements. United States v. McQuilkin, 97 F.3d 723, 727 (3d Cir. 1996).  

Because Roussos’s conviction is not a crime of violence under the statute and regulation, the BOP lacked authority to deny eligibility. The district court’s order should be reversed and the writ granted.
