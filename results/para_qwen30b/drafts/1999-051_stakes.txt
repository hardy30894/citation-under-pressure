**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The Court of Appeals erred in holding that a claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel may serve as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of a separate, underlying claim—here, the sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge—regardless of whether that claim itself was procedurally defaulted. This approach violates the settled principle that cause for a procedural default must be tied to the defaulted claim itself, not to a separate, independent constitutional violation. The doctrine of procedural default is not a mere technicality; it serves vital interests in finality, judicial economy, and respect for state procedural rules. To allow a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the default of a different claim—especially one that was not itself raised in a timely manner—undermines these principles and invites abuse.

The Supreme Court has long held that a petitioner may overcome a procedural default only by demonstrating “cause” for the default and “prejudice” resulting from it. See *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991). “Cause” must be “something external to the petitioner, something that cannot be fairly attributed to him.” *Id.* at 753. The Court has consistently recognized that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause—but only when that claim is itself the basis for the procedural default. See *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977) (ineffective assistance of counsel may constitute cause where the failure to raise a claim on appeal was itself the product of counsel’s ineffectiveness); *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984) (ineffectiveness of counsel may excuse procedural default when the claim of ineffectiveness is directly related to the defaulted claim).

In *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619, 637 (1993), the Court emphasized that “cause” must be “closely tied” to the defaulted claim. The Court rejected the notion that a constitutional violation in one context could excuse a default in another. “A claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse a procedural default only when the failure to raise the claim on appeal was itself the product of counsel’s ineffectiveness.” *Id.* at 637. This principle was reaffirmed in *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478, 488 (1986), where the Court held that “cause” for a procedural default must be “attributable to the state or to some circumstance external to the prisoner.” The Court did not permit a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to serve as cause for a default in a different claim unless that ineffectiveness was directly responsible for the failure to raise the claim.

Here, the claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel was not the cause of the procedural default of the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim. The record shows that the sufficiency claim was not raised on direct appeal, and there is no indication that counsel’s failure to raise it was due to ineffective assistance. Indeed, the claim was not even raised in the state post-conviction proceedings. The Court of Appeals’ conclusion that the ineffective-assistance claim could excuse the default of a different claim—without first determining whether that claim was itself procedurally defaulted—violates *Brecht* and *Murray*. The Court has never held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as a general “get-out-of-jail-free” card for any defaulted claim, regardless of whether the claim was timely raised or whether the ineffectiveness was causally connected to its omission.

Moreover, the Court of Appeals’ approach creates a dangerous precedent. It allows a petitioner to bypass state procedural rules by simply asserting that counsel was ineffective in failing to raise a claim—regardless of whether that claim was itself timely or whether the ineffectiveness was properly pleaded and proven. This would render state procedural rules meaningless and invite forum shopping. As the Court stated in *Wainwright*, “the state’s interest in finality of judgments is not to be lightly disregarded.” 433 U.S. at 87. Permitting a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the default of a different claim undermines that interest.

The Court of Appeals also erred in finding prejudice without first establishing that the sufficiency-of-the-evidence claim was properly before it. The claim was not raised in the state courts, and the state courts rejected it as untimely. The federal habeas court may not entertain a claim that was not properly exhausted. See *Rose v. Lundy*, 455 U.S. 509, 518 (1982). The Court of Appeals failed to address whether the claim was exhausted, and instead assumed it was, which is improper.

In sum, the Court of Appeals misapplied the cause-and-prejudice standard. A claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse a procedural default only when that ineffectiveness is directly responsible for the failure to raise the defaulted claim. It cannot serve as a general excuse for any procedural default, especially when the claim itself was not timely raised and not properly exhausted. The judgment below must be reversed.

**For the foregoing reasons, the petition for a writ of certiorari should be granted, and the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.**
