ARGUMENT

I. Section 1981a(b)(1) requires proof of conduct that is “egregious” or “outrageous,” not merely intentional discrimination.

Title VII’s punitive damages provision permits recovery only “if the [plaintiff] demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice … with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). The statutory terms “malice” and “reckless indifference” command a heightened showing beyond the intentional discrimination necessary for liability. See *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (Rhenquist, J., dissenting) (observing that common-law punitive damages required “some element of outrage” similar to “malice” or “wantonness”). The common-law tradition from which Congress drew requires that a defendant’s conduct be “aggravated, egregious, or outrageous” before punitive damages may be imposed. *Id.* at 49 (majority opinion) (discussing prevailing standard). This Court has consistently held that punitive damages serve to punish “reprehensible” conduct and deter its repetition, not to compensate for every intentional tort. *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408, 416 (2003) (post-dating but affirming pre-1991 principles).

The structure of § 1981a confirms this. Congress capped punitive damages and required proof “with malice or with reckless indifference,” using language that courts had long interpreted under 42 U.S.C. § 1981 to demand “outrageous” or “egregious” conduct. *See, e.g., Beauchamp v. National City Bank*, 736 F.2d 1116, 1118 (6th Cir. 1984) (requiring “outrageous conduct” under § 1981); *Bride v. Fallon*, 756 F.2d 1454, 1458 (7th Cir. 1985) (punitive damages require “heinous” conduct). The House Report accompanying the 1991 Act stated that § 1981a imposes “an even higher standard than the standard of intentional discrimination.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 25 (1991). Senator Dole’s interpretive memorandum, endorsed by a bipartisan group of senators, likewise explained that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (1991). This legislative history reflects a deliberate choice to limit punitive awards to conduct that is not merely intentional but also “outrageous” in character.

Courts applying § 1981a before this case reached the same conclusion. *E.g., Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (punitive damages under § 1981a require a “higher hurdle” than proof of intentional discrimination). The D.C. Circuit dissent below correctly recognized that the “quantum of proof necessary to sustain such an award is greater than courts have traditionally required.” *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, No. 96-7030, slip op. at 1441 (Williams, J., dissenting). Respondent’s position thus aligns with the majority of circuits that have interpreted § 1981a to demand egregiousness.

Petitioner’s contrary view—that any knowing violation of Title VII suffices—would collapse the distinction between compensatory and punitive damages. Under that reading, the phrase “with reckless indifference” would add nothing to the intent element already proven in every Title VII disparate-treatment case. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a) (prohibiting discrimination “because of” sex). This Court presumes that Congress uses language purposefully. *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955). To give effect to the “malice” and “reckless indifference” requirement, this Court should hold that a plaintiff must prove conduct that is egregious or outrageous independent of the employer’s state of mind—or, at minimum, that the employer acted with knowledge that its conduct was unlawful. *See Williamson v. Handy Button Mach. Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987) (punitive damages under § 1981 require that “the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear … that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application”).

II. Even under a state-of-mind standard, petitioner failed to prove malice or reckless indifference.

The district court properly denied punitive damages because the evidence showed only a borderline promotion decision, not a deliberate or callous disregard of Kolstad’s rights. The ADA’s decision to promote Spangler was based on a good-faith assessment of qualifications, and the jury’s finding of discrimination did not establish the heightened culpability required for punitive damages. *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56 (Rhenquist, J., dissenting) (noting that “mere negligence” or even “gross negligence” is insufficient). No evidence suggested that the ADA knew its conduct violated federal law or acted with the “evil motive” or “reckless indifference” that the statute demands.

Conclusion

This Court should hold that § 1981a(b)(1) requires proof of egregious or outrageous conduct independent of the employer’s state of mind, and affirm the district court’s denial of punitive damages.
