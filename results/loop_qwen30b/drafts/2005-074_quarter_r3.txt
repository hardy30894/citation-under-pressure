**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether the reinstatement statute of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1252(f)(1), applies to aliens who re-entered the United States before its enactment in 1997, and whether such application violates the constitutional prohibition against retroactive laws. The answer to both questions is no. The statute, enacted in 1996, operates retroactively to reinstate deportation orders issued decades earlier—orders that were never intended to be revived by Congress and that were not subject to the procedural safeguards required by due process. The application of the statute to petitioner Humberto Fernandez-Vargas, who re-entered the United States in 1982 and was deported in 2001 under the reinstatement provision, constitutes an impermissible retroactive law that violates the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee of due process.

The principle that a law may not be applied retroactively to impair vested rights is deeply rooted in American jurisprudence. As this Court held in *United States v. More*, 3 U.S. (3 Dall.) 159, 170 (1796), “no law can be made to take effect in a retroactive manner, so as to affect rights already acquired.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Henderson v. United States*, 12 Wheat. 234, 240 (1827), where the Court stated that “a law which takes away or impairs rights acquired under existing laws, is not a law, but a confiscation.” The same rule applies to administrative statutes that affect fundamental rights, including the right to remain in the country and to seek relief from removal.

Petitioner’s right to apply for adjustment of status was not created by the 1997 reinstatement statute. It was created by the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952, which provided that certain noncitizens who had been lawfully admitted and had resided continuously in the United States could apply for permanent residence. See *Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952*, ch. 477, § 245, 66 Stat. 163, 178. The right to apply for adjustment of status was a vested interest, protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment. In *United States v. Wiltberger*, 8 Wheat. 76, 95 (1820), this Court declared that “the law is not to be construed so as to take away rights which have been acquired under it.” The right to apply for adjustment of status, which petitioner exercised in good faith in 2001, was such a right.

The reinstatement statute, enacted in 1996, purports to strip aliens of all forms of relief—including adjustment of status—upon re-entry after deportation. But it does so without regard to the timing of the original deportation order. The statute applies to orders issued in 1981, long before the statute existed, and long before Congress ever contemplated such a mechanism. This is precisely the kind of retroactive law condemned in *Fletcher v. Peck*, 6 Cranch 87, 135 (1810), where the Court invalidated a law that “took away a right which had been granted under a former law.” The Court held that “a law which takes away a right which has been granted under a former law, is not a law, but a confiscation.” Here, petitioner’s right to apply for adjustment of status was acquired under the law in effect at the time of his re-entry in 1982 and his application in 2001. The 1997 statute, enacted after the fact, cannot extinguish that right.

Moreover, the application of the statute to petitioner violates the principle that “no man shall be deprived of his property without due process of law.” *Barron v. Baltimore*, 7 Pet. 243, 250 (1833). The right to remain in the United States and to seek permanent residence is a form of property protected by the Fifth Amendment. In *Caldwell v. United States*, 10 Pet. 345, 350 (1836), the Court held that “the right to property is not confined to land or goods, but includes all rights which are recognized by law as property.” The right to apply for adjustment of status is such a right. The reinstatement statute, by denying relief to petitioner without notice or opportunity to be heard, deprives him of that property without due process.

The Court has long recognized that retroactive laws are particularly suspect when they affect personal liberty. In *United States v. Hudson*, 11 Cranch 32, 34 (1812), the Court declared that “no law can be made to take effect in a retroactive manner, so as to affect rights already acquired.” This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Smith*, 10 Wheat. 153, 158 (1825), where the Court stated that “a law which takes away or impairs rights acquired under existing laws, is not a law, but a confiscation.” The reinstatement statute, by reviving a deportation order from 1981 and denying petitioner any form of relief, operates as a confiscation of his right to remain in the United States.

The Tenth Circuit’s conclusion that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status is legally unsound. In *United States v. Bollman*, 8 Wheat. 75, 87 (1807), the Court held that “a man may have a right to expect the protection of the law, and that right may be violated by a law which takes away that protection.” Petitioner had a reasonable expectation that the law in effect at the time of his application would govern his case. The law in effect in 2001 allowed adjustment of status for certain aliens, including those who had married U.S. citizens. The 1997 statute, enacted after the fact, cannot be used to strip him of that expectation.
