**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a plaintiff must demonstrate that an employer’s conduct was “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of the employer’s state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, as amended by the Civil Rights Act of 1991. The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), its legislative history, and the common-law tradition all require that the defendant’s conduct rise to a level of egregiousness beyond mere intentional discrimination. The District of Columbia Circuit’s en banc decision correctly recognized this threshold, and this Court should affirm the judgment below.

**I. The Statutory Text Requires a Showing of Malice or Reckless Indifference, Which Is a Higher Standard Than Mere Intentional Discrimination.**

Section 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages only if the plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice . . . with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” The terms “malice” and “reckless indifference” impose a state-of-mind requirement that is qualitatively different from the intent necessary to establish a Title VII violation. Every intentional discrimination does not reflect “malice” or “reckless indifference.” *See Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (Rehnquist, J., dissenting) (noting that punitive damages under § 1983 require “conduct that is outrageous, or motivated by an evil motive, or accompanied by a reckless indifference to the rights of others”). The word “malice” denotes “an evil motive or intent,” *id.* at 55 (majority opinion), while “reckless indifference” requires that the defendant act “in the face of a perceived risk that its actions will violate federal law,” *id.* at 37. A defendant who merely intends to discriminate—without a culpable state of mind regarding the unlawfulness of that conduct—does not satisfy this standard.

The lower courts have consistently held that punitive damages under § 1981a demand a higher threshold than simple proof of intentional discrimination. *Emmel v. Coca-Cola Bottling Co.*, 95 F.3d 627, 636 (7th Cir. 1996) (punitive damages under § 1981a require a “higher hurdle” than mere intentional discrimination); *Williamson v. Handy Button Machine Co.*, 817 F.2d 1290, 1296 (7th Cir. 1987) (under § 1981, punitive damages require that “the application of the law to the facts at hand was so clear at the time of the act that reasonably competent people would have agreed on its application”). The text of § 1981a(b)(1) codifies this common-law principle. To hold otherwise would collapse the distinction between compensatory and punitive damages, rendering the “malice or reckless indifference” requirement surplusage.

**II. The Legislative History Confirms That Congress Intended Punitive Damages Only for Egregious Cases.**

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991 underscores that Congress intended punitive damages to be reserved for “extraordinarily egregious cases.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interpretive Memorandum of Sen. Dole et al.). Although the House Report on the unadopted version of the bill stated that punitive damages should be available “to the same extent and under the same standards” as under 42 U.S.C. § 1981, 137 Cong. Rec. H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991) (Interpretive Memorandum of Rep. Edwards), this statement is indeterminate. Many circuits applying § 1981 have required egregious conduct. *See, e.g., Williamson*, 817 F.2d at 1296. The Dole memorandum, which reflects the views of the bipartisan sponsors of the final compromise, is the more reliable guide to Congress’s intent. Congress sought to “deter unlawful harassment and intentional discrimination,” 42 U.S.C. § 1981a note, but it did not intend to impose punitive liability for every garden-variety discrimination claim. The “egregious” standard gives meaning to the statutory requirement of malice or reckless indifference.

**III. The Common Law Supports Requiring Egregious Conduct.**

At common law, punitive damages are not available for every intentional tort. They are reserved for “conduct that is outrageous, because of the defendant’s evil motive or his reckless indifference to the rights of others.” *Restatement (Second) of Torts* § 908(2) (1979). This Court has applied a similar standard in the context of 42 U.S.C. § 1983, holding that punitive damages may be awarded only when the defendant’s conduct is “motivated by evil motive or intent, or when it involves reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others.” *Smith*, 461 U.S. at 56. There is no reason to read § 1981a differently. The “egregious” or “outrageous” nature of the conduct is an independent element that ensures punitive damages serve their traditional purposes of punishment and deterrence, not compensation.

**IV. The Record Below Fails to Demonstrate Egregious Conduct.**

Kolstad’s evidence—that the American Dental Association preselected a male candidate and gave pretextual reasons for its decision—shows intentional discrimination, which the jury found. But nothing in the record suggests that the ADA acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to Kolstad’s rights. The District Court properly found insufficient evidence of egregious conduct. The D.C. Circuit en banc correctly vacated the panel’s remand for a punitive damages trial, and this Court should affirm.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
