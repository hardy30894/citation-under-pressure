# ARGUMENT

## I. The Act Is a Valid, Content-Neutral Time-Place-Manner Regulation of the Use of Public Ways Outside Health-Care Facilities.

This Court has long recognized that public streets and sidewalks, though quintessential public forums, remain subject to reasonable regulation. *Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization*, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939) (streets and parks "have immemorially been held in trust for the use of the public" but government retains authority "to regulate the use... in the interest of all"). The right to speak on a public way "is not absolute at all times and under all circumstances." *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 304 (1940). A State may impose reasonable, non-discriminatory restrictions on the time, place, and manner of communicative activity so long as those restrictions serve a substantial governmental interest unrelated to the suppression of any message and leave open ample alternatives for communication. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 574 (1941) (upholding licensing scheme for parades because "civil liberties... imply the existence of an organized society maintaining public order").

The Act satisfies this framework. It does not single out any viewpoint or subject matter; it applies uniformly to all persons who would linger within thirty-five feet of an entrance, exit, or driveway of a reproductive health-care facility, regardless of what they wish to say. Where, as here, "speech" and "nonspeech" elements are combined in the same course of conduct—occupying a narrow strip of sidewalk that also serves as the only access route to a medical facility—"a sufficiently important governmental interest in regulating the nonspeech element can justify incidental limitations on First Amendment freedoms," provided the regulation furthers that interest and is no broader than necessary. *United States v. O'Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 376-77 (1968). The Commonwealth's interest in ensuring that patients and staff can safely and freely reach the doors of a medical clinic—an interest wholly independent of the content of any protester's message—readily meets that standard.

This Court has repeatedly sustained legislation protecting unobstructed ingress and egress to public and private buildings against First Amendment challenge. In *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611 (1968), the Court upheld a statute prohibiting picketing that "obstructs or unreasonably interferes with free ingress or egress" to a courthouse, holding that such a law was "not vague" and did not offend the First Amendment because it was directed at conduct, not expression. *Id.* at 616-17. Likewise, in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), the Court held that the State, "no less than a private owner of property, has power to preserve the property under its control for the use to which it is lawfully dedicated," and could exclude demonstrators from jailhouse grounds to preserve access and order. *Id.* at 47. The thirty-five-foot buffer here is a considerably lighter touch than the exclusions approved in those cases, and it serves the same core purpose: keeping open the path by which citizens reach a building's doors.

## II. The Act Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels for Petitioners' Speech.

A time-place-manner regulation is not rendered invalid merely because it makes a speaker's chosen method of communication less convenient. In *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), the Court upheld an ordinance banning sound trucks emitting "loud and raucous" noise on public streets, explaining that the First Amendment does not guarantee a speaker the right to any particular means of amplification or the precise location he prefers, so long as other avenues remain. *Id.* at 87-89. So too here: petitioners remain free to picket, chant, pray, sing, display signs, and distribute literature immediately outside the buffer zone; they may speak in a normal voice, use amplification where reasonable, and approach the many patients who must themselves cross the unregulated sidewalk before reaching the buffer. That some patients decline to engage does not establish inadequacy of the channels available; it reflects only the predictable reluctance of a private citizen to converse with a stranger on a sensitive medical matter. See *Breard v. Alexandria*, 341 U.S. 622, 644-45 (1951) (a regulation of the manner of solicitation is valid even though it reduces a speaker's persuasive effectiveness, because the State may act to protect citizens' "peace, comfort, or repose" while leaving other means of communication open).

Nor is it constitutionally significant that patients frequently arrive by automobile and walk only a short, unobstructed path from a private lot to the door, as at the Worcester and Springfield facilities. The Act does not regulate speech on private property at all; it regulates only the public way, consistent with the settled principle that "the public trust... to promote the free flow of traffic on public streets" may be protected through reasonable, viewpoint-neutral regulation. *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395, 405 (1953); *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 554-55 (1965) (State may protect citizens from "obstructing the streets and sidewalks").

## III. Whatever the Continuing Vitality of More Recent Precedent, the Act Is Independently Sustained by This Court's Longstanding First Amendment Jurisprudence.

Petitioners' challenge does not require reliance on any single, isolated precedent. The result the First Circuit reached follows directly from decades of settled doctrine—*Hague*, *Cantwell*, *Cox v. New Hampshire*, *Kovacs*, *Poulos*, *Adderley*, and *Cameron*—establishing that content-neutral regulations protecting access to buildings and preserving order on public ways are constitutional so long as ample alternatives for expression remain. Because the Act satisfies that enduring, multi-decade framework on its own terms, the judgment below should be affirmed regardless of how any single later decision is characterized.
