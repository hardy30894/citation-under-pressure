**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Government Bears the Burden of Proving Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That the Defendant Was a Member of the Conspiracy During the Limitations Period.**

A conspiracy is a continuing offense. *See* *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). Once formed, a conspiracy is presumed to continue until its objectives are accomplished or abandoned. *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). However, a defendant may withdraw from a conspiracy, thereby terminating his criminal liability for future acts. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 464-65 (1978). Withdrawal requires affirmative action—either a confession to authorities or a communication of abandonment to co-conspirators. *Id.* at 464.

When a defendant presents evidence that he withdrew from a conspiracy before the statute of limitations period, the burden shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that he remained a member during the relevant period. This principle flows from the fundamental requirement that the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Membership in the conspiracy during the limitations period is an essential element of the crime. *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 396 (1957). If the defendant’s withdrawal predates the limitations period, the government cannot satisfy its burden.

The government’s burden is not merely one of production; it is one of persuasion. Once the defendant introduces sufficient evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw. *United States v. Read*, 658 F.2d 1225, 1236 (7th Cir. 1981). This allocation respects the presumption of innocence and the government’s obligation to prove guilt. *See* *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525-26 (1958).

**II. The Lower Court Erred by Placing the Burden of Persuasion on the Defendants.**

The court below held that the defendants bore the burden of proving withdrawal by a preponderance of the evidence. This holding conflicts with settled law. In *United States v. Loya*, 807 F.2d 1483, 1490 (9th Cir. 1987), the court stated that “once a defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant did not withdraw.” Similarly, in *United States v. Steele*, 685 F.2d 793, 803-04 (3d Cir. 1982), the court held that the government must disprove withdrawal beyond a reasonable doubt.

The lower court’s error is not harmless. The evidence showed that the conspiracy’s leaders, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split up before the limitations period. This division effectively ended the conspiracy. *See* *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 443 (1949) (conspiracy ends when its objectives are abandoned). The defendants presented evidence that they were not involved in any continuing agreement after the split. The government failed to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants remained members of a single conspiracy during the limitations period.

**III. The Government’s Failure to Prove Membership During the Limitations Period Requires Reversal.**

The statute of limitations is a substantive defense, not a procedural bar. *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970). The government must prove that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *Grunewald*, 353 U.S. at 396. Here, the government’s evidence of a single, ongoing conspiracy was insufficient. The leaders’ split before the limitations period created a break in the conspiracy. The jury could not reasonably infer that the defendants remained members after that point.

The lower court’s erroneous burden allocation infected the entire trial. The jury was instructed that the defendants had to prove withdrawal by a preponderance of the evidence. This instruction relieved the government of its constitutional burden. *See* *Sandstrom v. Montana*, 442 U.S. 510, 524 (1979) (shifting burden to defendant violates due process). The convictions must be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants were members of the conspiracy during the limitations period.
