## ARGUMENT

A federal court of appeals conducting plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) must ask whether the unpreserved error affected the defendant’s "substantial rights" and whether it seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. That inquiry has never been confined to the four corners of the evidence actually placed before the jury. This Court's precedents make plain that reviewing courts routinely look to the whole record—including matters such as a defendant's own undisputed criminal history—to determine whether an error was harmless or prejudicial. Petitioner's proposed rule, which would blind appellate courts to readily available and uncontested facts, finds no support in this Court's decisions and should be rejected.

**A. Reviewing courts have always consulted the full record, including prior-conviction evidence, to assess the legal significance of an error.**

This Court has long recognized that the record of a defendant's prior convictions is a proper and necessary object of judicial notice in assessing criminal proceedings, even when that record was not the subject offered to the jury for the substantive charge at issue. In *In re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95 (1895), the prosecution proved a defendant's status as a second offender by "offering in evidence the record of his previous conviction," id. at 95, precisely the kind of extrinsic documentary proof of status that Greer contends may never be considered. Similarly, in *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967), this Court examined "a certified copy of the indictment in the prior Texas case," id. at 109, and the surrounding proceedings concerning that conviction, in order to determine the legal consequence of the error below—demonstrating that appellate scrutiny of a defendant's documented criminal history is a standard and unremarkable feature of criminal adjudication, not an impermissible excursion beyond the record.

The same principle undergirds *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), where the propriety of a sentence enhanced by the defendant's "prior criminal record," id. at 731 (Rutledge, J., dissenting), depended on the accuracy of that record as reflected outside the four corners of the trial evidence. If a court may examine an uncontested record of prior convictions to determine whether a *sentence* was lawfully imposed, there is no principled reason it may not examine that same uncontested record to determine whether an *instructional* error affected a defendant's substantial rights.

**B. Plain-error review is a practical, record-based inquiry, not a formalistic exercise confined to jury-presented evidence.**

This Court has consistently rejected efforts to elevate technical formalities over the substantive question whether a defendant was actually prejudiced. In *Garland v. Washington*, 232 U.S. 642 (1914), the Court held that a purely formal objection—one "not included in the general language in which the objection to the introduction of evidence was interposed before trial," id. at 642, and raised only to manufacture a new trial—could not defeat a conviction where the underlying requirements of due process had been "fully met by the proceedings had against the accused in the trial court." Id. Petitioner's rule would work the opposite result: it would grant a windfall reversal for an instructional defect that had no realistic effect on the outcome, based solely on the happenstance that the government did not formally re-introduce facts that no one disputes.

*Murphy v. Massachusetts*, 177 U.S. 155 (1900), likewise confirms that appellate correction of error need not unsettle proceedings that "stood unimpugned," id. at 156, when the record independently establishes that the defect was immaterial to the result. And in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), this Court's due process analysis proceeded by canvassing the entire course of proceedings—including the defendant's conduct "some two months after" his plea and his failure ever to seek withdrawal, id. at 238—rather than restricting itself to the moment of error in isolation. The lesson is the same: whether an error requires reversal depends on the totality of what the record shows, not a truncated snapshot of the evidence the jury happened to hear.

**C. Where the record does not permit confident resolution, this Court has directed remand for full consideration—not automatic reversal.**

Even where this Court has been unable to resolve on the existing record whether an evidentiary matter affected a conviction, its response has been to remand for full record development, not to presume prejudice. In *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969), the Court explained that resolving whether an error affected a conviction would require the court to "explore" the relevant law "and then examine the record in detail," and that such a determination was appropriately left to full consideration below. Id. at 784. That approach—consult the complete record, including matters not limited to the evidence the jury saw—is the antithesis of the artificial constraint Greer urges. Consistent with this practice, *Eskridge v. Washington State Board of Prison Terms & Paroles*, 357 U.S. 214 (1958), underscores the importance of a complete record, including a full transcript of proceedings, precisely so that reviewing courts can meaningfully assess whether "substantial errors" affected the defendant's rights. Id. at 214.

**D. Conclusion.**

Nothing in this Court's precedents supports blinding appellate courts to a defendant's own undisputed felony record when assessing whether an instructional error affected substantial rights. The Eleventh Circuit properly considered the entire record—including Greer's own prior felony convictions—in concluding that any *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness of his trial. That judgment should be affirmed.
