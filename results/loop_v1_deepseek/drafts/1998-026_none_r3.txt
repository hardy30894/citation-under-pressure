**ARGUMENT**

The district court's failure to advise petitioner of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not warrant habeas relief. The error was harmless, and petitioner suffered no prejudice because he knew of his right and voluntarily declined to appeal.

**I. The Rule 32 Violation Was Harmless Because Petitioner Already Knew of His Right**

Rule 32's advice requirement is a procedural safeguard, not a constitutional command. Its sole purpose is to ensure that defendants learn of their appeal right. When a defendant already knows of that right, the rule's purpose is served, and any omission is harmless.

In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946), this Court established that nonconstitutional errors are harmless unless they "affect the substantial rights of the parties." A defendant's substantial rights are not affected when he already possesses the information the rule was designed to convey. Here, the district court found that petitioner knew of his appeal right at the time of sentencing. That factual finding is supported by the record and is not clearly erroneous.

Even constitutional errors may be harmless. *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 22 (1967). The harmless-error standard applies with even greater force to a purely procedural omission like the one here. Because petitioner's actual knowledge satisfied the rule's objective, the district court's failure to recite the prescribed advice could not have affected his decision not to appeal. The error was therefore harmless.

**II. Habeas Relief Requires a Showing of Prejudice That Petitioner Cannot Make**

Section 2255 relief is available only for "fundamental" defects. *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502, 512 (1954). A prisoner must demonstrate that the alleged error "had a substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the outcome." *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962). A district court's failure to utter procedural words, when the defendant already knows the information those words would convey, is not fundamental.

Petitioner cannot show prejudice. He knew of his right to appeal, understood it, and chose not to appeal. That choice was a voluntary waiver of a known right. *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver is "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right"). Because no deprivation occurred, habeas relief is unavailable. *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 439 (1963) (habeas requires that petitioner "has been deprived of a right guaranteed by the Constitution or laws of the United States").

**III. The Rule's Purpose Was Satisfied, and Petitioner Forfeited His Claim**

Rule 32 is a housekeeping rule, "designed to facilitate the orderly administration of justice," not to create technical traps for unwary courts. *United States v. Hark*, 320 U.S. 531, 536 (1944). When a defendant already possesses the information the rule requires the court to convey, the rule's objective is achieved.

Petitioner's failure to appeal was a deliberate, informed choice. He cannot use a procedural omission to collaterally attack a sentence he could have challenged on direct appeal. *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947) (habeas does not permit review of "questions which could have been raised on appeal"). A defendant who knowingly declines to appeal cannot later claim that a procedural technicality deprived him of that opportunity. *Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 444 (1944) (failure to raise an issue on direct appeal waives it).

**IV. The Lower Courts Correctly Applied Harmless-Error Review**

The Third Circuit held that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review and that the error was harmless. This approach follows *Kotteakos* and *Chapman*. The district court's factual finding that petitioner had actual knowledge is dispositive. To obtain habeas relief, petitioner must show "a defect in the proceedings which ... resulted in a complete miscarriage of justice." *Frady v. United States*, 348 U.S. 76, 82 (1954). No such miscarriage occurred here.

Because petitioner suffered no prejudice, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
