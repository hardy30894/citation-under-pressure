**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court’s Supplemental Jury Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous and Prejudicial**

The district court erred in instructing the jury on the meaning of “shielding” under 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) because the instruction was vague, overbroad, and not grounded in the statutory text. The term “shielding” is not defined in the statute, and the court’s reliance on non-statutory standards from other circuits was improper. *See United States v. Kim, 193 F.3d 4 Kim, 193 F.3d 45 (2d Cir. 1999) (warning against judicial supplementation of undefined statutory terms absent clear congressional intent).* The instruction given here—“to protect or conceal from detection”—impermissibly expanded the scope of liability beyond the statute’s plain meaning.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that jury instructions must accurately reflect the law and avoid misleading the jury. *United States v. Gatto, 763 F.3d 790, 800 (7th Cir. 2014) (“A jury instruction must correctly state the law and not mislead the jury.”).* The instruction here failed that standard by suggesting that any act of concealment, no matter how tangential, could support a conviction. *Cf. United States v. Sineneng-Smith, 916 F.3d 1005, 1012 (9th Cir. 2019) (vacating conviction where jury instruction misstated the law and broadened liability).* The government’s proposed definition—“to protect or hide from detection”—was not tethered to the statute and invited the jury to convict based on conduct that did not constitute “shielding” as Congress intended.

Moreover, the instruction was unduly vague, leaving the jury without meaningful guidance on the scope of criminal liability. *See United States v. Bailey, 444 U.S. 394, 403 (1980) (holding that vague instructions risk arbitrary enforcement).* The district court compounded the error by failing to clarify that “shielding” requires proof of an intent to prevent governmental detection, not merely knowledge of an alien’s status. *See United States v. Garcia, 593 F.3d 798, 805 (7th Cir. 2010) (requiring specific intent for § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) convictions).* Because the instruction blurred the line between mere employment of illegal aliens and the affirmative act of shielding them, it prejudiced Ye’s substantial rights. *See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993) (prejudice occurs when error affects the jury’s deliberations).*

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Intent to “Shield” Illegal Aliens**

Even if the instruction were permissible, the evidence was insufficient to prove that Ye acted with the specific intent to “shield” illegal aliens from detection. The government’s theory rested on Ye’s provision of housing, transportation, and advice on obtaining fraudulent documents—none of which constitutes “shielding” under § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). *See United States v. Contreras, 593 F.3d 1135, 1142 (9th Cir. 2010) (holding that mere employment and housing of illegal aliens does not satisfy the “shielding” element).*

The government failed to present evidence that Ye took affirmative steps to conceal his employees from immigration authorities. *See United States v. Kim, 193 F.3d at 49 (requiring proof of affirmative acts to conceal).* The fact that Ye provided housing or transportation does not demonstrate an intent to prevent detection; such conduct is indistinguishable from ordinary employer-employee interactions. *Cf. United States v. Garcia, 593 F.3d at 806 (finding insufficient evidence where defendant’s conduct was consistent with lawful employment practices).*

Furthermore, the government’s reliance on Ye’s advice to employees to obtain fraudulent documents does not establish “shielding.” *See United States v. Contreras, 593 F.3d at 1143 (holding that advice on fraudulent documents does not satisfy the statute).* The evidence showed only that Ye knew his employees were illegal aliens and employed them—conduct that is criminalized under § 1324a, not § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii). *See 8 U.S.C. § 1324a (prohibiting hiring of illegal aliens); § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) (prohibiting shielding from detection).*

Because the evidence did not support a finding of specific intent to “shield,” the conviction must be reversed. *See Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979) (standard for sufficiency review: whether any rational trier of fact could have found guilt beyond a reasonable doubt).* The jury’s verdict was based on an erroneous instruction and insufficient evidence, warranting reversal.
