**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that regularly attempts to collect debts it purchased after default is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—must be answered in the negative. The FDCPA, enacted in 1977, was designed to regulate *debt collectors*, not creditors collecting their own debts. The statutory definition of “debt collector” in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) is clear and unambiguous: it applies only to persons who regularly collect debts *owed or due another*, not to those who collect debts they have purchased and now own. The Fourth Circuit correctly held that Santander, having acquired the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collecting them for its own account, was acting as a creditor—not a debt collector—and thus fell outside the FDCPA’s reach.

The foundational principle of statutory interpretation is that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The FDCPA defines “debt collector” in § 1692a(6) as “any person who uses any instrumentality of interstate commerce or the mails in any business the principal purpose of which is the collection of any debts, or who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The critical phrase is “debts owed or due another.” This language does not turn on the *origin* of the debt or its status at the time of purchase; it turns on the *relationship between the collector and the debt at the time of collection*. When a person collects a debt it owns, it is not collecting “debts owed or due another”—it is collecting its own debt. The distinction is not merely semantic; it is central to the Act’s design.

This interpretation is consistent with the settled understanding of statutory construction in the Supreme Court. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, the Court emphasized that “the words of a statute must be construed in their ordinary sense,” and that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language.” Similarly, in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188, 198 (1939), the Court held that “the words of a statute are to be given their natural and ordinary meaning unless a contrary intention clearly appears.” The phrase “debts owed or due another” is plain: it refers to debts owed to a third party, not to the collector. When a company purchases a debt, it becomes the new creditor. It is no longer collecting “debts owed or due another”—it is collecting its own debt.

The legislative history confirms this understanding. The Senate Report on the FDCPA explicitly states that the Act was intended to regulate “third persons who regularly collect debts for others,” not creditors collecting their own debts. S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698. The Report further clarifies that the Act was not meant to cover “the activities of mortgage service companies and others who service outstanding debts for others, so long as the debts were not in default when taken for servicing.” Id. This demonstrates Congress’s intent to distinguish between *servicing* debts for others and *owning* and collecting debts for oneself. The purchase of a defaulted debt does not transform a creditor into a “debt collector” any more than the purchase of a car transforms a buyer into a “car dealer.”

The petitioners’ argument—that a purchaser of defaulted debt is automatically a “debt collector” because the debt was in default at the time of acquisition—contradicts both the text and the purpose of the Act. The Act does not define “debt collector” by the *status* of the debt at the time of purchase, but by the *relationship* between the collector and the debt at the time of collection. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language.” The petitioners’ interpretation would render the phrase “owed or due another” meaningless, because every debt, at some point, was owed to someone else. Under their theory, every creditor who ever purchased a debt from another would be a “debt collector”—a result Congress clearly did not intend.

This interpretation is further supported by *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 119 (1911), where the Court held that “the words of a statute must be given their natural and ordinary meaning.” The phrase “debts owed or due another” has a natural meaning: debts owed to a person other than the collector. When Santander purchased the loans, it became the new creditor. It was no longer collecting “debts owed or due another”—it was collecting its own debt. The fact that the debt was in default at the time of purchase is irrelevant to the statutory definition.

The petitioners also rely on the exclusion in § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which excludes from the definition of “debt collector” any person collecting a debt that was not in default at the time it was obtained. But this exclusion does not create a rule of inclusion. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. at 543, “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language.” The exclusion applies only when a person collects a debt that was not in default at the time of acquisition. It does not apply when a person collects a debt it owns. The exclusion is not a basis for finding a person a “debt collector”—it is a basis for *excluding* a person from the definition.
