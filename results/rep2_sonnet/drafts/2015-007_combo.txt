**ARGUMENT**

**I. Florida's capital sentencing procedure, which reposes in the trial judge the ultimate responsibility for weighing aggravating and mitigating circumstances and does not require a unanimous jury recommendation, does not violate the Sixth Amendment.**

*A. The jury-trial guarantee protects the jury's historic office of adjudicating guilt; it does not extend to the discretionary selection of sentence.*

The right to trial by jury, though "fundamental to the American scheme of justice," Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145, 149 (1968), has always been understood to govern whether the accused committed the offense charged, not which lawful punishment follows conviction. See Patton v. United States, 281 U.S. 276 (1930). Once a Florida jury finds a defendant guilty of first-degree murder, he stands convicted of a capital offense for which death is already an authorized penalty; the ensuing weighing of aggravating and mitigating circumstances selects among lawful sentences, a function this Court has long entrusted to the sentencing court.

In Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), this Court sustained a judge's authority to impose death notwithstanding an advisory jury's recommendation of life, explaining that "[t]he belief no longer prevails that every offense in a like legal category calls for an identical punishment without regard to the past life and habits of a particular offender." Id. at 247. The Court declined to import trial-type procedural rules into sentencing, recognizing that individualized sentencing depends on information a jury need not itself certify. Id. at 246–49. Florida's scheme, under which the court renders the final sentence after considering an advisory jury recommendation, mirrors the structure this Court approved in Williams.

This Court reaffirmed that States retain wide latitude over sentencing procedure in Spencer v. Texas, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), upholding a recidivist procedure that permitted the sentencer to weigh prior-conviction evidence never found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt as an element of the charged offense. The Constitution tolerates considerable variation among the States in administering sentencing so long as the process is fundamentally fair. Id. at 560–64. That same latitude accommodates Florida's allocation of the weighing function chiefly to the trial judge.

*B. Unanimity in an advisory sentencing recommendation is likewise not constitutionally compelled.*

Because the jury-trial guarantee attaches to the adjudication of guilt, the historic unanimity requirement travels with the guilt verdict, not with an advisory sentencing recommendation. As Justice Cardozo explained in Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), due process in criminal procedure must be judged with the understanding that "justice, though due to the accused, is due to the accuser also," id. at 122, and the Constitution does not freeze state procedure into one inflexible model. Florida's treatment of the jury's sentencing recommendation as advisory, subject to independent judicial weighing, falls within the range the Constitution permits.

**II. Florida's procedure for treating mental retardation as mitigation, rather than as a jury-found categorical bar, satisfies the Eighth Amendment.**

The Eighth Amendment forbids punishment inconsistent with "the dignity of man," Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 100 (1958), and prohibits "cruelty inherent in the method of punishment," Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947). Nothing in that guarantee dictates the procedural mechanism—jury or judge—by which a State must resolve disputed facts bearing on a defendant's mental condition. This Court has consistently left the design of such procedures to the States. In Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), the Court upheld Oregon's placement of the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, holding that due process does not command a particular procedural framework for resolving a defendant's mental condition. Similarly, in Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968), a plurality declined to constitutionalize any single method for adjudicating the relationship between a defendant's mental condition and his culpability, leaving that judgment to the States' "substantial discretion." Id. at 535–36.

Florida's trial court did not disregard Hurst's mental-retardation evidence; it admitted that evidence as mitigation and, after full adversarial testing, expressly found that Hurst is not mentally retarded. That procedure—judicial factfinding on a developed record, subject to appellate review—affords the reliability the Eighth Amendment demands without requiring the jury unanimity or exclusive jury factfinding Hurst now urges. See Resweber, 329 U.S. at 463.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Florida should be affirmed.
