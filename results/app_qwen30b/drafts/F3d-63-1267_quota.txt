**ARGUMENT**

I. THE JURY’S VERDICT ON THE ROBINSON-PATMAN CLAIM MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE STELWAGON FAILED TO PROVE ACTUAL ANTITRUST DAMAGES, AS REQUIRED BY SECTION 4 OF THE CLAYTON ACT.

To recover treble damages under § 4 of the Clayton Act, 15 U.S.C. § 15(a), a plaintiff must prove not only a violation of § 2(a) of the Clayton Act (the Robinson-Patman Act), but also *actual damages* caused by the unlawful price discrimination. *See* *Brooke Group Ltd. v. Brown & Williamson Tobacco Corp.*, 509 U.S. 209, 220 (1993) (“[T]he plaintiff must prove that the antitrust violation caused actual damages.”); *Sears, Roebuck & Co. v. Stiffel Co.*, 376 U.S. 225, 230 (1964) (“[T]he plaintiff must show that the injury was caused by the violation.”). The burden of proving actual damages is not satisfied by speculative or hypothetical losses. *See* *Sears, Roebuck*, 376 U.S. at 230 (“[T]he plaintiff must show that the injury was caused by the violation.”); *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 89 F. Supp. 117, 125 (D. Mass. 1950), aff’d, 188 F.2d 356 (1st Cir. 1951) (“[T]he plaintiff must prove actual damages, not mere speculation.”).

Here, Stelwagon presented no credible evidence of actual damages. The jury awarded $450,383.60 in antitrust damages after the district court remitted the original $2.27 million award as “grossly excessive.” *Stelwagon Mfg. Co. v. Tarmac Roofing Sys., Inc.*, 862 F. Supp. 1361, 1369 (E.D. Pa. 1994). Yet even this reduced amount lacks a foundation in evidence. Stelwagon’s damages expert, Dr. Robert L. H. Smith, relied on a “but-for” analysis that assumed Tarmac’s price discrimination caused Stelwagon to lose sales to competitors. However, the expert’s model failed to account for market forces, competitive dynamics, or Stelwagon’s own business decisions, such as its decision to forego other product lines and its failure to expand its sales force. *See* *Sears, Roebuck*, 376 U.S. at 230 (damages must be “causally connected” to the violation); *Brooke Group*, 509 U.S. at 220 (plaintiff must prove causation and actual damages).

Moreover, Stelwagon’s own conduct undermines its claim of injury. It admitted that it did not sell Tarmac products to any customers outside the Philadelphia area, and that it had no sales data showing how many units it would have sold absent the alleged discrimination. *See* *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 89 F. Supp. at 125 (damages must be “supported by evidence,” not speculation). The absence of such data renders the damages calculation speculative. *See* *F. W. Woolworth Co. v. Contemporary Arts, Inc.*, 344 U.S. 228, 232 (1952) (“[T]he plaintiff must prove actual damages with reasonable certainty.”); *Burlington Truck Lines, Inc. v. United States*, 371 U.S. 156, 168 (1962) (“[T]he damages must be proved with reasonable certainty.”).

The district court’s remittitur did not cure this deficiency. Remittitur is not a substitute for proof. *See* *Burlington Truck Lines*, 371 U.S. at 168 (“[T]he court may reduce the award, but not to a figure unsupported by the evidence.”). The court reduced the award based on its view that the original amount was “grossly excessive,” but it did not find that the reduced amount was supported by evidence. *Stelwagon*, 862 F. Supp. at 1369. That is insufficient. *See* *Brooke Group*, 509 U.S. at 220 (“[T]he plaintiff must prove actual damages.”); *Sears, Roebuck*, 376 U.S. at 230 (damages must be “causally connected” to the violation).

The Third Circuit has long held that “a plaintiff cannot recover damages under the Clayton Act unless it proves actual damages with reasonable certainty.” *See* *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 89 F. Supp. at 125; *see also* *F. W. Woolworth Co.*, 344 U.S. at 232. Here, Stelwagon failed to meet that burden. The damages award, even after remittitur, is unsupported by evidence and must be vacated.

II. THE JURY’S VERDICT ON THE BREACH OF CONTRACT CLAIM MUST BE VACATED BECAUSE THE ORAL AGREEMENT WAS UNENFORCEABLE UNDER THE STATUTE OF FRAUDS.

Stelwagon’s breach of contract claim rests on an oral, exclusive distributorship agreement. But such an agreement is unenforceable under the Statute of Frauds, 13 Pa.C.S. § 2501, which requires that contracts for the sale of goods over $500, or contracts that cannot be performed within one year, be in writing. *See* *Pennsylvania Statute of Frauds*, 13 Pa.C.S. § 2501(a)(1)–(2). The agreement here was for an indefinite term and could not be performed within one year, as Tarmac’s obligation to refrain from selling to other distributors in the Philadelphia area was ongoing. *See* *Barnes v. Barnes*, 437 Pa. 455, 460 (1969) (contracts that cannot be performed within one year are unenforceable under the Statute of Frauds).
