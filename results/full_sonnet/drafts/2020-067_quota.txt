# ARGUMENT

A federal appellate court conducting plain-error review under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) may consult the whole record—not merely the evidence placed before the jury—in deciding whether an unpreserved error affected a defendant's substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. This rule follows directly from the structure of plain-error review, from this Court's precedent construing Rule 52(b), and from the practical reality that the inquiry is retrospective and remedial rather than a re-adjudication of guilt confined to the trial evidence.

## I. Plain-Error Review Places the Burden on the Defendant to Show Actual Prejudice, Which Requires Examination of the Full Record

Rule 52(b) review under *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), requires a defendant to establish four elements: (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* at 732-736. Critically, this Court held that "[i]t is the defendant rather than the Government who bears the burden of persuasion with respect to prejudice" under the third prong. *Id.* at 734. That burden-allocation reflects a deliberate departure from harmless-error review under Rule 52(a), where the government bears the burden once a defendant timely objects. *Id.* at 734-35. A rule confining courts to the four corners of the trial record would collapse this distinction, effectively restoring to unpreserved errors the same searching, government-burdened inquiry that Rule 52(a) reserves for preserved ones.

This Court reinforced that the prejudice inquiry is genuinely probabilistic and record-dependent in *United States v. Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. 74 (2004), holding that a defendant seeking relief for an unpreserved Rule 11 error must show "a reasonable probability that, but for the error, he would not have entered the plea." *Id.* at 83. Determining whether such a probability exists cannot sensibly be limited to the particular colloquy in which the error occurred; it necessarily requires the reviewing court to assess what actually happened and what the defendant actually knew, drawing on any information the record makes available. *Id.* at 83.

## II. *Vonn* Squarely Resolved This Question and Controls Here

This Court has already confronted—and rejected—the argument that plain-error review must be confined to a discrete slice of the proceedings. In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55 (2002), the defendant argued that a Rule 11 plea-colloquy omission should be assessed only by reference to the colloquy itself. This Court disagreed, holding that "a silent defendant has the burden to satisfy the plain-error rule and ... a reviewing court may consult the whole record when considering the effect of any error on substantial rights." *Id.* at 59. The Court reasoned that limiting the inquiry to the colloquy transcript "would be at odds with the object of the plain-error rule," which is to determine whether the error actually mattered to the outcome, not merely whether an isolated procedural step was technically deficient. *Id.* at 74-75. The same logic governs here: whether an omitted knowledge-of-status element under *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191 (2019), affected the outcome is a question about what actually happened, answerable only by looking beyond the evidence the jury happened to hear.

## III. This Court's Other Plain-Error Decisions Confirm That Courts Routinely Look Beyond the Trial Evidence

In *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625 (2002), this Court held that a grand-jury indictment defect did not seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings because "the evidence that petitioners were involved in a drug conspiracy involving 50 or more grams of cocaine base was overwhelming and essentially uncontroverted," a conclusion drawn from the record as a whole rather than from any particular charging document. *Id.* at 632-33. Similarly, in *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461 (1997), the Court concluded that failure to submit an element to the jury did not warrant relief given the strength of the "overwhelming" and "essentially uncontroverted" evidence of that element, again drawn from the full trial and pretrial record. *Id.* at 470. And in *Puckett v. United States*, 556 U.S. 129 (2009), the Court reiterated that the plain-error standard exists precisely to reserve correction for errors that "seriously affect the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings," an inquiry inherently comparative and contextual. *Id.* at 135.

## IV. Applying This Rule Here Confirms the Eleventh Circuit's Approach Was Correct

Applying these principles, the Eleventh Circuit did not err by consulting Greer's undisputed record of prior felony convictions—including years of actual incarceration—to determine that any *Rehaif* error did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness of his trial. As in *Cotton* and *Johnson*, such record evidence, if overwhelming and uncontested, forecloses any "reasonable probability" that the outcome would have differed, *Dominguez Benitez*, 542 U.S. at 83, and confirms that the proceedings' integrity was not compromised.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
