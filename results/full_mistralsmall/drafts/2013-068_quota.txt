**ARGUMENT**

**I. The First Circuit Correctly Upheld the Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Law as a Content-Neutral, Narrowly Tailored Time, Place, and Manner Regulation.**

The Massachusetts statute at issue, Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½, is a content-neutral regulation of speech that satisfies the First Amendment’s time, place, and manner framework. As this Court has repeatedly held, such regulations are permissible if they (1) are justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech, (2) are narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Clark v. Community for Creative Non-Violence*, 468 U.S. 288, 293 (1984). The First Circuit correctly applied these principles in affirming the statute’s constitutionality.

The buffer zone is content-neutral because it applies equally to all speakers, regardless of their viewpoint. *McCullen v. Coakley (McCullen I)*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-77 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d in part, rev’d in part on other grounds*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014). The statute does not single out abortion-related speech; it regulates all expressive activity within the 35-foot zone. *Id.* This Court has long recognized that facially neutral regulations are content-neutral unless their purpose or effect is to suppress particular ideas. *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 386 (1992); *Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95 (1972). The Massachusetts law meets this standard.

The statute is narrowly tailored to serve the Commonwealth’s significant interests in public safety, patient access, and orderly healthcare delivery. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000). The First Circuit properly deferred to the legislature’s judgment that a 35-foot buffer zone was necessary to prevent congestion, harassment, and interference with clinic operations. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 178-79. This Court has repeatedly deferred to legislative judgments in analogous contexts, recognizing that buffer zones are a permissible means of protecting public safety and access to healthcare facilities. *Id.* at 715-16 (citing *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 767-68 (1994)).

Finally, the statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. The plaintiffs remain free to speak outside the buffer zone, distribute literature, display signs, and engage in prayer. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The First Circuit correctly noted that the plaintiffs’ ability to communicate is not foreclosed, only regulated in a manner consistent with public safety. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 180-81. This Court has upheld similar regulations where ample alternatives remained. *See, e.g., Frisby v. Schultz*, 487 U.S. 474, 482-83 (1988) (upholding residential picketing ban where speakers could protest elsewhere).

**II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled.**

The First Circuit correctly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, in upholding the Massachusetts statute. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181-82. *Hill* squarely addressed a buffer-zone law regulating speech near healthcare facilities and held that such regulations are constitutional if they satisfy the *Ward* framework. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715-16. The Massachusetts law is materially indistinguishable from the Colorado statute upheld in *Hill*.

The plaintiffs’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* is unavailing. They argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled because subsequent decisions, such as *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), altered First Amendment jurisprudence. However, this Court has repeatedly declined to revisit *Hill* in analogous contexts. *See, e.g., Scheidler v. Nat’l Org. for Women*, 537 U.S. 393, 409 (2003) (declining to overrule *Hill* in RICO context). The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Sorrell* and *Citizens United* is misplaced, as those cases addressed content-based restrictions on political speech, not content-neutral time, place, and manner regulations. *Sorrell*, 564 U.S. at 566; *Citizens United*, 558 U.S. at 324.

Moreover, *Hill* remains good law because it comports with this Court’s precedents governing buffer zones and public forums. *See, e.g., Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 767-68 (upholding 36-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances); *Frisby*, 487 U.S. at 482-83 (upholding residential picketing ban). The plaintiffs’ argument that *Hill* should be overruled is a thinly veiled invitation to reconsider settled precedent, which this Court has no reason to do.

**III. The First Circuit Properly Rejected the Plaintiffs’ As-Applied Challenges.**

The plaintiffs’ as-applied challenges fail because the statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The First Circuit correctly noted that the plaintiffs remain free to speak outside the buffer zone, distribute literature, display signs, and engage in prayer. *McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 180-81. The plaintiffs’ contention that the buffer zone impedes their ability to communicate effectively is belied by the record, which shows that they continue to engage in expressive activity outside the zone. *Id.* at 181-82.

The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), is misplaced. *Snyder* addressed a tort claim arising from speech on a public sidewalk, not a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation. *Id.* at 452. The Massachusetts statute does not target the plaintiffs’ viewpoint or suppress their message; it merely regulates the *location* of their speech. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

**Conclusion**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer-zone law as a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time, place, and manner regulation that leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *Hill v. Colorado* controls and should not be overruled. The plaintiffs’ as-applied challenges fail for the same reasons. This Court should affirm the judgment below.
