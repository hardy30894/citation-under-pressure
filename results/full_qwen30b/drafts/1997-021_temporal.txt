**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL AND CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT.

The National Labor Relations Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll of employees is not only irrational but fundamentally at odds with the statutory scheme of the National Labor Relations Act. The Act, enacted in 1935, was designed to promote industrial peace by ensuring that collective bargaining relationships are stable and that employees’ rights to self-organization are protected. It does not, however, mandate that employers be deprived of any means to test the actual sentiment of their workforce when they have reason to believe that a union’s majority support has eroded.

The Board’s current standard renders polling an impractical and useless tool. As the Supreme Court has recognized, “the purpose of a union is to represent employees in collective bargaining,” and employers have a legitimate interest in knowing whether a union continues to represent a majority of employees before being compelled to bargain. *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp.*, 301 U.S. 1, 30 (1937). Yet under the Board’s rule, an employer may only poll if it already possesses sufficient objective evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition—a standard so high that it effectively precludes polling altogether. This creates a paradox: the Board permits polling only when the employer already has enough evidence to act without polling, thereby rendering the poll superfluous. Such a rule cannot be reconciled with the Act’s goal of facilitating informed decision-making.

The Board’s justification—that polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling”—is not a valid basis for eliminating a lawful method of determining employee sentiment. The Act does not prohibit employers from seeking information about employee preferences; it only prohibits coercion and interference. *NLRB v. Fansteel Metallurgical Corp.*, 306 U.S. 240, 245 (1939). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that employers may conduct polls to avoid the risk of bargaining with a minority union, which would violate § 8(a)(2). *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1298 (9th Cir. 1984) (citing *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395, 400 (1952)). But if the Board permits polling only when the employer already has a basis to withdraw recognition, then the employer gains no new information—only the illusion of certainty. This is not a rational application of the Act.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is inconsistent with the principle that an employer’s good-faith doubt, even if not objectively justified, may be sufficient to justify certain actions. In *NLRB v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 370 (1948), the Court held that an employer’s “good faith” belief, even if mistaken, may excuse conduct that would otherwise be unlawful. The Board’s rule, by requiring objective evidence before allowing a poll, effectively denies employers the right to test their good-faith doubts through a neutral, supervised process. This is contrary to the Act’s emphasis on fairness and procedural integrity.

The Board’s standard also fails to account for the practical realities of successorship. When a business is sold, as here, the new employer inherits the bargaining obligations of the predecessor. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 36–41 (1987) (citing *NLRB v. Burns International Security Services, Inc.*, 379 U.S. 1, 15 (1964)). But the new employer is not bound to accept the union’s continued majority status without question. The Act does not require blind adherence to past certifications. *NLRB v. American National Ins. Co.*, 343 U.S. 395, 400 (1952). Where the workforce has changed significantly—here, from 45 to 32 employees, with a reduction in mechanics and parts staff—the presumption of continuing majority support is weakened. The Board’s refusal to allow polling in such circumstances forces the employer to either bargain with a union it believes no longer represents a majority or risk an unfair labor practice claim.

The Board’s standard, therefore, is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the Act’s purpose. It undermines the employer’s ability to act in good faith and creates a disincentive to seek accurate information. The Act does not require employers to gamble on the union’s continued support; it allows them to test that support through lawful means. The Board’s rule, by restricting polling to cases where the employer already has sufficient evidence to withdraw recognition, defeats the very purpose of polling.

II. THE BOARD’S FINDING THAT ALLENTOWN MACK LACKED A GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT IS NOT SUPPORTED BY SUBSTANTIAL EVIDENCE.

The Board’s conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record. The Act requires that findings of fact be supported by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 477 (1951). The Board’s decision here fails that test.
