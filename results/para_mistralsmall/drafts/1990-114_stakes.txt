**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s state judges are not “employees” under the ADEA because they are “appointees on the policymaking level” within the meaning of 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603-06 (8th Cir. 1990), *aff’d*, 501 U.S. 452 (1991). This conclusion is compelled by the text of the statute, its structure, and the weight of judicial authority.

A. The plain text of § 630(f) excludes judges from the ADEA’s coverage.

Section 630(f) defines “employee” to exclude, *inter alia*, “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The statute does not limit this exception to executive or legislative appointees; it applies to *any* appointee on the policymaking level, regardless of branch. *See Massachusetts v. EEOC*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988) (“The policymaking level exception is not limited to the executive or legislative branches; it applies to any appointee performing policymaking functions.”). Judges exercise policymaking authority by interpreting statutes, developing common law, and resolving disputes that shape public policy. *See id.* (“Judges necessarily engage in policymaking when they interpret ambiguous statutes or fill gaps in the law.”); *cf. Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 385 (1989) (recognizing that federal judges exercise “a significant policymaking role” in sentencing).

The legislative history cited by Petitioners is not controlling. The Joint Explanatory Statement of the ADEA’s conferees refers to “cabinet officers” and “persons with comparable responsibilities,” but this is not an exhaustive list. *See* 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-80. Courts must look to the statute’s text, not isolated snippets of legislative history, to determine its scope. *See Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984). The text of § 630(f) is unambiguous: it excludes *all* appointees on the policymaking level, including judges.

B. The structure of the ADEA supports excluding judges from coverage.

The ADEA’s exemptions are grouped together in § 630(f), and none of them are limited to specific branches of government. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 630(f) (excluding, among others, “any person elected to public office,” “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff,” and “an appointee on the policymaking level”). If the policymaking exception were limited to executive or legislative appointees, judges would fall outside *all* of the ADEA’s exemptions, rendering them the only state employees entirely unprotected by the statute. This would be an absurd result that Congress could not have intended. *See United States v. Wilson*, 503 U.S. 329, 334 (1992) (“We must avoid constructions that lead to absurd results.”).

C. The weight of judicial authority supports the conclusion that judges are policymakers.

Every federal court to address this issue—save two district courts—has held that judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” and thus excluded from the ADEA. *See Massachusetts v. EEOC*, 858 F.2d at 55; *Illinois v. EEOC*, 721 F. Supp. 156, 159 (N.D. Ill. 1989); *Vermont v. EEOC*, 717 F. Supp. 261, 264 (D. Vt. 1989); *In re Stout*, 559 A.2d 489, 492 (Pa. 1989); *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 145 (Mass. 1988). The two outlier decisions—*Schlitz v. Virginia*, 681 F. Supp. 330 (E.D. Va. 1988), *rev’d on other grounds*, 854 F.2d 43 (4th Cir. 1988), and *Vermont*—are unpersuasive. They rely on an unduly narrow view of judicial policymaking, ignoring that judges shape the law through statutory interpretation and common-law development. *See Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55 (“Judges engage in policymaking whenever they interpret ambiguous statutes or create new common-law rules.”).

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT REQUIREMENT FOR STATE JUDGES DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement age for judges is constitutional because it bears a rational relationship to legitimate state interests. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606-07. This Court has repeatedly deferred to state classifications affecting the judiciary, recognizing that states have broad authority to regulate the qualifications and tenure of their judges. *See, e.g., Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam) (“States have broad discretion to establish age-based classifications for public employees, including judges.”); *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (“A mandatory retirement age for judges is rationally related to the state’s interest in ensuring the competence and efficiency of its judiciary.”), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979).

A. Missouri has a legitimate interest in ensuring judicial competence and efficiency.

States have a compelling interest in maintaining a capable and efficient judiciary. *Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313. Mandatory retirement ages advance this interest by removing judges whose faculties may have declined with age. *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 627 (“The physical and mental demands of judging increase with age, and mandatory retirement reduces the risk of error or delay caused by failing health.”). This Court has recognized that “the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode.” *Id.* Mandatory retirement rules are a rational means of addressing this concern.

B. Missouri’s rule is not arbitrary or irrational.

Petitioners argue that Missouri’s rule is underinclusive because it does not apply to other public officials. But the Equal Protection Clause does not require perfect tailoring; it only requires a rational basis. *See FCC v. Beach Commc’ns, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 315 (1993) (“A classification is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest.”). Missouri’s rule is rational because judges perform unique functions that demand peak cognitive and physical abilities. *Trafelet*, 594 F.2d at 627. The state’s decision to limit mandatory retirement to judges is not arbitrary; it reflects the special role judges play in the administration of justice.

C. The Court should defer to Missouri’s judgment.

This Court has consistently deferred to state legislatures and constitutional conventions in matters of judicial qualification and tenure. *See, e.g., Murgia*, 427 U.S. at 313; *Gregory*, 501 U.S. at 460 (recognizing that states have “considerable latitude” in structuring their judiciaries). Missouri’s mandatory retirement rule for judges is a classic example of such a judgment. The rule is not a pretext for age discrimination; it is a rational effort to ensure that the state’s highest judicial officers remain capable of performing their duties.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Respondent respectfully submits that the judgment below should be affirmed. The ADEA does not apply to Missouri’s state judges, and the mandatory retirement requirement does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.
