# ARGUMENT

## I. The Warrant Affidavit Failed to Establish a Nexus Between the Alleged Crimes and Joubert's Parents' Home

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant affidavit establish "a nexus between the objects sought and the place to be searched." *United States v. Feliz*, 182 F.3d 82, 87 (1st Cir. 1999). While probable cause is assessed under the "totality-of-the-circumstances" test of *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983), the affidavit must still permit a "reasonable inference" that evidence will be found at the specific location searched. *United States v. Ribeiro*, 397 F.3d 43, 49 (1st Cir. 2005).

Here, the affidavit's boilerplate assertion that persons who molest children "often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims" is precisely the kind of generalized, profile-based assertion that this Court has cautioned against when unmoored from particularized facts about the residence itself. The only fact tying evidence to the Manchester address was SJ's account that Joubert destroyed his computer tower and "stacked the removed components inside his parents' home." But SJ never saw Joubert discard—or retain—any data-bearing component, and nothing in the affidavit connects photographs or videotapes, as opposed to a gutted computer chassis, to that address. Probable cause "must be particularized with respect to the person" and place searched, *Maryland v. Pringle*, 540 U.S. 366, 371 (2003), and a stale, unspecified claim of general "training and experience" cannot substitute for facts linking contraband to Joubert's parents' residence. See also *United States v. Zayas-Diaz*, 95 F.3d 105, 111 (1st Cir. 1996) (nexus cannot rest on speculation).

## II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Uncharged Molestation Testimony

Even where Rule 414 permits propensity evidence in child-molestation prosecutions, that evidence remains subject to Rule 403's balancing test. *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180 (1997) (relevance is not "an absolute" but must be weighed against unfair prejudice). Courts applying Rule 414 have cautioned that admission is not automatic; the trial court must still conduct a "careful" balancing inquiry. *United States v. LeMay*, 260 F.3d 1018, 1026 (9th Cir. 2001) (upholding Rule 414 only because district court engaged in "a thorough" 403 analysis).

Here, the district court permitted SJ's decades-old, uncorroborated allegations—never charged, never tested by contemporaneous investigation—to be presented to the jury alongside the charged conduct involving KC. The risk that jurors would convict based on character rather than the specific counts charged was acute, particularly because the uncharged conduct was remote in time and lacked the visual corroboration (the VHS tape) that anchored the charged counts. This is the precise unfair-prejudice concern Rule 403 guards against, and the "unfair prejudice" here substantially outweighed any incremental probative value, contrary to the balance struck in *LeMay*. Cf. *United States v. Mound*, 149 F.3d 799, 801 (8th Cir. 1998) (recognizing prejudice concerns even while upholding Rule 413's parallel structure).

## III. Application of §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert's Purely Intrastate Conduct Exceeds Congress's Commerce Power

The Commerce Clause does not permit Congress to regulate conduct that is not itself economic and lacks a substantial connection to interstate commerce. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 559-60 (1995) (invalidating Gun-Free School Zones Act because possession of a firearm "has nothing to do with 'commerce'"); *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 613 (2000) (non-economic, intrastate violence cannot be aggregated to satisfy the substantial-effects test). Even accepting *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 22 (2005), which permits aggregation of purely intrastate activity where it is part of a broader regulatory scheme addressing an interstate market, Joubert's conduct—photographing and videotaping a child in New Hampshire, using materials of unknown origin—was never shown to implicate the interstate market for child pornography in the manner *Raich* contemplated for fungible commodities like marijuana.

While other circuits have sustained § 2251(a) against Commerce Clause challenges by relying on the statute's jurisdictional hook requiring that materials have "been mailed, or ... shipped or transported in interstate or foreign commerce," *United States v. Rodia*, 194 F.3d 465, 471 (3d Cir. 1999), the government here offered no evidence that the camera, videotape, or any instrumentality used by Joubert crossed state lines or was otherwise tied to interstate commerce beyond the fact of production itself. Absent that jurisdictional predicate being genuinely satisfied on this record, application of the statute to Joubert's conduct exceeds the outer boundary drawn in *Lopez* and *Morrison*.

## IV. Joubert's 480-Month Sentence Is Substantively Unreasonable

Appellate review of sentences for substantive reasonableness asks whether the sentence, in light of the totality of the circumstances, reflects an abuse of discretion. *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 51 (2007). A sentence may be reasonable in method yet still substantively excessive if it is "greater than necessary" to achieve the purposes of § 3553(a). *United States v. Zapata*, 589 F.3d 475, 484 (1st Cir. 2009).

The district court's forty-year sentence, effectively a life sentence for a 60-year-old defendant, gave disproportionate weight to uncharged, decades-old allegations introduced solely through SJ's testimony, while failing adequately to credit Joubert's lack of any prior conviction. Under *Gall*, a sentence resting on such an imbalanced weighing of § 3553(a) factors is substantively unreasonable and should be vacated.
