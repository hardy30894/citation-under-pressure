**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Guarantee a Freestanding Right to Use Mental-Disease Evidence to Negate Mens Rea Outside the Framework of the Insanity Defense.**

This Court has long recognized that the States possess wide latitude to define the elements of crimes, the defenses available to them, and the manner in which evidence bearing on mental condition may be channeled into the trial process. The insanity defense, as historically understood from *M'Naghten's Case*, 8 Eng. Rep. 718 (1843), treated mental disease as an affirmative defense to be raised and proved by the defendant, not as a mechanism for negating the prosecution's proof of intent element by element. Arizona's rule simply preserves that traditional separation between the defense of insanity and the prosecution's burden on the elements of the offense.

This Court has already sustained the exclusion of similar categories of psychiatric evidence offered to negate intent. In *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463 (1946), the Court upheld a trial court's refusal to instruct the jury that evidence of the defendant's mental deficiency could reduce murder to manslaughter by negating premeditation, concluding that the District of Columbia's adherence to a unitary standard of criminal responsibility, rather than a doctrine of partial responsibility, did not offend due process. *Id.* at 475–76. If the exclusion of mental-deficiency evidence offered to negate premeditation was constitutional in *Fisher*, Arizona's more modest rule—which still permits the evidence to be considered on the question of insanity—is a fortiori permissible.

This conclusion is reinforced by *Betts v. Brady*, 316 U.S. 455 (1942), which recognized that due process in state criminal proceedings does not impose a fixed, uniform code of procedure but instead accommodates variation among the States absent a showing of fundamental unfairness in the particular case. *Id.* at 461–62. Arizona's channeling rule, which leaves the defendant free to introduce and rely upon the same psychiatric evidence in support of an insanity defense, reflects precisely this sort of permissible state variation.

**II. Arizona's Evidentiary Rule Is a Reasonable Procedural Limitation That Does Not Offend Fundamental Fairness.**

Arizona's rule also survives scrutiny as a permissible procedural and evidentiary regulation. This Court has consistently held that due process does not require the wholesale constitutionalization of state evidentiary rules; it requires only that such rules not work a fundamental unfairness in the conduct of the trial. *Twining v. New Jersey*, 211 U.S. 78 (1908), established that the Fourteenth Amendment does not require the States to adopt uniform rules of procedure and evidence, and that the States retain broad authority to shape their own systems of criminal adjudication. *Id.* at 110–11. This Court reaffirmed that States retain substantial authority over the content of their own evidentiary rules in *Adamson v. California*, 332 U.S. 46 (1947), which held that the Fourteenth Amendment does not incorporate the specific procedural guarantees of the Bill of Rights against the States wholesale, but instead tests state procedure against the more general standard of fundamental fairness. *Id.* at 53–54.

This Court applied these principles directly to a state evidentiary rule limiting the use of certain evidence in *Spencer v. Texas*, 385 U.S. 554 (1967), where it upheld Texas's admission of prior-conviction evidence despite the defendant's claim that the rule improperly affected the jury's assessment of guilt, recognizing that the process of adjudication in criminal cases requires the drawing of lines and that state evidentiary rules are entitled to deference absent a showing that the rule renders the trial fundamentally unfair. *Id.* at 562–64. Arizona's limitation on the use of mental-disease evidence is analogous: it does not exclude the evidence altogether, but merely directs the jury to the historically established channel—the insanity defense—for its consideration.

The touchstone remains whether the challenged procedure offends the concept of fundamental fairness underlying the Due Process Clause. *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219 (1941), examined whether the conduct of a state trial denied the defendant the fair play essential to due process, and found no such denial where the proceeding, taken as a whole, afforded the defendant a fair opportunity to present his defense. *Id.* at 236–37. Likewise, in *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), this Court explained that state procedure violates due process only where the challenged conduct amounts to more than "offend[ing] some fastidious squeamishness or private sentimentalism," concluding that a due process violation exists only where the proceeding is one that "shocks the conscience." *Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165, 172 (1952). That standard is far removed from Arizona's evidentiary channeling rule, which leaves the defendant free to present his psychiatric evidence in full on the issue for which it has traditionally been received.

**III. Application to This Case**

Clark's expert evidence was fully considered by the trial court in adjudicating his insanity claim; the court simply declined to permit that same evidence, unconstrained by the insanity framework's burdens, to defeat the mens rea element the State must otherwise prove. That is precisely the kind of rule the above authorities sanction. Arizona's channeling rule does not offend the fundamental fairness principles discussed in *Twining*, *Adamson*, *Betts*, *Lisenba*, and *Rochin*, and this Court should affirm.
