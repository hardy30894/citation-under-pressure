**ARGUMENT**

**I. Facebook Was Required to Overcome the Statutory Presumption of Validity by Clear and Cogent Evidence, and It Failed to Do So**

A patent that has issued from the Patent Office carries with it a presumption that the claims are valid. This Court's predecessors made clear that such a presumption is a substantial one, "not to be overthrown except by clear and cogent evidence." *Radio Corp. of America v. Radio Eng'g Labs., Inc.*, 293 U.S. 1, 2 (1934). The burden Facebook bore was not the ordinary preponderance standard applicable to most civil claims; it was the heightened standard reserved for parties attacking the validity of an issued patent. *Id.* The jury's verdict here rested on a white paper describing Leader2Leader® in general marketing terms and on the largely uncorroborated recollection of witnesses about what the product did years earlier. That kind of proof does not satisfy the "clear and cogent" standard this Court's predecessor required in *Radio Corp.*, 293 U.S. at 2.

The danger of relying on such reconstructed testimony to invalidate a patent has long been recognized. In *The Barbed Wire Patent*, 143 U.S. 275, 284–85 (1892), the Supreme Court cautioned that oral testimony describing the features of a device used years before trial, when unaccompanied by contemporaneous documentary corroboration establishing the specific structure of the device, is inherently unreliable and insufficient to defeat a patent. The same infirmity infects the testimony Facebook offered here regarding the internal architecture of the pre-critical-date Leader2Leader® system: none of the documentary evidence—including the Wright-Patterson white paper—describes the metadata "tracking" and "dynamically updating" functions recited in the asserted claims. Absent such corroboration, the verdict cannot stand.

**II. The On-Sale and Public-Use Bar Required Proof That the Pre-Critical-Date Product Embodied Every Limitation of the Asserted Claims**

Invalidity under the on-sale and public-use provisions requires more than a showing that some version of a commercial product existed and was offered before the critical date. It requires proof that the very invention claimed—every limitation of it—was embodied in that product. *Smith v. Hall*, 301 U.S. 216, 226 (1937) (public use bar applies only where the device used or sold embodies the invention thereafter claimed). Facebook's proof did not meet this standard. The white paper touted general concepts—breaking down communication "silos" and achieving "true integration" of collaboration tools—but nowhere described the specific claimed steps of "dynamically associating metadata" with data and then "tracking movement" of a user between environments so as to "dynamically updat[e]" that metadata, as claim 9 requires. '761 patent col.21 ll.38–58.

This Court's predecessors have consistently held that anticipation, whether by prior invention or prior public use, demands identity between the claimed invention and the earlier device—an approximate or partial correspondence is not enough. *Eibel Process Co. v. Minnesota & Ontario Paper Co.*, 261 U.S. 45, 60–61 (1923) (rejecting invalidity defense where the prior structure did not perform the same function in the same way as the patented invention). Likewise, in determining whether a product was "on sale" within the meaning of the statute, the Supreme Court examined whether the device sold was substantially complete and actually embodied the invention, not merely whether a product bearing the same name was marketed. *Corona Cord Tire Co. v. Dovan Chem. Corp.*, 276 U.S. 358, 383–84 (1928). Leader's Digital Leaderboard® "engine" as marketed in the white paper was, on this record, not shown to perform the specific metadata-tracking and updating functions of the asserted claims; the testimony conflated the commercial brand name with the later-conceived and later-implemented claimed functionality. That conflation cannot substitute for the clear and cogent proof the law demands.

**III. The District Court Erred in Denying Leader's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law**

Where the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner, would not permit reasonable jurors to reach the conclusion the jury reached, judgment as a matter of law must issue. *Cities Serv. Oil Co. v. Dunlap*, 308 U.S. 208, 211–13 (1939) (a directed verdict is proper where "without weighing the credibility of the witnesses there can be but one reasonable conclusion" from the evidence); *Galloway v. United States*, 319 U.S. 372, 395–96 (1943) (reviewing court must determine whether there is a total lack of evidence to sustain the verdict). Here, no witness or document established that the pre-critical-date Leader2Leader® product performed the metadata-association, movement-tracking, and dynamic-updating steps of the asserted claims. Facebook's evidence went to the existence and sale of a commercial product bearing that name and to generalized descriptions of its market purpose—not to the specific structure required by *Smith v. Hall*, 301 U.S. at 226, and *Eibel Process Co.*, 261 U.S. at 60–61. Under the standard of *Cities Service Oil Co.*, 308 U.S. at 211–13, and given the heightened evidentiary burden confirmed in *Radio Corp.*, 293 U.S. at 2, no reasonable jury could have found, by clear and cogent evidence, that the asserted claims were anticipated.

For these reasons, the district court's denial of Leader's motion for judgment as a matter of law, or in the alternative for a new trial, should be reversed, and judgment should be entered—or a new trial ordered—in Leader's favor.
