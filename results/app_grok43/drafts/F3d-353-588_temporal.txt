**Argument**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment. Summary judgment is improper where the record presents genuine issues of material fact. Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc., 368 U.S. 464, 467 (1962).

I. Pine Lawn regarded Epps as disabled.

Chief Hardy’s memoranda cited Dr. Mirkin’s conclusion that Epps’s degenerative disk disease would require “a different type of occupation” and recommended termination because Epps could no longer “function as a regular officer.” This evidence creates a triable question whether Pine Lawn regarded Epps as substantially limited in the major life activity of working. In Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954), the Court recognized that official action branding an individual as inferior “generates a feeling of inferiority as to [his] status in the community.” The same principle applies when an employer terminates an employee on the basis of a physician’s assessment that he can never resume his occupation.

II. The termination constituted retaliation for filing workers’ compensation claims.

Epps filed claims after the 1998 on-duty accident and received benefits. Hardy expressly referenced “the number of workers’ compensation claims Epps filed” in recommending discharge. Missouri Revised Statutes § 287.780 prohibits retaliation for exercising rights under the workers’ compensation law. Protection against adverse action for invoking statutory remedies finds support in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 45 (1937), which condemned discrimination against employees for engaging in protected activity. The district court’s dismissal of the retaliation claim therefore cannot stand.

III. Sovereign immunity does not bar the claims.

Epps’s federal and state-law claims arise from conduct that violates rights secured by the Constitution and statutes. In Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 159-160 (1908), the Court held that sovereign immunity yields when state or local officers act in violation of federal law. Municipalities enjoy no greater immunity from such claims. The district court’s application of sovereign immunity to dismiss the retaliation and emotional-distress counts was therefore erroneous.

The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
