**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment does not require a public school to open its limited public forum to religious speech when doing so would violate the Establishment Clause.

The central question in this case is whether Milford Central School’s denial of access to the Good News Club violated the First Amendment’s Free Speech Clause. The answer is no—because the school did not engage in viewpoint discrimination, but instead applied a neutral, content-neutral policy that excluded religious use of school facilities to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. This Court has long recognized that a government may exclude religious speech from a limited public forum when such inclusion would impermissibly advance religion. *Lamb's Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993) (“[T]he government may exclude religious groups from a limited public forum if it does so consistently with its own policies and without discriminating against religion.”). Here, Milford’s Community Use Policy explicitly prohibits “religious purposes,” and the Club’s activities—centered on Bible lessons, prayer, and evangelism—are quintessentially religious in nature. The school’s refusal to allow such use was not based on hostility toward religion, but on a legitimate effort to remain neutral in matters of faith.

The Second Circuit correctly held that the Club’s activities were not merely “moral and character development” from a religious perspective, but rather constituted religious instruction and worship. *Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 507 (2d Cir. 2000). The Club’s meetings include mandatory prayers, scripture memorization, Bible story-telling, and explicit invitations to accept Jesus as Savior—all of which are integral to Christian worship. These activities are not simply expressive of a religious viewpoint; they are themselves acts of religious practice. As this Court held in *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981), “there is no indication when ‘singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles’ cease to be ‘singing, teaching, and reading’—all apparently forms of ‘speech,’ despite their religious subject matter—and become unprotected ‘worship.’” The Club’s conduct falls squarely within the category of religious worship, not mere expression of values.

Moreover, the Club’s claim fails under the doctrine of *Rosenberger v. Archdiocese of New York*, 515 U.S. 834 (1995), which held that a university could not deny funding to a student publication because it was religiously oriented. But *Rosenberger* involved a funding program, not facility access, and crucially, the University had already opened its forum to secular student groups. Here, Milford has not created an open forum for all viewpoints—it has maintained a narrow, content-neutral policy excluding religious use altogether. In *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983), this Court affirmed that a public employer may exclude certain types of speech from a limited public forum: “A State need not open a forum for speech at all, and once it does so, it may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, and manner of expression.” Milford’s policy is precisely such a restriction: it permits non-religious civic, social, and recreational uses, but excludes religious ones—a distinction that is both content-neutral and consistent with the Establishment Clause.

The fact that other secular youth organizations have been allowed to meet at the school does not establish viewpoint discrimination. *Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 507. The Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club are not engaged in religious instruction or worship. Their programs focus on outdoor skills, citizenship, and community service—activities that are inherently secular in purpose and execution. The presence of a religious component transforms the nature of the activity. As this Court emphasized in *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394–95, “the presence of religious content in a group’s message does not automatically render the entire activity religious in character,” but here, the religious content is not incidental—it is central. The Club’s curriculum is designed to convert children to Christianity through structured Bible study and evangelistic appeals. This is not moral education; it is religious indoctrination.

Further, the school’s decision was not based on the Club’s viewpoint, but on the nature of its activities. In *Board of Regents v. Ward*, 448 U.S. 268, 273 (1980), this Court rejected a challenge to a city’s ban on religious speech at a public forum, holding that “a government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech, including the exclusion of religious speakers, if the regulation is content-neutral and serves a significant governmental interest.” Milford’s interest in avoiding establishment of religion is unquestionably significant. *Employment Division v. Smith*, 494 U.S. 872, 879 (1990), reaffirmed that “the government’s interest in maintaining neutrality among religions and between religion and nonreligion is compelling.” Allowing religious groups to use public school facilities for worship-like activities risks creating the appearance of state endorsement of religion, which violates the Establishment Clause.

This Court has repeatedly upheld such limitations. In *Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier*, 484 U.S. 260, 270 (1988), the Court upheld a principal’s decision to censor student newspaper articles, noting that “school officials may exercise greater control over the content of student speech than private individuals.” Similarly, in *Baker v. General Motors Corp.*, 484 U.S. 104, 110 (1987), the Court upheld a company’s right to restrict employee speech in a workplace forum. These cases confirm that governments may set boundaries on speech in limited public forums to preserve institutional neutrality.

Finally, the Club’s argument that it must be allowed to express its religious viewpoint on morality is foreclosed by *Lamb’s Chapel*. There, the Court held that a school district could exclude a religious group from using school facilities even though the group’s message was about family values and child rearing.
