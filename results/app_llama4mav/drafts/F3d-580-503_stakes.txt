## Argument

I. The District Court Erred in Denying Winbush's Motion for a Fingerprint Identification Expert

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint identification expert was an abuse of discretion. The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to present a defense, which includes the right to expert assistance when necessary. See Ake v. Oklahoma, 470 U.S. 68, 76-77 (1985). In determining whether a defendant is entitled to expert assistance, courts consider whether the expert's testimony is necessary to present a viable defense. See United States v. Cravens, 275 F.3d 637, 639 (7th Cir. 2001).

Here, Winbush's defense relied heavily on challenging the government's evidence, particularly the lack of fingerprint evidence linking him to the contraband. The government's Notice of Expert Witnesses indicated that Michael Shay, a forensics examiner, would testify that no latent fingerprints of value were discovered on the physical evidence. Winbush's request for a fingerprint expert was necessary to rebut this testimony and provide an alternative explanation for the absence of fingerprints. See United States v. Kelly, 420 F.2d 26, 29 (2d Cir. 1969) (holding that a defendant is entitled to expert assistance when the government's expert testimony is critical to the prosecution's case).

The magistrate judge's denial of Winbush's motion was based on the untimeliness of the request, stating that it was "woefully late." However, the government's disclosure of its expert witnesses on October 3, 2007, just 40 days before trial, triggered Winbush's need for a rebuttal expert. See United States v. Stevens, 380 F. Supp. 2d 592, 597 (M.D. Pa. 2005) (holding that a defendant's request for expert assistance was timely when made within a reasonable time after the government's disclosure of its expert witnesses). Winbush filed his motion on November 8, 2007, just five days later, which is a reasonable time given the complexity of the case and the need to secure an expert.

The denial of Winbush's motion for a fingerprint expert prejudiced his defense, as he was unable to effectively challenge the government's evidence. See United States v. Frazier, 387 F.3d 1244, 1262 (11th Cir. 2004) (holding that the denial of a defendant's request for expert assistance was prejudicial when it prevented the defendant from presenting a viable defense). Therefore, the district court's denial of Winbush's motion was an abuse of discretion, and this Court should reverse.

II. The District Court Erred in Denying Winbush's Motion to Continue

The district court's denial of Winbush's motion to continue was also an abuse of discretion. A continuance is warranted when a defendant demonstrates that the denial will prejudice his defense. See United States v. Robbins, 197 F.3d 829, 846 (7th Cir. 1999). Here, Winbush's defense was prejudiced by the denial of his motion to continue, as he was unable to secure a fingerprint expert to rebut the government's evidence.

The government's disclosure of its expert witnesses on October 3, 2007, and Winbush's subsequent request for a fingerprint expert on November 8, 2007, demonstrate that Winbush acted diligently in seeking a continuance. See United States v. Issaghoolian, 42 F.3d 1395 (7th Cir. 1994) (unpublished) (holding that a defendant's request for a continuance was timely when made in response to the government's late disclosure of evidence). The district court's denial of Winbush's motion to continue, therefore, was an abuse of discretion.

## Conclusion

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court's judgment and remand for a new trial.
