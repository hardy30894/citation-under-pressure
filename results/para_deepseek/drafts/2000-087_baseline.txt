**ARGUMENT**

**THE POST-REMOVAL-PERIOD STATUTE DOES NOT AUTHORIZE THE ATTORNEY GENERAL TO DETAIN A REMOVABLE ALIEN INDEFINITELY BEYOND THE 90-DAY REMOVAL PERIOD.**

This Court has long recognized that statutes should be construed to avoid serious constitutional questions. *See Crowell v. Benson*, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932). Section 241(a)(6) of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), provides that certain aliens ordered removed “may be detained beyond the removal period.” The statute does not specify any temporal limit on such post-removal-period detention. Read literally, it could authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely. Such a reading, however, would raise serious constitutional concerns under the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

**I. INDEFINITE DETENTION OF A REMOVABLE ALIEN RAISES SERIOUS CONSTITUTIONAL CONCERNS.**

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law. This protection extends to all persons within the United States, including aliens who have been admitted to the country. *See Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228, 238 (1896). Petitioner Kestutis Zadvydas is a resident alien who has lived in the United States since 1956. His continued detention after the 90-day removal period expired, with no realistic prospect of removal, amounts to an indefinite deprivation of liberty.

This Court has consistently held that civil detention must be limited in duration and justified by a legitimate governmental purpose. *See Foucha v. Louisiana*, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992) (holding that a state may not indefinitely detain a person found not guilty by reason of insanity without a showing of dangerousness). Similarly, in *United States v. Salerno*, 481 U.S. 739, 747 (1987), this Court upheld pretrial detention only because it was limited to a specific period and served a compelling regulatory purpose. Indefinite detention, by contrast, “would raise a serious constitutional problem.” *Zadvydas v. Davis*, 533 U.S. 678, 690 (2001).

The government’s interest in detaining aliens pending removal is to ensure their presence at the time of removal. *See id.* at 699. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, that interest evaporates. Continued detention without any meaningful prospect of removal becomes punitive in nature, violating the core principle that “the Constitution deals with substance, not shadows.” *Cummings v. Missouri*, 71 U.S. 277, 325 (1867).

**II. THE STATUTE MUST BE CONSTRUED TO INCLUDE AN IMPLICIT “REASONABLE TIME” LIMITATION.**

To avoid the serious constitutional concerns raised by indefinite detention, this Court should construe § 1231(a)(6) to authorize detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure the alien’s removal. *See Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 689 (“[W]e construe the statute to contain an implicit ‘reasonable time’ limitation.”). This interpretation gives effect to Congress’s intent while respecting constitutional limits.

The statute’s purpose is to facilitate removal, not to punish. *See id.* at 699. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention ceases to serve that purpose. A “reasonable time” limitation ensures that detention is tied to the statute’s core objective. This Court has applied similar limiting constructions in other contexts to avoid constitutional problems. *See, e.g., Clark v. Martinez*, 543 U.S. 371, 380 (2005) (applying the same reasonable-time limitation to inadmissible aliens).

**III. THE SIX-MONTH PRESUMPTION PROVIDES A WORKABLE STANDARD.**

This Court should adopt a presumptively reasonable period of six months for post-removal-period detention. *See Zadvydas*, 533 U.S. at 701. After six months, if the alien provides good reason to believe that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the government must rebut that showing with evidence. *See id.* This framework balances the government’s interest in removal with the alien’s fundamental liberty interest.

In this case, petitioner has been detained for more than six months after the removal period expired. Germany and Lithuania have both refused to accept him, and there is no evidence that any other country will do so. The government has failed to demonstrate a significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future. Under the six-month presumption, petitioner’s continued detention is unreasonable and unauthorized.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that § 1231(a)(6) does not authorize indefinite detention and that petitioner is entitled to release.
