The Court should reject the government’s attempt to transform a timely assessment against a partnership into an unlimited collection period against individual partners. The Internal Revenue Code ties the ten-year collection period to an assessment made against the relevant “taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a); see 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 6501(a). A partnership and its individual partners are separate taxpayers under the Code. 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). Thus, a valid assessment against the partnership extends the collection period only for the partnership itself, not for each partner who was never separately assessed. That is the straightforward reading adopted by the Ninth Circuit, and it is correct. United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. 114, 119-20 (2004) (describing the assessment as one against the partnership, not the partners individually).

1. The statutory text forecloses the government’s position.

Section 6502(a) permits collection “within 10 years after the assessment of the tax.” 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). But “assessment” is the recording of the liability of “the taxpayer.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The Code defines “taxpayer” as “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” includes “an individual” and a “partnership.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(1), (14). The plain consequence is that the government must assess the taxpayer from whom it seeks to collect. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-23.

That principle is consistent with longstanding tax law. The Court has recognized that assessment is the act that “brings into being the tax lien” and authorizes collection remedies against the assessed taxpayer. Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935); United States v. Kales, 314 U.S. 186, 194 (1941). Where Congress wants a collection period to run from an assessment, it does so against the assessed person, not some other potentially liable party. See, e.g., 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a).

2. A partnership assessment does not substitute for an assessment of individual partners.

The government argues that because partners are jointly and severally liable under state law, a partnership assessment should automatically authorize collection from each partner. But liability and assessment are different. The Code permits the government to collect only after it has followed the statutory procedures that make the liability administratively enforceable against the person from whom collection is sought. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-24; see also Laing v. United States, 423 U.S. 161, 170 n.13 (1976) (assessment is the formal recording of tax liability and “the basis for the tax lien”).

Ninth Circuit precedent pointed in the same direction. In United States v. Coson, 286 F.2d 453, 458-63 (9th Cir. 1961), the court approved the principle that a valid assessment must identify the taxpayer against whom collection is pursued. And in Young v. Riddell, 283 F.2d 909, 910 (9th Cir. 1960), the court’s statement that the partner was “personally liable” for partnership debts was offered in the context of denying a refund after payment; it did not hold that the government may collect from an unassessed partner without first taking action against that partner. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 124-25 (explaining the limited reach of Young).

3. State-law partner liability does not enlarge the federal assessment period.

The government’s resort to state partnership law also fails. Although California law makes partners jointly and severally liable, it does not eliminate the need for a judgment or other enforceable process against the partner before personal assets may be reached. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c); see also In re: Galletti, 541 U.S. at 126-27 (noting California law requires a judgment against the partner). Federal tax collection does not bypass those requirements simply because the underlying debt arose from taxes. See Commissioner v. Stern, 357 U.S. 39, 45-47 (1958) (state law determines substantive liability where federal law does not supply it).

The government’s theory would also undercut the statute of limitations Congress enacted in § 6501(a). If assessment of one liable entity indefinitely preserved collection claims against every other potentially liable person, the Code’s three-year assessment limit would lose meaning. Statutes of limitations are to be enforced according to their terms. Badaracco v. Commissioner, 464 U.S. 386, 391-92 (1984).

4. The government’s contrary authorities are distinguishable.

The government relies on cases permitting collection from partners or transferees where the government timely pursued the proper party or where assessment issues were not before the court. Those cases do not authorize the shortcut urged here. See, e.g., United States v. Wright, 57 F.3d 561, 562-63 (7th Cir. 1995). The Supreme Court in Galletti made clear that Wright did not address whether a separate assessment was necessary before collecting from individual partners. 541 U.S. at 125.

In short, the IRS assessed only the partnership within the limitations period. It did not assess the individual partners within three years, and § 6502(a)’s ten-year collection period never began to run against them. The judgment should be affirmed.
