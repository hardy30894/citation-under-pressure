**ARGUMENT**

**I. The plain text of OCSLA § 1333(b) requires that the injury, not merely the operations, occur on the outer Continental Shelf.**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore Act coverage only to “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The Ninth Circuit erred by reading the phrase “as the result of” to eliminate any geographic limitation on the injury itself. The statute’s structure compels the opposite conclusion.

Section 1333(b) contains two distinct geographic modifiers: “operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” and “injury occurring as the result of” those operations. If Congress intended coverage for all injuries causally related to shelf operations regardless of location, the phrase “occurring as the result of” would be surplusage—the statute could simply have covered injuries “resulting from operations on the outer Continental Shelf.” Congress’s choice to include “occurring” confirms that the injury’s location matters. See *Duncan v. Walker*, 533 U.S. 167, 174 (2001) (courts should give effect to every word of a statute).

This Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), confirms that OCSLA’s coverage is “determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” *Id.* at 219. The Court expressly noted that § 1333(b) “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court did not suggest that § 1333(b) eliminated the situs requirement; it merely added a status requirement to the existing geographic limitation. The Ninth Circuit’s dismissal of this language as “unconsidered dictum” disregards this Court’s considered analysis of OCSLA’s overall structure.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s situs-of-injury test correctly interprets § 1333(b).**

In *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the Fifth Circuit held that OCSLA claimants must show both that the injury occurred on the outer Continental Shelf and that it resulted from shelf operations. *Id.* at 362. The court reasoned that OCSLA’s “explicit geographic limitation” requires that the injury occur on the shelf itself. *Id.* at 361. This interpretation respects the statute’s text and avoids the boundless coverage the Ninth Circuit’s approach invites.

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), adopted a “but for” test that would cover any injury with any causal connection to shelf operations—including a worker injured in a car accident while commuting to a helicopter. *Id.* at 811. That approach reads the geographic limitation out of the statute entirely. Congress could not have intended OCSLA to displace state workers’ compensation schemes for every injury remotely connected to offshore operations, no matter how far inland it occurs.

**III. The substantial nexus test adopted by the Ninth Circuit lacks statutory support and invites inconsistent application.**

The Ninth Circuit held that OCSLA covers any injury with “a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf.” That standard appears nowhere in § 1333(b). The statute does not use the word “nexus” or any similar term. By importing a vague, multi-factor test, the Ninth Circuit created precisely the kind of “inconsistent coverage” this Court warned against in *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 425 (1985).

The substantial nexus test also conflicts with OCSLA’s purpose. Congress enacted OCSLA to provide a uniform legal regime for activities on the outer Continental Shelf, not to create a new federal workers’ compensation program for onshore injuries. See *Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 365-66 (1969) (OCSLA’s purpose is to treat the shelf as “an area of exclusive federal jurisdiction”). The Ninth Circuit’s test would extend federal jurisdiction far beyond the shelf, displacing state law for injuries with only a tenuous connection to offshore operations.

**IV. Valladolid’s injury occurred on land, not on the outer Continental Shelf, and therefore does not satisfy § 1333(b).**

Juan Valladolid was killed at Pacific Operations’s onshore oil-processing facility, located 250-300 feet from the coast. The facility is not on the outer Continental Shelf, which begins three miles from the coastline. 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). Even under the Ninth Circuit’s substantial nexus test, the connection between Valladolid’s death and shelf operations is too attenuated. He was not injured while working on a platform or while traveling to or from one. He was killed while consolidating scrap metal that had been transported to shore weeks or months earlier. That is not the kind of injury Congress intended OCSLA to cover.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Section 1333(b) requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. Because Valladolid’s injury did not, his widow is not entitled to OCSLA benefits.
