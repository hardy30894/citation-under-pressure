The district court erred in granting summary judgment without an evidentiary hearing. When a habeas petitioner alleges facts that, if proved, would entitle him to relief, a hearing is required. Townsend v. Sain, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963). Riddle’s allegations concerning the prosecutor’s testimony and subsequent argument satisfy that standard.

A prosecutor who becomes a witness in the case he is trying deprives the accused of due process. The dual role permits the government’s representative to place his own credibility before the jury while simultaneously urging that credibility upon them. Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935). Here the district attorney testified to rebut Riddle’s account of threats made at arraignment, then resumed prosecution and permitted his assistant to argue that the testimony proved Riddle a liar. Such conduct is “inconsistent with the fair administration of justice.” Mooney v. Holohan, 294 U.S. 103, 112 (1935).

The resulting prejudice cannot be dismissed as harmless. When the prosecutor’s personal credibility is injected into the trial, the fundamental fairness of the proceeding is destroyed. See Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963). The state courts never conducted a hearing on this claim; the federal district court therefore could not properly resolve it on the existing record. Townsend, 372 U.S. at 313.

Riddle was also denied the effective assistance of counsel guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment. Gideon v. Wainwright, 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963). Counsel failed to object to the prosecutor’s continued participation after testifying and failed to develop the record concerning the effect of that testimony on the jury. These omissions were not strategic choices but “resulted in the deprivation of a fair trial.” Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 57 (1932).

Because the state courts provided no opportunity to litigate these constitutional claims, Riddle has not deliberately bypassed available procedures. Fay v. Noia, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). The district court therefore possessed both the power and the duty to hold an evidentiary hearing before denying relief. The judgment must be reversed and the case remanded for that hearing.
