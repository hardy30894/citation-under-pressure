# ARGUMENT

## I. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ADEA BECAUSE CONGRESS DID NOT CLEARLY MANIFEST AN INTENT TO INTRUDE UPON THE STATES' TRADITIONAL AUTHORITY TO ESTABLISH QUALIFICATIONS FOR THEIR JUDGES

### A. The Plain Statement Rule Requires Clear Congressional Intent Before Federal Law Is Read to Displace States' Authority Over Core Governmental Functions

This Court has long recognized that "if Congress intends to alter the usual constitutional balance between the States and the Federal Government, it must make its intention to do so unmistakably clear in the language of the statute." *Atascadero State Hospital v. Scanlon*, 473 U.S. 234, 242 (1985). This plain statement requirement applies with particular force where, as here, Congress legislates in an area of traditional state sovereignty—the qualifications and tenure of a state's own judiciary. As this Court explained in *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971), "in traditionally sensitive areas, such as legislation affecting the federal balance, the requirement of clear statement assures that the legislature has in fact faced, and intended to bring into issue, the critical matters involved."

The authority of a State to determine the qualifications of those who govern it lies at the heart of state sovereignty. This Court affirmed in *Sugarman v. Dougall*, 413 U.S. 634, 647 (1973), that a State has a legitimate interest in "defining and limiting the qualifications of persons who would exercise the functions of an important non-elective executive, legislative, and judicial office." Few decisions implicate this interest more directly than a State's determination of how long its judges may serve.

### B. Missouri's Judges Fall Within the ADEA's "Policymaking Level" Exception

The ADEA expressly excludes from its definition of "employee" any "appointee on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri's appointed judges fall within this exception, and this Court should affirm that holding.

Judicial decisionmaking is not merely mechanical application of settled rules. As the First Circuit recognized in the parallel case of *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), "'[p]olicymaking' is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." That court further observed that the discretion exercised by judges is "closely aligned with an interest referred to by phrases such as 'exercise of discretion' and 'exercise of judgment,'" which are "indisputably descriptive of most of the performance" of judges. *Id.*

Missouri's judiciary exercises substantial administrative and rulemaking authority beyond case adjudication. The Missouri Supreme Court, for example, adopted a wholesale revision of state tort law in *Gustafson v. Benda*, 661 S.W.2d 11 (Mo. 1983) (en banc), abandoning the doctrine of last clear chance in favor of comparative fault. Such authority is the essence of policymaking, not merely dispute resolution.

Reading the exception to exclude judges entirely, as Petitioners urge, would produce an implausible result: in every state employing an appointed judiciary, no judge would ever qualify for any of the ADEA's four enumerated exemptions, even though elected judges are categorically exempted. Congress is unlikely to have intended so anomalous a distinction based solely on a State's chosen method of judicial selection—a method that affects nothing about the substantive powers judges exercise once in office.

### C. The Rule of Lenity for Preemption Confirms This Result

Because the ADEA's application to state judges would work a significant intrusion on state sovereignty, this Court should resolve any ambiguity in favor of state authority. This Court's decision in *Will v. Michigan Department of State Police*, 491 U.S. 58, 65 (1989), reinforces that Congress must speak with clarity when it wishes to alter the federal balance, particularly regarding a State's control over its own governmental structure.

## II. MISSOURI'S MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SATISFIES RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW UNDER THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE

### A. Age Is Not a Suspect Classification, and Rational Basis Review Applies

This Court has squarely held that age is not a suspect classification triggering heightened scrutiny. In *Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam), this Court held that a mandatory retirement age for police officers need only satisfy rational basis review, explaining that "[t]his Court's decisions give no support to the proposition that a right of governmental employment per se is fundamental." The same principle governs here: Petitioners possess no fundamental right to continued judicial service past a lawfully established age.

### B. Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Age Rationally Serves Legitimate State Interests

Under rational basis review, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision need only bear a rational relationship to a legitimate state purpose. This standard is easily satisfied. As the Seventh Circuit recognized in *Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627, 629 (7th Cir. 1979), "it is rational to treat judges differently from other officials on the ground that the work of judges makes unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode," and mandatory retirement "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges." The Third Circuit likewise upheld a similar provision in *Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980), recognizing that such a rule "eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged . . . judges."

The Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court's reasoning in *Apkin v. Treasurer and Receiver General*, 401 Mass. 427, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (1988), is equally instructive: mandatory retirement "eliminates the anguish, time, delay, expense, and embarrassment of the supervision and removal of older judges of failing competence," while also fostering generational diversity on the bench.

This Court need not agree with every policy rationale to sustain the provision. As this Court held in *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 1, 35 (1973), rational basis review does not require perfect legislative wisdom—only a plausible connection between means and ends. Missouri's judgment that a fixed retirement age serves the orderly administration of justice is eminently rational.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
