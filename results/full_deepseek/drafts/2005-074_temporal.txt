**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 241(a)(5) Does Not Apply to Aliens Who Reentered the United States Before Its Enactment**

The principle that statutes should not be applied retroactively unless Congress clearly indicates such intent is deeply embedded in American law. *See United States v. Heth*, 3 Cranch 399, 413 (1806) (Marshall, C.J.) (“It is a principle which has been uniformly and firmly maintained in this court, that an act of Congress ought never to be construed to be retrospective, unless the words are so clear, positive, and unequivocal, that no other meaning can be ascribed to them.”). This canon of construction ensures that individuals are given fair notice of the legal consequences of their actions. *See Society for the Propagation of the Gospel v. Wheeler*, 22 F. Cas. 756, 767 (C.C.D.N.H. 1814) (Story, J.) (“Retrospective laws are justly regarded as founded in injustice and oppression.”).

Section 241(a)(5) provides that a prior deportation order “shall be reinstated from its original date” if an alien “reenters the United States illegally after having been removed.” 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5). Petitioner reentered the United States in 1982—fifteen years before the statute’s effective date in 1997. The statute does not contain any language expressly applying to reentries that occurred before its enactment. When Congress intended such application in other immigration provisions, it specifically said so. *See, e.g.,* Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996, § 309(c)(1) (applying certain provisions to “aliens” who were “in exclusion or deportation proceedings before” the effective date).

Where Congress uses “precise and unambiguous language” in some statutes but omits it in others, this Court presumes the omission was intentional. *See Gooch v. United States*, 297 U.S. 124, 128 (1936). The lack of such language in Section 241(a)(5) confirms that Congress did not intend the statute to apply to pre-enactment reentries.

**II. Applying Section 241(a)(5) to Pre-Enactment Conduct Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

Even if Section 241(a)(5) could be construed to apply to pre-enactment reentries, such application would violate the “well-settled” presumption against retroactive legislation. *See United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co.*, 213 U.S. 366, 408 (1909). A statute is impermissibly retroactive if it “impairs rights a party possessed when he acted, increases a party’s liability for past conduct, or imposes new duties with respect to transactions already completed.” *See Reynolds v. United States*, 292 U.S. 443, 449 (1934).

When petitioner reentered the United States in 1982, the law provided that a prior deportation order could not be reinstated. A “reentry after deportation” was a criminal offense, but an alien who reentered could still seek discretionary relief, such as adjustment of status based on marriage to a U.S. citizen. *See* 8 U.S.C. § 1182(h) (1982) (waiving certain grounds of inadmissibility). Petitioner had a “vested right” to apply for such relief at the time he reentered. *See Sturges v. Crowninshield*, 17 U.S. 122, 197 (1819) (protecting vested rights from retroactive impairment).

Section 241(a)(5) retroactively eliminated that right by barring all relief for aliens with reinstated removal orders. This Court has long held that “a statute shall not have a retrospective operation, where it would affect the vested rights of parties.” *See United States v. The Schooner Peggy*, 5 U.S. 103, 110 (1801). By foreclosing petitioner’s opportunity to adjust his status based on a marriage that occurred after the statute’s enactment, the government imposes a new legal consequence on conduct completed in 1982. That is precisely the type of retroactive effect this Court has consistently condemned. *See Calder v. Bull*, 3 U.S. 386, 391 (1798) (Chase, J.) (forbidding retroactive laws that “take away or impair vested rights”).

The Tenth Circuit erred in holding that petitioner had no “protectable expectation” of adjusting his status. Petitioner’s marriage to a U.S. citizen in 2001 occurred after the statute’s enactment, but his ability to seek adjustment depended on his 1982 reentry—conduct that preceded the statute. A retroactive statute “cannot be allowed to affect the legal force of a transaction which was completed before it was passed.” *See The Surprise*, 22 F. Cas. 827, 828 (C.C.D. Mass. 1861) (Story, J.). Because Section 241(a)(5) attaches new, severe consequences to petitioner’s pre-enactment reentry, its application to him is impermissibly retroactive.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, this Court should hold that Section 241(a)(5) does not apply to aliens who reentered the United States before its enactment, or, alternatively, that such application violates the presumption against retroactivity.
