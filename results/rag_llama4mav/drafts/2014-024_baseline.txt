The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because it failed to require proof of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten. The statute's text and the First Amendment's protection of speech necessitate a subjective intent standard for "true threats."

The Third Circuit erred in relying on its prior decision in United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee it would be interpreted as a threat. Although the Supreme Court's decision in Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), addressed the "true threats" exception to the First Amendment, it did not explicitly overrule the Third Circuit's objective intent standard in Kosma. However, the plurality opinion in Black emphasized that the speaker's intent is crucial in determining whether a statement constitutes a true threat, stating that "‘[t]rue threats’ encompass those statements where the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intention to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals" (Black, 538 U.S. at 359).

Requiring a subjective intent to threaten is consistent with the principle that the First Amendment protects speech unless it is integral to criminal conduct or likely to incite imminent lawless action. See Scheidler v. National Organization for Women, Inc., 547 U.S. 9 (2006). An objective standard, on the other hand, risks chilling protected speech, as speakers may self-censor to avoid being mistaken for a threatener. See Gormley v. Director, Connecticut State Department of Adult Probation, 449 U.S. 1023 (1980) (White, J., dissenting).

Furthermore, a subjective intent requirement aligns with the traditional mens rea requirement for criminal liability. See Chevron U. S. A. Inc. v. Echazabal, 536 U.S. 73 (2002). It ensures that individuals are not convicted for speech they did not intend to be threatening.

The government's interest in protecting individuals from the fear of violence can be achieved through a subjective intent standard. Prosecutors can prove a defendant's intent to threaten through circumstantial evidence, such as the context and tone of the statement. See Arizona v. Fulminante, 499 U.S. 279 (1991).

In conclusion, to preserve the First Amendment's protection of speech and to ensure that individuals are not unfairly convicted for unintended speech, a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit's decision should be reversed, and Elonis' conviction should be vacated due to the erroneous jury instruction that allowed his conviction under an objective intent standard.
