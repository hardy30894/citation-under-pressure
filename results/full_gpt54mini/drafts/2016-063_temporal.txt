Petitioner’s case belongs in district court, not the Federal Circuit. The controlling statute directs that “cases of discrimination” shall be reviewed under the Civil Service Reform Act’s special mixed-case route. 5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(2). That route exists because Congress created a single forum for employee claims that combine an adverse action with discrimination. See Johnson v. Robison, 415 U.S. 361, 373-74 (1974) (recognizing that statutes should be read to avoid unnecessary jurisdictional fragmentation). But even before the modern mixed-case statutes, this Court insisted that courts not construe jurisdictional provisions to deny meaningful review where Congress provided a remedial path. See Shapiro v. United States, 335 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1948).

The Federal Circuit’s rule below cannot stand because it turns the forum question on the Board’s own threshold jurisdictional characterization, rather than on the nature of the employee’s claim. That approach conflicts with the basic principle that jurisdiction should not depend on administrative labels alone. See Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 60-63 (1932) (distinguishing agency factfinding from the judicial responsibility to determine legal limits of administrative power); Ex parte Bakelite Corp., 279 U.S. 438, 449-50 (1929) (recognizing that special statutory review provisions must be read in light of the statute’s design). If an employee alleges discrimination and seeks review of an employment action made adverse by the agency, the suit remains a discrimination case for judicial-review purposes even if the agency first says it lacks jurisdiction. The contrary rule would let the Board decide the forum by deciding the threshold issue against its own jurisdiction. See Chicago, B. & Q.R. Co. v. Babcock, 204 U.S. 585, 593-94 (1907) (courts look to substance, not form, in applying review statutes).

This Court has long rejected reading remedial statutes in a cramped way that defeats their purpose. See United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 309-10 (1941) (statutes protecting federal rights are construed to effectuate their purpose); United States v. Datson, 53 U.S. 253, 259-60 (1851) (jurisdictional provisions should not be read to nullify the rights Congress created). The mixed-case statute was enacted to preserve an employee’s discrimination claims while allowing review of agency discipline. A rule that sends jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit fragments that unified remedial scheme and deprives the employee of the district court forum Congress selected for discrimination litigation. See Lane v. Wilson, 307 U.S. 268, 275 (1939) (“The broad purpose” of a statute controls its application).

Nor is there any sound basis for treating a jurisdictional dismissal differently from any other Board dismissal when the employee’s petition alleges discrimination. The question for review is whether the employee claims to have been aggrieved by an adverse action and alleges prohibited discrimination; if so, the case falls within the statutory discrimination-review channel. See Stainback v. Mo Hock Ke Lok Po, 336 U.S. 368, 378-79 (1949) (courts should construe statutes to avoid “technical” distinctions that defeat substantive rights). A rule that depends on whether the Board dismisses for “jurisdiction” or “procedure” elevates terminology over Congress’s chosen division of labor between district courts and the Federal Circuit. See FTC v. Morton Salt Co., 334 U.S. 37, 44-45 (1948) (administrative procedure should not be allowed to obscure statutory rights).

The better reading is that district court review applies whenever the employee alleges discrimination in connection with an adverse action otherwise within the MSPB’s review structure. That reading follows the ordinary rule that ambiguous jurisdictional statutes should be construed to preserve review rather than to foreclose it. See Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 370-71 (1886) (courts must guard against constructions that defeat equal protection and lawful remedies). Because Perry’s petition alleges discrimination and seeks review of the agency’s adverse action, the proper forum is the district court.

For those reasons, the judgment transferring this case to the Federal Circuit should be reversed.
