ARGUMENT

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON EPPS’S ADA AND MHRA CLAIMS BECAUSE A REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT PINE LAWN REGARDED EPPS AS DISABLED

Summary judgment is appropriate only where no genuine issue of material fact exists. The district court held that Epps could not show Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. That holding cannot be reconciled with the record.

A plaintiff proceeds under the “regarded as” prong of the ADA when the employer “mistakenly believes that [the employee] has a physical impairment that substantially limits a major life activity” or mistakenly believes that an existing, nonlimiting impairment is substantially limiting. Sutton v. United Air Lines, Inc., 527 U.S. 471, 489 (1999). The inquiry focuses not on the employee’s actual condition, but on the employer’s state of mind. See Murphy v. United Parcel Serv., Inc., 527 U.S. 516, 521-22 (1999) (explaining that “regarded as” claims turn on the employer’s perception, not on any objective medical assessment). Working itself qualifies as a major life activity, and an employer regards an employee as substantially limited in working when it believes the employee is foreclosed from “a broad class of jobs,” not merely a single position. Wooten v. Farmland Foods, Inc., 58 F.3d 382, 386 (8th Cir. 1995).

The record here supplies exactly that evidence. Chief Hardy’s September 1999 and March 2000 memoranda to the Mayor and Board of Aldermen relied repeatedly on Dr. Mirkin’s note that Epps had degenerative disk disease and “would very likely need to seek a different type of occupation.” Hardy went further in his second memorandum, concluding that Epps “would never be able to function as a regular officer.” That is not a determination that Epps could not perform the specific tasks of a Pine Lawn patrol officer; it is a categorical judgment that Epps was unfit for any comparable law-enforcement position—precisely the “broad class of jobs” showing this Court required in Wooten, 58 F.3d at 386.

This Court has recognized that an employer’s mere awareness of an impairment is not enough; the plaintiff must show the employer believed the impairment was substantially limiting. Land v. Baptist Med. Ctr., 164 F.3d 423, 425-26 (8th Cir. 1999). But Hardy’s memoranda go beyond mere awareness—they expressly conclude that Epps’s impairment permanently barred him from working as a police officer anywhere, not simply that he needed accommodation in his current role. That is the kind of broad occupational foreclosure this Court found sufficient to create a jury question in Berg v. Norand Corp., 169 F.3d 1140, 1145-46 (8th Cir. 1999), where the employer’s own statements about the plaintiff’s incapacity to work supported an inference of a “regarded as” disability.

The district court’s reliance on the narrow construction of “substantially limits” articulated in Toyota Motor Manufacturing, Kentucky, Inc. v. Williams, 534 U.S. 184, 198 (2002), was misplaced. Toyota addressed whether a plaintiff’s actual impairment substantially limited major life activities central to daily living; it did not disturb the settled rule that an employer’s own assessment—right or wrong—governs a “regarded as” claim. Sutton, 527 U.S. at 489. Here, Pine Lawn’s decisionmaker did not merely note a medical restriction; he predicated Epps’s termination on the conclusion that Epps could never again function as a police officer. That evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Epps, permits a reasonable jury to find Pine Lawn regarded him as disabled. Because the MHRA disability standard mirrors the ADA’s, the same evidence defeats summary judgment on Epps’s state-law claim.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN HOLDING THAT MISSOURI SOVEREIGN IMMUNITY BARRED EPPS’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION RETALIATION AND EMOTIONAL DISTRESS CLAIMS

Missouri sovereign immunity shields municipalities from tort liability unless immunity is waived by statute. Kanagawa v. State ex rel. Freeman, 685 S.W.2d 831, 835 (Mo. banc 1985) (holding immunity is the rule and statutory waivers are to be strictly, though not so narrowly as to defeat their purpose, construed). One such waiver arises when a political subdivision purchases liability insurance: Missouri law provides that the purchase of insurance operates as a waiver of sovereign immunity to the extent of the coverage obtained. Green v. Denison, 738 S.W.2d 861, 865 (Mo. banc 1987) (recognizing that a public entity’s procurement of liability insurance constitutes a waiver of sovereign immunity up to the policy limits). The record establishes that Pine Lawn carried workers’ compensation and liability insurance covering Epps’s employment; the district court never considered whether that coverage effected a waiver under Green.

Moreover, Epps’s retaliation claim arises under a specific statutory cause of action, Mo. Rev. Stat. § 287.780, which creates liability against “any employer” who discharges an employee for exercising workers’ compensation rights. That statute does not exempt municipal employers, and the district court’s reflexive application of common-law sovereign immunity—developed to protect governmental units from traditional tort liability—improperly extended immunity to a statutory remedy the legislature intended to apply broadly. See Kanagawa, 685 S.W.2d at 835 (immunity waivers must be construed to effectuate legislative purpose). At minimum, genuine factual questions regarding the scope of Pine Lawn’s insurance coverage preclude summary judgment on both the retaliation and intentional-infliction claims.

CONCLUSION

For these reasons, Epps respectfully requests that this Court reverse the district court’s grant of summary judgment and remand for further proceedings.
