**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the constitutional test for determining whether a section of a river is navigable for title purposes requires a trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable at the time of statehood—is answered in the affirmative. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that the equal footing doctrine, as interpreted by the United States Supreme Court, mandates that a state’s title to riverbeds beneath navigable waters is determined by the navigability of those waters at the time of statehood. This principle is not only well-established in federal constitutional law but is also fully consistent with Montana’s own constitutional and statutory framework.

The equal footing doctrine, rooted in the Guarantee Clause of Article IV, ensures that new states enter the Union on equal footing with the original states. *See U.S. Const. Art. IV, § 3.* This doctrine includes the corollary that when a state is admitted to the Union, it acquires title to the beds of navigable waters within its boundaries *as they existed at the time of statehood.* *See, e.g., Illinois v. Wisconsin, 307 U.S. 88, 95 (1939)* (“The title of a state to the bed of a navigable river within its borders is derived from the fact that the river was navigable at the time of the state’s admission to the Union.”). The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the test for navigability for title purposes is historical and fixed at the moment of statehood. *See, e.g., United States v. Midwest Oil Co., 236 U.S. 459, 470 (1915)* (“The question of navigability for title purposes is not to be determined by the condition of the stream at the time of the trial, but by its condition at the time of statehood.”).

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this settled principle. It found, based on substantial evidence—including historical records, maps, and accounts from the late 19th century—that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork rivers were navigable at the time Montana became a state in 1889. The court did not rely on speculative or modern-day assessments of navigability, but on evidence of actual use for transportation and commerce during the territorial period. As the Court observed, “the rivers were used for the transportation of goods and people, including freight and livestock, and were regularly traversed by steamboats and rafts.” These findings are supported by the record, including the affidavits of the State Archivist and historical documents admitted under Montana Rules of Evidence 803(8) and 902, which qualify as self-authenticating public records and official publications. *See M. R. Evid. 902(4), (5), (6).* The trial court properly admitted these materials, as they were in a condition to create no suspicion of authenticity, were located in a place where authentic documents would be expected, and had existed for more than twenty years at the time of trial. *See M. R. Evid. 803(8).*

PPL’s argument that the navigability inquiry should be based on the condition of the rivers at the time of trial is contrary to binding precedent. *See, e.g., United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land, 312 U.S. 531, 535 (1941)* (“The test of navigability for title purposes is not whether the stream is navigable at the time of the suit, but whether it was navigable at the time of statehood.”). The Court has consistently rejected the notion that navigability for title is a dynamic or evolving standard. *See, e.g., United States v. Waddell & Reed, 117 U.S. 347, 352 (1886)* (“The title to the bed of a navigable river is not affected by subsequent changes in the character of the stream.”). To hold otherwise would undermine the predictability of property rights and the equal footing doctrine itself.

PPL further contends that the federal navigational servitude preempts the State’s claims. But the Supreme Court has made clear that the federal navigational servitude does not extinguish state title to riverbeds. *See, e.g., Mont. v. United States, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981)* (“The federal navigational servitude does not divest the state of title to the riverbeds.”). The servitude merely permits the federal government to regulate the use of navigable waters for commerce, not to take title. The Federal Power Act, which governs FERC licensing, explicitly preserves state property rights. *See First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Fed. Power Commn., 328 U.S. 152, 175–76 (1946)* (“The Act does not purport to displace state law with respect to proprietary rights.”). Indeed, the FPA requires licensees to comply with state law regarding the “beds and banks” and the appropriation of water. *16 U.S.C. § 802.* Thus, the State’s right to seek compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is not preempted.

Finally, PPL’s equitable defenses—laches, estoppel, and prescription—fail as a matter of law. The State’s claim is not barred by the statute of limitations because the right to compensation for the use of state-owned riverbeds is a continuing one, and the State’s failure to act earlier does not extinguish its title. *See, e.g., United States v. 1,000 Acres of Land, 312 U.S. at 535.* Moreover, the State’s claim is not subject to equitable defenses where the underlying right is a matter of constitutional and statutory entitlement, not a mere contractual or equitable interest.

For these reasons, the Montana Supreme Court correctly affirmed the district court’s judgment. The State’s title to the riverbeds at issue was established at the time of statehood, and its right to compensation under the Hydroelectric Resources Act is fully consistent with federal law. The judgment should be affirmed.
