**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Concluding That the Commercial Activity Exception to the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) Does Not Apply Here.**

The FSIA confers immunity on foreign states from suit in United States courts unless one of its enumerated exceptions applies. *See* 28 U.S.C. § 1604. The commercial activity exception, at issue here, provides that a foreign state is not immune from jurisdiction in any case:

> in which the action is based upon a commercial activity carried on in the United States by the foreign state; or upon an act performed in the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere; or upon an act outside the territory of the United States in connection with a commercial activity of the foreign state elsewhere and that act causes a direct effect in the United States.

28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The district court erred in holding that neither the expropriation of Beg’s property nor the subsequent conduct of the Pakistani government constituted “commercial activity” under the FSIA, and that the alleged conduct did not cause a “direct effect” in the United States.

**A. The Expropriation and Subsequent Conduct Constitute Commercial Activity Under the FSIA.**

The FSIA defines “commercial activity” as “either a regular course of commercial conduct or a particular commercial transaction or act.” 28 U.S.C. § 1603(d). The Supreme Court has instructed that the commercial character of an act is determined by its “nature,” not its “purpose.” *Republic of Argentina v. Weltover, Inc.*, 504 U.S. 607, 611 (1992). Thus, even if the expropriation of Beg’s land was undertaken for a public or sovereign purpose, the act itself—taking property for use—is commercial in nature. *See id.* at 614 (noting that a foreign state’s issuance of bonds, though undertaken to stabilize the economy, was commercial because the act of issuing debt securities is an “ordinary commercial transaction”).

Here, the Pakistani government expropriated Beg’s land and transferred it to the military for use as housing. These acts—taking property and transferring it for use—are quintessentially commercial in nature. *See Permanent Mission of India to U.N. v. City of New York*, 551 U.S. 193, 200 (2007) (holding that the seizure and sale of property to satisfy tax liens was a commercial act). The district court’s contrary conclusion misapprehends the commercial nature of the acts at issue.

**B. The Alleged Conduct Caused a Direct Effect in the United States.**

The FSIA’s commercial activity exception also applies where the action is based upon an act outside the United States that “causes a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has explained that a “direct effect” is one that is “immediate and foreseeable,” *Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618, and that the effect must be sufficiently “substantial” to warrant jurisdiction. *See Saudi Arabia v. Nelson*, 507 U.S. 349, 364 (1993).

Beg alleges that the Pakistani government sent a representative to the United States to negotiate an exchange of property and that this representative offered him an alternative parcel of land. These negotiations, occurring in the United States, constitute a direct effect of the Pakistani government’s commercial activity. *See, e.g., Gilson v. Republic of Ireland*, 682 F.2d 1022, 1026 (D.C. Cir. 1982) (holding that a foreign state’s commercial negotiations in the United States caused a direct effect sufficient to satisfy the FSIA). Moreover, Beg’s decision to abandon further litigation in Pakistan and seek compensation in the United States is a foreseeable and substantial consequence of the Pakistani government’s actions. *See Weltover*, 504 U.S. at 618.

**II. The District Court Erred in Dismissing the Action for Lack of Subject Matter Jurisdiction.**

Because the commercial activity exception applies, the district court erred in dismissing Beg’s action for lack of subject matter jurisdiction. The FSIA’s immunity provisions are jurisdictional in nature, and a district court’s failure to apply an exception deprives it of jurisdiction. *See Verlinden B.V. v. Cent. Bank of Nigeria*, 461 U.S. 480, 493 (1983). Here, the district court’s conclusion that the commercial activity exception did not apply was erroneous, and thus the court lacked jurisdiction to dismiss the action on immunity grounds.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
