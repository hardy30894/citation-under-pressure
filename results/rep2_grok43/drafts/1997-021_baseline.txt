I.

The National Labor Relations Act does not prescribe any evidentiary standard governing an employer’s poll of employee support for an incumbent union. The Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union’s majority status” before polling, Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB, 482 U.S. 27, 41 n.8 (1987), is therefore reviewable for rationality and consistency with the Act. NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc., 494 U.S. 775, 786 (1990).

That standard is irrational. It permits an employer to withdraw recognition or petition for an RM election on the same showing required for a poll, yet denies the employer the very procedural device the Board itself has described as useful for obtaining “more certain, precise information about the union’s support.” Texas Petrochemicals Corp., 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). The Board’s own premise—that polling and RM elections serve identical purposes—logically requires a lower threshold for polling than for withdrawal of recognition. By equating the two, the Board renders polling “only marginally useful,” a result the Act neither commands nor justifies. Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978).

Three courts of appeals therefore correctly held that an employer may poll upon “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence would not alone justify withdrawal. A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984); Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982). The Board’s refusal to adopt this lower standard is arbitrary.

II.

The Board’s finding that Allentown Mack lacked reasonable doubt on January 25, 1991, is not supported by substantial evidence. Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Seven unit employees expressly repudiated the union. Three others—McArthur, Wehr, and Zoltack—were properly excluded only because they were no longer employed on the critical date; their statements remain probative of the unit’s sentiment immediately before the poll. The statements of Ridgick and Marsh, made after the employer announced a non-union operation, were improperly discounted; Board precedent treating such statements as presumptively insincere, Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc., 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978), does not apply when the employer has already decided to poll. Bloch’s report that the entire night shift opposed the union, and Mohr’s statement that the union would lose any vote, supplied additional objective grounds for doubt. The Board’s refusal to credit these statements contradicts its own precedents questioning only unverified hearsay, not direct reports by unit members. Westbrook Bowl, 293 N.L.R.B. 1000 (1989).

Because Allentown Mack possessed the requisite reasonable doubt, the poll was lawful and the results extinguished any duty to bargain.
