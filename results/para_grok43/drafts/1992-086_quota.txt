The School District’s facilities constitute a limited public forum. In such a forum, “the first amendment protections provided to traditional public forums only apply to entities of a character similar to those the government admits to the forum.” Calash v. City of Bridgeport, 788 F.2d 80, 82 (2d Cir. 1986); see Perry Education Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983). Once the government opens property “for use by the public for expressive activity,” regulation must satisfy the standards applicable to traditional public forums. Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educational Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 802 (1985). Content-based exclusions are permissible only when “necessary to serve a compelling state interest” and “narrowly drawn” to that end. Perry, 460 U.S. at 45.

The District opened its facilities to a wide array of community groups for lectures, performances, and discussions on family and social topics. This practice created a limited public forum. See Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 267 (1981); Board of Educ. of the Westside Community Schs. v. Mergens, 496 U.S. 226, 247 (1990). The District’s refusal to permit Lamb’s Chapel to present films addressing family values from a religious perspective therefore constitutes viewpoint discrimination. See Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92, 96 (1972) (“[G]overnment may not grant the use of a forum to people whose views it finds acceptable, but deny use to those wishing to express less favored or more controversial views.”).

The District’s asserted interest in avoiding an Establishment Clause violation does not justify the exclusion. In Widmar the Court held that equal access by religious groups to a university forum does not offend the Establishment Clause when the forum is otherwise open. 454 U.S. at 273. The same principle applies here. See Mergens, 496 U.S. at 247–48. The films at issue address child-rearing and family life—subjects already permitted when presented from nonreligious perspectives. Excluding only the religious viewpoint is not narrowly tailored to any compelling interest. See Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989).

The lower court’s finding that no prior religious uses had occurred is beside the point. Viewpoint discrimination, not subject-matter classification, is at issue. See Hazelwood School Dist. v. Kuhlmeier, 484 U.S. 260, 267 (1988). Because the District permitted secular speech on the same topics, it could not bar religious speech on those topics without violating the First Amendment. The judgment should be reversed.
