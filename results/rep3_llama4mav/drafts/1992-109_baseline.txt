The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened standard to determine the competency of a defendant to enter a plea of guilty or waive counsel. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the trial court's failure to apply this heightened "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of constitutional rights violated his due process rights.

This Court has long recognized that the competency standard for waiving constitutional rights is more stringent than the standard for determining competency to stand trial. See Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975); Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam). A defendant is competent to stand trial if they have a "rational and factual understanding of the proceedings" and can "assist[] in preparing his defense." Drope, 420 U.S. at 171-72. In contrast, a defendant is competent to waive constitutional rights only if they have the "capacity for reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him." Harding v. Lewis, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); see also Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214-15 (9th Cir. 1973).

The trial court's failure to apply the heightened "reasoned choice" standard to Moran's waiver of counsel and guilty plea was error. Moran's psychiatric evaluations, which were conducted to determine his competency to stand trial, did not assess his ability to make a reasoned choice among alternatives. See Moran v. Godinez, 972 F.2d 263, 270-71 (9th Cir. 1992). The state court's post-conviction ruling, which relied on these evaluations, therefore applied the wrong legal standard.

Furthermore, the record raises significant doubts about Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights. Moran had attempted suicide, was taking multiple medications, and gave monosyllabic responses to the court's questions. See Moran, 972 F.2d at 266-67. Under these circumstances, the trial court should have had a "good faith doubt" about Moran's competency to waive his constitutional rights, triggering the need for a competency hearing. See Chavez v. United States, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981).

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court's post-conviction ruling did not cure the trial court's error, as it applied the wrong legal standard. See Moran, 972 F.2d at 270-71. The record, when examined under the correct "reasoned choice" standard, does not support a finding that Moran was competent to waive his constitutional rights.

In sum, the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to apply a heightened "reasoned choice" standard to determine a defendant's competency to waive constitutional rights. The trial court's failure to do so in Moran's case was error, and the Ninth Circuit correctly reversed the district court's denial of Moran's habeas petition. This Court should affirm the Ninth Circuit's judgment.
