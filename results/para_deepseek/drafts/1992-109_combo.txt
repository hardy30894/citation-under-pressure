**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Does Not Require a Heightened “Reasoned Choice” Standard for Competency to Waive Counsel or Plead Guilty**

The court of appeals erred in holding that the Constitution demands a higher standard of competency for a defendant who waives counsel or pleads guilty than for a defendant who stands trial. The proper standard—one that applies uniformly to all stages of a criminal proceeding—is the test articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam): whether the defendant “has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding” and “has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.” *Id.* at 402. This Court has never required a separate, more demanding “reasoned choice” inquiry.

In *Pate v. Robinson*, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court held that due process requires a competency hearing whenever the evidence raises a “bona fide doubt” as to the defendant’s capacity to stand trial. *Id.* at 385. *Pate* did not distinguish between competency to stand trial and competency to waive constitutional rights; it applied the same *Dusky* standard to the entire proceeding. Two months later, in *Westbrook v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 150 (1966) (per curiam), the Court remanded because the trial court had “failed to determine petitioner’s competence to stand trial and to waive his constitutional right to the assistance of counsel.” *Id.* at 150. *Westbrook* did not announce a different or higher standard for the waiver inquiry; it simply required a separate determination under the same *Dusky* framework.

*Rees v. Peyton*, 384 U.S. 312 (1966) (per curiam), further confirms that the *Dusky* standard governs decisional competence. There, the Court considered whether a death-row inmate was competent to abandon his appeals, and directed the district court to determine whether he “has the capacity to understand his position and to make a rational choice.” *Id.* at 314. That language mirrors *Dusky*’s focus on rational understanding and ability to consult; it does not erect a new, heightened threshold.

The court of appeals derived its “reasoned choice” standard from *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), a post-1970 decision. No decision of this Court before 1970 supports such a bifurcated approach. To the contrary, the Court has consistently applied a unitary standard. In *Massey v. Moore*, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), the Court stated that a defendant who “lacks the capacity to understand the proceedings or to assist in his defense” cannot be tried or convicted. *Id.* at 108. That standard applies equally to the decision to waive counsel, because the constitutional right to counsel is itself a protection of the defendant’s ability to participate meaningfully. *See Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be “intelligent and competent,” but the Court did not articulate a separate competency test). *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506, 516 (1962), likewise required an “intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege,” but did not impose a higher competency burden.

*Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), held that a guilty plea must be “knowing and voluntary,” *id.* at 242, but said nothing about a heightened competency standard. The “knowing and voluntary” requirement addresses the validity of the waiver, not the defendant’s mental capacity. The competency inquiry remains anchored in *Dusky*.

**II. The “Reasoned Choice” Standard Lacks Constitutional Foundation**

The court of appeals reasoned that a defendant waiving multiple constitutional rights must have “the capacity for ‘reasoned choice’ among the alternatives.” That formulation is not found in any pre-1970 decision of this Court. The *Dusky* standard already encompasses rational decision-making: a defendant who has a “rational as well as factual understanding” of the proceedings and can “consult with his lawyer” is necessarily capable of making choices. Nothing in the Due Process Clause requires a trial court to assess whether the defendant’s choice is “reasoned” in a qualitative sense. To impose such a requirement would conflate the competency inquiry with the separate voluntariness inquiry, which is already protected by the requirement that waivers be knowing and intelligent. *See Johnson v. Zerbst, supra*.

The court of appeals also erred in suggesting that the state court’s post-conviction finding of competency was invalid because it applied the wrong legal standard. The state court correctly applied the *Dusky* standard, which is the only standard the Constitution requires. The psychiatric evaluations—conducted under *Dusky*—properly found that Moran understood the charges and could assist counsel. That was sufficient.

**III. The Trial Court Had No Duty to Conduct a Further Hearing**

Because the *Dusky* standard governs, the trial court’s failure to hold a competency hearing at the time of Moran’s waiver was not error. The record showed no “bona fide doubt” under *Pate*. Moran’s monosyllabic responses and medication status did not, alone, raise a genuine question about his rational understanding. The psychiatrists had already cleared him. The court therefore acted within its discretion.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
