**I. The First Amendment requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to establish a “true threat.”**

The First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within the narrow “true threat” exception. *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969). A “true threat” is one where the speaker “means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals.” *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003). Criminal liability under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) therefore requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten, not merely the effect of the speech on a reasonable person. The Third Circuit erred by adopting an objective standard that conflates the *mens rea* of the defendant with the *perception* of a reasonable observer.

**II. The objective standard adopted by the Third Circuit is inconsistent with Supreme Court precedent.**

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the context of a statement and the speaker’s intent are critical to determining whether speech constitutes a true threat. In *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705 (1969), the Court held that a statement like “I’d like to kill the President” was not a true threat because the context and the speaker’s intent indicated it was a political jest. The Court emphasized that “the words are to be interpreted in light of the context in which they were spoken.” *Id.* at 707. Under the Third Circuit’s standard, Elonis’s rap lyrics, which he testified were artistic expression, would be criminalized simply because a reasonable person might misinterpret them as a literal threat.

Furthermore, the objective standard is inconsistent with the narrow scope of the “true threat” exception. *R.A.V. v. City of St. Paul*, 505 U.S. 377, 381 (1992). The government bears the burden of proving that the defendant intended to communicate a threat. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. By shifting this burden to the defendant to prove that a reasonable person would not interpret his speech as a threat, the Third Circuit effectively eviscerates the First Amendment protection for artistic and expressive speech. Because Elonis did not subjectively intend to threaten his wife or the police, his conviction under § 875(c) must be reversed.
