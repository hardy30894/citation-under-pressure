## Argument

The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015)—that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act’s definition of “violent felony” is unconstitutionally vague—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This Court has long held that “a new rule for the conduct of criminal prosecutions” does not apply retroactively unless it falls within one of two narrow exceptions. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989). But *Johnson* announced a *substantive* rule, not a procedural one. Substantive rules—those that “alter[] the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes”—apply retroactively. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). *Johnson* did exactly that: it held that the residual clause “fails to give ordinary people fair notice of the conduct it punishes” and “invites arbitrary enforcement.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. By declaring the clause void for vagueness, *Johnson* “necessarily carr[ied] a significant risk that a defendant stands to be punished for an act that the law does not make criminal.” *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. 190, 200 (2016). A conviction or sentence enhanced under a void provision is invalid, regardless of when it became final.

The Eleventh Circuit erred in denying Welch a certificate of appealability. Welch’s 15-year mandatory minimum sentence under the ACCA was predicated on a prior Florida “strong arm” robbery conviction that the district court and the Eleventh Circuit concluded qualified as a violent felony under the residual clause. The Eleventh Circuit expressly relied on the residual clause in affirming Welch’s sentence, holding that Florida robbery “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” *Welch v. United States*, 745 F.3d 1120, 1123 (11th Cir. 2014). That holding is precisely the kind of “hypothetical risk” analysis that *Johnson* condemned. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. Because *Johnson* invalidated the very clause under which Welch’s predicate offense was deemed a violent felony, his sentence is unlawful.

This Court has already applied *Johnson* retroactively in the context of the Sentencing Guidelines’ identical residual clause. *Beckles v. United States*, 580 U.S. 256, 259 (2017) (Thomas, J., concurring) (noting that *Johnson* applies retroactively to Guidelines cases on collateral review). And in *Montgomery*, the Court held that a substantive rule “applies retroactively” because it “necessarily carries a significant risk that a defendant stands to be punished for an act that the law does not make criminal.” *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at 200. *Johnson* is such a rule. It does not announce a new procedure for determining guilt; it declares that a category of punishment is void for vagueness. A defendant sentenced under a void provision “may be incarcerated only until the sentencer reconsiders the penalty.” *Id.* at 212.

Welch’s case is indistinguishable. He was sentenced under a statute that this Court has now declared unconstitutional. The residual clause “produce[s] more unpredictability and arbitrariness than the Due Process Clause tolerates.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 598. The Eleventh Circuit’s reliance on that clause to enhance Welch’s sentence cannot stand. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for resentencing without application of the ACCA enhancement.
