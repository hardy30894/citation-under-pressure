ARGUMENT

I.

The evidence was insufficient to support the jury’s finding of sex discrimination. Title VII prohibits discrimination “because of . . . sex,” 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a)(1), but does not require employers to ignore legitimate performance deficiencies. See McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). Once an employer articulates a nondiscriminatory reason, the plaintiff must prove that reason is pretext for discrimination. St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 515 (1993). Feltmann’s low gross profits, excessive guest drives, and repeated violation of the demo-car restriction supplied nondiscriminatory grounds for the memos, probation, and termination. No male consultant matched this combination of deficiencies while receiving more favorable treatment. See Texas Dep’t of Cmty. Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 256 (1981). The single vulgar remark, promptly investigated and sanctioned, does not constitute actionable harassment. See Meritor Sav. Bank, FSB v. Vinson, 477 U.S. 57, 67 (1986) (requiring conduct that is “sufficiently severe or pervasive”).

II.

The retaliation claim likewise fails. To prevail, Feltmann had to show that her complaint was the but-for cause of an adverse action. See Womack v. Munson, 619 F.2d 1292, 1297 (8th Cir. 1980). The undisputed timeline demonstrates that performance concerns predated and continued after the complaint. Probation was imposed jointly on Feltmann and a male consultant for identical sales shortfalls. Exclusion from the Chicago trip followed the same performance-based criteria applied to others. Continued unauthorized use of the demo car supplied an independent, non-retaliatory basis for discharge. See Kiel v. Select Artificials, Inc., 169 F.3d 1131, 1136 (8th Cir. 1999) (en banc) (no retaliation where employer acts on legitimate rule violation). Because no reasonable jury could find pretext, judgment as a matter of law should have been granted.
