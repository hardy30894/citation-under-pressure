ARGUMENT

I. THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE REQUIRES ONLY THAT A DEFENDANT BE COMPETENT UNDER THE STANDARD ESTABLISHED IN DUSKY V. UNITED STATES.

The Ninth Circuit held that a defendant who is competent to stand trial nonetheless requires a "higher level of mental functioning" before he may plead guilty or waive counsel. 972 F.2d at 266. That holding cannot be reconciled with this Court's settled definition of competency. In Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam), this Court held that the test for competency is "whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." Id. at 402. This Court has never suggested that the Dusky standard varies with the decision the defendant is called upon to make. To the contrary, in Pate v. Robinson, 383 U.S. 375 (1966), the Court treated competency as a unitary threshold inquiry, holding that a defendant may not be subjected to trial, much less permitted to make binding decisions about his defense, unless he possesses the capacity described in Dusky. Id. at 384-86. Nothing in Pate contemplates a sliding scale of competency keyed to the gravity of the choice presented.

The Ninth Circuit's "reasoned choice" standard rests on the mistaken premise that pleading guilty or waiving counsel demands greater cognitive capacity than standing trial and assisting counsel through a full adversarial proceeding. The opposite is closer to the truth. A defendant who stands trial must track testimony, communicate with counsel, and make numerous strategic decisions throughout a proceeding that may last days or weeks. A defendant who pleads guilty need only understand the charges, the rights he is relinquishing, and the consequences of his plea—a discrete and more limited task. There is no principled basis, and the courts below identified none, for concluding that the latter decision requires the "higher level of mental functioning" the Ninth Circuit demanded. 972 F.2d at 266.

II. THE SEPARATE REQUIREMENT THAT A WAIVER BE KNOWING, INTELLIGENT, AND VOLUNTARY IS SATISFIED BY A SEARCHING COLLOQUY, NOT BY AN ELEVATED COMPETENCY THRESHOLD.

The Ninth Circuit's error stems from conflating two distinct constitutional inquiries: competency and the validity of the waiver itself. This Court has long recognized that once a defendant is competent, the question whether he validly waived a constitutional right is answered not by raising the competency bar, but by determining whether the waiver was made with sufficient awareness of the right relinquished and the consequences of relinquishing it. In Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), the Court held that waiver of the right to counsel must be "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege," id. at 464, and that courts must indulge "every reasonable presumption against waiver," id. But the Court did not hold, nor has it since held, that a defendant must satisfy some heightened mental-capacity standard beyond ordinary competency before that waiver can be deemed intentional. The safeguard lies in the searching inquiry the trial court must conduct, not in a freestanding psychiatric threshold.

That approach is confirmed in Von Moltke v. Gillies, 332 U.S. 708 (1948), where the Court required trial courts to conduct a "penetrating and comprehensive examination" of the defendant's understanding of the charges, possible defenses, and consequences of waiver before accepting a waiver of counsel. Id. at 724 (plurality opinion). The remedy for a defendant whose understanding is in doubt is a more probing colloquy—precisely what Johnson v. Zerbst and Von Moltke prescribe—not a categorically different competency standard. Likewise, in Carnley v. Cochran, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), the Court held that waiver of counsel "will not be presumed" from a silent record, id. at 516, again locating the constitutional protection in the adequacy of the record and colloquy, not in an enhanced mental-capacity test.

This Court's decisions concerning guilty pleas follow the same course. In Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court required an affirmative showing on the record that a guilty plea was "voluntarily and understandingly" entered, id. at 244, but the Court measured that requirement by what the record disclosed about the defendant's understanding of the rights waived—not by superimposing a distinct, elevated definition of competency. And in Massey v. Moore, 348 U.S. 105 (1954), the Court recognized that a defendant's mental condition bears on whether he can competently waive counsel and conduct his own defense, id. at 108, but resolved that question within the ordinary framework of competency and knowing waiver, not through a freestanding "reasoned choice" test. See also Adams v. United States ex rel. McCann, 317 U.S. 269, 279 (1942) (waiver of counsel valid where "competently and intelligently" made); Westbrook v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 150, 150 (1966) (per curiam) (remanding where record failed to show a hearing on defendant's competency to waive counsel, without imposing any distinct mental-capacity standard for waiver).

III. A SINGLE COMPETENCY STANDARD, COUPLED WITH A RIGOROUS WAIVER COLLOQUY, ADEQUATELY PROTECTS DEFENDANTS' RIGHTS.

The protections this Court has already erected—the Dusky competency standard, the presumption against waiver announced in Johnson v. Zerbst, and the searching colloquy required by Von Moltke and Boykin—fully safeguard defendants like respondent. Nevada's trial court found Moran competent under Pate and Dusky, and it questioned him about his understanding of the charges and the rights he was relinquishing. No additional constitutional layer, untethered to any standard this Court has ever articulated, is required. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed.
