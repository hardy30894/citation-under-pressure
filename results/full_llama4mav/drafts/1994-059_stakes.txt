The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution, Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 and Article I, Section 3, Clause 3, establish the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress. The Constitution sets forth specific requirements for age, citizenship, and residency that candidates must meet to be eligible for congressional office. The States are not permitted to add to or alter these qualifications.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the Qualifications Clauses are a constitutional ceiling, not a floor, and that States may not supplement them with additional requirements. In Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that Congress may not add to the qualifications specified in the Constitution, emphasizing that the framers intended to limit the power of the States and Congress to impose additional qualifications. The Court stated that "the [Constitutional] provision [that a representative be an inhabitant of the state in which he is chosen] was intended to operate as a limitation on the power of the States." Id. at 550.

The Court has also made clear that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive, and that any attempt by a State to impose additional qualifications is unconstitutional. In U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, the Eleventh Circuit Court of Appeals held that a state-imposed term limit on congressional candidates was unconstitutional, as it added a qualification not specified in the Constitution. 316 F. Supp. 3d 1332 (N.D. Ga. 2018), aff'd sub nom. U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Hill, 975 F.3d 1161 (11th Cir. 2020).

Here, Amendment 73's provisions limiting the terms of U.S. Representatives and Senators from Arkansas are an impermissible attempt to add to the qualifications specified in the Constitution. By preventing candidates who have served a certain number of terms from appearing on the ballot, Amendment 73 effectively imposes an additional qualification on congressional candidates. This is precisely the type of state-imposed qualification that the Qualifications Clauses are designed to prevent.

The argument that Amendment 73 merely regulates ballot access, rather than imposing an additional qualification, is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has rejected similar attempts to characterize qualifications as mere ballot access regulations. See Cook v. Gralike, 531 U.S. 510 (2001). In Cook, the Court held that a state constitutional amendment that required a warning label on the ballot next to the name of any congressional candidate who failed to support term limits was unconstitutional, as it was an attempt to influence the congressional election process in a way that was not permitted by the Constitution.

In light of the Supreme Court's precedents, it is clear that Amendment 73's term limits on congressional candidates are unconstitutional. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly held that Section 3 of Amendment 73, which imposes term limits on U.S. Representatives and Senators, violates the Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution.

Furthermore, the unconstitutionality of Section 3 renders the entire Amendment 73 invalid. The provisions of Amendment 73 are not severable, as they were presented to the voters as a single, unified package. The ballot title and the Preamble to Amendment 73 referred to "elected officials" generally, without distinguishing between state and federal officeholders. The voters had no opportunity to vote on term limits for state officeholders separately from term limits for congressional candidates. Therefore, the entire Amendment 73 must be invalidated.

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
