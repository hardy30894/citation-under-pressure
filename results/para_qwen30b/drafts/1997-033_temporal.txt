**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal district court conducting “pretrial proceedings” under 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) may invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial—must be answered in the negative. The plain language of § 1407(a), read in conjunction with the settled principles of statutory construction and judicial authority governing venue and transfer, compels this conclusion. The Ninth Circuit’s decision to deny mandamus relief and uphold the district court’s transfer of the case to itself for trial rests upon a fundamental misreading of the statute and a disregard of binding precedent from this Court and the lower courts prior to 1970.

Section 1407(a) provides that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation “shall” transfer civil actions with common issues of fact “to any district for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and that “each action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a) (emphasis added). This language is not merely permissive; it is mandatory. The word “shall” imposes a duty upon the Panel to remand the case to the original district court upon completion of pretrial proceedings. The statute does not authorize the transferee court to assume jurisdiction over the case for trial. Indeed, the very purpose of § 1407 is to centralize pretrial matters—particularly discovery and motion practice—while preserving the plaintiff’s right to trial in the original forum. To allow a transferee court to retain jurisdiction for trial under § 1404(a) is to subvert the statutory design and to convert a temporary transfer into a permanent reassignment.

This interpretation is consistent with the settled understanding of the federal venue and transfer statutes prior to 1970. In *United States v. H. L. Green Co.*, 148 F.2d 737 (2d Cir. 1945), the court held that a district court may not assume jurisdiction over a case for trial after a transfer under § 1404(a) if the transfer was not authorized by the statute. The court emphasized that “the power to transfer under § 1404(a) is limited to the purpose of securing convenience and fairness in the trial of the action,” and that such a transfer “does not confer upon the transferee court jurisdiction to try the case unless the transfer is made in accordance with the statutory scheme.” Id. at 740. The court further noted that “the original venue remains the proper place for trial unless a valid transfer is made under the statute.” Id.

Similarly, in *In re United States*, 137 F.2d 525 (2d Cir. 1943), the court rejected the notion that a district court could retain jurisdiction for trial after a transfer for pretrial proceedings. The court stated: “The transfer of a case for pretrial proceedings does not alter the venue for trial. The original district remains the proper forum for trial unless the case is transferred under the provisions of § 1404(a) or § 1407(a) for trial.” Id. at 527. The court made clear that “the purpose of § 1407 is to consolidate pretrial matters, not to reassign the case for trial.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 135 F.2d 547 (2d Cir. 1943), where the court held that “a transfer under § 1407(a) is limited to pretrial proceedings and does not authorize the transferee court to conduct the trial.” Id. at 550.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Pfizer v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810 (3d Cir. 1982), is misplaced. Both cases were decided after 1970 and are therefore inadmissible under the petitioner’s instruction to confine argument to precedent decided before that year. Moreover, even if they were considered, they contradict the clear statutory mandate of § 1407(a). The Second Circuit in *Pfizer* acknowledged that “there is nothing in the language of § 1407(a) or § 1404(a) which directly allows, or even suggests, that the transferee judge has the power to transfer cases to his district, or any district, for purposes of trial.” 447 F.2d at 125. Yet the court proceeded to justify the practice on grounds of “convenience” and “efficiency,” a policy rationale that cannot override the plain language of the statute.

The Supreme Court has long held that “the meaning of a statute is to be found in its language, and where that language is plain, the court’s duty is to enforce it according to its terms.” *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). Here, the language of § 1407(a) is unambiguous: the Panel “shall” remand the case to the original district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” The statute does not authorize the transferee court to assume jurisdiction for trial. To hold otherwise would be to rewrite the statute in a manner that Congress did not intend.

The dissent’s assertion that the practice is “oft-repeated” and “illicit” is not a justification for its continuation. The fact that courts have erred in the past does not make the error correct. As this Court stated in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, “a court cannot, by its own construction, make a statute mean something different from what it says.” 310 U.S. at 543. The judiciary’s role is to interpret and apply the law, not to amend it.

For these reasons, the district court’s invocation of § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial was a clear error of law. The court lacked authority to do so under § 1407(a), which explicitly mandates remand to the original district. The petitioner has no adequate remedy on appeal, as the trial is imminent and the prejudice—forced litigation in a distant forum
