# ARGUMENT

## I. The Buffer-Zone Provision Is a Content-Neutral Regulation of the Manner of Speech.

This Court has long recognized that government may impose reasonable restrictions on the time, place, and manner of expression without regard to content, so long as the restriction serves a substantial governmental interest and leaves open ample alternative avenues of communication. In *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569 (1941), the Court sustained a municipal licensing requirement for parades notwithstanding its incidental burden on expressive activity, because the ordinance was aimed not at suppressing ideas but at the orderly use of public streets. The same principle controlled in *Kovacs v. Cooper*, 336 U.S. 77 (1949), where the Court upheld an ordinance banning sound trucks emitting excessive noise, explaining that the regulation touched only the mode, not the substance, of speech. And in *Poulos v. New Hampshire*, 345 U.S. 395 (1953), the Court again approved a permit requirement for use of a public park, confirming that municipalities retain authority to regulate competing uses of public space even where speech is involved.

Section 120E½ operates on precisely this model. It does not single out any viewpoint, message, or speaker category; it restricts a discrete thirty-five-foot zone at clinic entrances regardless of what is said there. In *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), the constitutional infirmity arose because the ordinance flatly forbade distribution of literature on public streets—a total ban on a medium of expression untethered to any legitimate place-based interest. No such infirmity exists here: the Act leaves untouched every mode of communication petitioners employ everywhere else, and prohibits nothing based on content.

## II. Location-Based Restrictions Protecting Ingress and Egress to Sensitive Facilities Are Well Established.

The Commonwealth's decision to draw a modest buffer around clinic entrances follows precedent upholding similar restrictions to protect unimpeded access to sensitive public places. In *Cameron v. Johnson*, 390 U.S. 611 (1968), this Court upheld a statute "prohibiting picketing 'in such a manner as to obstruct or unreasonably interfere with free ingress or egress to and from any . . . county . . . courthouses.'" *Amalgamated Food Employees Union Local 590 v. Logan Valley Plaza, Inc.*, 391 U.S. 308, 320 (1968) (quoting Cameron). The Court likewise sustained restrictions on picketing near courthouses in *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 559 (1965), recognizing that the State's interest in the orderly administration of justice justified confining protest activity away from courthouse entrances. And in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), the Court confirmed that "[e]ven where municipal or state property is open to the public generally, the exercise of First Amendment rights may be regulated so as to prevent interference with the use to which the property is ordinarily put by the State." *Logan Valley Plaza*, 391 U.S. at 320.

The interest Massachusetts protects here is at least as weighty as the interests vindicated in *Cameron*, *Cox*, and *Adderley*. Patients approaching a reproductive health care facility are entitled to unimpeded access to lawful medical care, just as litigants are entitled to unimpeded access to a courthouse. The thirty-five-foot zone, like the courthouse buffers this Court has repeatedly approved, is calibrated to that narrow purpose and does not sweep in surrounding sidewalks or streets generally open to public discourse.

## III. The Act Leaves Ample Alternative Channels for Petitioners' Message.

The record confirms—and the Act's design guarantees—that petitioners retain robust means of reaching their intended audience. Sidewalk counselors may speak, sing, pray, hold signs, and distribute literature from just beyond the buffer's edge; nothing in the statute forecloses any medium of expression. This satisfies the requirement, recognized in *Kovacs*, that a manner regulation is valid where alternative methods "of course, are open," 336 U.S. at 89, even though a particular preferred means is curtailed. The same logic applies with full force here.

## IV. There Is No Occasion to Disturb Settled Doctrine.

Because the Act's validity rests on principles this Court established long before the decision below, there is no need to revisit or narrow that settled framework. As Mr. Justice Holmes explained in *Patterson v. Colorado*, 205 U.S. 454, 462 (1907), "the main purpose of such constitutional provisions is to prevent all such previous restraints upon publications as had been practiced by other governments," not to immunize speech from every incidental, content-neutral regulation of place. The Act imposes no previous restraint; it merely channels the location from which speech, of whatever viewpoint, may be delivered. Nor does the concern animating *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513 (1958)—where the State conditioned a tax exemption on a loyalty declaration risking a chill on protected advocacy—have any analogue here, since the Act conditions nothing on the content or viewpoint of speech; it regulates only the place from which speech, whatever its message, may be voiced.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
