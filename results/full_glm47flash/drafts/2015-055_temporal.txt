Petitioner argues that the Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson v. United States* should apply retroactively to set aside his sentence. This Court has held that new rules of criminal procedure are not applied retroactively on collateral review unless they fall within the narrow "breakthrough" exception. *Stovall v. Denno*, 388 U.S. 293, 299 (1967).

*Johnson* announced a new substantive due process rule regarding the vagueness of the ACCA residual clause. Under *Stovall*, a rule is "new" if it was not "dictated by the terms of the Constitution at the time of the trial." *Id.* at 299. The vagueness doctrine is a substantive limitation on the State’s power to define crimes, not a procedural safeguard for the conduct of trials. *Griffin v. California*, 380 U.S. 609, 615 (1965). Because *Johnson* was not dictated by the Constitution at the time of Welch’s 1996 trial, it is a "new rule" that does not apply retroactively.

Even if *Johnson* were considered a "breakthrough," it would not qualify for the retroactive exception. *Linkletter v. Walker*, 381 U.S. 618, 622 (1965). The "breakthrough" exception is limited to those new rules which "correct a fundamental defect or otherwise improve the accuracy of the criminal process." *Id.* *Johnson* is a substantive rule that changes the definition of a crime. It does not correct a trial error or improve the accuracy of the trial process itself. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961) (procedural rules are retroactive, but substantive rules are not). Therefore, *Johnson* does not apply retroactively to Welch’s sentence.
