The equal footing doctrine vests a new State with title to the beds of rivers that were navigable in fact at the time of statehood. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). The constitutional test therefore requires a court to determine, on the basis of the river's condition at that historic moment, whether the waters "were used, or were susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 76 (1931).

This inquiry is necessarily segment-specific. A river may be navigable in one reach and non-navigable in another; title passes only to those discrete portions that satisfied the federal test on the date of admission. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 77, 90; *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 15 (1935). Evidence of present-day use or post-statehood improvements is irrelevant unless it illuminates the river's condition in 1889. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82-83.

The District Court applied precisely this framework. It examined contemporaneous logs, surveys, and accounts to determine whether the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork segments at issue were navigable at statehood. That evidence established susceptibility to commercial use in their ordinary condition. The Montana Supreme Court correctly affirmed those findings. Because the constitutional standard was satisfied, title vested in the State in 1889 and remains with the State today.
