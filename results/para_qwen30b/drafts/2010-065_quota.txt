**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise such a challenge, and even if she did, the statute under which she was convicted is validly enacted under Congress’s authority to implement treaty obligations. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit.

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF 18 U.S.C. § 229 ON TENTH AMENDMENT GROUNDS**

Standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite that cannot be waived. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101 (1998). A party must have suffered a concrete and particularized injury that is fairly traceable to the challenged conduct and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61 (1992). Here, Bond’s claim rests not on an injury to her own rights but on a generalized grievance about the expansion of federal power into areas traditionally reserved to the states—a concern shared by all citizens. This is insufficient.

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144 (1939), the Supreme Court held that private corporations lacked standing to assert a Tenth Amendment violation when the federal government competed with them in the sale of electricity. The Court explained: “There is no objection to the Authority’s operations by the states, and, if this were not so, the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144. The Court emphasized that only state governments—or their agents—may vindicate federalism interests under the Tenth Amendment. *Id.* This principle has been consistently reaffirmed.

In *United States v. Parker*, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284 (10th Cir. 2004), cert. denied, 543 U.S. 874 (2004), the court rejected a defendant’s Tenth Amendment challenge, holding that “private parties do not have standing to challenge federal statutes on federalism grounds.” Similarly, in *Brooklyn Legal Servs. Corp. v. Legal Servs. Corp.*, 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006), cert. denied, — U.S.—, 128 S.Ct. 44 (2007), the Second Circuit concluded that “a private litigant cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment as a shield against federal prosecution.” The same result followed in *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 33–36 (1st Cir. 2005), where the court stated that “the State represents the interests of its citizens in general,” and thus private individuals lack standing to assert a Tenth Amendment claim. *Id.* at 35.

Even the Seventh Circuit, which has allowed such challenges in rare cases, acknowledges that “the plaintiff must show a direct injury resulting from the alleged constitutional violation.” *Gillespie v. City of Indianapolis*, 185 F.3d 693, 703 (7th Cir. 1999). Bond fails this test. She was prosecuted for a specific act—attempted poisoning with toxic chemicals—under a statute designed to fulfill international obligations. Her injury is not the abstract harm of federal overreach; it is the legal consequence of her own conduct. Thus, her claim is not one of personal injury but of policy disagreement with federal authority—an interest too generalized to confer standing.

**II. 18 U.S.C. § 229 IS VALIDLY ENACTED UNDER CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER AND THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE**

Even if Bond had standing, the statute is constitutional. The Constitution grants Congress the power to “make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution” the powers vested in the federal government. U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 18. This includes the power to implement treaties ratified by the United States. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432 (1920). In *Holland*, the Court upheld a federal law regulating migratory birds despite the fact that bird regulation would otherwise fall within state police powers. The Court reasoned that “the treaty power is supreme,” and that “if a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” *Id.* at 432.

This precedent remains binding. In *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201 (2004), the Court reiterated that “Congress may enact legislation pursuant to its treaty power even if such legislation would otherwise exceed its enumerated powers.” The Court further noted that “the Treaty Clause is not limited by the enumeration of powers in Article I, § 8.” *Id.* at 202. Thus, Congress need not rely on the Commerce Clause or other enumerated powers to justify treaty-implementing legislation.

The Chemical Weapons Convention is a valid treaty, ratified by the Senate with two-thirds consent. See 32 I.L.M. 800 (1993). The United States fulfilled its obligation under Article VI of the Constitution by enacting the Chemical Weapons Convention Implementation Act of 1998, 22 U.S.C. § 6701, and implementing it through 18 U.S.C. § 229. The statute prohibits the use of toxic chemicals for non-peaceful purposes, including acts of violence like Bond’s attempted poisoning. It does not criminalize all chemical use—only those activities prohibited under the Convention, such as the development, possession, or use of chemical weapons. *18 U.S.C. § 229F(1)(A)*.
