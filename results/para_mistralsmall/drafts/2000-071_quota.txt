**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S POLICY IS CONSTITUTIONALLY NEUTRAL BECAUSE IT EXCLUDES RELIGIOUS WORSHIP, NOT RELIGIOUS VIEWPOINTS.**

Milford’s Community Use Policy bars use of school facilities for “religious purposes,” which the Second Circuit correctly construed to exclude religious worship, not all speech with a religious viewpoint. *See Good News Club v. Milford Cent. Sch.*, 202 F.3d 502, 510 (2d Cir. 2000). This distinction is compelled by the Supreme Court’s decision in *Lamb’s Chapel v. Center Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 394 (1993), which upheld a school’s refusal to allow a religious group to show films containing religious messages because the policy at issue “bar[red] use only for religious purposes, not for discussions of religious subjects.” The Court explained that a policy excluding only worshipful activities is viewpoint-neutral because it “does not discriminate on the basis of the speaker’s viewpoint.” *Id.* at 393. Milford’s policy is indistinguishable: it permits moral instruction from a religious perspective but forbids acts of worship, such as prayer and scripture recitation as ends in themselves. *See id.* at 394 (distinguishing between religious *instruction* and religious *worship*).

**II. MILFORD’S POLICY DOES NOT ENGAGE IN UNCONSTITUTIONAL VIEWPOINT DISCRIMINATION.**

The Club erroneously conflates viewpoint neutrality with content neutrality. *See Rosenberger v. Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (explaining that a regulation is viewpoint-neutral if it “regulates speech without regard to the speaker’s philosophy, point of view, or ideology”). Milford’s policy does not suppress disfavored religious viewpoints; it excludes only those activities that constitute religious worship. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 510. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld such facially neutral policies. *See, e.g., Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 272 (1981) (upholding a university’s policy excluding “worship, religious speech, and religious teaching” from its open forum); *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394 (same). The Club’s reliance on *Rosenberger* is misplaced because that case involved a policy that excluded *all* religious speech, not merely worship. *See Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 825. Milford’s policy, by contrast, allows religious *discussion* but forbids religious *worship*—a distinction the Court has recognized as viewpoint-neutral. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394.

**III. PERMITTING THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES WOULD VIOLATE THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE.**

Milford permissibly excluded the Club to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 511. The Supreme Court has long held that a public school may not lend its imprimatur to religious worship or instruction. *See, e.g., Santa Fe Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Doe*, 530 U.S. 290, 308 (2000) (warning that school-sponsored prayer “places the school’s imprimatur on religious doctrine”); *Lee v. Weisman*, 505 U.S. 577, 592 (1992) (holding that a school’s inclusion of clergy-led prayer at graduation “sends a message to members of the audience who are nonadherents that they are outsiders, not full members of the political community”). The Club’s activities—singing hymns, reciting scripture, and praying—are indistinguishable from worship. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 510. Permitting such activities would impermissibly entangle the school in religious matters. *See Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971) (establishing the three-part test for Establishment Clause violations, including the entanglement prong).

**IV. THE SECOND CIRCUIT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES FALL OUTSIDE THE SCOPE OF THE POLICY’S PERMISSIVE USE.**

The Second Circuit properly characterized the Club’s activities as religious worship, not moral instruction. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 510. The Club’s own materials reveal that its meetings include prayer, scripture memorization, and an invitation to accept Jesus as Savior—activities that are quintessentially religious. *Id.* at 506-07. The Supreme Court has distinguished between permissible moral instruction and impermissible worship. *See Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 269 n.6, 102 S.Ct. at 274 n.6 (noting that “singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” are forms of speech despite their religious subject matter, but worship is not). The Club’s reliance on *Good News/Good Sports Club v. School Dist. of Ladue*, 28 F.3d 1501 (8th Cir. 1994), is unavailing because that case involved a policy that allowed *all* religious speech, not one that excluded worship. *Id.* at 1506-07.

**V. MILFORD’S POLICY IS NOT AN UNCONSTITUTIONAL PRIOR RESTRAINT ON SPEECH.**

The Club’s prior restraint argument fails because Milford’s policy is content-neutral and viewpoint-neutral. *See Good News Club*, 202 F.3d at 512. The Supreme Court has upheld facially neutral time, place, and manner restrictions that are “reasonable in light of the purpose served by the forum.” *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford’s policy is reasonable because it excludes only worship, not all religious speech. *See Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 394. The Club’s reliance on *City of Madison Joint Sch. Dist. v. Wisconsin PERC*, 429 U.S. 167 (1976), is misplaced because that case involved a discriminatory policy that suppressed a particular viewpoint. *Id.* at 174-75.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s policy is constitutional, and the judgment below should be affirmed.
