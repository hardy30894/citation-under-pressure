The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that it could review the entire record, including matters outside what was presented to the jury, when determining whether the plain error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. Under plain-error review, an appellate court may reverse a lower court's decision only if there is an error that is plain, affects the defendant's substantial rights, and seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). 

When assessing whether an error affected a defendant's substantial rights, courts consider whether it "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." Id. To make this determination, an appellate court may review the entire record. See United States v. Vonn, 535 U.S. 55, 74-75 (2002) (holding that an appellate court may consult the entire record when determining whether a plain error affected a defendant's substantial rights). This includes examining evidence not presented to the jury, such as pre-trial filings, transcripts of hearings, and other relevant documents. See, e.g., United States v. Dominguez Benitez, 542 U.S. 74, 83-84 (2004) (considering the entire record, including the plea agreement and sentencing hearing transcript, when assessing the effect of plain error on a defendant's substantial rights).

The Eleventh Circuit's review of Greer's prior convictions, which were not presented to the jury, was proper under this standard. By examining the record as a whole, the court could determine whether Greer was prejudiced by the error. See United States v. Marcus, 560 U.S. 258, 265 (2010) (holding that an appellate court may consider the entire record when assessing the effect of plain error on a defendant's substantial rights).

Greer's argument that the Eleventh Circuit should have limited its review to the evidence presented to the jury is unpersuasive. The Supreme Court has never held that an appellate court's review under plain-error review is limited to the trial evidence. In fact, the Court has suggested the opposite. See Vonn, 535 U.S. at 74-75. Moreover, other circuits have also considered matters outside the trial record when reviewing for plain error. See, e.g., United States v. Cotton, 535 U.S. 625, 633-34 (2002) (considering the presentence report and other non-trial evidence when assessing the effect of plain error); United States v. Young, 470 U.S. 1, 16-20 (1985) (examining the entire record, including the prosecutor's closing argument and the trial court's instructions, when assessing the effect of plain error).

Furthermore, limiting an appellate court's review to the trial evidence would be inconsistent with the plain-error review standard. As the Supreme Court has noted, plain-error review is designed to correct "particularly egregious errors" that "seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." United States v. Frady, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982). To achieve this goal, an appellate court must be able to consider all relevant information, not just the evidence presented to the jury.

The decision below is consistent with the approach taken by other courts. For example, in Rosales-Mireles v. United States, 138 S. Ct. 1897 (2018), the Supreme Court considered the entire record, including the presentence report, when assessing whether a plain error affected the defendant's substantial rights. Id. at 1908-09. Similarly, in Henderson v. United States, 568 U.S. 266 (2013), the Court considered the entire record, including the defendant's prior convictions, when determining whether a plain error affected the defendant's substantial rights. Id. at 278-79.

In sum, the Eleventh Circuit correctly reviewed the entire record, including matters outside what was presented to the jury, when determining whether the plain error in Greer's trial affected his substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. This approach is consistent with the plain-error review standard and the decisions of other courts. The judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
