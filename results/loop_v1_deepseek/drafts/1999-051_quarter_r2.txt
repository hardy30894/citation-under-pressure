**ARGUMENT**

The decision below erroneously permitted a procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel to excuse the procedural default of a separate habeas claim. This Court has long held that federal habeas review is unavailable when a state prisoner fails to comply with state procedural rules, unless the prisoner demonstrates that the default is excused by cause and prejudice. A claim that is itself procedurally defaulted cannot supply that cause.

I. A Procedurally Defaulted Claim Cannot Serve as Cause to Excuse Another Default.

The exhaustion requirement is fundamental to federal habeas jurisdiction. *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116–17 (1944) (state remedies must be exhausted before federal habeas relief). A claim that is procedurally defaulted under state law is not exhausted. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 210 (1950) (failure to comply with state procedural rules bars federal review). This Court has consistently held that federal courts will not review a state judgment that rests on an independent and adequate state procedural ground. *Murdock v. City of Memphis*, 87 U.S. 590, 626 (1874). A petitioner cannot circumvent that bar by invoking a claim that is itself defaulted.

The ineffective-assistance claim here was dismissed as untimely under Ohio’s procedural rules. That default is not excused. Under *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963), a federal court may consider a defaulted claim only if the petitioner did not “deliberately by-pass” state procedures. But *Fay* itself requires that the petitioner show the absence of a knowing waiver. Here, Carpenter’s failure to timely file his application to reopen was not excused by any external factor. A defaulted claim cannot be used as cause for another default because cause must be an objective factor external to the defense that prevented compliance with state procedures. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312 (1963) (habeas court must consider whether state procedures were adequate). A claim that is itself defaulted is not external; it is the very defect the petitioner seeks to excuse.

II. The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Cannot Be Cause Because It Was Not Properly Presented to the State Courts.

This Court has long required that a habeas petitioner give the state courts a fair opportunity to correct constitutional violations. *Ex parte Royall*, 117 U.S. 241, 251 (1886) (exhaustion requires presentation to state courts). In *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 486–87 (1953), the Court emphasized that federal courts must respect state procedural rules. A claim that is procedurally defaulted has not been “fairly presented.” *Darr*, 339 U.S. at 210. Carpenter’s ineffective-assistance claim was dismissed as untimely; the Ohio courts never addressed its merits. That claim cannot serve as cause because it is not a properly exhausted claim.

Moreover, the right to effective assistance of counsel, while fundamental, must be asserted in accordance with state procedures. *Powell v. Alabama*, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932) (right to counsel requires fair opportunity to raise claim). *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938) (waiver of counsel must be knowing and intelligent). Here, Carpenter waived any timely challenge to appellate counsel’s performance by failing to comply with Ohio’s procedural rule. A waived claim cannot be revived to excuse another default. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 438.

III. Permitting a Defaulted Claim to Serve as Cause Undermines Finality and Comity.

Federal habeas review is not a substitute for a direct appeal. *Brown*, 344 U.S. at 487. This Court has consistently recognized the importance of finality in state criminal judgments. *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 312. Allowing a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would encourage prisoners to bypass state procedures and then use the defaulted claim as a “back door” to federal review. That result would eviscerate the independent and adequate state ground doctrine. *Murdock*, 87 U.S. at 626.

The Sixth Circuit’s holding also conflicts with the principle that a petitioner must show cause and prejudice for each defaulted claim. *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 438. Here, Carpenter failed to show cause for the default of his ineffective-assistance claim. That claim cannot be both the default and the cause for another default. Such circular reasoning is unsupported by any pre-1970 precedent of this Court.

IV. Conclusion.

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.
