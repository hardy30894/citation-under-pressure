**ARGUMENT**

Petitioners respectfully submit that the Ninth Circuit erred in reversing the district court’s grant of summary judgment. Under controlling Supreme Court and Ninth Circuit precedent, Sulyma’s claims are time-barred because he had “actual knowledge” of the alleged breach more than three years before filing suit. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding—requiring knowledge that the investment was imprudent—contradicts settled law and undermines the purpose of ERISA’s limitations provision.

### I. ERISA’s Three-Year Limitations Period Requires Actual Knowledge of the Breach, Not Its Legal Consequences

The plain text of 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) (1976) bars claims “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” Courts have long held that “actual knowledge” means awareness of the underlying act, not its legal characterization. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), this Court held that trustees’ knowledge of a transaction—even if they did not know it violated ERISA—triggered the limitations period. The Court reasoned that § 1113 is “triggered by the [trustees’] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.” *Id.* at 991-92. This principle was reaffirmed in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), where the Court held that a plaintiff’s receipt of reduced benefit checks constituted “actual knowledge” of the breach, even if he did not know the reduction was unlawful. *Id.* at 1423.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding in this case—that “actual knowledge” requires awareness that the investment was imprudent—finds no support in precedent. As this Court explained in *Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund*, 944 F.2d 509 (9th Cir. 1991), the limitations period runs from the date of the first breach, not the date the plaintiff learns of its legal significance. *Id.* at 520-21. The Supreme Court has likewise emphasized that limitations provisions are not tolled by ignorance of the law. In *United States v. Habig*, 390 U.S. 222 (1968), the Court held that a defendant’s ignorance of the law did not toll the statute of limitations for a tax fraud charge, reasoning that “the law imputes knowledge of all statutes to every person within the jurisdiction.” *Id.* at 227. Similarly, in *Cheatham v. United States*, 92 U.S. 85 (1875), the Court held that a plaintiff’s ignorance of a legal right did not toll the limitations period, stating that “the law charges everyone with knowledge of its provisions.” *Id.* at 89.

### II. Actual Knowledge Does Not Require Reading or Retaining Disclosures

Petitioners disclosed all relevant information regarding the investment strategy to Sulyma through Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and website postings. The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that Sulyma’s failure to read or recall these disclosures precluded a finding of “actual knowledge.” Courts have consistently held that constructive or imputed knowledge suffices to trigger limitations periods where the plaintiff had access to the information. In *Ziegler v. Conn. Gen. Life Ins. Co.*, 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1991), this Court held that plan administrators had “actual knowledge” of a breach when the insurer informed them of the transaction, even if they did not fully understand its legal implications. *Id.* at 552. Similarly, in *Waller v. Blue Cross of California*, 32 F.3d 1337 (9th Cir. 1994), the Court held that plan participants had “actual knowledge” of a breach when the plan administrator disclosed the transaction, regardless of whether they read or retained the documents.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that limitations periods may run even where a plaintiff does not read or recall disclosures. In *United States v. Laches*, 19 F. Cas. 1042 (C.C.D. Mass. 1815) (No. 11,908), Justice Story held that a plaintiff’s failure to read a deed did not toll the limitations period, stating that “the law imputes knowledge of all instruments properly recorded.” *Id.* at 1044. Likewise, in *Jackson v. Lamphire*, 20 U.S. 280 (1822), the Court held that a plaintiff’s ignorance of a recorded deed did not toll the statute of limitations, reasoning that “the law charges everyone with knowledge of public records.” *Id.* at 290.

### III. The Ninth Circuit’s Holding Undermines ERISA’s Purpose

The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” frustrates ERISA’s goal of providing finality to fiduciaries. As this Court explained in *Ziegler*, a contrary rule would “essentially read the ‘actual knowledge’ standard out of the statute.” 916 F.2d at 550. The Supreme Court has emphasized that ERISA’s limitations provisions must be strictly enforced to prevent stale claims. In *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), the Court reaffirmed that “ERISA’s limitations provisions are not subject to equitable tolling absent extraordinary circumstances.” *Id.* at 536 (citing *Rotella v. Wood*, 528 U.S. 549 (2000)). The Ninth Circuit’s holding—requiring knowledge of legal impropriety—would invite endless litigation and undermine the finality that ERISA’s limitations provisions were designed to provide.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that the Ninth Circuit’s decision should be reversed. Sulyma’s claims are time-barred because he had “actual knowledge” of the alleged breach more than three years before filing suit. The judgment below should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to reinstate the district court’s grant of summary judgment.
