Respondent’s position is correct: the constitutional navigability-for-title inquiry is a historical one, and the relevant question is whether the river segment was navigable when the State entered the Union. That rule follows directly from the equal-footing doctrine and the Supreme Court’s navigability cases.

The United States “takes title” to the beds of waters that were navigable at statehood, and the States receive those beds upon admission to the Union. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 229-30 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 26-27 (1894). The Court has repeatedly held that state title depends on the condition of the waterway “at the time of statehood.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935). That rule is not discretionary; it is the constitutional measure of title.

In *The Daniel Ball*, the Court distinguished navigability as a commerce concept from title purposes, holding that rivers are navigable when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, as highways of commerce. 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). But for title, the inquiry is tied to the historical condition of the river at statehood, not to later alterations. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-77; *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940) (recognizing that navigability for regulatory purposes is broader than for title and must be kept distinct). The Court has thus rejected conflating title navigability with modern use or later improvements. *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14-16.

That distinction is especially important here because the question presented asks whether a trial court must determine navigability “at the time of statehood.” The answer is yes. In *United States v. Utah*, the Court explained that title turns on whether the river segment was navigable “at the time of admission into the Union.” 283 U.S. at 75. In *Utah v. United States*, the Court reaffirmed that the relevant inquiry is whether the water was navigable “when the State entered the Union.” 403 U.S. 9, 10-11 (1971). And in *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, the Supreme Court later made the point expressly, holding that navigability for title is assessed “at the time of statehood,” and that the inquiry is segment-by-segment. 565 U.S. 576, 592-93, 600-01 (2012).

The State’s proof may rely on historical evidence because the question is historical. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 82-83; *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 565 U.S. at 596-97. But historical evidence is a means of proving the statehood condition of the river, not a basis for shifting the legal standard to some later period. The Court in *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.* recognized that later improvements may bear on regulatory navigability, but not on title. 311 U.S. at 408-09. Likewise, *Montana v. United States* confirms that the federal navigational servitude and title navigability are distinct doctrines. 450 U.S. 544, 551-52 (1981).

Montana’s contrary approach cannot stand because it would sever title from the equal-footing doctrine. The doctrine exists to place new States on the same footing as the original States, which received title to the beds of navigable waters at sovereignty. *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 229-30; *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57-58. If navigability were measured at some later time, state title would become contingent on post-statehood changes in commerce, engineering, or river conditions, contrary to the settled rule in *Utah* and *Oregon*.

Accordingly, the proper constitutional test requires the trial court to determine whether the relevant river segment was navigable at the time of statehood. That is the rule announced by the Court’s title cases, reaffirmed in *PPL Montana*, and compelled by the equal-footing doctrine. *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1 (1894); *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870); *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1 (1935); *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377 (1940); *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544 (1981); *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 565 U.S. 576 (2012).
