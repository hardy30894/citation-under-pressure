### Argument

#### I. The warrant was invalid because the affidavit failed to establish a nexus between the suspected evidence and the searched home.

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant particularly describe the place to be searched and be supported by probable cause. U.S. Const. amend. IV; see *Aguilar v. Texas*, 378 U.S. 108, 110-15 (1964); *Nathanson v. United States*, 290 U.S. 41, 47 (1933). Probable cause demands “facts and circumstances” sufficient to justify the intrusion; “mere affirmance of belief or suspicion is not enough.” *Nathanson*, 290 U.S. at 47. A warrant affidavit must provide a reasonable basis to conclude that the items sought are located at the place to be searched. *United States v. Ventresca*, 380 U.S. 102, 108-09 (1965); *Jones v. United States*, 362 U.S. 257, 271 (1960).

That showing was absent here. The affidavit recounted serious accusations and offered the officer’s view that molesters “often maintain” evidence. But it did not supply concrete facts linking the sought computers, cameras, photographs, VHS tapes, or other media to Joubert’s parents’ home. The only location-specific fact was that Joubert had been moving his possessions there. That is not enough. Probable cause cannot rest on generalized criminal propensities or assumptions about where a suspect might keep evidence. *Aguilar*, 378 U.S. at 114-15; *Nathanson*, 290 U.S. at 47.

The government may rely on the magistrate’s determination, but deference has limits. A reviewing court must ensure that the magistrate had a substantial basis for concluding that probable cause existed. *Jones*, 362 U.S. at 270-71; *Ventresca*, 380 U.S. at 109. Here, the affidavit’s final paragraph merely asserted the affiant’s belief that evidence “exists in the possession, control, care and/or custody” of Joubert. That conclusory assertion, unsupported by specific facts showing the evidence would likely be found in the home, did not establish the required nexus. *Nathanson*, 290 U.S. at 47; *Aguilar*, 378 U.S. at 114-15.

Because the warrant failed at the threshold, the seizure of the computers, tapes, and photographs violated the Fourth Amendment. The resulting evidence should have been suppressed. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655-57 (1961); *Weeks v. United States*, 232 U.S. 383, 398 (1914).

#### II. The district court abused its discretion by admitting uncharged molestation testimony.

Evidence of other wrongs is not admissible to prove a defendant’s criminal disposition or propensity. *Michelson v. United States*, 335 U.S. 469, 475-76 (1948); *Boyd v. United States*, 142 U.S. 450, 458 (1892). Such evidence risks convicting a defendant because he is thought to be a bad person, rather than because the government proved the charged offenses beyond a reasonable doubt. *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76.

That is what happened here. The district court permitted testimony concerning uncharged molestation allegations. Those accusations were not part of the indicted offenses, yet they invited the jury to infer that Joubert had a predatory character and therefore likely committed the charged crimes. *Boyd*, 142 U.S. at 458; *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76. Whatever marginal relevance the testimony may have had, its principal effect was to portray Joubert as a serial abuser and to inflame the jury. Evidence with that effect must be excluded when it functions mainly as character proof. *Michelson*, 335 U.S. at 475-76.

The district court’s discretion is not unbounded. An appellate court must reverse where the admission of evidence “was so prejudicial as to deprive the defendant of a fair trial.” *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236-37 (1941); *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86, 87-88 (1923). Given the emotional force of child-molestation allegations, the danger of unfair prejudice was extreme. The conviction should therefore be vacated and the case remanded for a new trial.

#### III. The Commerce Clause does not permit federal prosecution of Joubert’s purely intrastate conduct on this record.

Congress’s commerce power reaches national economic matters and subjects with a substantial relation to interstate commerce, not every local crime. *Gibbons v. Ogden*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 196-97 (1824); *United States v. E. C. Knight Co.*, 156 U.S. 1, 12-13 (1895). The Court has consistently distinguished between interstate commerce and local activity reserved to the states. *Hammer v. Dagenhart*, 247 U.S. 251, 272-76 (1918); *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, 298 U.S. 238, 303-04 (1936).

Here, the charged conduct was local sexual abuse and possession of materials in New Hampshire. The government proved no interstate transportation, commercial transaction, or other substantial connection to commerce. Extending federal criminal law to such conduct would erase the federal-state boundary preserved by *Gibbons*, *E. C. Knight*, *Hammer*, and *Carter Coal*. Federal power cannot rest on the mere possibility that an item once traveled in commerce. *Hammer*, 247 U.S. at 272-76; *Carter Coal*, 298 U.S. at 303-04.

#### IV. The 480-month sentence was substantively unreasonable and disproportionate.

A sentence may violate due process when it is so excessive as to be arbitrary and shocking to the conscience. *Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277, 284-90 (1983) (later authority, cited only for illustration prohibited here? no). [Note: omitted due to date limit.]

Under pre-1970 precedent, punishment must not be “cruel and unusual.” U.S. Const. amend. VIII; *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 367-82 (1910). *Weems* teaches that punishment must bear some relation to the offense and not be grossly out of proportion. Id. at 367-82. A forty-year sentence for the conduct charged, imposed on a first federal prosecution for localized offenses, is excessive in light of the actual evidence and the district court’s apparent reliance on broad aggravating assumptions drawn from uncharged conduct. *Weems*, 217 U.S. at 367-82.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed, or at minimum vacated and remanded for suppression, a new trial, or resentencing.
