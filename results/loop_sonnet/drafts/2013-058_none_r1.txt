**ARGUMENT**

**I. Exempt status is measured by the character of the asset when the bankruptcy petition is filed, not by what the asset once was in a predecessor's hands.**

The Bankruptcy Code exempts "retirement funds" held in certain tax-favored accounts. 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). That exemption must be evaluated against the estate as it exists at the moment the petition is filed. This Court held long ago that a bankrupt's exemptions "are to be determined as of the date of the filing of the petition." *White v. Stump*, 266 U.S. 310, 313 (1924). The inquiry looks to the actual legal character of the property under governing law at that time, not to some earlier owner's use of it. *Lockwood v. Exchange Bank*, 190 U.S. 294, 299 (1903).

Measured at the proper moment, the account the Clarks seek to exempt bears none of the hallmarks of retirement savings. When Heidi Heffron-Clark filed for bankruptcy in 2010, the $300,000 she had inherited from her mother's IRA in 2001 was already subject to a mandatory distribution schedule wholly unrelated to her own retirement date. No new contributions could be made to it, and the balance could not be rolled over. The asset that mattered to Ruth Heffron's retirement in life is not the same asset—functionally speaking—that sat in Heidi's name nine years after Ruth's death, on the eve of a bankruptcy filing. The exemption statute asks what the fund *is* for the debtor now, not what it *was* for someone else.

**II. Courts look to an asset's function, not its label, in deciding whether it serves the purpose the exemption protects.**

This Court has consistently refused to let a formal label substitute for substance, whether in tax law or in bankruptcy. In *Gregory v. Helvering*, 293 U.S. 465, 469 (1935), the Court asked "whether what was done, apart from the tax motive, was the thing which the statute intended," rather than accepting the parties' chosen characterization. The word "IRA" in "inherited IRA" is exactly such a label: it identifies the *source* of the funds, not their current function. An account that must be drained on an accelerated schedule, with no possibility of continued deferral until the holder's own retirement, does not function as retirement savings merely because the Internal Revenue Code borrows the letters "IRA" to describe it.

This Court's own bankruptcy jurisprudence confirms that substance controls. In *Segal v. Rochelle*, 382 U.S. 375, 379 (1966), the Court looked past formal characterizations to the practical operation of the disputed interest in deciding whether it belonged to the estate. The same approach governed this Court's early insurance-exemption cases, which tied exemption to whether the asset continued to perform its protective function for the debtor and his dependents. *Holden v. Stratton*, 198 U.S. 202, 213 (1905); *Burlingham v. Crouse*, 228 U.S. 459, 471 (1913). Here, the inherited IRA performs no ongoing retirement-protective function for the Clarks. It is, and always was after Ruth Heffron's death, a pool of money available for present use—precisely the sort of asset creditors are entitled to reach.

**III. Treating an inherited IRA as exempt would defeat, not advance, the purpose of the bankruptcy exemption scheme.**

Exemptions exist to give the "honest but unfortunate debtor" a fresh start, not to immunize inherited windfalls from legitimate creditor claims. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934) (exemptions serve "to give to the honest but unfortunate debtor who surrenders for distribution the property which he owns at the time of bankruptcy, a new opportunity in life"). That rationale supports protecting funds the debtor has personally set aside through years of contribution and forbearance. It does not support shielding money the debtor never earned, never contributed to, and is legally required to spend down. Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would transform a narrow protection for self-funded retirement savings into a broad shelter for inheritances generally—a result the statutory text does not support and the purpose of the exemption does not require.

This Court has cautioned against reading statutes literally where doing so would produce results the legislature could not have intended, instructing courts instead to consider "the purpose of the act." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543–44 (1940). The purpose of §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) is to protect retirement savings from creditors during the debtor's working life, not to create a mechanism by which any tax-deferred account, once inherited, becomes permanently untouchable regardless of its actual use. Reading the exemption to cover the Clarks' inherited IRA would stretch the statute past its purpose and past its text, at the expense of the creditors the Bankruptcy Code is equally designed to protect.

For these reasons, the funds in Heidi Heffron-Clark's inherited IRA are not "retirement funds" within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. §522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12), and the judgment of the Seventh Circuit should be affirmed.
