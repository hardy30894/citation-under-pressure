**ARGUMENT**

**A Procedural Default That Is Not the Product of the Petitioner's Own Deliberate Choice Does Not Bar Reliance on the Underlying Constitutional Claim as Cause for a Related Default**

This Court has long recognized that the writ of habeas corpus is not to be shackled by mechanical procedural rules where a petitioner's own conduct did not produce the default. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), the Court held that a federal habeas judge may deny relief only to an applicant who has "deliberately by-passed the orderly procedure of the state courts and in so doing has forfeited his state court remedies." *Id.* at 438. The touchstone is the petitioner's own intelligent choice to forgo a state remedy—not the tactical or negligent omissions of counsel. Carpenter did not himself choose to abandon his sufficiency claim; his appellate lawyer did. Under *Fay*, that distinction is decisive.

This principle rests on the settled understanding, articulated in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458 (1938), that "[a] waiver is ordinarily an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." *Id.* at 464. A defendant cannot be said to have relinquished a constitutional claim merely because appointed counsel failed to raise it. Attributing counsel's default to the petitioner, as though it were his own considered waiver, inverts the very premise of *Zerbst*—that fundamental rights are surrendered only by the client's own knowing act, not by counsel's oversight.

**The Ineffective-Assistance Claim Retains Independent Constitutional Force Because the Right to Counsel Is Itself Fundamental**

The right to effective appellate representation is not a mere procedural nicety; it is an incident of the fundamental right to counsel this Court has repeatedly vindicated. In *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335 (1963), the Court held that "reason and reflection require us to recognize that in our adversary system of criminal justice, any person haled into court, who is too poor to hire a lawyer, cannot be assured a fair trial unless counsel is provided for him." *Id.* at 344. That same Term, in *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), the Court extended the guarantee to the first appeal as of right, holding that a State may not condition the vindication of federal rights on the fortuity of retained counsel while indigent appellants go unrepresented. *Id.* at 357–58. Carpenter's claim—that his appellate counsel failed to challenge the sufficiency of the evidence—asserts precisely the kind of constitutional deprivation *Gideon* and *Douglas* were designed to remedy. A default of that claim, itself born of the same disabling failure of counsel, should not extinguish its capacity to explain and excuse the companion default it caused.

**Comity Principles Underlying Exhaustion Do Not Require an Independently Exhausted Cause Claim Where the Default Flows from Counsel's Own Failure**

The exhaustion requirement exists, as this Court explained in *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114 (1944), so that "an application for habeas corpus by one detained under a state court judgment of conviction for crime will be entertained by a federal court only after all state remedies available . . . have been exhausted." *Id.* at 116–17. It is a rule that channels claims to the state courts in the first instance; it is not a rule of forfeiture. *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200 (1950), likewise treated exhaustion as a discretionary, judicially crafted requirement rather than a jurisdictional bar designed to extinguish meritorious federal claims. *Id.* at 203–04. Ohio had every opportunity to correct counsel's failure when Carpenter sought reopening of his appeal; the state court declined only on a timeliness ground unrelated to the merits. Nothing in the rationale of *Hawk* or *Darr* requires that the ineffective-assistance claim itself first survive a second, independent gauntlet of exhaustion before it may serve its traditional function of explaining a related default.

**This Court's Broader Habeas Corpus Jurisprudence Counsels Against Rigid, Mechanical Forfeiture Rules**

The great writ has always been treated with a flexibility that guards against the loss of liberty through default rather than through guilt. In *Moore v. Dempsey*, 261 U.S. 86 (1923), Justice Holmes explained that where a proceeding was fundamentally corrupted, "neither perfection in the machinery for correction nor the possibility that the trial court and counsel saw no other way of avoiding an immediate outbreak of the mob can prevent this Court from securing to the petitioners their constitutional rights." *Id.* at 91.

In *Sanders v. United States*, 373 U.S. 1 (1963), the Court likewise cautioned against rigid finality principles in the habeas context, explaining that habeas corpus has traditionally been administered with an eye to substantial justice rather than technical forfeiture. *Id.* at 17–18. And in *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443 (1953), the Court confirmed that federal courts bear an independent responsibility to examine the substance of constitutional claims presented in habeas, rather than treating state procedural rulings as automatically dispositive. *Id.* at 458.

Taken together, *Fay*, *Zerbst*, *Gideon*, *Douglas*, *Hawk*, *Darr*, *Moore*, *Sanders*, and *Brown* establish a consistent principle: procedural defaults attributable to counsel's own failings, rather than to the petitioner's deliberate choice, should not be permitted to compound themselves into an absolute bar to relief. The Sixth Circuit correctly recognized that Carpenter's defaulted ineffective-assistance claim retained sufficient constitutional substance to excuse the default of his sufficiency claim. This Court should affirm.
