**ARGUMENT**

The conviction of Anthony Elonis cannot stand. Section 875(c) makes it a crime to transmit “any threat to injure the person of another.” The statute does not define “threat,” so Congress adopted the term’s established common-law meaning, which includes a requirement that the speaker intend his words to be taken as a threat. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952) (“Mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent will not be read as dispensing with it.”). At common law, a threat is a statement made with the purpose of intimidating or placing another in fear. The Third Circuit’s objective standard—whether a reasonable speaker would foresee the statement as a threat—eliminates that intent requirement and reduces the crime to negligence. That is impermissible for a statute that reaches core expressive activity and carries serious penalties. *Morissette*, 342 U.S. at 250 (“The contention that an injury can amount to a crime only when inflicted by intention is no provincial or transient notion.”). This is not a public-welfare offense where strict liability may be tolerated. *United States v. Balint*, 258 U.S. 250, 252 (1922). The presumption of mens rea applies with full force, and nothing in §875(c) overcomes it.

The First Amendment independently demands a subjective intent standard. The “true threat” exception to speech protection is narrow and was designed to reach only statements the speaker intentionally communicates as a serious expression of an intent to commit unlawful violence. *Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 708 (1969). In *Watts*, this Court reversed a conviction under an analogous threat statute because the defendant’s words were “political hyperbole” and not a “true threat.” The Court examined the speaker’s apparent intent and the context, not merely how a reasonable listener would react. *Id.* at 707–08. The objective standard eliminates that constitutional inquiry. It punishes a speaker based on how a hypothetical reasonable listener interprets the words, regardless of what the speaker intended. That approach criminalizes artistic expression, sarcasm, emotional venting, and other speech the First Amendment protects.

This Court has consistently required proof of the speaker’s intent before speech may be punished. In *Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 395 U.S. 444, 447 (1969), the Court held that advocacy of force may not be proscribed unless “such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action.” The requirement is subjective: the speaker must intend incitement. *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940), reversed a breach-of-peace conviction because there was “no evidence that [the defendant] intended to incite” a breach of the peace. *Terminiello v. Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949), held that speech may not be punished simply because it “stirs people to anger.” Even *Schenck v. United States*, 249 U.S. 47, 52 (1919), the source of the clear-and-present-danger test, focused on “the intent with which the act was done.” These cases apply a fortiori to threats. If incitement requires subjective purpose to produce unlawful action, then threats—which are equally communicative—require at least the same mental state.

The government’s interest in protecting listeners from fear does not justify a lesser standard. The criminal law already punishes intentional threats. But where a speaker did not subjectively intend to threaten, punishment adds nothing to public safety and only chills protected expression. *Watts* demonstrates that the mere possibility of fear is not enough; otherwise, the most controversial speakers could be silenced by the sensibilities of their audiences. The objective standard invites juries to convict based on the offensiveness of the words themselves rather than the speaker’s culpable state of mind.

Finally, the objective standard renders §875(c) unconstitutionally vague. *United States v. L. Cohen Grocery Co.*, 255 U.S. 81, 89 (1921) (striking down statute that “forbids no specific or definite act” and leaves to courts and juries the task of fixing its meaning). A speaker cannot know in advance whether a reasonable person will deem his words threatening; liability turns on a jury’s after-the-fact evaluation of context and tone. That is intolerable in the realm of speech, where clarity is essential to avoid self-censorship. The rule of lenity therefore requires that §875(c) be construed to require subjective intent. *United States v. Reese*, 92 U.S. 214, 220 (1876) (“It would certainly be dangerous if the legislature could set a net large enough to catch all possible offenders, and leave it to the courts to step inside and say who could be rightfully detained.”).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial with instructions that the jury be required to find that Elonis subjectively intended his communications to be threats.
