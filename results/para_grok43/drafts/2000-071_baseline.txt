**ARGUMENT**

**I. Milford’s Exclusion of the Good News Club Was Permissible Subject-Matter Discrimination in a Limited Public Forum.**

Milford’s Community Use Policy opens school facilities after hours only for “social, civic and recreational meetings and entertainment events and other uses pertaining to the welfare of the community.” The policy expressly excludes “use for religious purposes.” This restriction creates a limited public forum. In such a forum the State “may reserve [it] for its intended purposes, communicative or otherwise, as long as the regulation on speech is reasonable and not an effort to suppress expression merely because public officials oppose the speaker’s view.” Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983).

Within a limited public forum, distinctions based on subject matter are constitutional. Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund, Inc., 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985). Milford has permitted secular organizations such as the Boy Scouts, Girl Scouts, and 4-H Club to discuss moral and character development. It has never permitted religious instruction or prayer. The Club’s meetings consist of prayer, memorization of scripture, Bible stories, and invitations to accept Jesus Christ as personal Savior. These activities are “the equivalent of religious worship” and “religious instruction itself,” not merely the discussion of secular subjects from a religious perspective. The court of appeals correctly held that the Club’s program therefore falls outside the forum’s boundaries. Because the exclusion turns on the religious character of the speech rather than on the Club’s Christian viewpoint, it is subject-matter discrimination, not viewpoint discrimination. Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 829 (1995) (distinguishing between “content discrimination” and “viewpoint discrimination”).

**II. Even If Viewpoint Discrimination Occurred, the Establishment Clause Justifies the Exclusion.**

If the exclusion were deemed viewpoint discrimination, it would still be required to avoid violating the Establishment Clause. In a public school, permitting an outside group to conduct religious instruction and prayer for elementary-age children immediately after the school day would have the primary effect of advancing religion. Lemon v. Kurtzman, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971). The school would appear to endorse the Club’s religious message by providing its facilities and the ready audience of released students. See Edwards v. Aguillard, 482 U.S. 578, 584 (1987). Milford’s interest in complying with the Establishment Clause supplies a compelling justification that satisfies strict scrutiny. Widmar v. Vincent, 454 U.S. 263, 271 (1981).

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
