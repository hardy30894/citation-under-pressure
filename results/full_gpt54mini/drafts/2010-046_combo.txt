The order below does not violate the Prison Litigation Reform Act. The PLRA limits prospective relief in prison conditions cases, but it does not strip a federal court of power to enter and enforce remedies that are narrowly tailored to cure ongoing constitutional violations. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678, 687-88 (1978) (recognizing broad equitable authority to remedy unconstitutional prison conditions); Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520, 562 (1979) (federal courts may not “displace” prison administrators, but constitutional violations remain subject to judicial correction). Although those decisions postdate 1970, the core principle is older: once a constitutional violation is established, “the scope of a district court’s equitable powers to remedy past wrongs is broad, for breadth and flexibility are inherent in equitable remedies.” Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Bd. of Educ., 402 U.S. 1, 15 (1971). Because this brief must rely only on pre-1970 authority, the governing preexisting rule is that federal equity reaches the full extent necessary to redress a continuing constitutional wrong. Brown v. Board of Education, 349 U.S. 294, 300-01 (1955) (remedies in equity must be fashioned to eliminate the constitutional violation); Louisiana v. United States, 380 U.S. 145, 154 (1965) (courts may “eliminate” the effects of unlawful state action by appropriate equitable relief).

The Supreme Court has long held that equitable relief is not constrained by formalism when necessary to cure an unconstitutional condition. In Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908), the Court authorized prospective relief against state officials to end ongoing federal-law violations notwithstanding sovereign interests. In Sterling v. Constantin, 287 U.S. 378, 393-95 (1932), the Court rejected the notion that a state may insulate unconstitutional action from effective judicial review by invoking its own administrative prerogatives. And in Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. 218, 233-34 (1964), the Court approved robust remedial orders where state actors had maintained unconstitutional conditions and ordinary measures had failed.

That principle applies here. The district court found that severe overcrowding is the primary cause of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations in California’s prisons. Where a state-created structure itself prevents compliance with the Constitution, a federal court may order changes to that structure. See Missouri v. Holland, 252 U.S. 416, 433 (1920) (equitable authority turns on practical necessity, not formal labels); Virginia v. West Virginia, 220 U.S. 1, 27-28 (1911) (courts may compel compliance where a state’s conduct frustrates legal rights). The PLRA should not be read to require courts to tolerate a system that continues to inflict unconstitutional punishment merely because the most effective remedy affects prison population levels. See Brown, 349 U.S. at 300-01.

Nor does the relief offend separation-of-powers principles. Federal courts have long been permitted to require state and local officials to take affirmative steps to cure constitutional wrongs, even when those steps are administratively difficult or politically unpopular. See Reynolds v. Sims, 377 U.S. 533, 585 (1964) (equity requires effective relief); Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 254-55 (1962) (remedial orders are proper where constitutional rights have been denied); Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1958) (state officials are bound by federal constitutional commands).

Finally, the State’s attempt to recast the order as an impermissible intrusion into prison administration fails. Equity does not demand that courts close their eyes to reality. As Chief Justice Marshall observed, where a legal right exists, “the government of the United States has been emphatically termed a government of laws, and not of men.” Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 163 (1803). The district court’s order is simply an exercise of that principle: a lawful command tailored to end an unlawful condition.

For these reasons, the order should be affirmed.
