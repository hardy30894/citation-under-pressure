# ARGUMENT

## I. The plain text of 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) distinguishes "debt collectors" from "creditors" according to whose debt is being collected, not when the debt fell into default.

The FDCPA defines a "debt collector," in relevant part, as one "who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due *another*." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6) (emphasis added). Nothing in that text asks whether the debt was in default before the collector obtained it. This Court's interpretive task begins and ends with that language. As this Court has long held, "the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed, and if that is plain, . . . the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms." *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917). Petitioners' default-based test finds no footing in that plain text; it asks this Court to read words into § 1692a(6) that Congress did not write.

Petitioners' reading also violates the settled canon against surplusage. They argue that any person excluded from the definition of "creditor" under § 1692a(4) must automatically become a "debt collector." But § 1692a(4) excludes only a person who receives a defaulted debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection . . . for another"—language petitioners' theory renders meaningless, since on their view the "for another" qualifier would do no independent work. This Court has cautioned that "a statute ought, upon the whole, to be so construed that, if it can be prevented, no clause, sentence, or word shall be superfluous, void, or insignificant." *Market Co. v. Hoffman*, 101 U.S. 112, 115 (1879). Congress's use of "for another" in § 1692a(4), like its use of "another" in § 1692a(6), signals that the creditor/debt-collector line turns on agency—whether the person collects on behalf of a third party—not on the debt's default history.

That grammatical point is reinforced by ordinary sentence construction. The phrase "owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another" in § 1692a(6) is most naturally read so that "another" governs the entire preceding clause. "When several words are followed by a clause which is applicable as much to the first and other words as to the last, the natural construction of the language demands that the clause be read as applicable to all." *Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor*, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920). So read, § 1692a(6) asks only whether the debt is due *another* at the time of collection—not whether it once was.

## II. The structure and history of the statute confirm that capacity, not chronology, is the touchstone.

Congress's chosen words are themselves "the most persuasive evidence of the purpose of a statute." *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940). The FDCPA's committee history confirms what the text already shows: Congress meant to regulate third parties who "regularly collect debts for others," not creditors collecting what is owed to themselves. That distinction tracks a familiar, centuries-old common-law line between a principal collecting its own obligation and an agent collecting on behalf of another. Where "Congress borrows terms of art in which are accumulated the legal tradition and meaning of centuries of practice, it presumably knows and adopts the cluster of ideas that were attached to each borrowed word in the body of learning from which it was taken." *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 263 (1952). The debt collector/creditor dichotomy imports that same agency logic: one who collects for its own account is a creditor; one who collects for another is a debt collector.

Nor does the timing of a person's acquisition of a debt change the character of the transaction being regulated. What matters is the *relationship* the entity occupies at the moment of the challenged conduct—not the debt's provenance. Cf. *SEC v. C.M. Joiner Leasing Corp.*, 320 U.S. 344, 352–53 (1943) (regulatory classification depends on the character of the transaction as it exists when the challenged conduct occurs). Here, once Santander purchased the receivables outright, it collected the debts for its own account, exactly as any creditor collecting a matured obligation does.

## III. Petitioners' default-based rule would require this Court to supply words Congress omitted—a legislative, not judicial, function.

Petitioners effectively ask the Court to add a temporal trigger—default at acquisition—that transforms every assignee of matured consumer debt into a "debt collector," no matter that it collects solely for itself. That request runs headlong into the settled principle that courts may not expand a statute's coverage beyond what its language supports, however sympathetic the result might seem. "To supply omissions transcends the judicial function." *Iselin v. United States*, 270 U.S. 245, 251 (1926). This caution has special force where, as here, the statute in question exposes defendants to liability, including statutory damages. It is "the legislature, not the Court, which is to define" the scope of liability-creating enactments. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820).

Congress knew how to draw a default-based line; it did so explicitly in the servicer exclusion of § 1692a(6)(F)(iii), which turns on whether debt "was not in default at the time it was obtained." Congress's choice to use that language in one narrow exclusion—but not in the threshold definition of "debt collector"—confirms that default status was not meant to control the broader inquiry. The judgment of the Fourth Circuit should be affirmed.
