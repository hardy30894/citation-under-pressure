**ARGUMENT**

I. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY HELD THAT “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” UNDER ERISA § 1113(2) REQUIRES KNOWLEDGE OF BOTH THE ALLEGED BREACH AND ITS IMPRUDENCE**

The Ninth Circuit’s construction of “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) is consistent with the statute’s text, structure, and this Court’s precedents. Section 1113(2) bars an action “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that this provision requires knowledge not only of the underlying transaction or event, but also of its character as a breach of ERISA’s fiduciary duties. *Sulyma v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee*, 936 F.3d 1043, 1050 (9th Cir. 2019) (“[A]ctual knowledge of the breach” requires knowledge that the defendant acted and that those acts were imprudent). This interpretation gives effect to the word “breach,” which presupposes knowledge of both the act and its legal wrongfulness. *See, e.g., United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 511 (1995) (holding that “knowingly” in a criminal statute requires knowledge of the “essential” facts constituting the offense).

The Ninth Circuit’s approach is also consistent with this Court’s decision in *Tibble v. Edison International*, 575 U.S. 523 (2015), which emphasized that ERISA’s fiduciary duties are defined by the statute itself. *Id.* at 530 (noting that ERISA “sets out a strict standard of care” for fiduciaries). Knowledge of imprudence is thus essential to “actual knowledge” of a breach, because imprudence is a statutory element of an ERISA fiduciary breach. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1)(B) (requiring fiduciaries to act “with the care, skill, prudence, and diligence under the circumstances then prevailing that a prudent man acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use”).

II. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT’S HOLDING DOES NOT IMPOSE AN IMPOSSIBLE OR UNFAIR BURDEN ON ERISA FIDUCIARIES**

Petitioners argue that the Ninth Circuit’s construction imposes an undue burden on fiduciaries to ensure that participants read and understand disclosures. This argument misapprehends the standard. The Ninth Circuit did not require fiduciaries to prove that participants read or understood disclosures, only that the participant had actual knowledge of the imprudent act. *Sulyma*, 936 F.3d at 1050. Actual knowledge is a subjective standard, not an objective one. *See, e.g., Merritt v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, 933 F.3d 742, 750 (9th Cir. 2019) (holding that actual knowledge requires “true awareness” of the facts constituting the breach).

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s approach is consistent with ERISA’s remedial purpose. *See Massachusetts Mut. Life Ins. Co. v. Russell*, 473 U.S. 134, 147 (1985) (noting that ERISA is “a comprehensive statute designed to promote the interests of employees and their beneficiaries in employee benefit plans”). Allowing fiduciaries to rely on disclosures alone to trigger the limitations period would undermine this purpose by permitting fiduciaries to insulate themselves from liability simply by burying participants in information. *See, e.g., In re Unisys Corp. Ret. Plan Litig.*, 242 F.3d 497, 504 (3d Cir. 2001) (rejecting the argument that constructive knowledge should apply under § 1113(2) because it would “frustrate ERISA’s remedial goals”).

III. **THE NINTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY REJECTED PETITIONERS’ ATTEMPT TO EQUATE “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” WITH MERE ACCESS TO INFORMATION**

Petitioners contend that the Ninth Circuit erroneously required participants to have read and understood disclosures to trigger the limitations period. This argument conflates “actual knowledge” with constructive knowledge, which Congress expressly repealed in 1987. *See Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330*. The Ninth Circuit’s decision properly distinguishes between mere access to information and true awareness of the breach. *Sulyma*, 936 F.3d at 1050.

This Court has repeatedly cautioned against interpreting ERISA’s limitations provisions to impose constructive knowledge standards. *See, e.g., Tibble*, 575 U.S. at 530 (noting that ERISA’s limitations provisions must be read in light of the statute’s remedial purpose). The Ninth Circuit’s construction avoids this pitfall by requiring actual, subjective knowledge of the breach and its imprudence.

**CONCLUSION**

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that “actual knowledge” under ERISA § 1113(2) requires knowledge of both the alleged breach and its imprudence. This construction is consistent with the statute’s text, structure, and this Court’s precedents. Petitioners’ arguments to the contrary are unavailing and should be rejected.
