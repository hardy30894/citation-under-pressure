Santander is not a “debt collector” under the FDCPA because it collected the loans for its own account after it purchased them. The Act’s text draws a line between one who “regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another,” 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6), and a creditor collecting its own debt. The ordinary meaning of “another” confirms that the statute reaches third-party collectors, not an owner collecting for itself. See, e.g., Porto Rico Ry., Light & Power Co. v. Mor, 253 U.S. 345, 348 (1920); cf. United States v. Missouri Pac. R. Co., 278 U.S. 269, 278 (1929). Once Santander purchased the receivables, the debts were no longer “owed or due another” when Santander sought payment. That is the critical statutory fact.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly instructed that statutory terms must be read in context and according to their ordinary sense. See Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917); United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, 310 U.S. 534, 542-44 (1940); Securities & Exchange Comm’n v. C. M. Joiner Leasing Corp., 320 U.S. 344, 350-51 (1943). Here, the FDCPA’s structure reinforces the ordinary reading. Congress defined “creditor” as one to whom the debt is owed, and separately defined “debt collector” as one collecting debts owed another. 15 U.S.C. §§ 1692a(4), 1692a(6). The Act therefore regulates conduct of third-party collectors, not creditors collecting their own accounts. See Federal Trade Comm’n v. Raladam Co., 283 U.S. 643, 649-50 (1931) (statutory text controls); United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co., 287 U.S. 77, 84 (1932) (court may not “substitute the language of [its] own” for Congress’s).

Petitioners’ contrary argument depends on treating default status as dispositive. But nothing in the main definition of “debt collector” turns on whether the debt was in default when acquired. Instead, default appears only in an exclusion for persons collecting debts “owed or due another” that were not in default when obtained. 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii). Exclusions cannot expand the statutory definition they qualify. See McBoyle v. United States, 283 U.S. 25, 27 (1931); Gregory v. Helvering, 293 U.S. 465, 469-70 (1935). The better reading is that the exclusion spares servicers and similar agents from coverage, while the principal definition continues to focus on whether the collector is acting for itself or for another. See Burnet v. Niagara Brewing Co., 282 U.S. 648, 654 (1931); Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 671 (1950).

This Court should also resist petitioners’ attempt to convert a narrow consumer-protection statute into a broad regulation of all creditors that buy distressed debt. Courts do not read statutes to create anomalies Congress did not express. See Crooks v. Harrelson, 282 U.S. 55, 60 (1930); United States v. Kirby, 74 U.S. (7 Wall.) 482, 486-87 (1869). Petitioners’ theory would sweep in any finance company that both originates and purchases debt, even when collecting solely for itself. Congress used the term “debt collector,” not “debt purchaser,” and courts are not free to rewrite that choice. See Caminetti, 242 U.S. at 485; Joiner Leasing, 320 U.S. at 350-51.

Nor does the FDCPA’s purpose overcome the text. Purpose can illuminate ambiguity, but it cannot displace clear language. See Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath, 339 U.S. 33, 40 (1950); United States v. Leslie Salt Co., 350 U.S. 383, 396 (1956). The Act’s stated aim is “to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors,” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e), not to regulate creditors when they collect debts they own. That distinction was central to Congress’s drafting.

Because Santander was collecting debts it owned, on its own behalf, after purchasing them from CitiFinancial Auto, it was not acting as a “debt collector” under 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). The judgment below should be affirmed.
