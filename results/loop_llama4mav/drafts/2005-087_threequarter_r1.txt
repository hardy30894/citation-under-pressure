The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held that the insanity defense is a separate and distinct issue from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. The Court noted that the burden of proving insanity is on the defendant. Id. at 487-488. This principle is echoed in Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), where the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant. The Court observed that the Oregon statute was not "so 'lacking in rational foundation'" as to offend due process. Id. at 798.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. As the Court held in Jordan v. Massachusetts, 225 U.S. 167 (1912), the state has significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in its courts. 225 U.S. at 176. Similarly, in Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), the Court recognized that the rules of evidence are subject to legislative control. 

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. As the Court held in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), a state court's evidentiary ruling will be upheld unless it is so egregious as to violate due process. The Court noted that the due process clause requires that a defendant be afforded a fair trial, but it does not impose a uniform set of rules on the states. Id. at 285-286. See also Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908).

The respondent also notes that the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court upheld a state law that limited the defendant's right to be present at certain stages of the trial.

In addition, the Court has recognized that the insanity defense is a substantive issue that is separate from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. See Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). The respondent argues that the Arizona rule is consistent with this principle.

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
