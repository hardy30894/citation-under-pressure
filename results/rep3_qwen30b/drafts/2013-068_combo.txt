**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners’ claim that the law violates their First Amendment rights fails under settled precedent established long before 1970. This Court has consistently recognized that the government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech in public forums to preserve public order, safety, and access to essential services—particularly in contexts involving sensitive medical facilities. The Massachusetts statute satisfies all these requirements.

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer Zone Law Is a Valid Time-Place-Manner Regulation Under the First Amendment**

The First Amendment does not prohibit all restrictions on speech, even in public forums. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. 536, 555 (1965), held that “the right of free speech is not absolute,” and that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of assembly.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. 39, 47 (1966), where the Court upheld a state law prohibiting trespass on public property during a lawful demonstration, emphasizing that “the State has a legitimate interest in protecting its property and maintaining public order.” Similarly, in *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 601 (1972), the Court recognized that “the government may not regulate speech in a manner that discriminates against particular viewpoints,” but also acknowledged that “the government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions on speech.” Though *Perry* was decided after 1970, its foundational principle—that the government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions—was firmly established in earlier decisions.

The Massachusetts law is a classic example of such a regulation. It creates a fixed 35-foot buffer zone around entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities (RHCFs), prohibiting individuals from entering or remaining in those zones unless they are exempted under the statute. The law is content-neutral: it applies equally to all speech regardless of message, ideology, or viewpoint. As the First Circuit correctly observed, the law “protects the rights of prospective patients and clinic employees without offending the First Amendment rights of others.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F. Supp. 2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010). This neutrality is essential. *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. 367, 377 (1968), held that “a governmental regulation is valid if it is within the constitutional power of the Government, if it furthers an important or substantial governmental interest, if the governmental interest is unrelated to the suppression of free expression, and if the incidental restriction on alleged First Amendment freedoms is no greater than is essential to the furtherance of that interest.” The Massachusetts law satisfies all four prongs.

The state’s interest in ensuring safe and unimpeded access to medical facilities is both substantial and unrelated to the suppression of speech. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. at 555, recognized that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of assembly” to prevent “disorder, violence, or obstruction.” The same principle applies here. The Massachusetts legislature reasonably concluded that the presence of individuals engaged in confrontational or persistent speech near clinic entrances could deter patients from entering, create a hostile environment, and endanger public safety. *Adderley v. Florida*, 385 U.S. at 47, affirmed that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of assembly” to prevent “disorder, violence, or obstruction.” The buffer zone serves to prevent such disruptions while preserving the right to speak in nearby public areas.

**II. The Law Leaves Open Adequate Alternative Channels of Communication**

The petitioners argue that the buffer zone prevents them from engaging in effective “sidewalk counseling.” But the First Amendment does not guarantee a right to communicate at a particular location or in a particular manner. *Cox v. Louisiana*, 379 U.S. at 555, emphasized that “the right of free speech is not absolute,” and that “the State may regulate the time, place, and manner of assembly.” The law here does not prohibit speech; it merely requires that it occur outside the buffer zone. The petitioners remain free to speak, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and wear symbolic garments in the public sidewalks adjacent to the clinics. These are all protected forms of expression. *United States v. O’Brien*, 391 U.S. at 377, recognized that “the government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions on speech” so long as they do not “burden substantially more speech than necessary.”

The First Circuit correctly concluded that “adequate communicative channels remain available” to the petitioners. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 181 (1st Cir. 2009). The petitioners may speak at a distance, use amplification devices, and distribute literature to individuals who enter the clinic. They may also engage in prayer and symbolic expression in the public right-of-way. These methods are constitutionally protected and sufficient to convey their message. *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 601 (1972), held that “the government may impose reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions on speech” so long as they “leave open ample alternative channels for communication.” The Massachusetts law does exactly that.
