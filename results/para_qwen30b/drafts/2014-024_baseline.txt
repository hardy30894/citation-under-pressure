**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—lies at the heart of the First Amendment’s protection against criminalizing speech absent a clear intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s adoption of an objective “true threat” standard, which permits conviction based solely on whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat, violates the constitutional guarantee of free speech. This Court has long held that the First Amendment protects speech unless it falls within a narrow category of unprotected expression, and the “true threat” exception is not a license to punish speech based on its perceived danger to others, but only when the speaker *intends* to threaten. The Court’s decision in *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003), makes clear that a true threat requires a subjective intent to intimidate or cause fear of violence. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding—endorsing an objective standard that disregards the speaker’s intent—creates a dangerous precedent that criminalizes speech based on the listener’s reaction, not the speaker’s mind.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991), to uphold an objective standard is misplaced. *Kosma* predated *Black* and did not consider the constitutional imperative that a true threat must be rooted in the speaker’s intent. In *Black*, the Court rejected the notion that a threat could be punished merely because it was “likely to be perceived as a threat” by a reasonable person. Instead, the Court emphasized that “a true threat is a statement in which the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of intent to commit an act of violence.” *Black*, 538 U.S. at 359. The Court further explained that “the speaker must actually intend to threaten,” and that “the speaker’s intent is the critical element.” Id. at 359–60. This constitutional standard is not a mere procedural formality—it is the bedrock of First Amendment protection. As the Court stated in *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 21 (1971), “The First Amendment protects speech even when it is offensive, disagreeable, or even repugnant.” To punish speech based on its potential to frighten others, without regard to the speaker’s intent, is to punish the *effect* of speech, not the *intent*, and thus to criminalize protected expression.

The Third Circuit’s insistence that an objective standard is necessary to “protect individuals from the fear of violence” misapprehends the purpose of the true threat exception. The exception exists not to punish speech that causes fear, but to punish speech that *is intended* to cause fear. As the Court recognized in *Black*, the true threat exception “does not prohibit speech that merely expresses a desire to harm or that is merely offensive.” 538 U.S. at 359. The fear generated by speech is not the test; the speaker’s intent is. The Court has repeatedly warned against allowing the government to punish speech based on its potential to provoke fear, because such a standard “would permit the government to suppress speech simply because it is unpopular or disturbing.” *Texas v. Johnson*, 491 U.S. 397, 414 (1989). The Third Circuit’s approach, which allows conviction based on a reasonable person’s perception, effectively permits the government to punish speech that is “offensive” or “disturbing” even when the speaker did not intend to threaten. This is precisely the kind of overbroad suppression the First Amendment forbids.

The facts of this case underscore the danger of the Third Circuit’s standard. Elonis’s Facebook posts were written in the form of rap lyrics, a genre known for hyperbolic and fictional expression. His wife testified that she took the posts seriously, but she also acknowledged that she had never seen Elonis write rap lyrics before. Trial Tr. 97. The jury was instructed that a statement is a true threat if a reasonable speaker would foresee it being interpreted as a threat—regardless of whether Elonis himself believed he was threatening. This instruction allowed the jury to convict based on the *perception* of the audience, not the *intent* of the speaker. Yet Elonis’s own testimony revealed that he was writing in a fictional, artistic mode, and that he did not intend to harm anyone. The jury was not permitted to consider this subjective intent, despite its constitutional significance.

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that “the jury’s verdict rests on legally sufficient evidence” is therefore flawed. The evidence may have supported a finding that a reasonable person would perceive the statements as threatening, but it did not support a finding that Elonis *intended* to threaten. The Constitution does not permit a conviction based on a reasonable person’s perception when the speaker did not intend to threaten. As the Court held in *United States v. Alvarez*, 567 U.S. 709, 720 (2012), “The First Amendment protects speech even when it is false, so long as it is not intended to deceive.” The same principle applies to threats: speech is not a true threat unless the speaker intends it to be one.

The judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed. A conviction under § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant *subjectively intended* to threaten. Without such proof, the conviction violates the First Amendment. The Court should hold that the true threat exception to the First Amendment requires a showing of the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten. To do otherwise is to allow the government to punish speech based on its effect, not its intent—precisely the kind of censorship the First Amendment was designed to prevent.

For these reasons, the petition should be granted, and the judgment of the Third Circuit reversed.
