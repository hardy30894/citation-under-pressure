The respondent respectfully submits that the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.

The Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent. In Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895), the Court held that the insanity defense is a separate and distinct issue from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. The Court noted that "the law is administered in the interests of the community" and that "the state may rightfully take measures to guard against the commission of crime." Id. at 488. This principle is echoed in Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), where the Court upheld an Oregon statute that placed the burden of proving insanity on the defendant.

The respondent argues that the trial court's limitation on the use of Clark's expert evidence did not deprive him of a fundamental right. As the Court held in Powell v. Texas, 392 U.S. 514 (1968) (plurality opinion), is not directly on point, but  Sinclair v. United States, 279 U.S. 263 (1929) and  Jordan v. Massachusetts, 225 U.S. 167 (1912) are. In Sinclair, the Court noted that "the due process clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present all evidence he desires." 279 U.S. at 269 (citing  Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893)). Similarly, in Jordan, the Court held that "the state has the right to regulate the presentation of evidence in its courts." 225 U.S. at 176.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in defining the rules of evidence and procedure in their courts. As the Court held in Brown v. Mississippi, 297 U.S. 278 (1936), "the due process clause does not impose a rigid set of rules on the states." Id. at 285. See also  Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908). The Arizona rule limiting the use of expert evidence to the insanity claim is a reasonable exercise of this latitude.

The respondent also notes that the Court has upheld state laws that restrict the use of certain types of evidence. In  Shepard v. United States, 290 U.S. 96 (1933), the Court held that a state law excluding certain evidence did not violate the due process clause. Similarly, in  Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court upheld a state law that limited the defendant's right to be present at certain stages of the trial.

In addition, the Court has recognized that the insanity defense is a "substantive" issue that is separate from the question of whether the defendant formed the requisite criminal intent. See  Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469 (1895). The respondent argues that the Arizona rule is consistent with this principle.

The respondent concludes that the Arizona Supreme Court's decision to limit the use of Clark's expert evidence to his insanity claim is consistent with established precedent and does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court.
