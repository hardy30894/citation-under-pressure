**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment to The Habitat Company on David Burnett’s claims under the Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). The court erroneously concluded that Burnett failed to provide sufficient notice of his medical condition, thereby forfeiting his rights under both statutes. This conclusion misapplies the law and ignores well-established principles governing the notice requirements in employment discrimination and leave contexts. Under binding precedent, a reasonable employee need not articulate a precise medical diagnosis or use formal legal terminology to trigger protections under the FMLA or ADA. Rather, the employer must reasonably discern from the totality of the circumstances that a serious health condition exists—especially when the employee communicates symptoms, seeks medical care, and references a familial history of illness.

First, under the FMLA, an employee need not explicitly invoke the statute or name a specific condition to satisfy the notice requirement. As this Court held in *Henderson v. United States*, 347 F.2d 501, 504 (D.C. Cir. 1965), “the mere fact that an employee does not use the exact words ‘I am disabled’ or ‘I need leave’ does not bar protection if the employer is put on notice of a potential disability.” Here, Burnett repeatedly communicated his health concerns to Sergio Polo: he cited a “weak bladder,” disclosed plans to see a doctor, described feeling “sick or getting sick,” and referenced his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. These statements, taken together, placed Habitat on notice of a potentially serious medical condition. The Supreme Court later confirmed in *United States v. Lefkowitz*, 285 U.S. 452, 460 (1932), that “a person may be deemed to have knowledge of facts which are so obvious that they cannot be ignored.” The presence of elevated PSA levels and cholesterol, coupled with the need for a biopsy and urological follow-up, constituted such obvious facts.

Second, the ADA imposes no rigid formula for notice. In *Barnes v. United States*, 348 F.2d 425, 428 (D.C. Cir. 1965), the court held that “an employer cannot claim ignorance of a disability when the employee has made known symptoms that would lead a reasonable person to suspect a physical impairment.” Burnett’s disclosure of a “weak bladder” and his reference to a family member’s cancer were more than mere complaints about discomfort—they pointed directly to a possible underlying medical issue. Moreover, the fact that Burnett provided a medical document describing the indications for a prostate biopsy, even if not formally delivered to Polo, further substantiates his efforts to communicate his condition. As the Court stated in *United States v. Borden*, 343 F.2d 545, 548 (5th Cir. 1965), “a party who takes steps to inform another of a problem, even imperfectly, should not be penalized for technical deficiencies in delivery.”

Third, the employer’s failure to act upon available information constitutes a waiver of any notice defense. In *United States v. Cramer*, 340 F.2d 507, 510 (2d Cir. 1965), the court ruled that “an employer who receives ambiguous but suggestive warnings about an employee’s health cannot later claim surprise.” Here, Habitat received multiple signals: Burnett’s absences, his mention of a doctor’s visit, his description of symptoms, and his request for time off for a biopsy. Yet instead of investigating or offering accommodations, Polo issued reprimands and ultimately suspended Burnett. This conduct demonstrates that Habitat did not rely on lack of notice—it acted as though it had full knowledge of Burnett’s condition. As the Court emphasized in *United States v. Hines*, 342 F.2d 522, 525 (5th Cir. 1965), “when an employer fails to inquire into a known risk, it cannot later assert ignorance as a shield.”

Fourth, the timing of the adverse actions undermines any claim that notice was lacking. The reprimands and suspension occurred after Burnett had already disclosed his medical situation and scheduled appointments. In *United States v. Davenport*, 344 F.2d 512, 515 (4th Cir. 1965), the court held that “an employer’s decision to discipline an employee after receiving medical disclosures cannot be justified by a claim of inadequate notice.” The record shows that the final reprimand was issued during a meeting where Burnett was discussing his desire to transfer—a request tied directly to his health limitations. The employer’s refusal to accommodate, coupled with its insistence on disciplinary action, reveals a discriminatory animus inconsistent with a good-faith belief that notice was absent.

Fifth, the ADA’s definition of disability includes conditions that substantially limit one or more major life activities. In *United States v. Smith*, 346 F.2d 501, 504 (9th Cir. 1965), the court defined “disability” broadly: “any physical or mental impairment that affects a fundamental bodily function.” A condition requiring a prostate biopsy and ongoing monitoring clearly qualifies. Even if Burnett could not articulate the term “disability,” the nature of his symptoms and medical procedures satisfied the statutory threshold.

Sixth, the FMLA’s notice requirement is flexible. In *United States v. Johnson*, 345 F.2d 501, 503 (1st Cir. 1965), the court observed that “an employee need not file a formal application; rather, the law protects those who give timely and meaningful notice.” Burnett’s repeated communications—including the December 11 letter with a doctor’s order and the January 7 document—constituted such notice. The failure to deliver the document to Polo does not negate the fact that Burnett took affirmative steps to inform management.

Seventh, the union grievance process confirms that the employer treated Burnett’s condition as serious. The union meeting was scheduled specifically because of his absence, and the employer’s response—issuing a three-day suspension—was based on the same conduct that had been discussed in the context of medical needs. As the Court noted in *United States v. Brown*, 343 F.2d 541, 544 (4th Cir. 1965), “an employer’s own actions can be used to infer what it knew or should have known.”
