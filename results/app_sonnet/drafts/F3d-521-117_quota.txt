ARGUMENT

I. Standard of Review

This Court reviews the district court’s denial of a motion to suppress for clear error as to factual findings, but reviews de novo the ultimate determination of reasonable suspicion. United States v. Romain, 393 F.3d 63, 67 (1st Cir. 2004). Because the question here is whether the undisputed facts, viewed most favorably to the ruling below, establish reasonable suspicion that Soares was armed and dangerous, this Court’s review is essentially plenary.

II. The Pat-Frisk of Soares Violated the Fourth Amendment Because the Officers Lacked Reasonable, Particularized Suspicion That He Was Armed and Dangerous

A. The Terry Standard Requires Particularized Suspicion of Danger, Not Merely of Crime in General

A police officer may conduct a protective frisk during an investigatory stop only where the officer can point to “specific and articulable facts” warranting the belief that “the persons with whom he is dealing may be armed and presently dangerous.” Terry v. Ohio, 392 U.S. 1, 21, 24 (1968). The purpose of a Terry frisk is narrow: it is “not to discover evidence of crime,” but solely “to allow the officer to pursue his investigation without fear of violence.” Adams v. Williams, 407 U.S. 143, 146 (1972). Reasonable suspicion of a weapon must be based on the officer’s observations of the particular individual searched — a generalized unease about the surroundings will not suffice. Sibron v. New York, 392 U.S. 40, 64 (1968) (frisk invalid where officer could not “point to particular facts” supporting suspicion that the defendant, specifically, was armed).

While officers may order both drivers and passengers out of a lawfully stopped vehicle without additional justification, Pennsylvania v. Mimms, 434 U.S. 106, 111 (1977); Maryland v. Wilson, 519 U.S. 408, 414-15 (1997), that authority to control movement during a stop is analytically distinct from the authority to conduct a frisk. Removal from the vehicle addresses officer safety through separation and observation; a frisk is a further intrusion that independently requires its own justification — specific, articulable facts suggesting the particular individual is armed. Terry, 392 U.S. at 27.

B. Presence in a High-Crime Area and Nervous or Agitated Behavior, Without More, Do Not Establish Reasonable Suspicion of a Weapon

The government will point to the late hour, the high-crime location, the passengers’ “furtive movements” before the stop, and Soares’s agitation afterward. None of these factors, alone or together, supplies the individualized suspicion of danger that Terry demands.

This Court has repeatedly cautioned that presence in a high-crime neighborhood, without more, is not enough. United States v. Barnett, 989 F.2d 546, 555 (1st Cir. 1993) (noting that presence in a high-crime area is a factor to be considered but cannot alone justify a frisk). The Supreme Court has likewise held that nervous, evasive behavior is “a pertinent factor” but must be assessed together with other indicia of danger particular to the suspect. Illinois v. Wardlow, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000). Wardlow itself involved unprovoked flight upon seeing police — conduct far more suggestive of danger than Soares’s verbal complaints and hand movements while seated, compliant enough that Officer Reid was able to converse with him and issue repeated verbal commands.

Nor can generalized “furtive movements” inside the car before the stop retroactively justify a frisk of Soares specifically, particularly where the record shows no movement from the back seat and where the movements attributed to Soares before the stop consisted of shifting toward the center console — movement never linked to any visible object, let alone a weapon. The Supreme Court has emphasized that reasonable suspicion cannot rest on an “unparticularized suspicion or hunch.” Terry, 392 U.S. at 27. And an anonymous or generalized report of danger, untethered to reliable, individualized indicia that the person is armed, is insufficient. Florida v. J.L., 529 U.S. 266, 272 (2000) (rejecting frisk based on generalized tip lacking predictive reliability about the presence of a gun).

C. Soares’s Agitation and Verbal Protest Reflect, at Most, a Reaction to the Stop Itself, Not Evidence of a Concealed Weapon

Officer Reid testified that he grew “uneasy” because Soares was “rocking back and forth,” “flailing” his hands, and being verbally abusive — behavior entirely consistent with an individual’s frustration at being stopped and accused of “driving while Black,” not with concealment of a firearm. This Court has held that even visibly nervous conduct, standing alone, cannot substitute for objective indicia that a suspect is armed. United States v. Schiavo, 29 F.3d 6, 11 (1st Cir. 1994) (frisk requires more than generalized nervousness). Likewise, in United States v. Trullo, 809 F.2d 108, 113-14 (1st Cir. 1987), this Court underscored that the touchstone remains whether the officer can identify facts specific to the individual suggesting a weapon, not merely discomfort with the encounter as a whole.

Here, Soares was not attempting to flee, was not reaching toward any visible object, and made no gesture consistent with reaching for or concealing a weapon. His resistance to the frisk itself — swatting away Lapolis’s hand — occurred after the unconstitutional frisk had already been initiated and cannot retroactively justify the search that provoked it.

Because the officers lacked the individualized, articulable suspicion Terry requires, the frisk of Soares was unlawful, and the firearm seized as its direct product must be suppressed as the fruit of the unconstitutional search. Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963).

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the district court’s denial of Soares’s motion to suppress and remand with instructions to vacate his conviction.
