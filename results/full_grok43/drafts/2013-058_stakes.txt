ARGUMENT

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) of the Bankruptcy Code exempts from the estate only “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation under” specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The statutory text requires that the funds be retirement funds in the hands of the debtor at the time of the bankruptcy filing. An inherited IRA fails that test.

An inherited IRA is subject to mandatory distribution rules that require the beneficiary to withdraw the balance within a fixed period, often as short as five years. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); see also 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C). Those withdrawals may be used for any purpose, without penalty, and no further contributions are permitted. The account therefore functions as a time-limited tax deferral vehicle, not as a fund “set aside for retirement.” The money “had been Ruth’s, but when she died they became no one’s retirement funds.” In re Clark, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013).

This Court has repeatedly held that exemptions are determined by the character of the asset in the debtor’s hands at the commencement of the case. See, e.g., Owen v. Owen, 500 U.S. 305, 308 (1991) (exemption turns on “the debtor’s interest” on the petition date). The absence of the phrase “the debtor’s” in § 522(b)(3)(C) does not convert every asset that was once someone’s retirement savings into exempt property forever. As the Seventh Circuit observed, a debtor who inherits a homestead cannot claim it exempt merely because it was the decedent’s domicile; the exemption depends on the debtor’s own use. Clark, 714 F.3d at 562 (citing 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(A)).

The contrary view—that funds retain their exempt status indefinitely because they were once retirement funds of another—would extend the exemption far beyond its text. “No legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987). Congress chose to protect only those funds that remain dedicated to the debtor’s own retirement, not every account that carries the label “IRA.”

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
