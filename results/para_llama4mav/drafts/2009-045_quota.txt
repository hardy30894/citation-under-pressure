The Supreme Court has consistently held that a district court has the discretion to enhance a lodestar attorney's fee award in "rare" and "exceptional" cases, supported by specific evidence on the record and detailed findings by the lower court. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984); Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air ("Delaware Valley I"), 478 U.S. 546, 565 (1986). The lodestar figure, which is calculated by multiplying the number of hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate, is presumed to be the reasonable fee, but it is not immutable. Delaware Valley I, 478 U.S. at 564-65.

In Blum, the Court held that an upward adjustment of the lodestar may be justified in the "rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged and that the success was 'exceptional.'" 465 U.S. at 899. The Court in Delaware Valley I reaffirmed this principle, stating that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible ... in certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." 478 U.S. at 565.

The Eleventh Circuit has consistently applied this principle, holding that a district court may enhance a lodestar fee award based on exceptional performance and results obtained. See Kenny A. v. Perdue, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008); Norman v. Housing Auth. of Montgomery, 836 F.2d 1292, 1302 (11th Cir. 1988); NAACP v. Evergreen, 812 F.2d 1332, 1336-37 (11th Cir. 1987) (per curiam). Other circuits have reached the same conclusion. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co., 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999).

The district court's enhancement of the lodestar fee award in this case was not an abuse of discretion. The court provided detailed findings to support its decision, citing the "stellar performance of plaintiffs' counsel throughout this long and difficult case" and the "exceptional" results obtained. Kenny A. v. Perdue, 454 F.Supp.2d 1260, 1286, 1290 (N.D.Ga. 2006). The court's observations and findings were based on its own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, as well as its 58 years as a practicing attorney and federal judge. Id.

The petitioners argue that the district court's use of its own experience and observations to enhance the fee award was improper. However, the Supreme Court has held that a district court's familiarity with the relevant market and its own experience are relevant factors in determining a reasonable attorney's fee. See Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 437 (1983); Blum, 465 U.S. at 898 n. 13. Furthermore, the Court has recognized that a district court has the discretion to consider its own knowledge and experience in making a fee determination. See Fox v. Vice, 563 U.S. 826, 838 (2011).

In this case, the district court's enhancement was supported by specific evidence on the record and detailed findings, as required by Delaware Valley I and Blum. The court's decision to enhance the lodestar fee award was not an abuse of discretion, and the Eleventh Circuit correctly affirmed the district court's decision. The Supreme Court should affirm the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit, as it is consistent with the principles established in Blum, Delaware Valley I, and other cases.
