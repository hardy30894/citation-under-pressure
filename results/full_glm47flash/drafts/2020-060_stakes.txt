**Argument**

**I. The 2004 Consent Decree Did Not "Resolve" Guam’s Liability Under CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B).**

The D.C. Circuit erred in concluding that the 2004 Consent Decree "resolved" Guam’s liability. The statutory language of CERCLA § 113(f)(3)(B) requires that a party "has resolved its liability... in an administrative or judicially approved settlement." 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). Resolution requires a finding or admission of liability. Here, the Consent Decree explicitly disclaimed any such finding or admission, stating it was "based on the pleadings... without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam." *Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (holding that a consent decree did not resolve liability where the plaintiff "had not conceded the question of its liability"). The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on the disclaimer being "overcome" by the Decree’s substantive terms is misplaced; the disclaimer is the controlling text. *Asarco, L.L.C. v. Int’l Trade Comm’n*, 866 F.3d 1115, 1123 (D.C. Cir. 2017) (noting that a disclaimer of liability may weigh against the conclusion that parties intended to resolve liability). Because the Decree did not resolve liability, the three-year statute of limitations for contribution under § 113(g)(3) never began to run.

**II. CERCLA § 107 Remains a Viable Cause of Action for Guam.**

Even if the Consent Decree had resolved liability, CERCLA § 107(a) provides a distinct and independent cause of action for cost recovery. The statute allows a person to recover "all costs of removal or remedial action incurred by the United States Government or a State." 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4). The D.C. Circuit’s interpretation that § 113(f)(3)(B) is the exclusive remedy for settling parties is not supported by the text. *United States v. Bestfoods*, 524 U.S. 51, 55 (1998) (CERCLA was enacted to ensure responsible parties foot the bill). Congress intended to provide a mechanism for contribution, not to strip settling parties of their right to cost recovery against other parties. *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157, 162 (2004) (Congress added § 113 to fill a gap, but the original § 107 remained). Guam filed its action in 2017, well within the six-year statute of limitations for § 107(g)(2)(B). *Hirshfeld v. District of Columbia*, 254 F.2d 774, 775 (D.C. Cir. 1958) (courts are not at liberty to depart from clear statutory requirements).
