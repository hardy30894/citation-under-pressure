# ARGUMENT

## THE EXECUTION OF MENTALLY RETARDED OFFENDERS DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EIGHTH AMENDMENT

### I. This Court Correctly Held in *Penry* That the Eighth Amendment Does Not Categorically Bar Execution of Mentally Retarded Offenders

In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), this Court squarely considered and rejected the argument Petitioner now renews. The Court held that "mental retardation" does not, standing alone, remove an offender from the class of persons eligible for capital punishment. *Id.* at 340. Rather, the Court concluded that mental retardation is a factor to be weighed by the sentencing jury in its individualized assessment of moral culpability, not a categorical bar to the ultimate penalty. *Id.* at 328. Nothing has changed in the constitutional analysis that would justify departing from that precedent now.

This Court's Eighth Amendment jurisprudence requires that categorical exemptions from capital punishment be grounded in objective indicia reflecting a genuine national consensus. *See Stanford v. Kentucky*, 492 U.S. 361, 369 (1989) (plurality opinion) (the "primary indicator" of society's standards is "the pattern of federal and state legislation"). At the time of *Penry*, only two States and the federal government had enacted statutes exempting the mentally retarded from execution. 492 U.S. at 334. The Court found this insufficient to establish the required consensus. That reasoning remains sound today. While Petitioner points to additional state legislation enacted since 1989, the relevant inquiry under *Stanford* and *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279 (1987), is whether a consensus is so clear and consistent that it reflects a genuine "evolving standard of decency," not whether a bare numerical majority of jurisdictions have acted. The continued existence of numerous States—including Virginia—that decline to enact categorical exemptions demonstrates that the necessary consensus does not exist.

### II. The Virginia Jury Properly Made the Individualized Determination *Penry* Requires

*Penry*'s framework does not ignore the moral significance of mental retardation; it ensures that significance is weighed by the constitutionally appropriate decisionmaker—the capital sentencing jury—rather than imposed as a categorical rule by courts. This Court has consistently emphasized that capital sentencing must permit individualized consideration of the offender's characteristics and circumstances. *See Lockett v. Ohio*, 438 U.S. 586, 605 (1978) (plurality opinion) (the sentencer must "not be precluded from considering, as a mitigating factor, any aspect of a defendant's character or record"); *Eddings v. Oklahoma*, 455 U.S. 104, 112 (1982) (same). *Penry* extended this principle to mental retardation evidence, holding that such evidence must be considered by the jury as mitigation, but that the Constitution does not remove the ultimate weighing function from the jury's hands. 492 U.S. at 328.

That is precisely what occurred below. The record demonstrates that Atkins' claim of mental retardation was not merely permitted but was fully litigated before the jury. His own expert, a forensic psychologist, testified that Atkins was mildly mentally retarded. The Commonwealth presented rebuttal evidence contesting that characterization. The jury—the body this Court has recognized as uniquely suited to make such individualized moral judgments, *see Woodson v. North Carolina*, 428 U.S. 280, 305 (1976) (plurality opinion) (a jury "maintains a link between contemporary community values and the penal system")—weighed the competing evidence and concluded that a death sentence was warranted notwithstanding Atkins' claimed impairment. The Virginia Supreme Court did not err in deferring to that determination; it correctly applied *Penry*'s mandate that mental retardation be treated as a mitigating factor for the sentencer's consideration, not a per se bar to the penalty. *See* Atkins v. Commonwealth, 257 Va. 160, 510 S.E.2d 445 (1999).

Notably, this is not a case in which mitigating evidence was excluded or the jury was foreclosed from considering it, the constitutional infirmity condemned in *Penry* itself. 492 U.S. at 328 (finding error because the jury instructions "did not provide the jury with a vehicle for expressing its reasoned moral response" to the mitigating evidence). Here, the jury heard the evidence, considered it, and rendered its verdict. The process *Penry* demands was fully honored.

### III. A Categorical Rule Would Improperly Displace the Jury's Constitutionally Assigned Role and Create Intractable Line-Drawing Problems

Adopting Petitioner's categorical rule would require this Court to fashion, as a matter of federal constitutional law, a clinical definition of "mental retardation" sufficient to exempt an offender from capital punishment regardless of the crime committed or the jury's assessment of moral culpability. This Court has been rightly cautious about constitutionalizing such line-drawing exercises. *See Stanford*, 492 U.S. at 377-78 (declining to draw categorical age lines absent clear societal consensus). The clinical definitions of mental retardation vary in their diagnostic criteria and thresholds, and no consensus definition commands the certainty necessary to serve as a bright-line constitutional rule triggering automatic exemption from the death penalty.

Moreover, individualized sentencing—not categorical exemption—has long been this Court's preferred mechanism for ensuring that capital punishment is reserved for the most culpable offenders. *See Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 189 (1976) (plurality opinion) (praising sentencing schemes that "focus the jury's attention on the particularized nature of the crime and the particularized characteristics of the individual defendant"). The Virginia capital sentencing scheme, which permitted full presentation and jury consideration of Atkins' claimed mental retardation, satisfies precisely this constitutional preference.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reaffirm *Penry v. Lynaugh* and hold that the Eighth Amendment does not categorically prohibit the execution of mentally retarded offenders, and should affirm the judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia.
