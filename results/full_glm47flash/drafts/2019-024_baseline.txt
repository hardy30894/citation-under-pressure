**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE “ACTUAL KNOWLEDGE” STANDARD REQUIRES KNOWLEDGE OF THE BREACH, NOT KNOWLEDGE OF IMPRUDENCE.**

The Ninth Circuit erred by holding that a plaintiff must have knowledge that the alleged act was imprudent to trigger the three-year statute of limitations under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The statute bars an action “more than three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The “breach” refers to the act itself, not the legal conclusion that the act was unlawful. The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation effectively requires a plaintiff to know the law to know when the statute of limitations has run, which is contrary to the text of the statute and controlling Ninth Circuit precedent.

In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, the court held that the statute of limitations is “triggered by the [trustees’] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law.” 760 F.2d 989, 991 (9th Cir. 1985). This rule was reaffirmed in *Meagher v. Int’l Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan*, where the court stated that a plaintiff “had knowledge of the transaction, though he may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.” 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988). Under these precedents, “actual knowledge” is satisfied by knowledge of the underlying transaction or act, not by knowledge of the illegality of that act.

Here, the alleged breach was the investment of plan assets in alternative investments. Intel disclosed this strategy to Sulyma through Fund Fact Sheets and other plan documents. Sulyma had actual knowledge of the breach—the act of investing in alternative assets—when he accessed these documents. He did not need to know that the investment was imprudent to know that the breach had occurred. The Ninth Circuit’s requirement that Sulyma also know the investments were imprudent is a novel and unwarranted expansion of the statute of limitations that conflates the occurrence of a breach with the legal characterization of that breach.

**II. THE PLAINTIFF’S FAILURE TO READ DISCLOSURES DOES NOT RESET THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.**

The Ninth Circuit’s decision turns the statute of limitations into a subjective inquiry based on the plaintiff’s memory, rather than an objective inquiry based on the availability of information. The statute of limitations is a bar to relief, and it is triggered when the plaintiff has the opportunity to know of the breach. *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1832 (2015). A participant in an ERISA plan has a duty to investigate the management of their assets, just as a fiduciary has a duty to manage them with care. *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996).

Intel provided Sulyma with all relevant information regarding the investment strategy and the risks associated with alternative investments. The documents were hosted on the company’s website and were available to Sulyma during his employment. The fact that Sulyma chose not to read these documents or cannot recall having done so does not reset the statute of limitations. The “actual knowledge” inquiry is objective; if the information was available to the plaintiff, the statute of limitations begins to run. By requiring Sulyma to prove that he *actually* read the documents, the Ninth Circuit effectively allowed the statute of limitations to be extended indefinitely based on the plaintiff’s subjective recollection, which is inconsistent with the purpose of the limitations period.
