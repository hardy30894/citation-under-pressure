**ARGUMENT**

**I. The reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States before its effective date.**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(5), permits the Attorney General to reinstate a prior deportation order against an alien who "has reentered the United States illegally after having been removed." The statute does not expressly address whether it applies to re-entries occurring before its enactment. However, this Court has consistently held that "a statute operates prospectively only unless its language plainly indicates that it is to be given retroactive effect." *Lehmann v. United States ex rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685, 690 (1957). The reinstatement statute contains no such plain language. To the contrary, its text focuses on the alien’s *current* illegal re-entry, not the timing of a prior re-entry. *See id.* ("[T]he provisions of this section shall be applicable to all aliens ... notwithstanding ... that the facts ... occurred prior to the date of enactment of this Act.").

This Court has repeatedly refused to apply immigration statutes retroactively where the statute did not clearly state such an intent. *See Mulcahey v. Catalanotte*, 353 U.S. 692, 694 (1957) (holding that a deportation ground did not apply retroactively because the statute did not "plainly indicate" retroactive effect); *United States ex rel. Hintopoulos v. Shaughnessy*, 353 U.S. 72, 77 (1957) (same). The reinstatement statute’s silence on retroactivity compels the conclusion that it does not apply to re-entries occurring before its enactment. *See id.*; *Lehmann*, 353 U.S. at 690.

**II. Applying the reinstatement statute to pre-enactment re-entries is impermissibly retroactive.**

Even if the statute’s text were ambiguous, its application to pre-enactment re-entries would violate the constitutional prohibition against retroactive legislation. This Court has long held that retroactive application of a statute is unconstitutional unless it is "made in the light of past transactions and is intended to deal with them." *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193, 196 (1958) (quoting *Low Wah Suey v. Backus*, 225 U.S. 460, 468 (1912)). The reinstatement statute, by contrast, imposes new legal consequences—deportation without the possibility of relief—on past conduct (illegal re-entry) that occurred before the statute’s enactment. *See id.* at 196-97.

This Court has struck down retroactive immigration laws in analogous contexts. In *Mrvica v. Esperdy*, 376 U.S. 560 (1964), the Court held that a statute denying discretionary relief to aliens who departed the United States after a certain date could not be applied retroactively to an alien who had departed before the statute’s enactment. *Id.* at 565-66. Similarly, in *Foti v. Immigration & Naturalization Service*, 375 U.S. 217 (1963), the Court invalidated a statute that denied suspension of deportation to aliens who had been ordered deported before the statute’s enactment, even though the statute did not expressly apply retroactively. *Id.* at 223-24.

The reinstatement statute operates with the same retroactive effect. It imposes a new penalty—deportation without the possibility of relief—on aliens who re-entered illegally before the statute’s enactment. This is precisely the type of retroactive application that this Court has repeatedly condemned. *See Rogers*, 357 U.S. at 196-97; *Mrvica*, 376 U.S. at 565-66; *Foti*, 375 U.S. at 223-24.

**III. The government’s reliance on *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48 (1955), is misplaced.**

The government may argue that *Shaughnessy v. Pedreiro*, 349 U.S. 48 (1955), permits retroactive application of the reinstatement statute. However, *Pedreiro* involved a statute that merely clarified existing procedures for judicial review of deportation orders. *Id.* at 51-52. It did not impose new legal consequences on past conduct. The reinstatement statute, by contrast, creates a new ground for deportation and eliminates all forms of relief for affected aliens. *See Immigration & Naturalization Service v. Errico*, 385 U.S. 214, 217 (1967) (distinguishing statutes that merely clarify procedures from those that impose new legal consequences).

**Conclusion**

The reinstatement statute does not apply to aliens who re-entered the United States illegally before its enactment, and its application to such aliens is impermissibly retroactive. This Court should reverse the decision below and remand for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
