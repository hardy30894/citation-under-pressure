**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE CONTROLLED SUBSTANCES ACT DOES NOT UNCONSTITUTIONALLY DELEGATE LEGISLATIVE POWER TO THE ATTORNEY GENERAL.**

The non-delegation doctrine does not invalidate the temporary scheduling authority granted to the Attorney General under 21 U.S.C. § 811(h). The Supreme Court has long held that Congress may delegate authority to executive officials so long as it provides an “intelligible principle” to guide their discretion. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 372 (1989) (quoting *J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The temporary scheduling provision satisfies this standard.

Section 811(h) directs the Attorney General to temporarily schedule a substance only if it poses “an imminent hazard to the public safety” and meets at least three of eight specified factors, including the history and current pattern of abuse and the risk to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These statutory criteria are sufficiently definite to cabin the Attorney General’s discretion. *See Touby v. United States*, 909 F.2d 1419, 1423 (3d Cir. 1990) (upholding the delegation as constitutional). The Court has repeatedly upheld delegations where Congress has provided similarly clear standards. *See, e.g., Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 424-26 (1944) (upholding delegation to the Price Administrator to fix commodity prices based on “fair and equitable” standards).

The Toubys’ reliance on *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), is misplaced. Those cases involved delegations that lacked any meaningful standards, whereas § 811(h) provides explicit criteria. *See Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1423. The Court has since clarified that the non-delegation doctrine is “dormant” except in “extreme cases.” *Mistretta*, 488 U.S. at 372-73.

**II. THE ATTORNEY GENERAL LAWFULLY DELEGATED TEMPORARY SCHEDULING AUTHORITY TO THE DEA ADMINISTRATOR.**

The Attorney General’s subdelegation of authority to the DEA Administrator is authorized by 21 U.S.C. § 871(a), which permits the Attorney General to “delegate any of his functions” under the Controlled Substances Act. The Supreme Court has recognized that executive officials may subdelegate authority where Congress has expressly or implicitly authorized it. *See United States v. Grimaud*, 220 U.S. 506, 517 (1911). The Third Circuit has upheld this subdelegation in the context of the CSA. *United States v. Spain*, 825 F.2d 1426, 1429 (10th Cir. 1987) (citing *United States v. Hovey*, 674 F. Supp. 161, 165 (D. Del. 1987)).

The DEA Administrator’s temporary scheduling of Euphoria complied with the statutory and regulatory framework. The Administrator followed the notice-and-comment procedures of § 811(h)(4), considered the relevant factors, and made the statutorily required findings. The delegation to the DEA was thus a permissible exercise of executive authority.

**III. THE STATUTORY BAR ON JUDICIAL REVIEW OF TEMPORARY SCHEDULING ORDERS DOES NOT PRECLUDE CONSTITUTIONAL REVIEW.**

Section 811(h)(6) provides that temporary scheduling orders “are not subject to judicial review.” The Supreme Court has held that such jurisdictional bars do not apply to constitutional challenges. *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99, 109 (1977) (“[C]onstitutional questions are always open for judicial review.”). The Third Circuit correctly concluded that § 811(h)(6) does not foreclose review of the Toubys’ non-delegation claim. *Touby*, 909 F.2d at 1421.

**CONCLUSION**

The Controlled Substances Act’s temporary scheduling provision does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power, and the Attorney General’s subdelegation to the DEA was lawful. The judgment below should be affirmed.
