ARGUMENT

The district court properly certified the class under Rule 23(b)(3) because it conducted a rigorous analysis and found, based on adequate admissible evidence, that common questions predominated and that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. The court of appeals correctly affirmed that decision, and this Court should do the same.

I. THE DISTRICT COURT APPLIED THE CORRECT LEGAL STANDARD AND ITS FACTUAL FINDINGS ARE NOT CLEARLY ERRONEOUS.

A district court’s class certification decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion, which occurs only if the decision rests on a clearly erroneous finding of fact, an errant conclusion of law, or an improper application of law to fact. *United States v. U.S. Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). A finding of fact is clearly erroneous only if the reviewing court is “left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” *Id.* The district court’s account of the evidence must be accepted if it is “plausible in light of the record viewed in its entirety.” *Anderson v. Bessemer City*, 470 U.S. 564, 573–74 (1985). That standard is not met here.

The district court held a four-day evidentiary hearing, considered 32 expert reports, and heard live testimony from fact and expert witnesses. It then issued an 81-page opinion explaining why plaintiffs had demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that antitrust impact could be proved through common evidence and that a common methodology existed to measure class-wide damages. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 154 (E.D. Pa. 2010). The court’s findings are supported by the record and are not clearly erroneous.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY FOUND THAT COMMON QUESTIONS PREDOMINATE.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This Court has long recognized that the class action device is designed to “achieve economies of time, effort, and expense, and promote uniformity of decision.” *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 391 U.S. 555, 560 (1968). The predominance inquiry does not require plaintiffs to prove their case on the merits at the certification stage. *Id.* at 558–59. Rather, the district court must determine whether the plaintiffs have “produced enough evidence to show that common questions predominate.” *Id.* at 561.

Here, the district court found that plaintiffs’ theory of antitrust impact—that Comcast’s clustering scheme deterred overbuilder entry, leading to supra-competitive prices—was capable of proof through common evidence. That finding is supported by the expert testimony of Dr. James McClave, who used a common methodology to measure the overcharges paid by all class members. The court also properly defined the relevant geographic market as the Philadelphia Designated Market Area, consistent with this Court’s holding that a geographic market “may be as small as a single metropolitan area.” *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 337 (1962). Comcast’s argument that the market should be defined at the individual household level is contrary to *Brown Shoe* and would render the concept of a geographic market meaningless.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT DID NOT ERR IN ACCEPTING PLAINTIFFS’ DAMAGES METHODOLOGY.

Comcast contends that the district court certified the class without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. That contention is false. The district court heard extensive testimony from Dr. McClave, who presented a regression-based model that calculated the difference between actual prices and prices that would have prevailed but for Comcast’s anticompetitive conduct. The court found that this methodology was “common evidence available to measure and quantify damages on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 154. That finding is not clearly erroneous.

This Court has never required that damages be proven with certainty at the class certification stage. In *Eisen*, the Court emphasized that the district court’s role is to determine whether the class action is “superior to other available methods for the fair and efficient adjudication of the controversy.” 391 U.S. at 560. Requiring plaintiffs to prove damages with precision before certification would defeat the purpose of the class action device, which is to allow “the vindication of the rights of groups of people who individually would be without effective strength to bring their opponents into court at all.” *Hansberry v. Lee*, 311 U.S. 32, 42 (1940). The district court correctly recognized that a common methodology for calculating damages is sufficient to satisfy Rule 23(b)(3).

IV. CONCLUSION

The district court conducted a rigorous analysis, considered all relevant evidence, and made findings that are supported by the record. Those findings are not clearly erroneous, and the court did not abuse its discretion in certifying the class. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
