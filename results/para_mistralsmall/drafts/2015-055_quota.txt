**ARGUMENT**

I. **Johnson’s Rule Regarding the Residual Clause Applies Retroactively Under Teague v. Lane**

The Supreme Court has long held that new constitutional rules of criminal procedure apply retroactively to cases on collateral review unless they fall within one of two narrow exceptions articulated in *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989). *See, e.g., Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351 (2004). In *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), the Court held that the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA), 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), was unconstitutionally vague in violation of the Due Process Clause. This rule is a "new rule" for *Teague* purposes because it was not "dictated by precedent existing at the time the defendant’s conviction became final." *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 301. However, it nonetheless applies retroactively because it falls within *Teague*’s second exception: it "place[s] certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe." *Id.* at 307 (plurality opinion).

The Court has repeatedly emphasized that the vagueness doctrine serves to ensure that criminal laws provide fair warning of proscribed conduct. *See, e.g., Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357-58 (1983). In *Johnson*, the Court concluded that the residual clause failed to provide such notice because it "le[ft] grave uncertainty about how to estimate the risk posed by a crime." 135 S. Ct. at 2557. This conclusion necessarily implicates the fundamental fairness of the underlying convictions, rendering the rule a watershed rule of criminal procedure that applies retroactively. *See, e.g., Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 353-54 (1964) (holding that a judicial enlargement of a criminal statute’s scope violates due process by failing to provide fair warning).

II. **Welch’s ACCA Sentence Was Based on a Prior Conviction That Would Not Qualify as a “Violent Felony” Under Johnson**

Welch’s ACCA sentence was enhanced based on a 1996 Florida conviction for "strong arm" robbery. Under the categorical approach, this conviction qualifies as a "violent felony" only if it necessarily involved the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force against another person. *See, e.g., Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 257 (2013). The Florida robbery statute under which Welch was convicted criminalized "the taking of money or other property from the person or custody of another" with "force, violence, assault, or putting in fear." Fla. Stat. § 812.13(1) (1996). However, as the Eleventh Circuit acknowledged, Florida courts were divided on whether mere snatching—such as the theft of a purse or jewelry from a person’s body—constituted robbery under this statute. *See, e.g., Goldsmith v. State*, 573 So. 2d 445, 445 (Fla. 2d Dist. Ct. App. 1991); *Larkins v. State*, 476 So. 2d 1383, 1385 (Fla. 1st Dist. Ct. App. 1985).

This ambiguity demonstrates that Welch’s conviction did not necessarily involve the use of violent force, as required by the ACCA’s elements clause. *See, e.g., Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 602 (1990) (holding that predicate offenses must be determined by reference to the elements of the offense, not the underlying facts). Moreover, even if the residual clause were still valid, Welch’s conviction would not qualify because mere snatching does not present a "serious potential risk of physical injury to another." *See, e.g., James v. United States*, 550 U.S. 192, 208 (2007) (holding that the risk must be "substantial" and "real").

III. **The Eleventh Circuit Erred in Failing to Apply Johnson Retroactively**

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in this case was issued before *Johnson* was decided, and it relied on the residual clause to classify Welch’s prior conviction as a violent felony. *See Welch v. United States*, 728 F.3d 1087, 1092 (11th Cir. 2013). Because *Johnson* invalidated the residual clause as unconstitutionally vague, the Eleventh Circuit’s decision is no longer valid. *See, e.g., Welch v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 128 Welch’s sentence must be vacated because his ACCA enhancement was based on a prior conviction that does not qualify as a violent felony under the categorical approach.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that *Johnson* applies retroactively and that Welch’s prior conviction for Florida "strong arm" robbery does not qualify as a violent felony under the ACCA. Accordingly, his sentence should be vacated.
