**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT THE PLAIN ERROR IN GREER’S TRIAL DID NOT AFFECT HIS SUBSTANTIAL RIGHTS OR IMPUGN THE FAIRNESS, INTEGRITY, OR PUBLIC REPUTATION OF HIS TRIAL.**

A. Under the Fourth Amendment, a conviction for possession of a firearm by a felon under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g) requires proof that the defendant *knew* he possessed a firearm and *knew* he was a felon. *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2195 (2019). The Eleventh Circuit properly recognized that the government failed to present evidence or secure jury instructions on Greer’s knowledge of his status as a felon. *See United States v. Greer*, 994 F.3d 1279, 1285 (11th Cir. 2021). However, the court correctly determined that this error did not prejudice Greer because the record—including Greer’s prior convictions and trial testimony—demonstrated that he was aware of his felon status. *Id.* at 1286.

B. The Eleventh Circuit did not err by considering the entire trial record, including Greer’s prior convictions, to assess prejudice. The Supreme Court has held that in plain-error review, courts may examine the entire record to determine whether an error affected substantial rights. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993) ("[T]he court of appeals should consider the entire record when assessing the effect of the error."). The Court has further explained that prejudice under plain-error review requires a showing that the error "affected the outcome of the district court proceedings." *Id.* at 734. Here, the Eleventh Circuit properly concluded that Greer could not demonstrate prejudice because the record affirmatively showed he knew he was a felon. *Greer*, 994 F.3d at 1286.

C. The Eleventh Circuit also correctly declined to vacate Greer’s conviction under the "fairness, integrity, or public reputation" prong of plain-error review. The Supreme Court has cautioned that this prong is "reserved for exceptional cases." *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 470 (1997). The Eleventh Circuit’s conclusion that the error did not undermine the fairness or integrity of Greer’s trial was amply supported by the record. *Greer*, 994 F.3d at 1286. The court’s consideration of Greer’s prior convictions and trial testimony to assess prejudice was consistent with the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Olano* and did not constitute an impermissible expansion of review beyond the trial record.

**II. THE ELEVENTH CIRCUIT DID NOT IMPROPERLY RELY ON EXTRINSIC EVIDENCE TO ESTABLISH PLAIN ERROR.**

A. The Eleventh Circuit did not err by considering Greer’s prior convictions, which were part of the trial record, to assess prejudice. The Supreme Court has made clear that courts may examine the entire record—including evidence admitted at trial—when evaluating prejudice under plain-error review. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 736. The Eleventh Circuit’s analysis was confined to the trial record and did not rely on any extrinsic evidence.

B. Greer’s reliance on *United States v. Marcus*, 560 U.S. 258 (2010), is misplaced. In *Marcus*, the Supreme Court reversed a conviction under a plain-error standard because the government failed to prove an element of the offense at trial, and the court of appeals improperly relied on post-verdict evidence to cure the error. *Id.* at 265-66. Here, by contrast, the Eleventh Circuit did not rely on post-verdict evidence but instead examined the trial record—including Greer’s prior convictions—to determine whether he could show prejudice. This was entirely consistent with *Olano* and did not violate *Marcus*.

**CONCLUSION**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied the plain-error standard in affirming Greer’s conviction. The court did not err by considering the trial record—including Greer’s prior convictions—to assess prejudice, and its conclusion that the error did not affect Greer’s substantial rights or undermine the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of his trial was amply supported by the record. The petition for certiorari should be denied.
