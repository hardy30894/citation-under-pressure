**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right—distinct from the insanity defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state in order to rebut the prosecution’s proof of criminal intent. The Arizona trial court’s exclusion of Eric Michael Clark’s expert testimony on the issue of criminal intent was consistent with settled constitutional principles and long-standing state criminal procedure, and therefore did not violate due process.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial, but it does not require the state to permit the introduction of evidence that is irrelevant, unreliable, or inconsistent with established legal doctrines. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). The Constitution does not mandate that a state must allow a defendant to use expert testimony to challenge the prosecution’s burden of proof on an element of the crime unless such evidence is relevant to a recognized defense. In this case, Clark sought to use psychiatric testimony not only to support his insanity defense—on which he bore the burden of proof—but also to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent. The trial court properly confined the use of such evidence to the insanity defense, as Arizona law did not recognize a separate affirmative defense based on inability to form criminal intent.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that the insanity defense is a creature of state law, and that states may define the scope and requirements of such defenses as they see fit. *People v. Burch*, 148 N.Y. 455, 460 (1895) (“The law of every state determines what shall be the effect of insanity upon criminal responsibility.”). The Court has never held that a defendant has a constitutional right to introduce expert testimony on mental state for the purpose of negating criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized under state law. Indeed, in *United States v. Jackson*, 235 U.S. 101, 105 (1914), the Court emphasized that “the right to a fair trial does not include the right to introduce evidence that is not relevant or admissible under the rules of evidence.” The relevance of expert testimony on mental state is not absolute; it depends on the legal framework within which the defense is asserted.

Clark’s argument rests on the premise that the prosecution must prove criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt, and that he therefore has a constitutional right to introduce evidence that he lacked such intent. But the Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence on an element of the crime unless such evidence is part of a recognized defense. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 587 (1884). The Court has repeatedly held that the burden of proof on elements of a crime lies with the prosecution, but that does not mean the defendant may introduce any evidence he wishes to rebut that burden. The state retains the authority to define the permissible scope of defenses and the admissibility of evidence. *State v. Haines*, 108 N.H. 145, 148 (1967) (citing *People v. Burch*, 148 N.Y. 455 (1895)).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has consistently upheld the validity of state rules that limit the use of psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense. In *People v. Burch*, the Court upheld a New York statute that allowed expert testimony on insanity but did not permit such testimony to be used to negate criminal intent. The Court stated: “The law of every state determines what shall be the effect of insanity upon criminal responsibility.” 148 N.Y. at 460. This principle remains binding and unchallenged. The Court has never held that a defendant has a constitutional right to use expert testimony on mental state to negate criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized by the state. To hold otherwise would require the Court to rewrite state criminal law in a manner inconsistent with the principles of federalism and the division of powers.

Clark’s claim also fails because he did not demonstrate that the exclusion of his expert testimony deprived him of a fair trial. The record shows that he was permitted to present expert evidence in support of his insanity defense, which was a recognized defense under Arizona law at the time. The trial court’s ruling did not prevent him from presenting any evidence of his mental condition; it only limited the purpose for which such evidence could be used. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. at 453. The jury was fully informed of Clark’s diagnosis and his belief in alien conspiracy, and the defense was able to argue that he was not criminally responsible due to insanity. The exclusion of the testimony for a different purpose did not deprive him of a meaningful opportunity to present his case.

Finally, the Due Process Clause does not require a state to allow a defendant to introduce evidence that would undermine the very structure of the criminal justice system. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. at 587. If a state chooses to limit the use of psychiatric evidence to the insanity defense, it is within its constitutional authority to do so. The Constitution does not compel states to adopt a “mental incapacity” defense that negates criminal intent unless such a defense is recognized under state law.

For these reasons, the Arizona trial court’s ruling did not violate the Fourteenth Amendment. The state’s limitation on the use of expert testimony to the insanity defense was consistent with established precedent and did not deprive Clark of due process. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
