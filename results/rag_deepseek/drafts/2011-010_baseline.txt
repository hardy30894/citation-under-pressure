**ARGUMENT**

The Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act coverage only to workers who sustain injury on the outer Continental Shelf itself. The plain text, structure, and purpose of OCSLA, together with this Court’s precedents, compel that conclusion.

**I. The Statutory Text and Structure Require a Situs-of-Injury Requirement**

Section 1333(b) provides LHWCA benefits for “disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” modifies “injury.” While the text does not explicitly state “injury occurring on the outer Continental Shelf,” the overall structure of OCSLA demonstrates that Congress intended coverage to be geographically limited to the OCS.

OCSLA is a federal enclave statute. It establishes federal jurisdiction over the OCS and applies federal law to that area. *See Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co.*, 395 U.S. 352, 355-56 (1969). The choice-of-law provision, § 1333(a)(2)(A), applies the law of the adjacent state as surrogate federal law “to the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon.” That provision is explicitly tied to locale. The compensation provision in § 1333(b) is the only provision that “superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement.” *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 n.2 (1986). The Court’s footnote confirms that situs remains a prerequisite; § 1333(b) merely adds a status requirement for the employee. *Id.*

This Court has recognized the “explicit geographic limitation” of OCSLA’s incorporation of the LHWCA. *Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414, 415-16 (1985). In *Herb’s Welding*, the Court noted that the “inconsistent coverage” for workers on platforms in state waters “results primarily from the explicit geographic limitation to [OCSLA’s] incorporation of the LHWCA.” *Id.* at 416. That geographic limitation would be meaningless if an injury could occur anywhere.

**II. The Fifth Circuit’s Situs-of-Injury Test Is Correct**

In *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the Fifth Circuit held that OCSLA requires the injury to occur on the OCS. The court reasoned that OCSLA’s “primary focus is on the situs of the injury, not the status of the employee.” *Id.* at 361. The court relied on *Tallentire*’s statement that OCSLA coverage is “determined principally by locale.” *Id.* (quoting *Tallentire*, 477 U.S. at 219). The Fifth Circuit also noted that the LHWCA itself has a situs requirement, and OCSLA’s extension should be read consistently. *Id.* at 360.

The Third Circuit’s contrary decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), adopted a “but for” test that would cover injuries anywhere, so long as they are causally connected to OCS operations. That approach ignores the statutory structure and this Court’s guidance. The *Curtis* test would extend OCSLA coverage to onshore injuries that Congress never intended to remove from state workers’ compensation systems. *See Mills*, 877 F.2d at 361-62.

**III. The Ninth Circuit’s “Substantial Nexus” Test Is Unworkable and Contrary to Congressional Intent**

The Ninth Circuit held that OCSLA applies to any injury with a “substantial nexus” to OCS operations, regardless of where the injury occurs. That standard is vague and would create uncertainty. It would displace state compensation schemes for onshore injuries that have only a tangential connection to OCS operations. Congress did not intend OCSLA to reach so far. The OCSLA was designed to fill a gap for workers on the OCS who were not covered by state law. *See Gulf Offshore Co. v. Mobil Oil Corp.*, 453 U.S. 473, 480 (1981) (OCSLA “provide[s] a federal remedy for injuries occurring on the Outer Continental Shelf”). Expanding coverage to onshore injuries would upset the balance between federal and state compensation systems.

**IV. The Facts of This Case Confirm That OCSLA Does Not Apply**

Juan Valladolid was killed at an onshore processing facility 250-300 feet from the ocean. He was not on the OCS. His injury did not occur on the OCS. Therefore, OCSLA does not provide coverage. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary holding should be reversed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
