**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE DOES NOT PRECLUDE RETRIAL OF PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATIONS IN NONCAPITAL SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS.**

The Double Jeopardy Clause provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." U.S. Const. amend. V. By its plain terms, the Clause applies to prosecutions for "offence[s]"—not to sentencing determinations. This Court has long recognized that the Double Jeopardy Clause's protections do not extend to noncapital sentencing proceedings. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246 (1949) (holding that the Due Process Clause does not require formal trial procedures at sentencing, and sentencing judges may consider a wide range of information).

**A. The Double Jeopardy Clause Does Not Apply to Sentencing Proceedings in Noncapital Cases.**

This Court has never held that the Double Jeopardy Clause applies generally to noncapital sentencing proceedings. To the contrary, this Court has consistently declined to extend its protections to such proceedings. *See Witte v. United States*, 515 U.S. 389, 398 (1995) ("sentencing enhancements do not punish a defendant for the uncharged conduct, but instead reflect the seriousness of the offense of conviction"); *United States v. Watts*, 519 U.S. 148, 155 (1997) (per curiam) (sentencing court may consider conduct of which defendant was acquitted, so long as proven by a preponderance of the evidence).

The few cases in which this Court has applied double jeopardy protections to sentencing involved the unique context of capital sentencing, where the proceeding is "like a trial on guilt or innocence." *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430, 439 (1981). In *Bullington*, this Court held that because Missouri's capital sentencing procedure required the prosecution to prove aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, it "resemble[d] a trial on the issue of guilt," and thus double jeopardy barred retrial of the death penalty. *Id.* at 445. Similarly, in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203, 209-10 (1984), this Court held that double jeopardy applied to capital sentencing because Arizona's procedures "closely resemble[d] the trial on the issue of guilt."

But this Court has repeatedly declined to extend *Bullington* and *Rumsey* beyond the capital context. *See Monge v. California*, 524 U.S. 721, 730 (1998) ("We have never extended double jeopardy protections to sentencing proceedings in noncapital cases."). In *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383, 392 (1994), this Court noted that "both *Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases, and our reasoning in those cases was based largely on the unique circumstances of a capital sentencing proceeding." The Court emphasized that "a reasonable jurist reviewing our precedents at the time respondent's conviction and sentence became final would not have considered the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause to a noncapital sentencing proceeding to be dictated by our precedents." *Id.* at 393.

**B. California’s Prior Conviction Proceedings Do Not Resemble a Trial on Guilt or Innocence.**

Even if the Double Jeopardy Clause could apply to some noncapital sentencing proceedings, it does not apply to California's prior conviction allegations. The key inquiry is whether the prior conviction proceeding "requires the jury to find the existence of an aggravating circumstance beyond a reasonable doubt." *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 730. But California's procedures lack the "hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence" that justified applying double jeopardy protections in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*.

First, the state statutory right to a jury trial of prior conviction allegations, Cal. Pen. Code § 1025, does not transform the proceeding into a trial for constitutional purposes. This Court has recognized that "a State may provide a defendant a statutory right to a jury trial at sentencing without triggering double jeopardy protections." *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 731. Indeed, in *Bullington* itself, the capital sentencing proceeding included a jury trial and proof beyond a reasonable doubt—protections not constitutionally required. *See Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 439. Yet this Court held double jeopardy applied only because the capital context made the proceeding "like a trial on guilt or innocence." *Id.*

Second, California's prior conviction proceedings do not require proof of "elements of a separate offense." *Monge*, 524 U.S. at 731. Instead, the prior conviction allegation "simply authorize[s] a court to increase a defendant's sentence." *Id.* Unlike in capital cases, where the sentencer must find specific aggravating circumstances that are "distinct from the underlying offense," *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 211, California's three-strikes law enhances punishment based solely on the fact of a prior conviction—a fact already established by the defendant's own guilty plea or jury verdict. *See* Cal. Pen. Code §§ 667(d)(1), 1170.12(b)(1).

Third, the consequences of a sentencing enhancement—a longer prison term—do not implicate the same "life or limb" concerns that justify double jeopardy protections in capital cases. As this Court observed in *Monge*, "the defendant's interest in finality at sentencing is less compelling than it is at trial." 524 U.S. at 733 (citation omitted). A defendant sentenced to a longer term due to a prior conviction allegation "does not face the 'risk' of being 'subject to the grievous and severe punishment of death.'" *Id.* (quoting *Rumsey*, 467 U.S. at 211).

**C. Retrial of the Prior Conviction Allegation Is Permissible Because the State Provided Only a Statutory Right to a Jury Trial.**

The California Court of Appeal erred in holding that double jeopardy bars retrial of Monge's prior conviction allegation. Because California was not constitutionally required to provide a jury trial or proof beyond a reasonable doubt for the prior conviction allegation, the state's decision to do so by statute does not trigger federal double jeopardy protections. *See Monge*, 524 U.S. at 734 ("A State may provide a defendant a statutory right to a jury trial at sentencing without triggering double jeopardy protections."). The Double Jeopardy Clause "does not require that a State adhere to any particular procedures for sentencing." *Id.* at 730. Accordingly, where the State provides such procedures voluntarily, it may correct errors in those proceedings without running afoul of the Double Jeopardy Clause.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the California Supreme Court should be affirmed. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not bar retrial of a prior conviction allegation in a noncapital sentencing proceeding.
