**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts Buffer-Zone Statute Is a Content-Neutral, Viewpoint-Neutral Time, Place, and Manner Regulation That Satisfies the First Amendment.**

The First Circuit correctly held that the Massachusetts statute is a permissible regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989) (explaining that such regulations are valid if they are content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication). The statute at issue here meets each of these requirements.

First, the statute is content-neutral. It does not target speech based on its message, nor does it single out any particular viewpoint for disfavored treatment. *See Police Dep’t of Chicago v. Mosley*, 408 U.S. 92, 95–96 (1972) (holding that a ban on picketing near schools was content-neutral where it applied regardless of the message conveyed). The statute applies equally to all individuals, regardless of whether they are advocating for or against abortion. *See id.* at 96 ("[T]he First Amendment means that government has no power to restrict expression because of its message, its ideas, its subject matter, or its content."). The fact that the statute burdens speech in a particular location does not render it content-based. *See Heffron v. Int’l Soc’y for Krishna Consciousness*, 452 U.S. 640, 648–49 (1981) (upholding a content-neutral regulation of speech in a public forum).

Second, the statute is narrowly tailored to serve the significant governmental interest of ensuring public safety and preventing interference with access to reproductive healthcare facilities. *See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 376 (1997) (upholding a buffer zone around abortion clinics as a permissible regulation of speech). The Commonwealth has a substantial interest in protecting the free flow of traffic to and from medical facilities and in preventing harassment and intimidation of patients and staff. *See id.* at 377 (recognizing the state’s interest in "ensuring public safety and order, promoting the free flow of traffic on streets and sidewalks, and protecting medical facilities and their patients from unwanted physical contact"). The 35-foot buffer zone is narrowly tailored because it is the smallest restriction necessary to achieve these interests. *See id.* at 378 (noting that a 15-foot buffer zone was narrowly tailored to protect access to clinics).

Third, the statute leaves open ample alternative channels for communication. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The plaintiffs remain free to speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive conduct outside the buffer zone. *See id.* at 802 (explaining that a regulation does not violate the First Amendment if it does not "prevent[ ] [speakers] from reaching a significant audience with their message"). The plaintiffs have not demonstrated that the buffer zone prevents them from effectively communicating their message. *See Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 377 (finding that a 15-foot buffer zone did not prevent speakers from communicating their message).

**II. The First Circuit Correctly Relied on *Hill v. Colorado* in Upholding the Statute.**

The First Circuit properly relied on *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), in upholding the Massachusetts statute. *Hill* upheld a Colorado statute that prohibited certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances, including "knowingly approach[ing] within eight feet of another person, without that person’s consent, for the purpose of passing a leaflet or handbill to, displaying a sign to, or engaging in oral protest, education, or counseling with such other person." *Id.* at 707. The Supreme Court held that the statute was a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral regulation of speech that satisfied the First Amendment. *Id.* at 725–26.

The Massachusetts statute is materially indistinguishable from the Colorado statute upheld in *Hill*. Both statutes regulate speech outside healthcare facilities to protect patients and staff from harassment and to ensure access to medical care. *See id.* at 715 (recognizing the state’s interest in protecting "the unwilling listener" and ensuring "the free flow of traffic to and from the clinics"). The fact that the Massachusetts statute creates a 35-foot buffer zone while the Colorado statute created an eight-foot no-approach zone does not render the Massachusetts statute unconstitutional. *See id.* at 725 (upholding a regulation that was "much narrower" than the one at issue in *Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753 (1994)).

The plaintiffs’ argument that *Hill* should be limited or overruled is unavailing. *Hill* remains good law, and this Court has not indicated any intention to revisit it. *See, e.g., Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376 (reaffirming *Hill*). The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552 (2011), is misplaced. *Sorrell* involved a content-based restriction on speech and is therefore inapposite. *See id.* at 563–64 (holding that a statute that disfavored certain speakers was content-based and therefore subject to strict scrutiny).

**III. The Statute Does Not Violate the First Amendment as Applied to the Plaintiffs.**

The plaintiffs’ as-applied challenge fails because the statute does not prevent them from effectively communicating their message. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802. The plaintiffs remain free to speak, distribute literature, and engage in expressive conduct outside the buffer zone. *See id.* at 791. The fact that the plaintiffs must take a few steps back from the clinic entrance does not render the statute unconstitutional. *See Heffron*, 452 U.S. at 654 (upholding a regulation that required speakers to move from a fixed location to a designated area).

The plaintiffs’ reliance on *Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147 (1939), is misplaced. *Schneider* involved a ban on all leafleting in public streets, which the Court held was not narrowly tailored to serve the city’s interest in preventing litter. *Id.* at 162. The Massachusetts statute is not a total ban on leafleting; it merely restricts where leafleting may occur. *See Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791. The statute is therefore constitutional. *See id.*

**Conclusion**

The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a content-neutral, viewpoint-neutral regulation of the time, place, and manner of speech that satisfies the First Amendment. The First Circuit correctly upheld the statute, and this Court should affirm its decision.
