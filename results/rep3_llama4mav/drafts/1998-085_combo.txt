The American Dental Association (ADA) respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act.

The Civil Rights Act of 1991 allows for punitive damages when an employer engages in discriminatory practices "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). However, the statute does not define these terms. To understand the intent of Congress, we must look to the common law principles that existed at the time of the statute's enactment.

At common law, punitive damages were awarded in cases where the defendant's conduct was "outrageous" or demonstrated "reckless indifference" to the rights of others. See, e.g., Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202, 214 (1858) (punitive damages available when defendant's conduct is "wanton" or "reckless"); Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875) (punitive damages available when defendant's conduct is "willful" or "wanton"). The Restatement of Torts also supports this interpretation, stating that punitive damages are available when the defendant's conduct is "outrageous" or demonstrates "reckless indifference" to the rights of others. See Restatement of Torts § 908(2) (1939).

The courts have long recognized that punitive damages are not available for every intentional tort, but rather require a higher level of culpability. See, e.g., Lake Shore & M.S. Ry. Co. v. Prentice, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893) (punitive damages require "gross fraud" or "malice"); Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550, 565 (1886) (punitive damages require "wanton" or "reckless" conduct). This principle is consistent with the language of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which requires "malice or reckless indifference" for punitive damages.

In the context of employment discrimination, the courts have applied similar principles. See, e.g., Texas & Pac. Ry. Co. v. Behymer, 189 U.S. 468, 470 (1903) (employer's conduct must be "wanton" or "reckless" to support punitive damages); The Amiable Nancy, 16 U.S. 546, 558 (1818) (punitive damages available when defendant's conduct is "wanton" or "malicious"). These cases demonstrate that punitive damages are not available for every instance of intentional discrimination, but rather require a higher level of culpability.

The Court of Appeals erred in holding that the jury's finding of intentional discrimination was sufficient to support an award of punitive damages. Rather, the Court should require a showing of "egregious" or "outrageous" conduct, consistent with the common law principles and the language of the statute. See, e.g., Scott v. Donald, 165 U.S. 58, 89 (1897) (punitive damages require "gross" or "wanton" conduct); R. R. Co. v. Hanning, 82 U.S. 649, 656 (1872) (punitive damages require "malice" or "reckless indifference").

In conclusion, the ADA respectfully submits that the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous" to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. This interpretation is consistent with the common law principles and the language of the statute, and is supported by a long line of cases decided before 1970.
