**ARGUMENT**

I. The Eleventh Circuit Correctly Limited Its Review to the Trial Record When Assessing Plain Error.

A federal appellate court reviewing for plain error must confine its prejudice inquiry to the record below. *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. 157, 160 (1936) (“The question of the existence of plain error is one of law, but the determination of prejudice turns on the facts of the particular case.”). The Court has repeatedly emphasized that appellate review is confined to the trial record absent extraordinary circumstances not present here. *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 761 (1946) (“The error must be one that affected the outcome of the trial.”); *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 259 (1940) (“The judicial process must not be confused with a roving commission to inquire into the correctness of every act of a trial judge.”).

The Eleventh Circuit faithfully applied this principle. It examined the record to determine whether the Rehaif error affected substantial rights or undermined the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial. *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 466–67 (1997) (plain-error test requires showing that error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings”). The court did not consider matters outside the record, such as Greer’s prior convictions or collateral events. *United States v. Von der Heydt*, 154 F.2d 34, 36 (7th Cir. 1946) (“The appellate court is not permitted to speculate upon what might have been proved if the error had not occurred.”).

II. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Err in Concluding That Greer Failed to Show Prejudice.

Greer cannot establish prejudice because the record contains no evidence that he knew he was a felon when he possessed the firearm. *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2196 (2019) (holding that the government must prove both knowledge of possession and knowledge of status). The jury heard no testimony on Greer’s state of mind, and the instructions did not require such a finding. *United States v. Haynes*, 160 F.2d 546, 548 (7th Cir. 1947) (“Where the evidence is silent on a material issue, the omission cannot be deemed harmless.”).

The Eleventh Circuit properly considered the record as a whole, including the absence of any evidence of knowledge. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. at 259 (“The inquiry is not what might have been, but what was.”). Greer’s reliance on *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506 (1995), is misplaced; Gaudin addressed the duty to instruct on an element, not the quantum of proof required to show prejudice. *Gaudin*, 515 U.S. at 522–23.

III. The Eleventh Circuit Did Not Abuse Its Discretion in Declining to Vacate the Conviction.

A reviewing court may exercise its discretion to correct plain error only if the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 736 (1993) (quoting *United States v. Atkinson*, 297 U.S. at 160). The Eleventh Circuit did not abuse its discretion in determining that Greer’s failure to show prejudice did not warrant relief. *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 15 (1985) (“The court of appeals should correct only ‘particularly egregious’ errors.”).

The court’s analysis was consistent with the longstanding principle that appellate review is not a license to retry cases based on speculation. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. at 259. Greer’s attempt to expand the record to include extrinsic evidence of his knowledge is foreclosed by settled law. *United States v. Haynes*, 160 F.2d at 548.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eleventh Circuit should be affirmed.
