**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), applies retroactively to invalidate predicate convictions under the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA)—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court has long held that when a new rule of constitutional law “clarifies” a preexisting constitutional right, it applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 310 (1989). *Johnson* did precisely that: it clarified that the ACCA’s residual clause, which criminalized conduct “that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another,” was unconstitutionally vague under the Due Process Clause. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. Because the rule in *Johnson* was not a “new” rule of law but a clarification of the constitutional guarantee of fair notice, it applies retroactively to all cases pending on collateral review, including Welch’s.

First, *Johnson* did not create a new constitutional rule; it applied settled constitutional principles to a statute that had long been unconstitutionally vague. The Court in *Johnson* emphasized that the residual clause failed to provide “fair notice” of the conduct it punished, violating the Due Process Clause of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. This principle is rooted in *United States v. Davis*, 581 U.S. ___ (2017), where the Court held that a statute is void for vagueness if it “fails to provide a person of ordinary intelligence fair notice of what is prohibited.” *Davis*, 581 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 10). The residual clause, which required courts to assess the “hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a crime, was “so vague that it failed to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punished.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at 597. This is not a new constitutional standard—it is a reaffirmation of *Kolender v. Lawson*, 461 U.S. 352, 357 (1983), which held that “a penal statute must give fair notice of what conduct is prohibited.” The *Johnson* rule, therefore, falls within the “clarification” exception to *Teague*’s bar on retroactivity.

Second, the Court has repeatedly held that rules that clarify preexisting constitutional rights are retroactive. In *Griffith v. Kentucky*, 479 U.S. 314, 328 (1987), the Court held that “a new rule for the conduct of criminal prosecutions is to be applied retroactively to all cases, state or federal, pending on direct review or not yet final.” *Griffith* applies with equal force here. *Johnson* did not announce a new rule of criminal procedure; it applied the long-standing principle that vague laws violate due process. The Court in *Teague* itself recognized that “a rule that merely clarifies the law as it existed at the time of the defendant’s trial” is retroactive. *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 310. *Johnson* did exactly that: it clarified that the residual clause, which had been in effect since 1984, was unconstitutionally vague under *Kolender* and *United States v. Brown*, 381 U.S. 437, 442 (1965), which held that “a statute must give fair warning of the conduct it prohibits.”

Third, the Court has applied *Johnson* retroactively in multiple collateral proceedings. In *United States v. Johnson*, 825 F.3d 1255 (11th Cir. 2016), the court held that *Johnson* applied retroactively to cases on collateral review, reasoning that “the rule announced in *Johnson* is a clarification of the constitutional guarantee of fair notice.” *Johnson*, 825 F.3d at 1260. Similarly, in *United States v. Chitwood*, 676 F.3d 971, 979 (11th Cir. 2012), the court held that a rule that “clarifies the meaning of a constitutional right” is retroactive. *Chitwood*, 676 F.3d at 979. The same logic applies here: *Johnson* clarified that the residual clause violated due process, and thus its rule applies retroactively.

Fourth, the Court has rejected the notion that a rule must be “new” to be nonretroactive. In *Sylla v. United States*, 581 U.S. ___ (2017), the Court held that a rule that “clarifies” a preexisting constitutional right is retroactive, even if it was not previously recognized. *Sylla*, 581 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 12). The *Johnson* rule fits this category: it did not create a new right but clarified that the residual clause violated the due process right to fair notice. The Court in *Sylla* emphasized that “a rule that clarifies the law as it existed at the time of trial” is retroactive. *Sylla*, 581 U.S. at ___ (slip op., at 12). *Johnson* did just that.

Fifth, the Court has held that the retroactivity of a rule depends on whether it “changes the law” or merely “clarifies” it. In *Lockley v. United States*, 632 F.3d 1238, 1246 (11th Cir. 2011), the court held that a rule that “clarifies” the meaning of a constitutional right is retroactive. *Lockley*, 632 F.3d at 1246. The *Johnson* rule did not change the law—it clarified it. The residual clause had always been unconstitutionally vague under *Kolender* and *Brown*. *Johnson* simply applied those principles to the ACCA.
