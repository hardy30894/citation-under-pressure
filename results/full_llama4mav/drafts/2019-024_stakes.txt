The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case misinterprets the "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). The court held that a plaintiff must have actual knowledge not only of the transaction or action that constitutes the alleged breach, but also that the transaction or action was imprudent. However, this interpretation is inconsistent with the plain meaning of the statute and the relevant case law.

As this Court has not provided an authoritative construction for section 1113(2) since the 1987 amendment, the Ninth Circuit's interpretation is guided by its own precedent. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that the statute of limitations is triggered by the plaintiff's knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by their knowledge of the law. The court reiterated this principle in Meagher v. Int'l Ass'n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), stating that the plaintiff had knowledge of the transaction, though they may not have known at the time that the reduction in benefits was unlawful under ERISA.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in this case is at odds with its own precedent. By requiring that Sulyma have actual knowledge that the investments were imprudent, the court is effectively imposing a knowledge-of-the-law requirement, which is contrary to the holdings in Blanton and Meagher. The correct interpretation is that the plaintiff need only have actual knowledge of the transaction or action that constitutes the alleged breach.

In this case, Intel disclosed all relevant information about the investments to Sulyma through various documents hosted on two websites. The documents disclosed both the fact of the alternative investments and the basic strategy behind the decision to invest in them. As the Ninth Circuit acknowledged, Intel's evidence demonstrates that Sulyma had sufficient information available to him to know about the allegedly imprudent investments before October 29, 2012. Under the correct interpretation of "actual knowledge," this is sufficient to trigger the three-year statute of limitations.

The Ninth Circuit's decision should be reversed, and the district court's grant of summary judgment to Intel should be reinstated. The "actual knowledge" requirement under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) is satisfied when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the transaction or action that constitutes the alleged breach, regardless of whether they understand the legal implications of that transaction or action.
