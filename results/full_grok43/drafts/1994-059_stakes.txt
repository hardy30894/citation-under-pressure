**ARGUMENT**

The Qualifications Clauses fix the exclusive requirements for service in Congress. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; art. I, § 3, cl. 3. Arkansas Amendment 73 adds an eligibility restriction based on prior service that the Constitution does not contain. That addition is invalid.

In *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), the Court held that the Qualifications Clauses are “fixed” and “exclusive.” *Id.* at 548. The opinion explained that the constitutional text, the debates at the Convention, and the consistent practice of Congress demonstrate that neither the House nor the Senate may impose qualifications beyond those enumerated. *Id.* at 522-48. The same reasoning precludes the States from doing so. Allowing each State to engraft its own service limits would produce “a patchwork of qualifications” incompatible with the national character of the federal legislature. *Id.* at 548.

The Court has repeatedly recognized that the power to regulate the “Times, Places and Manner” of elections, U.S. Const. art. I, § 4, cl. 1, does not extend to altering the qualifications themselves. *See, e.g.*, *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299, 311 (1941) (distinguishing procedural regulations from substantive qualifications). Amendment 73’s ballot-access mechanism is merely a functional bar to re-election; it therefore operates as an additional qualification. *See Powell*, 395 U.S. at 547 (rejecting attempts to achieve indirectly what the Constitution forbids directly).

Because Section 3 of Amendment 73 is inseparable from the remainder of the measure, the entire Amendment must fall. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that the voters were presented with a single package and that no reliable basis exists for determining whether the state-office provisions would have passed standing alone. *See Combs v. Glens Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 375 S.W.2d 809 (1964).

The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be affirmed.
