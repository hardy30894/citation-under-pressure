**ARGUMENT**

The Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”) provides that “[a]ny party aggrieved by a final decision or order of the Board may obtain a review of such decision or order” in the court of appeals. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application was a “final decision” subject to judicial review. The Fifth Circuit’s contrary holding conflicts with the plain text of § 355(f), the settled meaning of “final decision” in administrative law, and the decisions of the D.C. and Eighth Circuits.

**I. The Board’s Denial of Reopening Is a Final Decision Under § 355(f)**

A “final decision” is one that “ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment.” *Abbruzzese v. R.R. Ret. Bd.*, 63 F.3d 972, 974 (10th Cir. 1995) (internal quotation marks omitted). The Board’s order denying reopening did precisely that: it conclusively resolved Salinas’s request for redetermination of his 2006 claim, imposed no further proceedings, and left no administrative avenue open. As the D.C. Circuit recognized, “[t]he Board’s decision not to reopen a prior benefits determination is a final decision” because it “ends the administrative proceedings on the reopening request.” *Stovic v. R.R. Ret. Bd.*, 826 F.3d 500, 503 (D.C. Cir. 2016). The Eighth Circuit similarly held that a “refusal to reopen is a final decision” under § 355(f) because it “disposes of the petitioner’s claim.” *Sones v. R.R. Ret. Bd.*, 933 F.2d 636, 638 (8th Cir. 1991).

The plain language of § 355(f) draws no distinction between merits decisions and reopening decisions. It grants review of any “final decision.” The Board’s own regulations treat a reopening denial as a “decision” that may be appealed administratively. 20 C.F.R. § 260.2(a)(2). Once the Board issues that decision, the administrative process is exhausted. To hold that such a decision is unreviewable would read into § 355(f) a limitation that Congress did not write.

**II. *Califano v. Sanders* Does Not Bar Review of RRA Reopening Decisions**

The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on *Califano v. Sanders*, 430 U.S. 99 (1977), is misplaced. *Sanders* held that a refusal to reopen a Social Security disability claim was not reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act because the regulations governing reopening were “committed to agency discretion.” *Id.* at 108. But the RRA’s judicial review provision, § 355(f), is not derived from the APA. It is a standalone jurisdictional grant that “confer[s] jurisdiction on the courts of appeals to review final Board decisions.” *Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 503. The APA’s limitations on reviewability have no bearing on the RRA. Congress enacted § 355(f) to provide comprehensive judicial review of Board actions, and a denial of reopening is a final action that falls within that grant.

Moreover, the Board’s reopening regulations are not discretionary in the sense that *Sanders* addressed. The Board must reopen a claim within four years if the applicant shows “new and material evidence” or “administrative error.” 20 C.F.R. § 261.2(b). The Board’s determination that Salinas did not meet those standards is a legal conclusion reviewable for abuse of discretion. *See Szostak v. R.R. Ret. Bd.*, 370 F.2d 253, 254–55 (2d Cir. 1966) (reviewing Board’s refusal to reopen for abuse of discretion). When an agency’s decision is guided by objective criteria, it is not “committed to agency discretion” and is presumptively reviewable. *See Citizens to Preserve Overton Park v. Volpe*, 401 U.S. 402, 410 (1971).

**III. The Majority View Relies on a Misreading of § 355(f)**

The Fifth Circuit joined several circuits in holding that reopening decisions are not reviewable. *Roberts v. R.R. Ret. Bd.*, 346 F.3d 139, 140 (5th Cir. 2003). Those courts reasoned that § 355(f) only permits review of “decisions on the merits of a claim for benefits after administrative appeals have been exhausted.” *Id.* But nothing in the statute imposes such a limitation. The phrase “final decision” is capacious. It encompasses any Board action that brings a discrete administrative proceeding to a close. A reopening denial is a self-contained proceeding distinct from the underlying merits determination. *See Stovic*, 826 F.3d at 503–04. The majority’s cramped reading would insulate from review a host of Board decisions that finally resolve rights under the RRA, contrary to the statute’s remedial purpose.

Furthermore, the circuits that deny review have created an anomalous regime: a claimant who is denied a benefit on the merits may seek judicial review, but a claimant who is denied a reopening—even if the Board applied the wrong legal standard or ignored compelling evidence—has no recourse. That outcome cannot be squared with Congress’s intent to provide meaningful appellate review of agency action.

**CONCLUSION**

The Board’s decision not to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is a final decision under § 355(f). The Fifth Circuit erred in dismissing the petition for lack of jurisdiction. The judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for review of the Board’s reopening denial on the merits.
