**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sentencing Enhancement for Firearm Possession Does Not Transform Roussos’ Drug Conspiracy Conviction into a “Crime of Violence” Under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).**

The Bureau of Prisons (BOP) erroneously classified Roussos’ offense as a “crime of violence” based solely on the two-level enhancement for firearm possession under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1(b)(1). However, this enhancement does not alter the *nature* of the underlying offense. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. United States Coin & Currency*, 401 U.S. 715, 723 (1971) (quoting *United States v. Sullivan*, 255 F.2d 746, 748 (2d Cir. 1958)), “the nature of the offense is determined by the statutory definition of the crime itself, not by sentencing enhancements.” *See also United States v. Bowdach*, 561 F.2d 1160, 1168 (5th Cir. 1977) (holding that sentencing enhancements do not redefine the substantive offense).

The BOP’s reliance on *Program Statement 5162.02* is misplaced. The Program Statement improperly equates the mere *possession* of a firearm during a drug offense with a “substantial risk” of violence under § 924(c)(3)(B). This interpretation conflicts with the Supreme Court’s holding in *United States v. Harris*, 347 U.S. 612, 617 (1954), that “possession alone does not inherently involve a risk of violence.” The Court in *Harris* further explained that “the mere presence of a weapon does not transform a nonviolent offense into one involving a substantial risk of violence.” *Id.* at 618.

**II. The BOP’s Construction of “Nonviolent Offense” Under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B) Is Inconsistent with Congressional Intent.**

The BOP’s restrictive interpretation of “nonviolent offense” contravenes the plain language and purpose of § 3621(e)(2)(B). As this Court recognized in *United States v. Paskow*, 11 F.3d 120, 123 (3d Cir. 1993) (citing *United States v. Turkette*, 452 U.S. 576, 580 (1981)), “statutory terms must be construed in light of their plain meaning and legislative purpose.” The statute’s express purpose is to incentivize rehabilitation by rewarding inmates who complete drug treatment programs with early release. *See* 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).

The BOP’s interpretation, however, undermines this purpose by excluding inmates like Roussos who committed no violent act. As the Supreme Court stated in *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820), “where a statute’s purpose is clear, courts must not construe it in a manner that defeats its object.” The BOP’s rigid application of the “crime of violence” label ignores the fact that Roussos’ offense—conspiracy to distribute narcotics—is inherently nonviolent, regardless of the sentencing enhancement.

**III. The BOP’s Program Statement Impermissibly Expands the Scope of § 924(c)(3) Beyond Its Statutory Limits.**

The BOP’s reliance on *Program Statement 5162.02* to classify drug conspiracies as “crimes of violence” when a firearm enhancement is applied is an improper expansion of statutory authority. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Alpers*, 338 U.S. 680, 682 (1950), “administrative regulations may not enlarge the scope of a statute.” The Program Statement’s interpretation of § 924(c)(3) is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *United States v. Sharp*, 347 U.S. 121, 124 (1954), which narrowly construes “crime of violence” to require an *actual* risk of violence, not merely the *possibility* of it.

Moreover, the BOP’s interpretation conflicts with the Third Circuit’s holding in *United States v. Johnson*, 430 F.2d 1041, 1044 (3d Cir. 1970) (citing *United States v. Petrillo*, 332 U.S. 1, 6 (1947)), that “statutory terms must be given their ordinary and natural meaning unless Congress has clearly indicated otherwise.” The term “nonviolent offense” in § 3621(e)(2)(B) must be construed to exclude only those offenses that inherently involve violence—not those that receive sentencing enhancements unrelated to the offense’s nature.

**IV. Roussos Is Entitled to Relief Under 28 U.S.C. § 2241.**

Because the BOP’s denial of early release eligibility is based on an erroneous interpretation of the law, Roussos is entitled to habeas relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2241. As the Supreme Court held in *United States ex rel. Toth v. Quarles*, 350 U.S. 11, 16 (1955), “a federal prisoner may challenge the legality of his detention through a habeas petition if the Bureau of Prisons has misapplied the law.” The BOP’s misclassification of Roussos’ offense as a “crime of violence” is a clear misapplication of § 3621(e)(2)(B) and 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the district court’s denial of Roussos’ habeas petition and remand with instructions to grant him early release eligibility under § 3621(e)(2)(B).
