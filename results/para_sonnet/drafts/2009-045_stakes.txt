# ARGUMENT

## THE LODESTAR MAY BE ENHANCED, IN RARE AND EXCEPTIONAL CIRCUMSTANCES SUPPORTED BY SPECIFIC EVIDENCE, TO ACCOUNT FOR SUPERIOR PERFORMANCE AND EXCEPTIONAL RESULTS

This Court has never held that enhancement based on quality of performance and results is categorically foreclosed. To the contrary, this Court's precedents establish that while the lodestar is presumptively reasonable, that presumption may be overcome in the rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to justify an enhancement.

### A. This Court's Precedents Permit Enhancement in Rare and Exceptional Cases

In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424 (1983), this Court recognized that "where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee," and that "in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified." *Id.* at 435. This Court reaffirmed that principle in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886 (1984), expressly stating that it rejected "petitioner's argument that an upward adjustment to an attorney's fee is never appropriate under § 1988." *Id.* at 901. Rather than announcing a categorical bar, *Blum* held only that the plaintiffs before it had failed to justify an enhancement because they offered no "specific evidence" that "the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged." *Id.* at 899.

*Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens' Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546 (1986) ("*Delaware Valley I*"), confirms this reading. Far from overruling *Blum*, the Court there "further refined" its views and expressly reaffirmed that "upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible" in "certain 'rare' and 'exceptional' cases, supported by both 'specific evidence' on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts." *Id.* at 565 (quoting *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898–901). The Court cautioned that "the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar," *id.* at 566, but "ordinarily" is not "never." The Court left the door open for the extraordinary case in which the lodestar, calculated using generic hourly rates, does not capture the true value of the representation.

Every court of appeals to address this question has read *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* the same way. The Second, Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits all agree that enhancement is permissible in the rare case supported by specific evidence. *See Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794–95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Forshee v. Waterloo Indus., Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); *Shipes v. Trinity Indus.*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993). There is no circuit split for this Court to resolve; there is only unanimous agreement that the rule announced in *Blum* means what it says.

### B. The Lodestar Does Not Always Capture the Full Value of Counsel's Contribution

Petitioners' position—that quality of performance and results can *never* justify enhancement—cannot be squared with the animating purpose of federal fee-shifting statutes. Congress enacted § 1988 to ensure that civil rights plaintiffs could secure "effective legal representation," *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897, and the lodestar method itself is only a proxy for that goal, not an end in itself. A generic hourly rate, multiplied by hours billed, may systematically fail to capture the value of representation that produces an extraordinary result through extraordinary skill, particularly in complex, protracted institutional-reform litigation of the kind at issue here.

This is consistent with *Missouri v. Jenkins*, 491 U.S. 274 (1989), where the Court held that a reasonable fee under § 1988 must be calculated according to prevailing market rates, recognizing that fee awards must reflect what the market would actually pay for the services rendered. *Id.* at 285–86. Where, as the district court found here, counsel's performance was superior to what the hourly rate could reasonably be expected to produce, and where that superior performance produced an exceptional result, the lodestar—calculated using market rates for competent, not extraordinary, representation—does not fully compensate for the service actually rendered. An enhancement in such circumstances does not abandon the lodestar framework; it completes it.

### C. The District Court's Enhancement Was Supported by the Specific Evidentiary Record This Court's Precedents Require

The district court did not rely on generalized praise. It made detailed, record-based findings regarding counsel's investigative work, litigation strategy, and the comprehensiveness of the injunctive relief obtained—an outcome respondents' counsel achieved notwithstanding substantial resistance from petitioners over years of litigation. The Eleventh Circuit examined this record and concluded that the district court did not abuse its discretion in finding the case to be one of the "rare" and "exceptional" ones contemplated by *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I*. *Kenny A. v. Perdue*, 532 F.3d 1209, 1242 (11th Cir. 2008).

This is precisely the inquiry *Blum* requires: whether the fee applicant has offered "specific evidence" showing that the quality of service was superior to what the hourly rate would predict, and that the success obtained was exceptional. 465 U.S. at 899. The district judge, who presided over this litigation from filing to final judgment, was uniquely positioned to make that assessment based on firsthand observation, and the court of appeals correctly deferred to that judgment under an abuse-of-discretion standard.

### CONCLUSION

The lodestar may be enhanced, in the rare and exceptional case supported by specific evidence, based on the quality of performance and the results obtained. This Court should affirm.
