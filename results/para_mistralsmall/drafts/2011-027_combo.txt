**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title to Riverbeds in the State Only if the River Was Navigable at the Time of Statehood.**

The equal footing doctrine, first articulated in *Pollard’s Lessee v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. 212 (1845), holds that when a state enters the Union, it takes title to the beds and banks of rivers within its borders that are navigable for title purposes. *Pollard’s Lessee*, 44 U.S. at 223. This doctrine is rooted in the principle that navigable waters are held in trust for the public and that the federal government, upon admitting a state to the Union, transfers title to such waters to the state. *Id.* The critical inquiry, as established in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), is whether the river was navigable *at the time of statehood*. *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 83-84. The Court in *Utah* explicitly rejected the notion that later improvements in navigation could retroactively render a river navigable for title purposes. *Id.* at 84. Thus, the trial court correctly applied the *Pollard’s Lessee* test by examining the navigability of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as they existed in 1889, the year Montana achieved statehood.

**II. The "Title" Test for Navigability Requires a Determination of Navigability at Statehood, Not at Some Later Date.**

The distinction between the "title" test and the "purpose" test for navigability was clarified in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557 (1870). In *The Daniel Ball*, the Court held that for purposes of determining title under the equal footing doctrine, a river is navigable if it is "navigable in fact" at the time of statehood, regardless of whether it was later rendered navigable by artificial improvements. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563. The Court explained that the test is whether the river was "susceptible of being used, in its ordinary condition, as a highway for commerce." *Id.* This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1 (1935), where the Court reiterated that the navigability inquiry for title purposes is fixed at the time of statehood. *Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14. The Montana Supreme Court’s reliance on historical evidence to determine navigability at statehood was consistent with these precedents.

**III. Federal Preemption Does Not Bar the State’s Claims for Compensation.**

The Federal Power Act (FPA), 16 U.S.C. §§ 791a–823d (1940), does not preempt the State’s ability to seek compensation for the use of state-owned riverbeds. In *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Co-op. v. Fed. Power Comm’n*, 328 U.S. 152 (1946), the Court held that the FPA establishes a "dual system" of regulation, preserving the role of state law in matters concerning proprietary rights, including the use of riverbeds. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76. The Court further explained that the FPA does not divest states of their property rights or prevent them from seeking compensation for the use of such property. *Id.* at 176. This principle was reiterated in *Fed. Power Comm’n v. Niagara Mohawk Power Corp.*, 347 U.S. 239 (1954), where the Court held that the federal navigational servitude does not preempt state law claims for compensation unless Congress has clearly authorized such preemption. *Niagara Mohawk*, 347 U.S. at 249-51. The trial court correctly concluded that neither the FPA nor the federal navigational servitude preempted the State’s claims.

**IV. The State’s Title to Riverbeds Is Not Affected by Federal Licensing of Hydroelectric Projects.**

The fact that PPL’s dams were licensed by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) under the FPA does not extinguish the State’s title to the riverbeds. In *United States v. Chandler-Dunbar Water Power Co.*, 229 U.S. 53 (1913), the Court held that federal licensing of a hydroelectric project does not confer any right to use the riverbeds underlying the project without compensating the owner of those beds. *Chandler-Dunbar*, 229 U.S. at 62-63. The Court explained that the federal government’s power to license projects does not extend to the power to take or use private or state property without just compensation. *Id.* at 63. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316 (1917), where the Court held that the federal government’s navigational servitude does not permit the uncompensated use of state-owned riverbeds. *Cress*, 243 U.S. at 326-27. Thus, PPL’s FERC licenses do not preclude the State from seeking compensation for the use of its riverbeds.

**V. The State’s Claims Are Not Barred by Laches or the Statute of Limitations.**

The State’s claims for compensation are not barred by laches or the statute of limitations. In *United States v. Nashville, C. & St. L. Ry. Co.*, 118 U.S. 120 (1886), the Court held that the United States is not subject to the defense of laches in enforcing its property rights. *Nashville*, 118 U.S. at 125-26. While this principle applies to the federal government, it is equally applicable to states acting in their sovereign capacity to enforce their property rights. *See also United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1 (1935) (holding that the United States is not barred by laches in asserting its property rights). Moreover, the Court has held that the statute of limitations does not run against the sovereign in enforcing its property rights. *United States v. California*, 297 U.S. 175 (1936) (holding that the United States is not bound by state statutes of limitations in enforcing its property rights). Thus, the State’s claims are not barred by these defenses.

**Conclusion**

The trial court correctly applied the equal footing doctrine by determining that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were navigable at the time of Montana’s statehood in 1889. The State’s title to the riverbeds underlying PPL’s dams is not affected by federal licensing, and the State’s claims for compensation are not preempted by federal law or barred by laches or the statute of limitations. The judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
