Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062 (1967)). However, several courts of appeals have rejected this standard, holding that a lesser showing should suffice. For example, the Sixth Circuit has held that an employer may poll its employees to determine their union sentiment if it has substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support (NLRB v. John S. Swift Co., 302 F.2d 342 (7th Cir. 1962)).

Similarly, in Celanese Corp. of America, 95 N.L.R.B. 664 (1951), the NLRB itself recognized that an employer may have a good-faith doubt about a union's majority status, even if the doubt is not objectively reasonable.

The NLRB's standard is overly restrictive and fails to account for the employer's legitimate interest in ascertaining employee sentiment. In this case, Allentown Mack had substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support, including statements from several employees indicating their dissatisfaction with the union.

The Supreme Court has held that the NLRB's decisions must be based on substantial evidence and that the court must review the NLRB's findings to ensure that they are not arbitrary or capricious (NLRB v. Pittsburgh Plate Glass Co., 270 U.S. 394 (1926)). The NLRB's determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt about the union's majority status is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.

The NLRB's standard may actually destabilize collective-bargaining relationships. Mayer, 313 F.2d 216 (6th Cir. 1963)).

In conclusion, the NLRB's standard for polling employees on union support is not rational and consistent with the NLRA, and the NLRB's factual determination that Allentown Mack lacked a good-faith doubt is not supported by substantial evidence in the record.
