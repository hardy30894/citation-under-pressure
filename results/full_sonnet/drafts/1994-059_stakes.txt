# ARGUMENT

Amendment 73's ban on ballot access for Arkansas's most experienced federal officeholders is not a neutral election regulation—it is an additional qualification for federal office that the Constitution does not permit any state to impose. The Qualifications Clauses of Article I fix the sole requirements for service in Congress, and no state may add to them, whether directly or through the subterfuge of a ballot-access restriction that produces the identical disqualifying effect.

## I. The Qualifications Clauses Establish Exclusive Requirements for Congressional Service.

Article I specifies, with precision, the qualifications for the House and Senate: age, citizenship, and inhabitancy. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2, cl. 2; *id.* § 3, cl. 3. The Framers debated and rejected proposals to permit additional qualifications, including property requirements, and settled on this fixed, minimal list. This Court confronted the closed nature of that list in *Powell v. McCormack*, 395 U.S. 486 (1969), holding that even the House of Representatives itself—expressly empowered by Article I, § 5 to "be the Judge of the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members"—may not exclude a duly elected member who satisfies the Constitution's enumerated qualifications. The Court held that "the Constitution leaves the House without authority to exclude any person, duly elected by his constituents, who meets all the requirements for membership expressly prescribed in the Constitution." *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 522. In reaching that conclusion, the Court relied on Alexander Hamilton's explanation in The Federalist No. 60 that the qualifications for federal office "are defined and fixed in the Constitution, and are unalterable by the legislature." *Id.* at 539–40 (quoting The Federalist No. 60).

If Congress—the very body Article I, § 5 empowers to judge the qualifications of its own members—lacks authority to add to the constitutional list, it follows *a fortiori* that a single state lacks such authority. Arkansas is not merely one house of a national legislature policing its own membership under an express textual grant; it is an individual state attempting to impose, unilaterally, a disqualification that applies only to candidates from that state, for offices that represent the entire nation. Nothing in Article I, § 4—which reserves to the states the power to prescribe the "Times, Places and Manner of holding Elections"—confers any comparable authority to add qualifications. The Elections Clause is a grant of procedural, not substantive, authority.

## II. Section 3 Is a Qualification in Substance, Regardless of Its Ballot-Access Label.

Arkansas's sponsors, aware of the constitutional infirmity of an outright bar on federal service, styled Section 3 as a restriction on ballot access rather than eligibility for office. But the label chosen by the drafters cannot alter the provision's operation. Section 3 does not regulate the mechanics of an election—filing deadlines, signature requirements, or the like. It permanently forecloses an entire category of persons, defined solely by their prior electoral success, from having their names placed before the voters. The practical effect is indistinguishable from outright disqualification: a three-term member of the House, or a two-term Senator, cannot appear on the ballot no matter how popular, how qualified under Article I's actual requirements, or how strongly the voters of Arkansas wish to return that person to office.

This Court's decisions addressing genuine ballot-access regulations confirm the distinction. In *Storer v. Brown*, 415 U.S. 724 (1974), the Court evaluated California's restrictions on independent candidacies as regulations of the electoral process, subject to balancing against the interests they served, precisely because they governed the *manner* of seeking office rather than barring an entire class of persons from service based on status. *Id.* at 730. Section 3 does not fit that mold. It does not ask whether a candidate has complied with filing procedures; it asks how many times the candidate has previously been elected—a status-based exclusion functionally identical to the qualification struck down in *Powell*. A state cannot evade the Qualifications Clauses simply by shifting the mechanism of exclusion from the certificate of election to the ballot itself. The Arkansas Supreme Court's own characterization of the provision as "a restriction on the qualifications of persons seeking federal congressional offices," rather than a bona fide manner regulation, was correct.

## III. Permitting States to Supplement the Qualifications Clauses Would Destroy the Uniformity the Framers Required.

The Framers designed the qualifications for Congress to be uniform nationwide precisely because members of Congress, once elected, serve the interests of the entire nation, not merely their home states. Allowing each state to layer additional disqualifications onto the constitutional minimum would produce exactly the "hodge-podge" of local restrictions the Arkansas Supreme Court itself recognized as inconsistent with the structure the Framers adopted. A citizen otherwise fully qualified under Article I could be barred from Congress in one state and not another, based on nothing more than the political preferences embodied in a particular state's initiative process. That result cannot be squared with *Powell*'s recognition that the qualifications for federal office are fixed by the Constitution and "unalterable" by any legislative body short of constitutional amendment. *Powell*, 395 U.S. at 540.

## CONCLUSION

Section 3 of Amendment 73 imposes an additional qualification for federal office that Article I's Qualifications Clauses do not authorize any state to create. This Court should affirm the judgment holding Section 3 unconstitutional.
