**ARGUMENT**

The design patent damages statute, 35 U.S.C. § 289, permits a patentee to recover “the total profit” an infringer made “from the article of manufacture to which [the patented] design has been applied.” The central question is what constitutes the “article of manufacture” when the design covers only a component of a multi-component product. The correct answer, rooted in the statute’s text, history, and this Court’s precedents, is that the article of manufacture is the specific component that embodies the design, not the entire product.

**I. The Statutory Text and This Court’s Precedents Require that the “Article of Manufacture” Be the Component That Embodies the Design**

Section 289’s phrase “article of manufacture” has a settled meaning. In *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439, 446 (1885), this Court held that the “article of manufacture” is the thing “to which the design is applied”—here, the component that incorporates the patented design. The Court explained that the patentee “can recover profits only on the article which is the subject of the design.” *Id.* at 447. That language forecloses a rule allowing recovery of profits from the entire product when the design is limited to a component.

Moreover, this Court has consistently defined “article of manufacture” as a discrete item produced by human labor, not a combination of unrelated components. *See Smith v. Whitman Saddle Co.*, 148 U.S. 674, 678 (1893) (defining “article of manufacture” as “a thing made by the hand of man”); *see also* *Gorham Mfg. Co. v. White*, 81 U.S. 511, 524 (1872) (design patent covers “some new and original shape or configuration of a manufactured article”). A smartphone screen or a bezel is a “thing made by the hand of man” and qualifies as an article of manufacture. The entire smartphone, by contrast, is a collection of many articles, each with its own design.

**II. The Federal Circuit’s Approach Conflicts with the Statute and This Court’s Precedents**

The decision below allowed Apple to recover all profits from Samsung’s smartphones, even though the asserted design patents cover only components like the front face, bezel, and graphical user interface. That approach ignores the statutory requirement that profits be “from the article of manufacture.” When a design is applied only to a component, the profits recoverable under § 289 are limited to the profits attributable to that component. *See* *Birdsall v. Coolidge*, 93 U.S. 64, 69 (1876) (damages in patent cases must be “commensurate with the injury sustained”).

This Court’s decision in *Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476 (1964), reinforces the principle of proportionality. Although *Aro* involved utility patents, its reasoning applies with equal force to design patents. The Court held that when a patent covers only a component of a combination, damages for infringement must be limited to the value of that component, not the entire combination. *Id.* at 482–83. Section 289 does not displace that fundamental rule. *See* *Westinghouse v. Wagner Mfg. Co.*, 225 U.S. 604, 614 (1912) (statutes in derogation of common law are strictly construed).

**III. The Proper Test: Identify the Article of Manufacture as a Fact Question and Apportion Profits to That Article**

The correct approach is to determine the “article of manufacture” as a factual question—what is the component that embodies the design?—and then calculate the infringer’s profits attributable to that article. This is consistent with this Court’s directive in *Sheldon v. Metro-Goldwyn Pictures Corp.*, 309 U.S. 390, 399 (1940), that in copyright cases, profits must be “apportioned” between infringing and non-infringing elements. The same principle applies here. *See* *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 517 U.S. 370, 384 (1996) (claim construction defines the scope of the patented invention).

The jury must be instructed to identify the “article of manufacture” from the perspective of an ordinary observer, considering how the design is applied and the component’s role in the product. *See* *Gorham*, 81 U.S. at 528 (ordinary observer test for design patent infringement). Once the article is identified, the jury should determine the profits attributable to that article—not the entire product. This approach respects the statutory text and avoids the windfall that the Federal Circuit’s rule permits.

**IV. The Federal Circuit’s Rule Would Allow Absurd Results**

Under the court below, a patent covering only the shape of a smartphone’s power button could entitle the patentee to all profits from the entire smartphone. That cannot be what Congress intended. *See* *Dobson*, 114 U.S. at 446 (Congress intended to prevent “unreasonable and oppressive” recovery). The statute was designed to protect the value of the design itself, not to grant a monopoly over unrelated components. *See* *Whitman Saddle*, 148 U.S. at 679 (design patents protect “the appearance of the article,” not its function).

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for a determination of the article of manufacture and the profits attributable to it.
