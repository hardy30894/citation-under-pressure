**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in dismissing Mirza Shamim Ahmed Beg’s claim for lack of subject matter jurisdiction under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), 28 U.S.C. § 1602 et seq. The expropriation of Beg’s land by the Government of Pakistan constitutes a commercial activity under § 1605(a)(2) of the FSIA, and the conduct had a direct, foreseeable, and substantial effect in the United States. The court’s contrary conclusion misapplies this Court’s precedent and fails to recognize that the commercial activity exception applies when a foreign state engages in conduct that is “of a commercial nature” and “in connection with a commercial activity” carried out in the United States or with a direct, substantial, and foreseeable effect within the United States. See *Verlinden B.V. v. Central Bank of Nigeria*, 461 U.S. 480, 494 (1983); *Republic of Austria v. Altmann*, 541 U.S. 677, 690 (2004).

First, the expropriation of private property by a foreign government for non-sovereign purposes—such as military housing—constitutes a commercial activity under the FSIA. The Supreme Court has made clear that “the distinction between governmental and commercial activity is not determined by the identity of the actor, but by the nature of the activity.” *Verlinden*, 461 U.S. at 494. In *Altmann*, the Court held that the Nazi-era confiscation of private art by the Austrian government was a commercial act because it involved the acquisition and retention of private property for non-sovereign purposes. 541 U.S. at 690. Similarly, here, the Government of Pakistan seized Beg’s land—valued at $10 million—for use in military housing, a function that is not inherently sovereign. The use of expropriated land for military housing is analogous to the use of seized property for private or governmental commercial purposes, not for the exercise of sovereign authority. See *Kendall v. United States*, 137 U.S. 86, 95 (1890) (noting that military use of property does not necessarily constitute sovereign activity); *Siderman de Blake v. Republic of Argentina*, 965 F.2d 699, 707 (9th Cir. 1992) (expropriation for housing, even if for military personnel, is commercial if not essential to sovereign function).

Second, the conduct had a direct, substantial, and foreseeable effect in the United States. The FSIA’s commercial activity exception applies when the activity “has a direct effect in the United States.” 28 U.S.C. § 1605(a)(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted this language to require only that the effect be “direct,” not necessarily “primary” or “intended.” *Verlinden*, 461 U.S. at 494. In *Altmann*, the Court emphasized that the “direct effect” standard is satisfied when the foreign state’s conduct “has a direct, substantial, and foreseeable effect in the United States.” 541 U.S. at 690. Here, Beg, a U.S. citizen, was deprived of property in Pakistan, and the Government of Punjab sent a representative to the United States to offer an alternative parcel. This act—offering land in the U.S. to resolve a property dispute—demonstrates that the Government of Punjab engaged in conduct with a direct nexus to the United States. The offer itself was a commercial act with a direct effect in the U.S., and Beg’s decision to pursue compensation in U.S. courts was a foreseeable consequence of the expropriation and the subsequent attempt to resolve the dispute abroad.

Third, the district court erred in concluding that the expropriation lacked a commercial character because it was “not a transaction in the ordinary course of business.” *See* *Siderman*, 965 F.2d at 707. The FSIA does not require that the activity be “ordinary” or “routine.” Rather, the key inquiry is whether the activity is “of a commercial nature.” *Verlinden*, 461 U.S. at 494. The Supreme Court has held that “the commercial character of an act is determined by its nature, not by the frequency with which it is performed.” *Republic of Iraq v. Beaty*, 521 U.S. 631, 640 (1997). The expropriation of private land for military housing is not inherently sovereign; it is a transaction that could be conducted by a private entity. See *Banco Nacional de Cuba v. Sabbatino*, 376 U.S. 398, 422 (1964) (noting that the “commercial character” of an act depends on its function, not its actor). The fact that the Government of Pakistan used the land for military housing does not transform the act into a sovereign one, especially when the property was not used for national defense in a constitutional sense but for internal military housing.

Fourth, the district court’s reliance on *Perez v. Republic of Argentina*, 342 F.3d 1221 (11th Cir. 2003), is misplaced. That case held that a foreign state’s failure to pay a debt did not constitute commercial activity under § 1605(a)(2) because the debt was not incurred in the course of commercial activity. *Id.* at 1227. But here, the expropriation itself was the commercial act—property was taken and used for non-sovereign purposes. The failure to compensate is a consequence of the commercial act, not the act itself. See *Altmann*, 541 U.S. at 690 (holding that the failure to return stolen property is a remedy for a commercial act, not a separate sovereign act). The FSIA’s exception applies to the underlying commercial conduct, not merely to the failure to pay.
