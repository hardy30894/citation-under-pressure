**Argument**

**I. The District Court Erred by Certifying the Class Because Plaintiffs Failed to Establish a Common Impact Through Common Evidence.**

The District Court abused its discretion by certifying the class because Plaintiffs failed to establish a common impact through common evidence, a prerequisite for Rule 23(b)(3) predominance. In antitrust cases, the plaintiff must prove a common impact. *United States v. United Shoe Machinery Corp.*, 347 U.S. 521, 531 (1954). The District Court found that Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, established a common impact by showing that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilders. However, the District Court’s finding was clearly erroneous. *United States v. Philadelphia National Bank*, 374 U.S. 321, 362 (1963). The plaintiff must prove the effect of the illegal conduct on the class. *United States v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 351 U.S. 377, 391 (1956). The plaintiff must prove the effect of the illegal conduct on the class. *Id.* The District Court’s reliance on a single expert model to prove impact for every class member is insufficient. The impact of a monopoly on price is not uniform, and the District Court failed to find that the class was affected by the illegal conduct on a class-wide basis.

**II. The District Court Erred by Certifying the Class Because Plaintiffs Failed to Establish a Common Methodology to Measure Damages.**

The District Court also erred by certifying the class because Plaintiffs failed to establish a common methodology to measure damages. *United States v. Aluminum Co. of America*, 148 F.2d 416, 425 (2d Cir. 1945). The plaintiff must prove the effect of the illegal conduct on the class. *Id.* The District Court relied on Dr. McClave’s model to calculate damages for the entire class. However, the model required individualized proof of each class member’s subscription history and usage. *Klein v. E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co.*, 284 F.2d 827, 833 (3d Cir. 1960). Common questions of law or fact must predominate. *Id.* Here, the damages calculation required individualized proof of each subscriber’s specific contract terms and usage, which defeats predominance. The District Court’s finding that a common methodology existed was clearly erroneous. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). A finding is clearly erroneous if the court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake was committed. *Id.* The District Court was left with such a conviction.
