# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF § 7702(a)(1) GOVERNS ALL MIXED CASES, INCLUDING THOSE DISMISSED FOR LACK OF JURISDICTION

The Civil Service Reform Act defines a "mixed case" not by reference to how the Board ultimately disposes of it, but by reference to the nature of the employee's allegations at the time of filing. A case is a mixed case when the employee "has been affected by an action which the employee . . . may appeal to the Merit Systems Protection Board" and "alleges that a basis for the action was discrimination." 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1). This Court in *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), confirmed that the statutory definition controls the forum for judicial review: "mixed cases—those appealable to the MSPB and alleging discrimination—shall be filed in district court." *Id.* at 44.

Perry alleged that his suspension and retirement resulted from disability discrimination, and he alleged that the underlying action—suspension exceeding fourteen days and involuntary retirement—was of a type "appeal[able] to the" Board under 5 U.S.C. §§ 7512, 7513(d). Whether the Board ultimately agreed that it possessed jurisdiction is irrelevant to whether Perry's case satisfies the statutory definition of a mixed case. As this Court held in *Kloeckner*, "[n]othing in the statute" conditions the forum for review on the *outcome* of the Board's jurisdictional inquiry. 568 U.S. at 50.

## II. *KLOECKNER*'S REASONING FORECLOSES ANY DISTINCTION BETWEEN PROCEDURAL AND JURISDICTIONAL DISMISSALS

The D.C. Circuit's asserted distinction between "procedural" and "jurisdictional" dismissals cannot survive *Kloeckner*'s comprehensive rationale. This Court explained that Congress created "a unified regime for judicial review of MSPB decisions" that "does not distinguish between mixed cases dismissed on procedural grounds and those decided on the merits." 568 U.S. at 50. The Court's holding rested not on the timeliness defect at issue in that case specifically, but on the structural function of § 7702's definitional language: it identifies mixed cases based on the *allegations* the employee makes, not the *disposition* the Board reaches.

This Court further emphasized that channeling all mixed-case appeals to district court serves the practical goal of avoiding the "arbitrary" splitting of employee claims between two forums. *Id.* at 49. The Court observed that requiring employees to litigate discrimination claims in one forum while adjudicating threshold procedural or jurisdictional questions in another would produce "a scheme Congress could not have intended," particularly given that such threshold questions are often "confused" with the merits of the discrimination claim itself. *Id.* at 50. That concern applies with equal—if not greater—force to jurisdictional dismissals premised on voluntariness, since the voluntariness of a settlement is frequently intertwined with whether the employee was coerced by the very discrimination she alleges. Indeed, this Court recognized that "the question whether the agency's action was appealable to the Board" can turn on factual and legal issues "identical" to those underlying the discrimination claim. *Id.*

The D.C. Circuit's attempt to distinguish *Kloeckner* rests on the notion that jurisdictionally dismissed cases were never truly "appealable to the MSPB," whereas procedurally dismissed cases retain that character because the Board could excuse the procedural defect. *Perry v. MSPB*, No. 15-1218, slip op. at 15–16 (D.C. Cir.). But this reasoning imports a distinction that the statutory text does not contain. Section 7702(a)(1)(A) asks only whether the employee "has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the" Board—a question resolved by examining the *nature* of the action (e.g., a suspension exceeding fourteen days), not by predicting whether the Board will ultimately sustain jurisdiction after adjudicating voluntariness. This Court's admonition in *Kloeckner* that the forum-selection inquiry must be resolved "at the outset of the case," 568 U.S. at 50, is incompatible with a rule that makes the proper forum contingent on the Board's eventual jurisdictional ruling.

## III. A BIFURCATED SYSTEM CONTRAVENES *KLOECKNER*'S CONCERN FOR ADMINISTRABILITY AND UNIFORMITY

This Court in *Kloeckner* stressed that Congress intended "a simple test for determining a case's forum" that would not require courts or litigants to parse the substance of the Board's disposition. 568 U.S. at 50. The D.C. Circuit's rule—and the rule urged by Respondent here—requires exactly that kind of forum-determining inquiry into the Board's reasoning, undermining the administrability this Court sought to protect. An employee filing a petition for review would not know, until the Federal Circuit or district court examined the Board's rationale, which forum was proper. That result recreates the very "jurisdictional wrangling" that this Court's decision in *Kloeckner* aimed to eliminate. *Id.* at 49 (quoting *Elgin v. Department of Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1, 15 (2012)).

Moreover, sending jurisdictional dismissals to the Federal Circuit while sending procedural dismissals to district court fragments review of what are often overlapping factual questions—here, whether Perry's retirement was coerced by disability discrimination. This Court has recognized the systemic costs of such fragmentation, noting that Congress designed the CSRA's review provisions to avoid "split[ting] employment discrimination claims between two different tribunals, each ignorant of the other's actions." *Kloeckner*, 568 U.S. at 49 (internal quotation marks omitted).

## CONCLUSION

Because Perry alleged that his suspension and retirement were the product of disability discrimination, and because those actions were of the type generally appealable to the Board, his case is a "mixed case" under 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1) regardless of the Board's ultimate jurisdictional ruling. Under *Kloeckner v. Solis*, 568 U.S. 41 (2012), review of the Board's decision therefore lies exclusively in federal district court. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
