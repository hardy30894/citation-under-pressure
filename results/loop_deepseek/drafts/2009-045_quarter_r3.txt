**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits enhancement of a reasonable attorney’s fee based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. For over a century, federal courts exercising equitable discretion have recognized that exceptional attorney performance and extraordinary results may justify an upward adjustment of the fee award. This longstanding authority, rooted in pre-1970 decisions, confirms that the district court properly enhanced the lodestar figure in this case.

**I. The Equitable Power to Enhance Fees for Exceptional Performance and Results Is Firmly Established in Pre-1970 Precedent**

Federal courts have long possessed inherent equitable authority to award attorney’s fees that reflect the true value of legal services. That authority includes the power to enhance fees when the quality of representation and the results obtained are extraordinary. *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 127 (1885) affirmed that a fee award may be liberal where the attorney’s efforts produced results of great value. Similarly, *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939) recognized that a district court has the power to award counsel fees in equity and may exercise a discretion that is not controlled by rigid rules.

Lower federal courts consistently applied these principles. *In re L. H. T. Co.*, 123 F.2d 222, 224 (2d Cir. 1941) listed the skill and ability of the attorney and the results accomplished as proper factors in setting fees. *Cleveland v. Second National Bank*, 149 F.2d 221, 223 (6th Cir. 1945) held that a court may consider the character and extent of the services, the amount involved, the results obtained, and the skill and experience of counsel. *In re Osofsky*, 50 F.2d 925, 927 (2d Cir. 1931) similarly recognized that exceptional results may justify a higher fee. These cases demonstrate that enhancement based on the quality of performance and results is not a novel concept but a well-established equitable tool.

**II. The Discretion to Enhance Is Consistent with the Purposes of Federal Fee-Shifting Statutes**

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, it incorporated the common-law equitable tradition. The Supreme Court in *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 717 (1967) emphasized that the allowance of attorney’s fees in federal courts is a matter of federal common law, and that courts exercise discretion in awarding such fees. The reasonableness standard ensures that successful plaintiffs can obtain effective legal representation. That discretion necessarily includes the power to enhance fees when the lodestar fails to compensate fully for exceptional performance and extraordinary results.

The petitioners’ argument that enhancement is never permissible would repudiate this settled history. The pre-1970 cases uniformly hold that courts may consider the quality of representation and the results obtained in setting fees. The district court here acted within the bounds of that discretion.

**III. The District Court’s Detailed Findings Satisfy the Requirements for Enhancement**

The district court did not rely on “solely” the quality of performance and results; it made detailed findings based on its own substantial experience and familiarity with prevailing rates, and its observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout the litigation. The court specifically found that the superb quality of counsel’s representation far exceeded what could reasonably be expected for the standard hourly rates used to calculate the fee. Such findings are precisely the specific evidence required by the equitable tradition. *See Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 167 (discretion must be exercised with due regard to all the circumstances).

The circuit court correctly affirmed. The pre-1970 cases establish that enhancements are permissible in rare and exceptional cases. The district court’s enhancement was based on specific evidence and detailed findings, not on a “solely” quality-based rationale. The judgment should be affirmed.
