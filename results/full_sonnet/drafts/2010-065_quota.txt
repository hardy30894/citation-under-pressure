ARGUMENT

I. The Tenth Amendment Protects the States as Sovereign Entities, Not Private Litigants, and Petitioner Therefore Lacks Standing to Invoke It.

The Tenth Amendment provides that powers not delegated to the United States "are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. This Court has long recognized that the Amendment safeguards the prerogatives of state governments as sovereigns within the federal system, not personal liberties belonging to individual citizens. In Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utility companies argued that a federal agency's sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment because it usurped power reserved to the States. This Court squarely rejected that theory, holding that the companies, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." Id. at 144. That holding controls here. Petitioner, like the utility companies in Tennessee Electric, is a private party seeking to vindicate an interest that belongs, if to anyone, to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania.

Because standing is jurisdictional, this Court must resolve it before reaching the merits of any constitutional claim. See Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Environment, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998) (requiring courts to decide jurisdictional issues, including standing, before addressing the substance of a claim). The jurisdictional character of the standing inquiry means that the reasoning in Tennessee Electric is not, as petitioner suggests, mere dictum, but an independent and controlling ground for decision. See Medeiros v. Vincent, 431 F.3d 25, 34 (1st Cir. 2005) (explaining that this Court "dismissed the Tenth Amendment claim after analyzing both the standing issue and the merits," making that holding "an alternative ground, rather than obiter dictum").

II. The Weight of Appellate Authority Confirms That Private Parties Lack Standing to Raise Tenth Amendment Claims.

Consistent with Tennessee Electric, a substantial majority of the courts of appeals have held that private litigants may not invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal legislation. In United States v. Hacker, 565 F.3d 522 (8th Cir. 2009), the court held that a criminal defendant could not raise a Tenth Amendment defense because the Amendment protects state sovereignty, which the State itself is best positioned to defend. Id. at 525–27. The Ninth Circuit reached the same conclusion in Oregon v. Legal Services Corp., 552 F.3d 965, 971–72 (9th Cir. 2009), as did the Second Circuit in Brooklyn Legal Services Corp. v. Legal Services Corp., 462 F.3d 219, 234–35 (2d Cir. 2006), and the Tenth Circuit in United States v. Parker, 362 F.3d 1279, 1284–85 (10th Cir. 2004). Each of these courts recognized that permitting private parties to assert Tenth Amendment claims would allow individuals to litigate a generalized grievance about the allocation of power between sovereigns—precisely the sort of claim this Court has repeatedly held insufficient to support Article III standing. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 573–74 (1992) (a plaintiff raising only a generally available grievance about government does not state an Article III case or controversy).

This rule flows naturally from the structural understanding of federalism that this Court articulated in Massachusetts v. Mellon, 262 U.S. 447 (1923). There, the Court explained that a State's interest in the maintenance of the constitutional balance of powers is one belonging to it as a "sovereign," to be vindicated—if at all—by the State itself, not by private citizens acting on its behalf. Id. at 485–86. The same logic controls the Tenth Amendment context: because "[u]nder our federal system[,] the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States," Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945), it is the States—not individual defendants prosecuted under valid federal statutes—who possess the sovereign interest that the Tenth Amendment protects.

III. Permitting Individual Criminal Defendants to Raise Tenth Amendment Claims Would Undermine, Rather Than Vindicate, the Structural Protections of Federalism.

Petitioner's asserted interest in her prosecution is not the sort of "injury" contemplated by Article III or the Tenth Amendment. If a State disagrees with federal encroachment on its reserved powers, "the citizens of that state may have recourse to local political processes to effect change in the state's policy of acquiescence." Medeiros, 431 F.3d at 35. Pennsylvania has taken no position suggesting that 18 U.S.C. § 229 offends its sovereign prerogatives; to the contrary, its law enforcement officers referred petitioner's conduct to federal postal inspectors for investigation and prosecution. Where the State itself has not objected, a private defendant's invocation of the Tenth Amendment amounts to nothing more than an attempt to enlist the courts in a freestanding challenge to the wisdom of federal prosecution—precisely the kind of undifferentiated grievance that Article III forecloses. See Dillard v. Chilton County Commission, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007) (denying private-party Tenth Amendment standing because the claim reflected only an "undifferentiated, generalized grievance").

For these reasons, the Third Circuit correctly held that petitioner lacks standing to challenge her conviction on Tenth Amendment grounds, and that judgment should be affirmed.
