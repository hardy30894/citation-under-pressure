**ARGUMENT**

**I. Treble damages imposed under state consumer-fraud statutes are dischargeable because they are not a debt “obtained by” actual fraud within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A).**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” The statutory text limits nondischargeability to the portion of the debt actually obtained by the fraud; it does not reach additional sums awarded as punishment or deterrence. Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991) (“a central purpose of the Code is to provide a procedure by which certain insolvent debtors can reorder their affairs … and be given a new opportunity in life … unhampered by the pressure and discouragement of preexisting debt”).

The Ninth Circuit correctly applied this limitation in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1992). There the court held that “the phrase ‘to the extent obtained by’ modifies the word ‘debt’ and limits the nondischargeable debt to the amount actually obtained by the fraud.” Id. Because punitive damages “are not obtained by the fraud; they are obtained by the court’s award,” they remain dischargeable. Id.

The contrary view adopted below—that any judgment “related to” fraud is nondischargeable—finds no support in the statutory language. In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672, 679 (11th Cir. 1993), read the word “debt” broadly but ignored the restrictive clause “to the extent obtained by.” That reading would render the limiting phrase superfluous, contrary to the rule that “every word” in a statute must be given effect. See, e.g., United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955).

The New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act’s mandatory trebling does not change the analysis. The additional two-thirds of the award is not money the debtor obtained from the tenants; it is a statutory penalty triggered by the violation. Allowing discharge of that penalty preserves the Code’s distinction between compensatory recovery and punitive sanctions while still denying the debtor any benefit from the fraudulently obtained rents.

**II. The “fresh start” policy requires that only debts representing the creditor’s actual loss be excepted from discharge.**

Grogan emphasized that exceptions to discharge are “narrowly construed” to serve the “fresh start” objective. 498 U.S. at 286-87. Extending nondischargeability to treble damages would permit creditors to leverage state punitive remedies to trap debtors in perpetual liability for conduct that, while wrongful, did not enrich the debtor by the full amount of the judgment. Such a result conflicts with the congressional judgment, reflected in the text of § 523(a)(2)(A), that only the sum “obtained by” the fraud survives bankruptcy.

The judgment below should therefore be reversed to the extent it declares the treble-damages portion of the award nondischargeable.
