**ARGUMENT**

The Fifth Circuit erred in holding that OSHA lacks jurisdiction over Rig 52. The Act applies to “employment performed in a workplace in a State.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(a). Rig 52 is a workplace, and the Coast Guard has not exercised the kind of comprehensive regulatory authority that would trigger preemption under Section 4(b)(1).

**I. Rig 52 Is a “Workplace” Under the Act**

Rig 52 is not a “vessel in navigation.” In *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. 557, 559 (1870), this Court defined a vessel as any contrivance that can be used as a means of transportation on water. Rig 52 was a stationary drilling platform; it was not used for transportation but was moored while drilling an oil well. *Senko v. LaCrosse Dredging Corp.*, 352 U.S. 484, 485 (1957), held that a dredge moored while performing its work was not a vessel in navigation. *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565, 572 (1944), similarly held that a floating workshop permanently affixed to the shore or riverbed is not a vessel. Rig 52, by contrast, was a floating platform whose only function was drilling. It was therefore a land-based workplace subject to OSHA’s jurisdiction.

**II. Section 4(b)(1) Does Not Preempt OSHA Jurisdiction**

Section 4(b)(1) exempts working conditions only where another federal agency actually exercises statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The test is whether the matter is “in any way regulated by the federal government.” *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 236 (1947). The Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited to specific equipment—fire extinguishers, life preservers, ventilation, and similar items. 46 U.S.C. § 4102. It has not regulated well control, blowout prevention, or the general workplace conditions that caused the explosion here. Thus, the Coast Guard has not exercised authority over the conditions at issue.

The lower court’s contrary view rests on the notion that the Coast Guard’s jurisdiction is exclusive. But maritime law is not a complete and perfect system; it is subject to modification by Congress. *The Lottawanna*, 88 U.S. 558, 575 (1874). Congress created OSHA to fill gaps in workplace safety, and exemptions from remedial statutes are construed narrowly. *Federal Land Bank v. Bismarck Lumber Co.*, 314 U.S. 95, 99 (1941). Even if Rig 52’s workers could be considered seamen, their remedies under maritime law do not displace federal safety statutes. *Chelentis v. Luckenbach S.S. Co.*, 247 U.S. 372, 384 (1918). The Coast Guard’s limited regulation of uninspected vessels leaves room for OSHA’s complementary authority.

The judgment below should be reversed.
