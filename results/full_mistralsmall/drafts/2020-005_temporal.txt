**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Due Process Clause Requires Only That the Defendant Have Minimum Contacts with the Forum State Such That the Exercise of Jurisdiction Does Not Offend “Traditional Notions of Fair Play and Substantial Justice.”**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment does not demand that a defendant’s in-state contacts be causally linked to the plaintiff’s claims. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 316 (1945) (“[T]he essential thing is that in each case ‘due process’ is satisfied if the suit is brought to enforce a claim that arises out of or relates to the defendant’s activities in the forum.”). Rather, the constitutional touchstone is whether the defendant has “minimum contacts” with the forum state such that the exercise of jurisdiction comports with “traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.” *Id.* at 316; *Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 463 (1940) (“[T]he authority of the state over one who has left its territory and become subject to the authority of another state does not necessarily terminate; it may continue.”).

Here, Ford has purposefully availed itself of the Montana market by selling and servicing vehicles and parts within the state. *See McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957) (holding that jurisdiction was proper where the insurer had “by the terms of its policy, entered into a continuing relationship with the citizens of California” by mailing renewal notices and accepting premiums). Ford’s systematic and continuous contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts and the provision of warranty and service support—demonstrate that it has purposefully directed its activities toward the state. *See Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643, 647 (1950) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant maintained ongoing contractual relationships with forum residents).

**II. The “Arise Out Of or Relate To” Standard Does Not Require a Causal Nexus Between the Defendant’s Contacts and the Plaintiff’s Claims.**

The modern jurisdictional framework does not require that the plaintiff’s claims be causally connected to the defendant’s forum contacts. *See Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 779-80 (1984) (holding that jurisdiction was proper where the defendant’s forum contacts were “related to the subject matter of the litigation” even though the plaintiff’s claims did not arise from those contacts). Instead, the relevant inquiry is whether the defendant’s forum activities are sufficiently related to the litigation to render jurisdiction reasonable. *See Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 788 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where the defendant’s forum contacts were “purposefully directed” at the forum and the plaintiff’s claims arose from those contacts).

Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts and the provision of warranty and service support—are sufficiently related to the litigation to justify jurisdiction. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980) (holding that jurisdiction is proper where the defendant’s forum contacts are “purposefully directed” at the forum). The fact that the plaintiff’s claims arise from a vehicle sold outside Montana does not defeat jurisdiction, as Ford’s ongoing business activities in Montana provide a sufficient basis for the exercise of jurisdiction. *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 475 (1985) (holding that jurisdiction is proper where the defendant’s forum contacts are “purposefully directed” at the forum and the litigation arises from those contacts).

**III. The Exercise of Jurisdiction Over Ford in Montana Comports with the Principles of Fair Play and Substantial Justice.**

The exercise of jurisdiction over Ford in Montana does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice. *See Asahi Metal Indus. Co. v. Superior Court*, 480 U.S. 102, 113 (1987) (holding that jurisdiction is proper where the defendant’s forum contacts are “purposefully directed” at the forum and the exercise of jurisdiction is reasonable). Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts and the provision of warranty and service support—demonstrate that it has purposefully availed itself of the Montana market. *See McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223 (holding that jurisdiction was proper where the defendant had “entered into a continuing relationship with the citizens of California”).

Moreover, the plaintiff’s claims arise from a vehicle sold and serviced in Montana, and the state has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents. *See Kulko v. Superior Court*, 436 U.S. 84, 98 (1978) (holding that jurisdiction is proper where the defendant’s forum contacts are “purposefully directed” at the forum and the exercise of jurisdiction is reasonable). The exercise of jurisdiction over Ford in Montana will not impose an unreasonable burden on the defendant, as Ford has purposefully availed itself of the Montana market and maintains ongoing business activities in the state. *See World-Wide Volkswagen*, 444 U.S. at 297 (holding that jurisdiction is proper where the defendant’s forum contacts are “purposefully directed” at the forum).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed. Ford’s extensive contacts with Montana—including the sale of replacement parts and the provision of warranty and service support—demonstrate that it has purposefully availed itself of the Montana market and that the exercise of jurisdiction over it in Montana comports with the Due Process Clause.
