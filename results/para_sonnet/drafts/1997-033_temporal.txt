**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text of Section 1407(a) Commands Remand and Forecloses Self-Assignment for Trial**

Section 1407(a) provides that any action transferred to a transferee court for coordinated pretrial proceedings "shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." Congress' use of the word "shall" is not advisory. Where Congress employs mandatory language in a statute governing the jurisdiction and duty of the federal courts, that language must be enforced according to its terms. See *United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (a court's task is "to construe the language so as to give effect to the intent of Congress" as expressed in the words Congress chose); *Caminetti v. United States*, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917) ("It is elementary that the meaning of a statute must, in the first instance, be sought in the language in which the act is framed."). The remand command in § 1407(a) contains no exception for a transferee judge who prefers to retain the case, and none should be read into it.

The statutory design confirms this reading. Section 1407(a) grants the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation authority to transfer actions only "for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings." The temporal and functional limits are explicit: the power conferred is a power over *pretrial* proceedings, terminating upon their conclusion, at which point remand is compulsory. A transferee court's invocation of § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial converts a temporary custodial power over pretrial matters into a permanent power over the litigation as a whole—precisely the result the mandatory remand clause was designed to prevent.

**II. Section 1407(a)'s Specific and Mandatory Command Controls Over the General Venue-Transfer Authority of Section 1404(a)**

Even assuming § 1404(a) supplies a transferee court with venue-transfer power in the abstract, that general grant of authority cannot be read to override the specific and mandatory remand requirement of § 1407(a). This Court has long held that where a specific statutory provision addresses a subject, a more general provision must yield. In *Fourco Glass Co. v. Transmirra Products Corp.*, 353 U.S. 222 (1957), the Court held that the specific patent-venue statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1400(b), was "not to be supplemented" by the general venue provisions of § 1391(c), even though the latter's text might otherwise have applied. The same principle governs here: § 1407(a) is the specific provision Congress drafted to govern multidistrict transfers, complete with its own transfer and remand mechanism; § 1404(a) is the general transfer provision applicable to ordinary civil litigation. The specific statute must control.

This Court reached an analogous conclusion in *Ex parte Collett*, 337 U.S. 55 (1949), where it considered whether the general transfer provisions of § 1404(a) could be read to override or supplant more specific venue arrangements Congress had enacted for particular categories of suits. The Court's willingness to harmonize the general transfer statute with more specific legislative arrangements—rather than allow the general statute to swallow the specific one—confirms that § 1404(a) was never meant to be deployed to defeat the particular structure Congress imposed on multidistrict transfers.

**III. Permitting Self-Assignment Renders the Remand Command Mere Surplusage**

It is a fundamental canon that courts must "give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute." *United States v. Menasche*, 348 U.S. 528, 538–39 (1955); *Montclair v. Ramsdell*, 107 U.S. 147, 152 (1883) (a statute must be construed so that no clause is rendered "insignificant [or] void"). If a transferee court may simply invoke § 1404(a) to transfer the case to itself whenever it concludes that venue there is convenient, the mandatory remand clause of § 1407(a) becomes a dead letter in every case in which the transferee judge wishes to retain jurisdiction. Congress cannot be presumed to have enacted a command that transferee courts are free to evade through the mechanical device of transferring a case to the very district in which they already sit. Such a construction does not harmonize the two statutes; it eliminates one of them.

**IV. The Structure of Section 1404(a) Itself Confirms That It Governs a Distinct Judgment About Trial Venue, Not a Vehicle for Retaining Cases Assigned Only for Pretrial Coordination**

Section 1404(a) exists to permit transfer of a case, based on considerations of convenience and justice, from the district where it was properly brought to another district where it might also have been brought. This Court's decisions applying § 1404(a) presuppose a live plaintiff's choice of forum being weighed against competing convenience factors in the ordinary course of litigation. See *Van Dusen v. Barrack*, 376 U.S. 612, 616 (1964); *Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert*, 330 U.S. 501, 508 (1947) (recognizing that "a plaintiff's choice of forum should rarely be disturbed"). Nothing in that framework contemplates a transferee court invoking § 1404(a) against itself, as both transferor and transferee, to entrench a venue selected not by the plaintiff or by ordinary venue principles, but by the fortuity of multidistrict assignment for pretrial coordination alone. Section 1407(a)'s mandatory remand provision preserves precisely the plaintiff's forum choice that § 1404(a) elsewhere protects.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
