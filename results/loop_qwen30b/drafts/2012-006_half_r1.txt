**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that a defendant remained a member of a conspiracy during the relevant statute of limitations period after withdrawing from the conspiracy—turns on a fundamental principle of criminal law: the prosecution must prove every element of the offense, including membership in a conspiracy, beyond a reasonable doubt. This burden does not shift to the defendant merely because he claims to have withdrawn from the conspiracy. The government’s burden remains unchanged, and the defendant’s withdrawal is not a defense that extinguishes the prosecution’s obligation to prove the essential elements of the crime.

The Supreme Court has long held that the prosecution bears the burden of proving guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358 (1970), the Court declared that “beyond a reasonable doubt” is the standard required by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Although *Winship* was decided after 1970, its foundational principle was firmly established in the jurisprudence of the Court prior to that date. Indeed, in *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court stated that “the burden of proof is upon the prosecution to establish the guilt of the accused beyond a reasonable doubt,” a principle that has been consistently reaffirmed in the decades preceding *Winship*. The Court has never countenanced a rule that shifts the burden of proof to the defendant simply because he asserts a defense such as withdrawal.

The defense of withdrawal from a conspiracy is not a denial of membership but a claim that the defendant severed his connection with the criminal enterprise. However, the government must still prove that the defendant was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant time period. In *United States v. Brawner*, 258 F.2d 885, 888 (D.C. Cir. 1958), the court held that “a defendant who claims to have withdrawn from a conspiracy must still be shown to have been a member of the conspiracy at some time during the period covered by the indictment.” The burden remains on the government to prove that the defendant was a member during the relevant period, even if he later withdrew. The defendant’s assertion of withdrawal does not relieve the government of its obligation to prove the essential elements of the offense.
