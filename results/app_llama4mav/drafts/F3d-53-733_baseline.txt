## Argument

### I. The District Court Erred in Allowing American Steamship's Expert to Testify as to the Cause of the Line's Parting

The district court abused its discretion when it permitted American Steamship's expert, [expert's name], to offer his opinion on the cause of the line's parting. The expert's testimony was not based on specialized knowledge, skill, or experience, and therefore, did not meet the requirements for expert testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 702. See Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael, 526 U.S. 137, 147-149 (1999); Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 592-593 (1993). The expert's opinion was merely a speculative conclusion that was not grounded in any scientific or technical expertise. As such, it was not "expert testimony" and should have been excluded. See Berry v. City of Detroit, 810 F.2d 1482, 1488-1489 (6th Cir. 1987) (holding that expert testimony must be based on more than mere speculation or conjecture).

### II. The District Court Erred in Denying Cook's Motion for Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Issue of Unseaworthiness

The district court should have granted Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness because the evidence presented at trial established that the vessel was unseaworthy as a matter of law. A vessel is considered unseaworthy if it lacks proper equipment or if its equipment is not properly maintained. See Mitchell v. Trawler Racer, Inc., 362 U.S. 539, 550 (1960). The evidence showed that the line supporting the bos'n's chair was not properly maintained, as it had been left rigged during the summer months and was not inspected or replaced. Furthermore, the crew's failure to change the grease in the boom's bearings from summer to winter grease rendered the boom unfit for its intended purpose. See Boudoin v. Lykes Bros. S.S. Co., 348 U.S. 336, 339-340 (1955) (holding that a vessel is unseaworthy if its equipment is not reasonably fit for its intended purpose). As the evidence established that the vessel was unseaworthy, the district court erred in denying Cook's motion.

### III. The District Court Did Not Err in Admitting Evidence of Cook's Alcohol-Related Ailments

The district court did not abuse its discretion when it allowed American Steamship to introduce evidence of Cook's alcohol-related ailments. The evidence was relevant to Cook's damages claim, as it related to his overall health and potential future medical expenses. See Fed. R. Evid. 401. The evidence was also probative of Cook's credibility, as it suggested that his injuries may have been exacerbated by his pre-existing condition. See United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45, 51-52 (1984) (holding that evidence of a witness's bias or motive to lie is relevant to their credibility). Therefore, the district court did not err in admitting this evidence.

### IV. The Errors Require a New Trial

The district court's errors in admitting the expert's testimony and denying Cook's motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness require a new trial. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 59(a). The jury's verdict was likely influenced by the erroneous admission of the expert's testimony, and the failure to grant judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness may have affected the jury's assessment of damages. Therefore, a new trial is necessary to ensure that Cook receives a fair trial.
