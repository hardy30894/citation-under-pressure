Moran’s plea and waiver of counsel were valid if, and only if, he possessed the competence required by the Constitution to make those decisions. But the Due Process Clause does not compel a special “reasoned choice” formulation that is higher than the standard for competence announced by this Court. The controlling question is whether the defendant has sufficient present ability to consult with counsel and a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings. Dusky v. United States, 362 U.S. 402, 402 (1960) (per curiam); Drope v. Missouri, 420 U.S. 162, 171-72 (1975); Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389, 396-99 (1993).

This Court has repeatedly rejected the notion that competence depends on the gravity of the decision to be made. In Dusky, the Court established a single constitutional standard for competence to stand trial. 362 U.S. at 402. In Drope, the Court reaffirmed that standard, explaining that the inquiry is whether the defendant can understand the proceedings and assist counsel. 420 U.S. at 171-72. Nothing in those cases suggests a distinct constitutional test for a defendant who chooses to plead guilty or forgo counsel. To the contrary, the Court has recognized that the competence inquiry is a “minimal” one and focuses on the defendant’s capacity, not on whether the decision itself appears wise. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. at 399-400.

The Ninth Circuit’s “reasoned choice” approach cannot be squared with this Court’s precedents. In Godinez v. Moran, this Court rejected the view that competence to plead guilty or waive counsel requires a higher level of mental functioning than competence to stand trial. 509 U.S. at 397-98. The Court explained that the Constitution does not demand a separate standard for “making decisions” because the question of competence is distinct from whether a waiver is knowing and voluntary. Id. at 400-01. A defendant may be competent under Dusky, yet still fail to make a valid waiver if the record does not show that the waiver was knowing and voluntary. Id. at 400. The proper safeguard, therefore, is not a heightened competency threshold, but the ordinary requirement that the trial court ensure a knowing, voluntary, and intelligent waiver. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 U.S. 238, 242-44 (1969); Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 464-65 (1938).

That distinction answers Moran’s claim. The Due Process Clause requires the court to determine competence under the Dusky standard, and then separately to determine whether the plea or waiver is knowing and voluntary. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. at 401-02. It does not authorize courts to collapse those inquiries into a more demanding “reasoned choice” test. Indeed, such a test would improperly turn competence into a function of the decision’s complexity, even though competence under Dusky has never depended on the decision at issue being easy or difficult. Id. at 397-400; Rees v. Peyton, 384 U.S. 312, 314 (1966) (per curiam) (asking whether the defendant has the capacity to appreciate his position and make a rational choice).

Moran also cannot rely on the notion that a more exacting standard is required in capital cases. The Court has made clear that the Constitution does not impose a different competency threshold merely because the consequences are grave. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. at 398-99. The Eighth Amendment may require particular procedures at sentencing, but it does not alter the basic due process standard for competence to plead guilty or waive counsel. Id.; see also Sieling v. Eyman, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973), overruled by Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993).

Finally, the record here does not support a constitutional rule that trial judges must search for some extra layer of “reasoned choice” whenever a defendant seeks to plead guilty or proceed without counsel. The Constitution requires a defendant be competent, and that his waiver be knowing and voluntary. Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. at 400-03. It does not require a heightened standard untethered to Dusky. Because the Ninth Circuit imposed that extra requirement, its judgment cannot stand. See Godinez v. Moran, 509 U.S. 389 (1993).
