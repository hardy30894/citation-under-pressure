**Argument**

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars the State from making repeated attempts to obtain an enhanced punishment after it has already failed to prove the factual allegation on which that enhancement depends. U.S. Const. amend. V; *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794-96 (1969); *North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). That principle is not limited to the guilt phase of a criminal prosecution. Once the State has chosen to litigate a sentencing allegation and the tribunal has resolved that allegation adversely to the prosecution, the defendant has been placed in jeopardy on that issue, and the Constitution forbids a second prosecution of the same allegation. *United States v. Ball*, 163 U.S. 662, 671-72 (1896); *United States v. Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. 85, 87-88 (1916); *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187-88 (1957).

This Court’s double jeopardy cases have always focused on substance, not labels. The Clause protects against “a second prosecution for the same offense after acquittal.” *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Benton*, 395 U.S. at 795. It also protects against the “embarrassment, expense and ordeal” of repeated prosecutions and against the State’s “repeated attempts to convict an individual.” *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671. Those concerns are fully present when the State seeks to prove a prior conviction allegation that will add years to a defendant’s prison term. The State has one fair opportunity to establish the allegation, and if it fails, it may not try again. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 128-29 (1904); *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 173-74 (1873).

The State will argue that the proceeding here was “sentencing,” not a trial. But the Double Jeopardy Clause does not turn on that characterization. A proceeding that determines whether the defendant will receive a substantially greater punishment because of a specific historical fact is a prosecution of that fact in everything but name. This Court has long recognized that a judgment on a factual issue, once finally made, cannot be relitigated by the sovereign. *Oppenheimer*, 242 U.S. at 87-88; *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72. The State may not avoid the Constitution by relabeling an adjudication as part of sentencing when the practical effect is to retry the same allegation.

Nor is there any constitutional basis for limiting double jeopardy protection to capital sentencing. The Fifth Amendment speaks broadly: no person shall be “twice put in jeopardy of life or limb.” U.S. Const. amend. V. This Court has never said that the Clause protects against successive litigation of a factual predicate for punishment only when death is at stake. To the contrary, the Court’s cases teach that the key question is whether the defendant has already been exposed to a final adverse determination on the same offense or issue. *Ball*, 163 U.S. at 671-72; *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 128-29; *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88. That rule applies with equal force here, where the prior conviction allegation is the sole basis for doubling petitioner’s sentence.

The California Supreme Court’s contrary rule creates the very evil the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids. If the prosecution’s evidence is insufficient the first time, the State may simply regroup and try again until it secures the enhancement. That invites precisely the governmental overreaching the Clause was adopted to prevent. *Green*, 355 U.S. at 187-88; *Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717; *Sanges v. United States*, 144 U.S. 310, 312-13 (1892). The Constitution does not permit a second attempt at the same prior conviction allegation after the first has failed. The judgment below should therefore be reversed.
