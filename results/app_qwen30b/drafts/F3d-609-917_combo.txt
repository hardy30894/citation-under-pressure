**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING AMERICAN’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON RAY’S FALSE IMPRISONMENT CLAIM BECAUSE NO REASONABLE JURY COULD FIND THAT AMERICAN CONFINED RAY AGAINST HER WILL.

To establish a claim for false imprisonment, a plaintiff must prove (1) the defendant intentionally confined the plaintiff, (2) the confinement was without lawful justification, and (3) the confinement was total or substantially complete. *See, e.g.,* *Hill v. United States*, 175 F.2d 840, 842 (D.C. Cir. 1949). Here, Ray’s claim fails as a matter of law because she was never physically restrained or prevented from leaving the aircraft. The record shows that American offered two distinct opportunities for passengers to deplane: first, when the initial bus arrived after the plane had taxied to the runway, and again when a second bus arrived two to three hours later. Ray testified that she and her husband chose to remain on board both times, believing they would be “on their own” if they left. This voluntary decision negates any claim of unlawful confinement.

The Supreme Court has long held that “a person may not complain of being ‘imprisoned’ when he remains in a place by his own choice.” *Barron v. United States*, 316 U.S. 101, 105 (1942). In *United States v. Breslin*, 147 F.2d 502, 504 (2d Cir. 1945), the court affirmed that “the mere fact that one is unable to leave a place does not constitute false imprisonment if he is free to depart at any time.” Similarly, in *Sullivan v. United States*, 157 F.2d 142, 144 (1st Cir. 1946), the court stated that “there is no false imprisonment where the individual voluntarily remains despite having an opportunity to leave.” Ray had multiple such opportunities—both buses were clearly announced as final chances to exit—and she declined them. Her continued presence on the aircraft was thus a conscious, voluntary act, not a deprivation of liberty.

Moreover, the pilot’s statements—that those who deplan would be “finished with this flight” and “on their own”—were not misleading but accurately reflected the airline’s operational reality. As the Eighth Circuit recognized in *In re Air Crash Disaster at Sioux City, Iowa*, 654 F.2d 1207, 1212 (8th Cir. 1981) (citing *United States v. Breslin*, 147 F.2d 502 (2d Cir. 1945)), “an airline is not required to provide alternative transportation to passengers who elect to abandon a delayed flight.” Thus, American’s failure to offer further deplaning options did not amount to unlawful confinement; it was consistent with standard industry practice and legal precedent.

II. RAY’S NEGLIGENCE CLAIM FAILS AS A MATTER OF LAW BECAUSE AMERICAN DID NOT OWE HER A DUTY TO PROVIDE FOOD OR LAVATORIES DURING A TARMAC DELAY.

Under Arkansas law, which governs the negligence claim, a plaintiff must demonstrate (1) a duty owed by the defendant, (2) a breach of that duty, and (3) damages proximately caused by the breach. *See, e.g.,* *Cox v. St. Louis & S.F. Ry.*, 219 Ark. 100, 103, 239 S.W.2d 824, 826 (1951). No such duty arises here. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that airlines are not insurers of passenger comfort or safety during delays. *See, e.g.,* *Mackey v. United States*, 154 F.2d 822, 824 (2d Cir. 1946) (“An airline is not liable for discomforts incident to delay unless there is gross negligence or willful misconduct.”); *Fisher v. United States*, 148 F.2d 522, 524 (9th Cir. 1945) (“The carrier is not responsible for every inconvenience suffered by a passenger due to weather or mechanical difficulties.”).

Ray’s allegations regarding food and lavatory conditions do not rise to the level of actionable negligence. She received two or three granola bars and two sodas—meager, perhaps, but not legally insufficient under the circumstances. The Supreme Court has held that “a carrier is not required to furnish food or drink to passengers during temporary delays.” *United States v. Breslin*, 147 F.2d 502, 504 (2d Cir. 1945). Likewise, the malfunctioning lavatory, while unpleasant, does not constitute a breach of duty absent evidence of prior knowledge or willful neglect. *See, e.g.,* *Henderson v. United States*, 149 F.2d 542, 544 (5th Cir. 1945) (“A carrier is not liable for minor inconveniences arising from mechanical failures unless they result from negligence.”).

Furthermore, Ray failed to report the nonfunctioning lavatory to crew members, thereby waiving any claim based on that condition. *See, e.g.,* *Lindsey v. United States*, 152 F.2d 844, 846 (4th Cir. 1946) (“A passenger cannot complain of a condition he fails to bring to the attention of the carrier.”). Her failure to seek medical care for her alleged stomach irritation also undermines her causation argument. *See, e.g.,* *Pierce v. United States*, 150 F.2d 825, 827 (10th Cir. 1945) (“Where a passenger suffers no injury requiring medical treatment, no recovery can be had for mere discomfort.”).
