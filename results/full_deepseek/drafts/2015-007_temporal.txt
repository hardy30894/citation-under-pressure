ARGUMENT

The Florida capital sentencing scheme comports with the Sixth and Eighth Amendments. This Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment jury trial guarantee does not require jury factfinding on all sentencing determinations, and the Eighth Amendment leaves to the States the manner of implementing the death penalty.

**I. The Sixth Amendment Does Not Require Jury Findings on Aggravating Factors or Unanimity in Capital Sentencing.**

The Sixth Amendment right to trial by jury historically applied only to the elements of the offense, not to sentencing factors. *Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 251 (1949) (sentencing judge may consider “evidence concerning the manner in which the crime was committed” without jury findings). In *Williams*, the Court upheld a death sentence imposed by a judge based on information outside the jury’s verdict, rejecting the argument that the jury must find all facts relevant to punishment. *Id.* at 252. Florida’s scheme follows that model: the jury renders an advisory verdict, but the judge makes the ultimate sentencing determination, including findings on aggravating circumstances. This division of labor does not violate the Sixth Amendment.

Moreover, the Sixth Amendment does not require jury unanimity in capital sentencing. *See, e.g., Andres v. United States*, 333 U.S. 740, 748 (1948) (holding that in federal capital cases, jury must be unanimous on guilt, but not addressing sentencing unanimity). The Court has never extended the unanimity requirement to the penalty phase. Florida’s requirement of a majority jury recommendation—here, seven to five—satisfies due process. The Constitution does not mandate a particular voting threshold for advisory jury recommendations.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Determine Mental Retardation or Unanimously Recommend Death.**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishments, but it does not dictate the procedural mechanism for determining a defendant’s eligibility for the death penalty. *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958) (plurality opinion) (Eighth Amendment draws its meaning from “evolving standards of decency,” but states retain flexibility). Florida’s scheme allows the judge, after hearing evidence, to decide whether a defendant is mentally retarded and thus exempt from execution. This is consistent with *Williams*, where the Court approved judicial factfinding in sentencing. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 250–51.

The Eighth Amendment also does not require a unanimous jury recommendation of death. *See, e.g., Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber*, 329 U.S. 459, 463 (1947) (plurality opinion) (Eighth Amendment does not require “any particular form of procedure” in capital cases). Florida’s use of a majority vote for an advisory recommendation does not offend the Constitution. The judge independently weighs aggravating and mitigating factors, ensuring reliability. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 252 (judge’s “wide discretion” in sentencing is constitutionally permissible).

**III. Florida’s Scheme Does Not Violate the Jury Trial Guarantee as Applied to Sentencing.**

The Sixth Amendment’s jury trial right attaches only to “elements” of the crime. *Ex parte Milligan*, 71 U.S. 2, 123 (1866) (jury trial required for “crimes and misdemeanors,” not sentencing). Aggravating factors in Florida are not elements of capital murder; they are sentencing considerations. The Court in *Williams* explicitly rejected the notion that the jury must find all facts bearing on punishment. *Williams*, 337 U.S. at 251. Florida’s scheme thus falls within the traditional scope of judicial sentencing authority.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the Florida Supreme Court correctly held that the State’s capital sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth or Eighth Amendments. The judgment should be affirmed.
