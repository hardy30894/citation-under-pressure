**ARGUMENT**

**I. Mrs. Parker's Convictions Under § 201(b)(2)(C) Must Be Reversed Because Her Forged Approvals Were Not "Official Acts" Performed Within Any Place of Trust.**

Section 201(b)(2)(C) reaches only acts that a public official performs "in such official's official capacity, or in such official's place of trust." 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3). This Court has recognized that duties may be defined by "established usage" as well as by written regulation, because "duties not completely defined by written rules are established by settled practice, and action taken in the course of their performance must be regarded as within the provisions of the above-mentioned statutes against bribery." *United States v. Birdsall*, 233 U.S. 223, 231, 34 S.Ct. 512 (1914). But *Birdsall*'s "settled practice" doctrine presupposes that the official actually occupies a position from which the challenged conduct could plausibly flow — it does not convert every act of a low-level clerk into an official act merely because she works inside a federal office. See *Dixson v. United States*, 465 U.S. 482, 496 (1984) (public-official status under § 201 turns on whether the person is "entrusted with a portion of the government's sovereign powers").

Mrs. Parker had no authority — actual, apparent, or by settled practice — to approve or deny SSI claims. Her duties were clerical: assisting at hearings, taking notes, and typing and mailing ALJ Aronson's decisions. Forging his signature on counterfeit approval letters was not an exercise of any function ever "brought before" her "in such official's official capacity," 18 U.S.C. § 201(a)(3); it was usurpation of a decision-making authority she never possessed. Courts requiring a nexus between the bribe and the official's actual sphere of duty have declined to stretch § 201 to cover conduct entirely outside any color of authority. See *United States v. Rabbitt*, 583 F.2d 1014, 1025-26 (8th Cir. 1978) (bribery statute requires a connection between the payment and the official's actual duties). The government's reliance on *United States v. Gjieli*, 717 F.2d 968, 972 (6th Cir. 1983), for the proposition that specific authority is unnecessary proves too much: Gjieli involved an officer with genuine, if limited, decision-making power, not a clerk who fabricated documents from whole cloth.

Bribery statutes further require proof of a quid pro quo tied to the official's actual power to act. *McCormick v. United States*, 500 U.S. 257, 273 (1991) (payments must be made in return for an "explicit promise or undertaking" by the official to perform an official act). Because Mrs. Parker could not lawfully perform the "official act" for which she was paid, the required quid pro quo linking payment to genuine official power is absent, and the convictions under § 201(b)(2)(C) cannot stand.

**II. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Convict Mr. Parker of Conspiracy.**

A conspiracy conviction requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt of an agreement between two or more persons to violate the law, and knowing, voluntary participation in that agreement. *Direct Sales Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 703, 711 (1943). Mere presence at the scene of a crime, or association with conspirators, is not enough to sustain a conspiracy conviction. *United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205, 210 (1940) (knowledge alone, without an intent to further the illegal enterprise, cannot establish participation in a conspiracy). This Circuit likewise requires more than presence and opportunity. *United States v. Jackson*, 700 F.2d 181, 185 (5th Cir. 1983); *United States v. Maltos*, 985 F.2d 743, 746 (5th Cir. 1992).

The record shows only that Mr. Parker accompanied his wife to Simmons's home and that the two of them accepted $500 on videotape. There is no evidence that Mr. Parker knew of the underlying scheme to falsify SSI decisions, negotiated any payment, or performed any act to further the conspiracy beyond his presence at a single meeting. Such evidence, at most, establishes association, not agreement, and cannot support a conspiracy conviction consistent with *Falcone* and *Jackson*.

**III. Mrs. Parker's Post-Arrest Statement Should Have Been Suppressed as Involuntary.**

Even where warnings are given and a waiver signed, a confession must still be the product of a free and voluntary choice; *Miranda v. Arizona*, 384 U.S. 436, 444 (1966), guarantees only the opportunity to exercise the privilege against self-incrimination, not the substance of a voluntary statement. Voluntariness turns on whether "coercive police activity is a necessary predicate to the finding that a confession is not 'voluntary.'" *Colorado v. Connelly*, 479 U.S. 157, 167 (1986). The circumstances of Mrs. Parker's interrogation — a surprise confrontation by federal agents who had already built a case against her through recorded calls and video surveillance, followed by an immediate, sweeping confession implicating herself, her husband, and two coworkers — warrant an evidentiary hearing on whether the waiver was the product of overreaching rather than free will, a hearing the district court did not adequately afford.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse Mrs. Parker's convictions under § 201(b)(2)(C), reverse Mr. Parker's conspiracy conviction for insufficient evidence, and remand for a hearing on the voluntariness of Mrs. Parker's statement.
