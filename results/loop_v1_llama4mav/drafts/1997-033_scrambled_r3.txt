The plain language of 28 USC section 1407(a) mandates that the Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation "shall" remand any action transferred for pretrial proceedings to the original district "at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings." The statute's clear directive is reinforced by a long line of cases emphasizing the importance of adhering to the statutory text. See, e.g., United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co., 287 U.S. 77, 83 (1932) ("The plain meaning of the statute cannot be overcome by speculation as to the probable intent of the legislature.").

The District of Arizona's invocation of section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial contravenes the statutory scheme established by section 1407(a). The specific mandate of section 1407(a) takes precedence over the general transfer authority granted by section 1404(a), as is consistent with the principle that specific statutes control over general ones. See, e.g., D. Ginsberg & Sons v. Popkin, 285 U.S. 204 (1932); United States v. Hill, 123 U.S. 681, 686 (1887) (specific provisions govern over general ones).

The Ninth Circuit's decision in the instant case, as well as the Second Circuit's holding in cases that have permitted transferee courts to invoke section 1404(a) to retain cases for trial, are inconsistent with the statutory text and the principles of statutory construction. In Ginsberg, the Court held that "where there are two provisions, one specific and the other general, covering the same subject matter, the former controls." 285 U.S. at 208. Similarly, in this case, the specific mandate of section 1407(a) should control over the general transfer authority granted by section 1404(a).

Furthermore, the practice of allowing transferee courts to retain cases for trial under section 1404(a) undermines the policy choice reflected in section 1407(a), which is to limit the transfer of cases to pretrial proceedings and preserve the plaintiff's traditional right to select the forum for trial. See, e.g., Robertson v. Railroad Labor Board, 268 U.S. 619, 622-23 (1925) (emphasizing the importance of statutory provisions governing the jurisdiction of courts).

The Supreme Court has consistently held that statutory provisions should be interpreted in a manner that gives effect to their plain meaning. See, e.g., United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. 76, 95-96 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) ("The intention of the legislature is to be collected from the words they employ."); FTC v. Bunte Bros., 312 U.S. 349, 352-53 (1941) (noting that "the plain words of the statute" should be given effect).

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the District of Arizona erred in invoking section 1404(a) to assign the transferred case to itself for trial, and the Ninth Circuit's affirmance of that decision was incorrect. The plain language of section 1407(a) and the principles of statutory construction require that the case be remanded to the original district for trial.
