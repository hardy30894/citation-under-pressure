**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien beyond the 90-day removal period. This Court construes statutes to avoid serious constitutional doubts when fairly possible. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932); United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366, 407-08 (1909). Section 1231(a)(6) admits such a reading by limiting detention to a period reasonably necessary to effect removal.

Indefinite detention raises grave due-process concerns. The Fifth Amendment protects “all persons within the territory of the United States,” Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886), including resident aliens, Mathews v. Diaz, 426 U.S. 67, 77 (1976). Civil detention must be justified by a “special justification” and must not be “excessive in relation to the purpose” served. Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997); Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992). Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention serves no legitimate purpose and becomes punitive.

The statute’s text and structure confirm the limitation. Section 1231(a)(6) permits detention “pending removal” and subjects released aliens to supervision under paragraph (3). These provisions presuppose an eventual end to detention. See United States v. Witkovich, 353 U.S. 194, 199 (1957) (construing predecessor statute to avoid constitutional questions). The six-month presumptively reasonable period recognized in the lower courts aligns with this purpose and permits individualized review.

Habeas corpus supplies the appropriate remedy. Federal courts retain authority under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 to review the legality of detention. Felker v. Turpin, 518 U.S. 651, 663-64 (1996); Swain v. Pressley, 430 U.S. 372, 381 (1977). The writ tests whether detention exceeds statutory authority, INS v. St. Cyr, 533 U.S. 289, 301 (2001), and whether removal remains reasonably foreseeable, Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Comm., 525 U.S. 471, 482 (1999).

The Fifth Circuit’s “good faith efforts” standard would permit detention until removal becomes literally impossible, a result the statute cannot bear. Because Zadvydas has shown no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, continued detention is unauthorized. The judgment ordering his release should be affirmed.
