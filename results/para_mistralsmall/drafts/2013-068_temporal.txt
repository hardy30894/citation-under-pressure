**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation under settled First Amendment principles.**

The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to communicate at all times or in all places. *Cox v. New Hampshire*, 312 U.S. 569, 575–76 (1941). Rather, it permits reasonable regulation of the “time, place, and manner” of speech in public forums, so long as such regulations are (1) content-neutral, (2) narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and (3) leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *See Schneider v. State*, 308 U.S. 147, 160–61 (1939); *Cantwell v. Connecticut*, 310 U.S. 296, 304–05 (1940).

The Massachusetts statute satisfies each of these requirements. It is content-neutral, as it applies equally to all speakers regardless of viewpoint. *See Cox*, 312 U.S. at 576 (upholding a permit requirement for parades without regard to the message conveyed). It advances the significant governmental interest of ensuring safe and unimpeded access to medical facilities. *See id.* at 577 (recognizing the state’s interest in maintaining public order). And it leaves open ample alternative channels for communication, including speech outside the 35-foot buffer zone and the distribution of literature to willing listeners. *See Schneider*, 308 U.S. at 163 (finding alternatives adequate where speech could occur elsewhere).

The First Circuit correctly applied these principles in upholding the statute. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176–81 (1st Cir. 2009), *aff’d*, 134 S. Ct. 2518 (2014). This Court has long deferred to legislative judgments in balancing speech rights against public safety concerns. *See Cox*, 312 U.S. at 574–77.

**II. Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), is controlling and does not warrant overruling.**

The petitioners argue that *Hill* should be limited or overruled, but that decision is indistinguishable from the present case. *Hill* upheld a Colorado statute prohibiting certain activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances, finding it a valid time, place, and manner regulation. *Id.* at 715–20. The Court expressly relied on *Cox* and *Schneider*, concluding that the statute was content-neutral and left open ample alternatives for communication. *Id.* at 719–20.

The Massachusetts statute is materially identical. It restricts speech only within a 35-foot buffer zone around clinic entrances, applies equally to all speakers, and leaves open ample alternatives outside the zone. *See McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 176–81. The petitioners’ attempt to distinguish *Hill* on the ground that the Colorado statute was broader is unavailing. *Hill* itself rejected a similar argument, noting that the statute’s reach did not render it unconstitutional. 530 U.S. at 720.

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly declined to overrule its prior decisions absent a clear break in precedent. *See, e.g., Helvering v. Hallock*, 309 U.S. 106, 119–20 (1940). *Hill* does not represent such a break; it merely applied settled First Amendment principles to a specific factual context. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715–20.

**III. The statute does not constitute an unlawful prior restraint or viewpoint discrimination.**

The petitioners contend that the statute is an unlawful prior restraint because it restricts speech before it occurs. *See Near v. Minnesota*, 283 U.S. 697, 712–13 (1931). But the statute is not a prior restraint; it is a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation. *See Cox*, 312 U.S. at 575–76. Unlike a prior restraint, it does not require a permit or pre-approval of speech. *Compare id.*, with *Near*, 283 U.S. at 712–13.

The petitioners also argue that the statute discriminates against their viewpoint, but the statute is viewpoint-neutral. *See Cox*, 312 U.S. at 576. It applies equally to all speakers, regardless of their message. *See id.* The First Circuit correctly rejected this claim, noting that the statute does not target any particular viewpoint. *McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 176–81.

**Conclusion**

The Massachusetts buffer-zone statute is a valid time, place, and manner regulation that satisfies the First Amendment. It is indistinguishable from the statute upheld in *Hill*, and it does not constitute an unlawful prior restraint or viewpoint discrimination. The judgment below should be affirmed.
