**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Reinstatement Statute Is Impermissibly Retroactive**

The reinstatement statute, 8 U.S.C. § 241(a)(5), is retroactive and violates the principle that Congress may not alter the rights of aliens retroactively. Under *Landgraf v. USI Film Products*, 511 U.S. 244 (1994), a statute will not be applied retroactively if it "substantially alters the legal landscape" or "impair[s] the defendant's ability to defend against the charge." 511 U.S. at 269. The reinstatement statute strips aliens of the ability to adjust their status, a right they possessed under the pre-1997 legal regime. By applying the statute to orders issued before 1997, Congress has retroactively altered the legal landscape for Petitioner, who re-entered the country in 1982.

**II. Petitioner Possessed a "Protectable Expectation" of Relief**

Petitioner possessed a "protectable expectation" of relief, which is a necessary element of a claim of retroactive application. In *INS v. Pang*, 506 U.S. 238 (1993), the Supreme Court held that a "protectable expectation" of relief is a necessary element of a claim of retroactive application of a statute. 506 U.S. at 244. At the time Petitioner re-entered the United States, 8 U.S.C. § 1255(a) allowed an alien who had re-entered illegally to adjust status if married to a U.S. citizen. This right was a "substantial right" that Petitioner could reasonably expect to retain. The reinstatement statute destroys this expectation, violating the principle that Congress may not retroactively alter the rights of aliens. See *INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 329 (2001).
