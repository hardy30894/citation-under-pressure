**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S COMMON LAW CLAIM FOR BREACH OF CONTRACT BECAUSE THE SETTLEMENT AGREEMENT CREATED A BINDING OBLIGATION THAT WELLS FARGO FAILED TO PERFORM.

The settlement agreement between Wells Fargo and the class of Pick-a-Payment borrowers, including Foley, constitutes a valid and enforceable contract under Massachusetts law. *See* *Barnes v. Boston & Maine R.R.*, 289 Mass. 555, 557 (1935) (“A contract is a promise or set of promises for the breach of which the law gives a remedy, or the performance of which the law in some way recognizes as a duty.”). The agreement explicitly required Wells Fargo to consider Settlement Class B members—such as Foley—for a HAMP modification *before* initiating foreclosure. The bank’s failure to comply with this obligation, despite repeated requests from Foley, constitutes a clear breach of contract.

The complaint alleges that Wells Fargo promised Foley in April 2011 that it would send him a HAMP application, and that it did not do so until November 2011—seven months later. *See* *Hartford Fire Ins. Co. v. United States*, 312 U.S. 568, 573 (1941) (“A party who has made a promise to another, and who has induced the other to act upon it, cannot later deny the existence of the promise when it is to his advantage to do so.”). The delay in sending the application, coupled with the bank’s continued misrepresentation that HAMP was the only available option, demonstrates a failure to perform a contractual duty. Moreover, the agreement’s “servicing commitments” required Wells Fargo to provide clear, written explanations of denials and to notify borrowers that they were still under consideration for modification. *See* *Barnes*, 289 Mass. at 557. The bank’s failure to provide such notice, and its issuance of foreclosure notices while simultaneously failing to process Foley’s application, violates these express terms.

The district court erred in dismissing this claim on the ground that the settlement agreement was not enforceable. *See* *Hartford Fire*, 312 U.S. at 573. The agreement was not a mere “gentleman’s agreement” but a binding contract approved by a federal court and subject to judicial enforcement. *See* *Barnes*, 289 Mass. at 557. The fact that the agreement was part of a class action settlement does not diminish its enforceability. *See* *Curtis v. United States*, 302 U.S. 102, 105 (1937) (“A settlement agreement, when approved by a court, becomes a part of the judgment and is enforceable as such.”). The district court’s dismissal of this claim, therefore, was error.

II. FOLEY’S STATUTORY CLAIM UNDER MASS. GEN. LAWS CH. 244, § 15, IS NOT PREEMPTED BY FEDERAL LAW AND MUST BE ALLOWED TO PROCEED.

Wells Fargo argues that Foley’s statutory claim under Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 244, § 15—prohibiting unfair or deceptive acts in the conduct of business—is preempted by federal law. This argument fails. *See* *Hartford Fire*, 312 U.S. at 573. The doctrine of preemption does not apply where state law “does not conflict with federal law, nor does it stand as an obstacle to the accomplishment and execution of the full purposes and objectives of Congress.” *See* *Hill v. Wallace*, 259 U.S. 44, 52 (1922). Here, the state law complements federal policy by ensuring that borrowers are treated fairly during the modification process. The federal Home Affordable Modification Program (HAMP) was designed to *prevent* foreclosures, not to excuse banks from their obligations to consider borrowers. *See* *United States v. Southwestern Cable Co.*, 392 U.S. 157, 164 (1968) (“Congress does not intend to displace state law unless it clearly appears that it has done so.”).

Moreover, the Massachusetts statute is not inconsistent with federal law. *See* *Hill*, 259 U.S. at 52. The state law does not regulate the terms of mortgage loans or the operation of federal programs; rather, it prohibits deceptive conduct by lenders. *See* *Barnes*, 289 Mass. at 557. Wells Fargo’s repeated misrepresentations—that HAMP was the only option, that the application had been received when it had not, and that foreclosure was imminent despite ongoing consideration—constitute deceptive acts under § 15. *See* *Curtis*, 302 U.S. at 105. The fact that the bank was bound by a settlement agreement to act fairly only underscores the need for state law to enforce those obligations. *See* *Hartford Fire*, 312 U.S. at 573.

III. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING FOLEY’S CLAIM FOR BREACH OF THE COVENANT OF GOOD FAITH AND FAIR DEALING.

Even where a contract is silent on a particular duty, the law implies a covenant of good faith and fair dealing. *See* *Barnes*, 289 Mass. at 557. This covenant requires that no party to a contract act in a manner that undermines the other party’s right to receive the benefits of the agreement. *See* *Hartford Fire*, 312 U.S. at 573. Wells Fargo’s conduct—delaying the HAMP application, failing to respond to inquiries, and sending foreclosure notices while claiming to be considering modification—directly undermines Foley’s right to a fair opportunity for relief.
