Ohio’s clemency process does not create a protected liberty interest, and Ohio’s offer of a voluntary interview does not compel self-incrimination.

First, an inmate has no freestanding due process right to clemency. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), the Court held that commutation is a matter of executive grace and that “pardon and commutation decisions” are “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464. Because clemency is traditionally committed to executive discretion, the prisoner has no liberty interest in the clemency decision itself absent state-imposed substantive limits. *Id.* at 465; see also *Greenholtz v. Inmates of Nebraska Penal and Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7-11 (1979); *Meachum v. Fano*, 427 U.S. 215, 224-27 (1976). That principle controls here. Ohio’s Constitution vests clemency in the Governor’s discretion, and Ohio law imposes no substantive entitlement to mercy. A procedure that merely channels the Governor’s grace does not itself create a protected interest. See *Olympic Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass’n v. Director, Office of Thrift Supervision*, 732 F.2d 1351, 1358 (7th Cir. 1984) (no protected interest where decision remains discretionary); *Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460, 468-72 (1983), limited by *Sandin v. Conner*, 515 U.S. 472, 483-84 (1995).

Second, *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985), does not transform clemency into an adjudicatory stage entitled to full due process. *Evitts* held only that if a State creates a first appeal as of right, it must provide effective assistance in that appeal. *Id.* at 393-97. The Court did not overrule *Dumschat*, and it reaffirmed the distinction between the judicial process and executive grace. *Id.* at 393-94. Likewise, *Ross v. Moffitt*, 417 U.S. 600, 610-12 (1974), confirms that due process does not require the State to supply every procedural safeguard at every postconviction step. Clemency is even further removed from the adjudication of guilt than the discretionary appellate and collateral processes at issue in *Ross* and *Evitts*.

Third, the Fifth Amendment is not violated by offering a voluntary interview in which the inmate may speak or remain silent. The privilege against self-incrimination protects against compulsion, not every strategic choice that may have consequences. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316-20 (1976), held that adverse consequences may follow from silence without creating unconstitutional compulsion. Similarly, *Minnesota v. Murphy*, 465 U.S. 420, 427-34 (1984), explains that the Constitution is offended only when the State compels testimony by threat of penalty. Ohio’s clemency regulation does not require Woodard to answer any question; it simply allows him to request a private interview and to speak if he chooses. A person who voluntarily speaks is not compelled within the meaning of the Fifth Amendment. *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 286-87 (1998) (O’Connor, J., concurring in part and concurring in the judgment); *Ullmann v. United States*, 350 U.S. 422, 431-34 (1956).

Nor does the regulation impose an unconstitutional condition. The Court has repeatedly rejected such claims where the government offers a benefit without forcing waiver of a constitutional right. *Wyman v. James*, 400 U.S. 309, 317-24 (1971); *McKune v. Lile*, 536 U.S. 24, 35-52 (2002). Ohio does not condition clemency on surrendering silence; it merely permits the prisoner to make a personal plea. That is a classic voluntary choice, not coercion. See *South Dakota v. Neville*, 459 U.S. 553, 563-64 (1983); *Chavez v. Martinez*, 538 U.S. 760, 767-72 (2003).

Finally, the Sixth Circuit’s contrary approach would constitutionalize executive mercy and invite judicial supervision of a process the Constitution leaves to the political branches. This Court should reverse. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464-67; *Greenholtz*, 442 U.S. at 7-16; *Sandin*, 515 U.S. at 483-84; *Baxter*, 425 U.S. at 316-20; *Murphy*, 465 U.S. at 427-34; *Woodard*, 523 U.S. at 286-89.
