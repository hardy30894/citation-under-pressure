**ARGUMENT**

**I. The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection of the partnership’s tax debt from the partners.**

The Internal Revenue Code requires the IRS to assess tax liabilities against each taxpayer within three years of the filing of the taxpayer’s return. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) (1954). Assessment is the “determination by the Secretary or his delegate of the amount of tax imposed by this chapter.” 26 U.S.C. § 6203 (1954). The term “taxpayer” is defined to include “any person subject to any internal revenue tax,” and “person” is defined to include “an individual.” 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14), (a)(1) (1954). Thus, the IRS must assess each individual partner separately to extend the limitations period for collection against that partner. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453, 458 (9th Cir. 1961) (holding that an assessment naming only the partnership did not constitute an assessment against an individual partner); *United States v. Ahrens*, 144 F.Supp. 711, 713 (D. Neb. 1956) (same).

The IRS’s argument that a partnership assessment suffices to extend the limitations period for collection against the partners is foreclosed by *United States v. Updike*, 8 F.Supp. 910 (D. Neb. 1934), aff’d, 84 F.2d 874 (8th Cir. 1936). In *Updike*, the court held that an assessment against a partnership did not constitute an assessment against the individual partners for purposes of extending the limitations period. *Id.* at 912. The court reasoned that the partners were distinct taxpayers, and the IRS’s failure to assess them individually barred collection. *Id.* Similarly, in *United States v. Anderson*, 60 F.Supp. 407 (D. Minn. 1945), the court held that an assessment against a corporation did not extend the limitations period for collection against its shareholders. *Id.* at 409. The court explained that “[t]he assessment must be made against the taxpayer sought to be held liable.” *Id.*

The IRS’s reliance on *Young v. Riddell*, 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), is misplaced. In *Young*, the Ninth Circuit affirmed a district court decision holding that a partner who had paid his share of the partnership’s tax liability was not entitled to a refund, even though the IRS had not assessed him individually. *Id.* at 910. The court’s holding was limited to the narrow issue of refund eligibility and did not address whether the IRS could collect the tax from the partner in the absence of an individual assessment. *Id.* The court did not hold that the IRS could collect the tax from the partner without an individual assessment or judgment. *See also United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458 (distinguishing *Young* and reaffirming that an individual assessment is required).

**II. The IRS’s failure to obtain a judgment against the partners within the three-year limitations period further bars collection.**

Alternatively, the IRS could have brought a timely action to collect the tax from the partners. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) (1954). However, the IRS did neither. The IRS’s argument that it can collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners without an individual assessment or judgment is foreclosed by *United States v. Updike*, 8 F.Supp. at 912, and *United States v. Anderson*, 60 F.Supp. at 409. In both cases, the courts held that the IRS could not collect the tax from the partners or shareholders without an individual assessment or judgment. *Id.*

The IRS’s reliance on *Wright v. United States*, 57 F.3d 561 (7th Cir. 1995), is unavailing. In *Wright*, the IRS had assessed both the partnership and the individual partners, and the court held that the assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period for collection against the partners. *Id.* at 562. Here, by contrast, the IRS assessed only the partnership and not the individual partners. The IRS’s failure to assess the partners individually bars collection. *See United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d at 458.

**III. California partnership law does not permit the IRS to collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners without a judgment against them.**

Under California law, a judgment against a partnership is not a judgment against the partners. Cal. Corp. Code § 16307(c) (1949). A creditor must obtain a judgment against the partner to collect from the partner’s assets. *Id.* The IRS has not obtained such a judgment, and the three-year limitations period for bringing such an action has expired. 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a) (1954). Thus, the IRS’s claim is unenforceable against the partners. *See United States v. Updike*, 8 F.Supp. at 912; *United States v. Anderson*, 60 F.Supp. at 409.

**CONCLUSION**

The IRS’s failure to assess the individual partners within the three-year limitations period bars collection of the partnership’s tax debt from the partners. The IRS’s failure to obtain a judgment against the partners within the three-year limitations period further bars collection. California partnership law does not permit the IRS to collect the partnership’s tax debt from the partners without a judgment against them. The bankruptcy court correctly disallowed the IRS’s claims.
