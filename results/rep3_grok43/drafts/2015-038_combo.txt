ARGUMENT

The Property Clause empowers Congress to make all needful rules respecting federal territory. U.S. Const. art. IV, § 3, cl. 2; Camfield v. United States, 167 U.S. 518, 525 (1897). That power extends to the regulation of activities affecting federal lands, even when the regulated conduct occurs on adjacent nonfederal property. Id. at 525-526; Light v. United States, 220 U.S. 523, 537 (1911). Regulations governing the use of navigable waters within reservation boundaries likewise rest on this authority. United States v. Utah, 283 U.S. 64, 89 (1931); The Daniel Ball, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870).

Congress may condition the use of federal enclaves without regard to state ownership of submerged lands. Pollard's Lessee v. Hagan, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 224 (1845); United States v. California, 332 U.S. 19, 38-39 (1947). The United States retains paramount authority to protect its reservations from injury caused by activities on state waters. United States v. Texas, 339 U.S. 707, 718-719 (1950); Minnesota v. Hitchcock, 185 U.S. 373, 387 (1902). This authority is not diminished by state title to the bed of a navigable river. Id.

Federal power to regulate commerce among the states supplies an independent basis for uniform rules governing transportation on navigable waters. Gibbons v. Ogden, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1, 197 (1824). Once Congress has acted, conflicting state policy must yield. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 426 (1819). The same principle permits the United States to enforce reasonable restrictions on access to its own lands. United States v. Grimaud, 220 U.S. 506, 521 (1911).

These settled doctrines confirm that the National Park Service may apply its hovercraft prohibition throughout the Yukon-Charley preserve, including on the Nation River. No pre-existing rule of construction limits that authority to federally owned parcels.
