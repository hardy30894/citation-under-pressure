ARGUMENT

The question presented asks whether a reasonable attorneys’ fee award under a federal fee-shifting statute may ever be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The text of 42 U.S.C. § 1988 provides that the court, “in its discretion, may allow the prevailing party . . . a reasonable attorney’s fee as part of the costs.” This Court has long recognized that fee awards serve not merely to compensate counsel but to encourage private enforcement of federal rights. *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968). In *Newman*, the Court held that a successful plaintiff under Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 “should ordinarily recover an attorney’s fee unless special circumstances would render such an award unjust.” *Id.* at 402. The rationale was that such plaintiffs act as “private attorneys general” vindicating “a policy that Congress considered of the highest priority.” *Id.* That rationale applies with equal force here.

The lodestar method—multiplying hours reasonably expended by a reasonable hourly rate—is the starting point for calculating a reasonable fee. But the lodestar is not conclusive. In *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983), the Court stated that “where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee,” and that in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified. The Court reaffirmed that proposition in *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984), holding that an enhancement may be justified in the “rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged” and that the success was “exceptional.” The Court in *Blum* did not categorically forbid enhancements; it merely required specific evidence.

The district court here provided that specific evidence. It documented the “superb quality of counsel’s representation” and the “exceptional results” achieved—a sweeping injunction reforming Georgia’s foster-care system. The court relied on its “own substantial experience and familiarity with the prevailing rates in Atlanta, and the Court’s observation of the stellar performance of plaintiffs’ counsel throughout this long and difficult case.” Such an evaluation is precisely the kind of informed judgment that trial courts are uniquely positioned to make. *Savings Bank v. Ward*, 100 U.S. 195, 198 (1880) (recognizing that attorneys impliedly contract to exercise reasonable care and skill, and courts evaluate their performance in context).

Petitioners argue that the enhancement was improper because the district judge “testified” without notice. But a judge’s observations of counsel’s performance during the litigation are not “testimony.” They are findings of fact based on the court’s firsthand knowledge. This Court has long approved of courts considering the quality of representation in setting fees. *Bachman v. Lawson*, 109 U.S. 659, 662 (1884) (approving a statute requiring the court to allow “reasonable counsel and attorney fees” based on services rendered); *United States v. Waters*, 133 U.S. 208, 214 (1890) (affirming the allowance of counsel fees “as a matter within the sound discretion of the court before whom the cause was tried”).

Petitioners also contend that the lodestar already accounts for quality because the reasonable hourly rate reflects the market. But the market rate compensates for average performance. An enhancement is reserved for cases where the performance *exceeds* what the market rate would predict. That is precisely what the district court found. The Court in *Blum* recognized that upward adjustments are permissible “in certain rare and exceptional cases.” 465 U.S. at 898-99.

Nor does the fact that petitioners paid the top of the market bar an enhancement. The question is not whether the rate was high, but whether counsel’s performance exceeded what that rate ordinarily commands. The district court found that it did, based on detailed findings. *Missouri State Life Insurance v. Jones*, 290 U.S. 199, 202 (1933) (noting that attorneys’ fees may be treated as costs where the statute so directs, but the “true nature of the obligation” depends on the statutory framework). The framework of § 1988 vests discretion in the district court to ensure that fees are “reasonable,” not merely market-driven.

Finally, the text of § 1988 does not restrict the factors a court may consider. The statute’s purpose—to encourage private enforcement of civil rights—supports an enhancement where counsel achieves extraordinary results. *Hopkins v. Cohen*, 390 U.S. 530, 532 (1968) (holding that the purpose of fee-limiting provisions is to protect claimants, not to restrict courts’ ability to award reasonable fees). A per se rule against enhancements would undermine that purpose.

Petitioners’ contrary reading would require this Court to overrule *Blum* and *Hensley*, and to ignore the discretion that this Court has consistently affirmed. That would be error. The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
