## Argument

The Tax Court erred in disallowing Meruelo's claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness of $9 million running directly to him is contrary to established law.

Under the Internal Revenue Code, a shareholder's ability to deduct his proportionate share of an S corporation's net operating losses is limited by the sum of his basis in his stock and the corporate indebtedness to him. 26 U.S.C. § 1366(d)(1). To establish basis in the corporate indebtedness, the shareholder must demonstrate that the corporation owes a "bona fide indebtedness" to him. Treas. Reg. § 1.1366-2(a)(2)(i).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that a shareholder can establish basis in a corporate indebtedness by making an "actual economic outlay" toward the debt. See Interstate Transit Lines v. Comm'r , 319 U.S. 590, 593 (1943) (holding that a shareholder's guarantee of a corporate loan does not constitute an "actual economic outlay" unless the shareholder is called upon to make good on the guarantee). However, the Court has also held that a shareholder can establish basis by lending money to the corporation or by having the corporation indebted to him. See Helvering v. Richmond, F. & P.R. Co. , 90 F.2d 971, 974 (4th Cir. 1937) (holding that a shareholder's loan to the corporation creates basis), cert. denied, 302 U.S. 740 (1937); see also Burnet v. Commonwealth Imp. Co. , 287 U.S. 415, 419 (1932) (holding that a corporation's indebtedness to its shareholder can create basis for the shareholder).

In this case, Meruelo made an actual economic outlay by obtaining a personal loan to fund his share of the deposit for the condominium complex. He then transferred the loan proceeds to Akoya, which in turn transferred the funds to Merco. The Commissioner does not dispute that this transfer gave Meruelo a shareholder basis in Merco.

Moreover, the transactions between Merco and its affiliates, which were later reclassified as loans from Meruelo to Merco, established a bona fide indebtedness running directly to Meruelo. The fact that Merco's books and records showed substantial net accounts payable to its affiliates, and that Carreras reported these amounts as "shareholder loans" on Merco's tax returns, demonstrates that the parties treated the transactions as loans.

The Tax Court's ruling that Meruelo failed to establish a bona fide indebtedness running directly to him is inconsistent with the principles established in Helvering v. Richmond, F. & P.R. Co. and Burnet v. Commonwealth Imp. Co. The court's focus on the fact that the debt ran from Merco to the affiliates, rather than directly to Meruelo, elevates form over substance. As the Supreme Court has held, "the incidence of taxation depends upon the substance of a transaction." Commissioner v. Court Holding Co. , 324 U.S. 331, 334 (1945).

In conclusion, Meruelo established a bona fide indebtedness of $9 million running directly to him, and the Tax Court erred in disallowing his claimed deduction for his share of Merco's losses. The court's ruling should be reversed, and Meruelo's deduction should be allowed.
