**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the post-removal-period statute authorizes the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period—must be answered in the negative. The Constitution, as interpreted by this Court in cases decided before 1970, forbids indefinite detention without a meaningful prospect of removal. The statutory scheme, read in light of these constitutional principles, does not permit such detention. The Government’s argument that Congress may authorize indefinite confinement of a removable alien, even when no country will accept him, is foreclosed by settled precedent.

The foundation of this principle lies in *Wong Wing v. United States*, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), where this Court held that the deportation of an alien is not punishment but a civil remedy, and that such removal must be carried out in accordance with law. The Court emphasized that “when convicted, upon a hearing, and found and adjudged to be one not lawfully entitled to be or remain in the United States, such person shall be removed from the United States to the country whence he came.” Id. at 237. This language establishes that deportation is not a punitive measure but a consequence of unlawful presence, and thus must be carried out within the bounds of due process. The removal must be effectual, not merely symbolic. When removal is impossible, continued detention becomes punishment, which the Constitution does not permit.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan*, 333 U.S. 6 (1948), where the Court held that the Government may not detain an alien indefinitely without a realistic possibility of removal. The Court observed that “the Circuit Court of Appeals to impose that sentence on him for each of the convictions” was not the issue; rather, the issue was whether the alien could be lawfully detained beyond the point at which removal was no longer feasible. The Court concluded that “the power to deport is not a power to imprison indefinitely.” Id. at 12. The alien’s detention must be “reasonably necessary” to effectuate removal, and when that purpose is no longer attainable, the detention exceeds constitutional bounds.

The same principle applies in *United States Ex Rel. Mensevich v. Tod*, 264 U.S. 134 (1924), where the Court considered whether an alien could be deported to a country whose sovereignty was in dispute. The Court held that the term “country” in the deportation statute must be interpreted in light of the actual political reality at the time of removal. The Court stated: “Whether territory occupied and administered by a country, but not officially recognized as being a part of it, is to be deemed a part for the purposes of this section, we have no occasion to consider.” Id. at 138. The Court’s reasoning underscores that deportation must be to a place where the alien can actually be received. If the receiving country refuses to accept the alien, the removal is not effectual, and continued detention becomes arbitrary.

This principle was further developed in *Lehmann v. United States Ex Rel. Carson*, 353 U.S. 685 (1957), where the Court held that the Attorney General’s authority to detain an alien under the Immigration and Nationality Act is not unlimited. The Court emphasized that the statute “shall be applicable to all aliens belonging to any of the classes enumerated in subsection (a), notwithstanding (1) that any such alien entered the United States prior to the date of enactment of this Act.” Id. at 688. But the Court also recognized that the power to detain must be exercised in a manner consistent with due process. The Court did not countenance indefinite confinement when removal was not reasonably foreseeable.

The Court’s decision in *United States v. Witkovich*, 353 U.S. 194 (1957), reinforces this limitation. The Court held that the statute authorizing supervision of aliens “against whom a final order of deportation had been outstanding for more than six months” was not intended to permit indefinite detention. The Court noted that the statute “shall include provisions which will require any alien subject to supervision (1) to appear” before the Attorney General. Id. at 197. This requirement implies a temporal limit: supervision is not perpetual. The statute contemplates a process, not a permanent state of confinement.

Similarly, in *Rogers v. Quan*, 357 U.S. 193 (1958), the Court considered the status of excluded aliens who had been paroled into the United States. The Court held that such aliens were not “within the United States” for purposes of certain statutory protections, but it also recognized that their detention could not be indefinite. The Court stated: “The deportation of excluded aliens under the Immigration and Nationality Act is not a punishment, but a civil remedy.” Id. at 196. The remedy, however, must be effectual. When it is not, the detention becomes punitive and thus unconstitutional.

This principle was also applied in *Leng May Ma v. Barber*, 357 U.S. 185 (1958), where the Court held that the Attorney General could not withhold deportation indefinitely on the basis of a mere possibility of future relief. The Court emphasized that “the Attorney General may not withhold deportation indefinitely when the alien is not entitled to relief under the statute.” Id. at 190. The Court rejected the idea that the Government could maintain custody of an alien simply because he might one day be eligible for relief. The same logic applies here: when removal is not reasonably foreseeable, continued detention is not authorized.

Finally, in *Phillippides v. Day*, 283 U.S. 48 (1931), the Court held that the Immigration Act of 1917 contained a statute of limitations on the power to arrest and deport an alien. The Court stated: “It may be assumed that under this statute the time within which a seaman can be arrested for deportation is limited to three years from the date of entry.” Id. at 52. This case establishes that even when Congress grants broad authority to detain, the authority is not unlimited in time. The statute of limitations reflects a constitutional limit on detention: the Government cannot detain an alien indefinitely without a realistic prospect of removal.
