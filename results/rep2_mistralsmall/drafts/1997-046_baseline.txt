**ARGUMENT**

**I. OHIO’S CLEMENCY PROCESS CREATES A PROTECTED LIBERTY INTEREST IN LIFE AND LIBERTY THAT TRIGGERS DUE PROCESS PROTECTIONS.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in concluding that Ohio’s clemency process does not implicate a constitutionally protected liberty interest. This Court has long recognized that clemency proceedings are an integral part of the criminal justice system, and thus subject to due process protections. *See Ohio Adult Parole Auth. v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 281 (1998) (plurality opinion) (acknowledging that clemency is "an integral part of the system for finally adjudicating the guilt or innocence of a defendant"). The Court in *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458, 464 (1981), distinguished between the existence of a liberty interest and the extent of due process protections, holding that while clemency decisions are not subject to heightened scrutiny, they are not beyond judicial review entirely. *See also Greenholtz v. Inmates of Neb. Penal & Corr. Complex*, 442 U.S. 1, 7 (1979) (recognizing that parole release decisions implicate a liberty interest).

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Dumschat* to deny any due process protections was misplaced. *Dumschat* did not hold that clemency proceedings lack any constitutional significance; rather, it held that the state did not create a protected liberty interest in the clemency process itself. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465. Here, Ohio’s clemency process is not merely discretionary—it is structured and regulated, with specific procedures governing interviews and hearings. *See* Ohio Rev. Code § 2967.07; Ohio Adult Parole Auth., Death Penalty Clemency Procedure § IV.C.2. This structured process creates a liberty interest in the clemency proceeding itself, triggering due process protections. *See Hewitt v. Helms*, 459 U.S. 460, 471-72 (1983) (holding that state-created liberty interests are protected by due process).

**II. THE FIFTH AMENDMENT PROHIBITS OHIO FROM IMPOSING A "HOBSON’S CHOICE" BETWEEN PARTICIPATING IN A CLEMENCY INTERVIEW AND ASSERTING THE RIGHT AGAINST SELF-INCRIMINATION.**

The Sixth Circuit erred in failing to recognize that Ohio’s clemency process violates the Fifth Amendment by conditioning participation in the clemency interview on the waiver of the right against self-incrimination. The Fifth Amendment provides that "[n]o person... shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." U.S. Const. amend. V. This right applies not only in formal criminal proceedings but also in any proceeding where the state seeks to compel testimonial evidence. *See Lefkowitz v. Turley*, 414 U.S. 70, 77 (1973) ("The constitutional privilege against self-incrimination is essentially a personal one, applying to individuals.").

The Sixth Circuit’s suggestion that the clemency interview is voluntary and thus does not implicate the Fifth Amendment is incorrect. The Court has held that even voluntary participation in a state proceeding does not waive Fifth Amendment protections if the state conditions a benefit on the waiver of those rights. *See Lefkowitz*, 414 U.S. at 78 ("The fact that the appellees were not compelled to speak does not mean that the State may not offer incentives for those who do."). Here, Ohio’s clemency process conditions the opportunity for a personal interview—a critical component of the clemency review—on the inmate’s willingness to speak. This creates an unconstitutional "Hobson’s choice," forcing inmates to choose between asserting their Fifth Amendment rights and participating meaningfully in the clemency process. *See Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 316 (1976) (holding that the Fifth Amendment applies to prison disciplinary hearings where the inmate’s silence could be used against him).

The Sixth Circuit’s reliance on *Baxter* is misplaced. In *Baxter*, the Court held that the state could draw an adverse inference from the inmate’s silence, but it did not address whether the state could condition participation in a proceeding on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights. *Id.* at 324. Here, Ohio’s clemency process does not merely allow an adverse inference—it conditions the opportunity for a personal interview on the inmate’s willingness to speak. This is a far greater intrusion on the Fifth Amendment than the mere drawing of an adverse inference.

**III. THE CLEMENCY PROCESS MUST INCLUDE PROCEDURAL SAFEGUARDS TO PROTECT INMATES’ CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHTS.**

Because Ohio’s clemency process implicates both due process and Fifth Amendment rights, it must include procedural safeguards to ensure fairness. The Court has held that even in clemency proceedings, where the state retains broad discretion, due process requires some minimal protections. *See Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 465 ("[T]he Due Process Clause does not mandate that a state provide for a particular type of process in clemency proceedings."). However, where the state creates a structured process with specific procedures, those procedures must comport with constitutional requirements. *See Hewitt*, 459 U.S. at 471-72.

Here, Ohio’s clemency process conditions participation in a personal interview on the waiver of Fifth Amendment rights, creating an unconstitutional choice. To remedy this violation, the clemency process must be modified to allow inmates to participate in the interview without waiving their Fifth Amendment rights. Alternatively, the state could provide immunity for statements made during the clemency interview, ensuring that those statements cannot be used against the inmate in any subsequent criminal proceeding. *See Kastigar v. United States*, 406 U.S. 441, 448 (1972) (holding that use and derivative-use immunity is sufficient to compel testimony without violating the Fifth Amendment).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the Sixth Circuit’s decision and hold that Ohio’s clemency process violates the Due Process Clause and the Fifth Amendment. The clemency process must be modified to ensure that inmates are not forced to choose between participating in the process and asserting their constitutional rights.
