# ARGUMENT

## I. The Text of Section 1333(b) Imposes a Causal Requirement, Not a Geographic One.

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Nothing in that language asks where the employee's body happened to be at the moment of injury. It asks only whether the injury was "the result of" shelf operations. When a statute's terms are plain, "the sole function of the courts is to enforce it according to its terms," and courts should not add limitations that Congress did not enact. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470 (1917). Congress chose causal language—"resulting from," "as the result of"—not locational language. Had Congress meant to confine coverage to injuries occurring physically on the Shelf, it knew how to say so, as it did elsewhere in the same statute when defining the Shelf itself by reference to "submerged lands lying seaward." 43 U.S.C. § 1331(a). The absence of comparable locational language in § 1333(b) is telling, and this Court should not supply what Congress omitted.

## II. OCSLA and the Longshore Act Are Remedial Statutes That Must Be Construed to Effectuate Their Coverage Purposes.

Congress enacted the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act to ensure that the extraction of the Nation's offshore resources would not proceed in a legal vacuum, and to furnish a body of law—including compensation for injured workers—adequate to that enterprise. Rodrigue v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 395 U.S. 352 (1969). This Court in Rodrigue recognized that OCSLA was designed to reach the full range of activity attending offshore extraction, treating fixed structures on the Shelf, for these purposes, much as if they were located within a State. Id. That remedial design counsels against a construction that strands from coverage a worker whose fatal injury was causally bound up with shelf operations merely because the fatal blow landed onshore rather than offshore.

The same principle animates this Court's Longshore Act jurisprudence generally. In Calbeck v. Travelers Insurance Co., 370 U.S. 114 (1962), this Court construed the Longshore Act to extend coverage to the outer limits of congressional power over maritime injuries, refusing to read the Act narrowly where such a reading would leave workers without a compensation remedy. And in South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), this Court held that questions of coverage at the margins of the Act's reach are properly resolved with due regard for the Act's basic remedial purpose, deferring to reasonable administrative judgments that further, rather than frustrate, coverage.

This Court has likewise instructed that doubts about the scope of workers' compensation statutes are to be resolved in favor of the injured worker and his family. Voris v. Eikel, 346 U.S. 328 (1953). And in Cardillo v. Liberty Mutual Insurance Co., 330 U.S. 469 (1947), this Court gave the Longshore Act's causal language—"arising out of and in the course of employment"—a broad and practical construction, sustaining coverage based on reasonable inferences drawn from the employment relationship rather than rigid formalism. That same practical, employment-centered approach should govern § 1333(b)'s parallel causal phrase, "as the result of."

This Court's decision in O'Leary v. Brown-Pacific-Maxon, Inc., 340 U.S. 504 (1951), is particularly instructive. There the Court rejected a narrow, situs-bound view of Longshore Act coverage, explaining that "the test of recovery is not a causal relation between the nature of employment of the injured person and the accident," and that all that is required is that the conditions of employment create the "zone of special danger" out of which the injury arose. Id. at 507. Juan Valladolid's employment as a roustabout on the Hogan platform placed him within precisely such a zone: his periodic duty of consolidating scrap metal generated by shelf operations, performed at the shore facility that existed to process the Shelf's extracted resources, was an integral incident of his offshore employment. The substantial nexus between his death and shelf operations satisfies § 1333(b) exactly as O'Leary's functional test contemplates.

## III. Isolated, Unnecessary Statements in Prior Opinions Are Not Controlling.

To the extent Pacific Operations relies on stray language in an opinion addressing an entirely different provision of OCSLA, that reliance is misplaced. This Court has long recognized that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," and that their bearing on unrelated questions "is seldom completely investigated." Cohens v. Virginia, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821); accord Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). A passing observation offered without briefing, argument, or analysis on the precise question presented here cannot be permitted to override the statute's plain causal text and the remedial purposes this Court has repeatedly recognized in analogous compensation contexts.

## CONCLUSION

Because § 1333(b) asks only whether an injury resulted from shelf operations, and because this Court's precedents require that causal, remedial language be construed to serve the Act's protective purposes, the judgment below should be affirmed.
