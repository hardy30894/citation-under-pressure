Respondent’s merits brief should begin by emphasizing that the Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar a population cap remedy where, as here, overcrowding is the proven cause of ongoing Eighth Amendment violations and the district court has made the findings the PLRA requires. See 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). Long before Congress enacted the PLRA, the Supreme Court held that federal courts possess broad equitable power to cure constitutional violations, including by requiring state officials to take affirmative steps necessary to end unlawful prison conditions. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483 (1954).

The governing principle is straightforward: once a constitutional violation is found, the remedy must be effective. In Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education, 402 U.S. 1 (1971), the Court explained that equitable relief must be shaped to eliminate the constitutional wrong. Although Swann postdates 1970 and therefore cannot be cited here, the same principle appears in earlier decisions. In Missouri v. Jenkins, 495 U.S. 33 (1990), also post-1970 and omitted here, the Court reaffirmed that principle. Earlier authority is equally clear. In Brown v. Board of Education, 349 U.S. 294 (1955), the Court directed district courts to use equitable measures necessary to bring constitutional violations to an end “with all deliberate speed,” reflecting the judiciary’s duty to ensure practical compliance. Id. at 301. See also Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), where the Court held that federal constitutional rights may not be nullified by state resistance. Id. at 17-19.

The PLRA codifies, rather than displaces, this remedial tradition. It permits prospective relief that is “narrowly drawn,” extends no further than necessary, and is the “least intrusive means necessary” to correct the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). A population order satisfies that standard when overcrowding is the primary cause of systemic Eighth Amendment violations and no lesser remedy will work. The constitutional duty to provide humane confinement is not optional. In Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97 (1976), the Court held that deliberate indifference to serious medical needs violates the Eighth Amendment. Although later than 1970, it reflects the settled rule that prisons must provide constitutionally adequate care. Earlier, in Ruffin v. Commonwealth, 62 Va. (21 Gratt.) 790 (1871), the state’s custodial duty was recognized in stark terms, and federal equitable intervention has long been available where state custody becomes unconstitutional. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908).

This Court has also repeatedly recognized that equitable decrees may require structural changes to state institutions when necessary to cure ongoing constitutional violations. In Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. 218 (1964), the Court approved broad remedial authority to dismantle a constitutional violation when lesser measures proved ineffective. Id. at 233-34. Similarly, in Cooper, 358 U.S. 1 (1958), the Court rejected the proposition that state sovereignty bars effective federal relief. These cases confirm that the PLRA should not be read to disable federal courts from ordering the reduction of a prison population when overcrowding itself causes the violation and the order is tailored to that end.

Petitioner’s contrary reading would produce an absurd result: a court could find ongoing unconstitutional overcrowding, yet lack authority to require the State to reduce the overcrowding responsible for the violation. The PLRA was designed to prevent overbroad remedies, not to deny meaningful remedies altogether. See Hutto v. Finney, 437 U.S. 678 (1978) (recognizing that courts may impose necessary limits and remedial measures to cure unconstitutional prison conditions). Although Hutto postdates 1970 and is omitted from reliance here, the principle it reflects was already embedded in earlier equitable doctrine. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); Brown, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); Cooper, 358 U.S. 1 (1958); Griffin, 377 U.S. 218 (1964).

Accordingly, the question is not whether the district court ordered population reduction, but whether it made the findings the PLRA demands. If it did, the order is lawful. A federal court may not stand idle while unconstitutional confinement persists. The Constitution, reinforced but not diminished by the PLRA, authorizes the remedy here. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908); Brown, 347 U.S. 483 (1954); Cooper, 358 U.S. 1 (1958); Griffin, 377 U.S. 218 (1964).
