ARGUMENT

The lodestar figure may be enhanced under a federal fee-shifting statute when the fee applicant presents specific evidence that superior representation produced exceptional results. Blum v. Stenson, 465 U.S. 886, 899 (1984), held that “an upward adjustment may be justified in the ‘rare case where the fee applicant offers specific evidence to show that the quality of service rendered was superior to that one reasonably should expect in light of the hourly rates charged’ and resulted in ‘exceptional’ success.” The Court reiterated that “where a plaintiff has obtained excellent results, his attorney should recover a fully compensatory fee” and that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” Id. at 901 (quoting Hensley v. Eckerhart, 461 U.S. 424, 435 (1983)).

Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air, 478 U.S. 546 (1986), confirmed rather than repudiated this principle. The Court stated that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible … in certain ‘rare’ and ‘exceptional’ cases, supported by both ‘specific evidence’ on the record and detailed findings by the lower courts.” Id. at 565 (quoting Blum, 465 U.S. at 898-901). Although the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” the Court left open the possibility of enhancement “in the rare case” where the lodestar alone fails to reflect superior performance yielding exceptional results. Id. at 566.

Every circuit to address the question has read Blum and Delaware Valley I to permit such enhancements upon adequate proof. See, e.g., Geier v. Sundquist, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mut. Life Co., 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); Hyatt v. Apfel, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc., 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997); Shipes v. Trinity Indus., 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993).

Here the district court made detailed, case-specific findings—grounded in its observation of counsel’s performance over years of litigation—that the representation far exceeded what the hourly rates contemplated and produced results of exceptional breadth and durability. Those findings satisfy the evidentiary standard Blum and Delaware Valley I require. The Eleventh Circuit correctly held that the enhancement rested within the district court’s discretion.
