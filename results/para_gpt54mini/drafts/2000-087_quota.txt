**ARGUMENT**

The post-removal-period detention statute does not authorize indefinite detention beyond the 90-day removal period. This Court should hold, as the Supreme Court did in **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678 (2001)**, that 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6) permits detention only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal and not forever. **Id.** at 689, 699, 701.

First, the statutory text does not clearly authorize perpetual imprisonment. Section 1231(a)(6) provides that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period,” but it says nothing about indefinite detention. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 697 (2001)**. Because Congress did not speak with the clarity required to authorize grave liberty deprivations, the canon of constitutional avoidance applies. **Id.** at 689, 700; **Edward J. DeBartolo Corp. v. Florida Gulf Coast Building & Construction Trades Council, 485 U.S. 568, 575 (1988)**. As the Court explained in **Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 62 (1932)**, courts should construe statutes to avoid serious constitutional questions where fairly possible.

Second, indefinite civil detention would raise serious due process concerns. Liberty from bodily restraint is at the core of the Due Process Clause. **Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992)**; **Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 356-57 (1997)**. Civil confinement is constitutionally permissible only so long as it serves a legitimate regulatory purpose and is not excessive in relation to that purpose. **United States v. Salerno, 481 U.S. 739, 747-48 (1987)**. Here, once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, detention no longer serves the statute’s basic purpose of securing the alien’s presence at the moment of removal. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 699-700, 701 (2001)**.

Third, the Government’s reading would allow detention with no endpoint, even where removal cannot occur. That is precisely what the Court rejected in **Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953)**, which the Court in **Zadvydas** distinguished as involving an alien stopped at the border. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 693, 700-01 (2001)**. The Court also rejected reliance on cases such as **Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952)**, because those cases involved detention tied to a valid and limited governmental objective, not potentially permanent confinement. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 696-97 (2001)**.

Fourth, the Government’s interpretation would permit imprisonment without the procedural and substantive safeguards that ordinarily attend preventive detention. In **Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 79-83 (1992)**, the Court held that dangerousness alone does not justify continued confinement absent a sufficient connection to a legitimate regulatory end. And in **Jackson v. Indiana, 406 U.S. 715, 738 (1972)**, the Court held that commitment cannot be indefinite when its purpose cannot be achieved. Those principles apply with full force here: once there is no significant likelihood of removal in the reasonably foreseeable future, continued detention becomes arbitrary. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 701-03 (2001)**.

Fifth, the Court’s own limiting construction provides the governing rule. After six months, if the alien provides good reason to believe that removal is not significantly likely in the reasonably foreseeable future, the Government must rebut that showing. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 701 (2001)**. That standard preserves Congress’s authority to detain during the removal period and for a reasonable time beyond it, while preventing constitutionally suspect confinement that may last months or years. **Id.** at 690, 699-701.

Finally, the Government cannot convert a detention statute into a general dangerousness regime. The Court specifically noted that § 1231(a)(6) is not limited to “a small segment of particularly dangerous individuals,” and it declined to create special rules for terrorism or national security cases not before it. **Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 697, 700 (2001)**. If Congress wishes to authorize broader preventive detention, it must do so expressly and within constitutional limits. **Salerno, 481 U.S. at 746-52**; **Crowell, 285 U.S. at 62**.

Because petitioner has shown that removal is not reasonably foreseeable, the statute no longer authorizes detention. The judgment below should be reversed, and the writ granted.
