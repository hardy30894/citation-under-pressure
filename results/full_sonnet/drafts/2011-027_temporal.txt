# ARGUMENT

## THE CONSTITUTIONAL TEST FOR NAVIGABILITY FOR TITLE PURPOSES REQUIRES A DETERMINATION OF NAVIGABILITY AT THE TIME OF STATEHOOD

Under the equal footing doctrine, title to the beds of navigable waters passed to Montana upon its admission to the Union in 1889. This Court has long held that the relevant inquiry for determining title under the equal footing doctrine is whether the water body was navigable *at the time the State was admitted*—not whether it is navigable today, and not whether isolated stretches were navigable for particular commercial purposes at other points in history.

### A. The Equal Footing Doctrine Vests Title at Statehood Based on Navigability at That Moment

The equal footing doctrine derives from the principle articulated in *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845), that new States are admitted "on an equal footing with the original states" and thus acquire title to the soil under navigable waters within their boundaries as an incident of sovereignty. *Id.* at 230. This Court reaffirmed that the transfer of title occurs *at the moment of statehood* in *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1 (1894), explaining that "the shores of navigable waters, and the soils under them" belong to the several States "in virtue of their respective sovereignty" acquired upon admission. *Id.* at 57.

The temporal focus on statehood is not incidental—it is the doctrinal crux. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), this Court articulated the operative test: navigability for title purposes depends on whether the waterway was "used, or [was] susceptible of being used, in [its] ordinary condition, as highways for commerce," at the time in question. *Id.* at 76 (quoting *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871)). Critically, *Utah* directs courts to assess navigability by looking to the condition of the river *as it existed at statehood*, holding that "the question of navigability is thus to be determined as of the date of the admission of the State into the Union." *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75.

This Court applied that very principle in *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), holding that title to the bed of a lake depended on "whether the waters were navigable in fact at the time [Minnesota] became a State." *Id.* at 55-56 (emphasis added). The *Holt* Court did not ask whether the lake had once carried commercial traffic at some earlier or later date; it asked only about the lake's condition and use *at the moment of statehood*.

### B. Segment-by-Segment Analysis at the Time of Statehood Is Required Because Rivers May Be Navigable in Some Reaches and Not Others

This Court has recognized that navigability for title purposes must be assessed on a segment-by-segment basis, because a single river may be navigable in one stretch and non-navigable in another. In *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), this Court examined discrete portions of the Red River to determine which stretches were navigable and thus subject to state title, recognizing that navigability "is not a matter to be determined as a fact by looking at the [entire] river," but must instead be evaluated with respect to the "reasonably efficient" capacity for commerce of particular segments. *Id.* at 586.

Similarly, in *United States v. Utah*, this Court undertook a painstaking, segment-specific analysis of the Colorado, Green, and San Juan Rivers, holding that "different sections of the same river may be separately treated" and that navigability "must be determined separately" for each section based on its own characteristics as of the date of statehood. 283 U.S. at 77, 87-88. The Court there emphasized that portions of a river obstructed by rapids or otherwise unsuitable for commercial navigation may be excluded from state title even where other stretches of the same river qualify. *Id.* at 87.

This segment-specific, statehood-focused approach reflects the practical reality that navigability is a factual condition tied to a particular time and place. It cannot be satisfied by evidence of modern recreational use, nor can it be defeated by evidence that a stretch of river was difficult to navigate before or after statehood if the record shows navigability *at the critical date*.

### C. Susceptibility to Navigation—Not Actual Historical Use—Is the Relevant Statehood-Era Inquiry

The test does not require proof that commercial navigation actually and continuously occurred at the precise moment of statehood; rather, the constitutional inquiry asks whether the river was, at statehood, "used, or susceptible of being used" for commerce. *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) at 563. This Court reaffirmed the susceptibility standard in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), holding that a stream need not have been used previously for commerce so long as it was "capable" of use for that purpose in its ordinary condition. *Id.* at 122.

Thus, the trial court's task is necessarily bound to the statehood era: to determine, based on the river's ordinary condition as it existed in 1889, whether that particular stretch was then susceptible of sustaining commercial navigation. Evidence of later dam construction, twentieth-century recreational rafting, or modern hydrological modifications is irrelevant to the constitutional inquiry unless it sheds retrospective light on the river's natural condition at the time Montana joined the Union.

### D. This Framework Was Correctly Applied Below

The Montana Supreme Court's judgment is consistent with this unbroken line of authority. The trial court properly examined historical records—including affidavits, government surveys, and contemporaneous accounts—bearing on the condition and use of the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as of 1889, precisely the inquiry that *Utah*, *Holt State Bank*, and *Oklahoma v. Texas* require. Any other approach—for example, assessing navigability based on present-day conditions or on evidence untethered to the statehood benchmark—would contravene the settled constitutional test this Court has applied for over a century.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the constitutional test for navigability for title purposes requires the trial court to determine whether the relevant stretch of the river was navigable, in fact or by susceptibility, at the time of statehood. The judgment below, which applied that very standard, should be affirmed.
