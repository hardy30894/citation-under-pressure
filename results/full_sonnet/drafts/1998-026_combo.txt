**ARGUMENT**

**I. A violation of a procedural sentencing rule does not, standing alone, entitle a federal prisoner to collateral relief.**

This Court has long held that not every departure from the formalities of criminal procedure amounts to a defect cognizable on collateral review. In *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424 (1962), the sentencing court failed to comply with the then-existing requirement of Rule 32(a) that a defendant be personally addressed and given an opportunity to speak before sentence was imposed. This Court nevertheless held that the omission did not warrant relief under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, explaining that collateral relief is reserved for defects that are "inconsistent with the rudimentary demands of fair procedure," *id.* at 428, and that a mere failure to follow the formal script of the Rule, without more, does not rise to that level. The logic of *Hill* controls here: the District Court's omission of an advisement that Peguero already possessed was, at most, a technical noncompliance, not a defect striking at the fairness of the proceeding.

This limitation on collateral relief reflects the settled understanding, articulated in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174 (1947), that habeas corpus and its statutory analogue are not substitutes for direct appeal and are not available to remedy every trial error that Congress has otherwise addressed through ordinary appellate procedure. See also *United States v. Hayman*, 342 U.S. 205 (1952) (§ 2255 was enacted to provide a remedy coextensive with habeas corpus, not to expand collateral review beyond fundamental defects). Peguero's claim—that a court's failure to recite an advisement automatically entitles a defendant to relief regardless of his actual knowledge—cannot be reconciled with this line of authority.

**II. Harmless-error principles foreclose relief where the defendant suffered no prejudice.**

Even where a procedural rule is violated, this Court has consistently required a showing that the error affected the outcome or the defendant's substantial rights. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946), the Court held that an error should be disregarded unless the reviewing court cannot "say, with fair assurance . . . that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." That standard, developed under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a), applies with equal force to violations of Rule 32. The purpose of the advisement requirement is to ensure that a defendant knows of his right to appeal; where, as here, the district court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero already possessed that knowledge, the failure to recite the advisement could not have "substantially swayed" his decision not to appeal.

This Court's harmless-error jurisprudence in the constitutional context confirms the same principle. In *Chapman v. California*, 386 U.S. 18, 24 (1967), the Court held that even certain constitutional errors do not require reversal if they are "harmless beyond a reasonable doubt." If constitutional violations may be excused as harmless when they do not affect the outcome, a fortiori a purely procedural, non-constitutional omission—such as an unnecessary advisement—cannot compel relief where the defendant's independent knowledge rendered the omission inconsequential.

**III. Peguero's actual knowledge of his right to appeal satisfied the purpose of Rule 32 and forecloses any claim of prejudice.**

The touchstone of this Court's waiver jurisprudence is whether a defendant's choice was made with knowledge of the right at issue, not whether a court recited a particular formula. In *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), the Court defined waiver as "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege." Peguero's decision not to appeal, made with full awareness of his right to do so, was precisely such an intentional relinquishment. The District Court's advisement exists to create that awareness; where the defendant already has it, the rule's purpose has been fully served, and no additional protection is gained by vacating a valid sentence.

This Court's decision in *Coppedge v. United States*, 369 U.S. 438 (1962), likewise underscores that procedural safeguards surrounding the right to appeal are designed to ensure meaningful access to appellate review, not to create an independent basis for collateral attack divorced from actual prejudice. Peguero was not deprived of access to appeal; he simply chose not to exercise a right he knew he had.

**IV. The narrow scope of collateral review counsels against transforming a harmless procedural omission into an automatic ground for relief.**

Finally, this Court's recognition in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963), that habeas corpus review exists to remedy fundamental unfairness—not technical noncompliance with procedural rules—supports affirmance. Extending collateral relief to every Rule 32 advisement omission, regardless of actual knowledge or prejudice, would convert a rule designed to protect defendants into a mechanical trap for sentencing courts, contrary to the harmless-error principles reaffirmed in *Kotteakos* and *Chapman*, and contrary to the "rudimentary demands of fair procedure" standard set forth in *Hill*.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
