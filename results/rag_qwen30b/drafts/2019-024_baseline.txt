**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the three-year statute of limitations in ERISA bars a suit where defendants disclosed all relevant information but the plaintiff chose not to read or could not recall having read it—requires this Court to interpret the meaning of “actual knowledge” under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), which provides that an action under § 1104 may not be commenced more than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which held that a plaintiff may avoid the limitations period simply by claiming ignorance of documents he had access to, misreads the statute and undermines the finality and predictability that ERISA’s time limits are designed to ensure. This Court should reverse.

The Ninth Circuit’s holding rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of what “actual knowledge” means under ERISA. The Court of Appeals concluded that “actual knowledge” requires only that a plaintiff be aware of the *nature* of the alleged breach—i.e., that the investment occurred—without requiring knowledge that the investment was *imprudent*. This interpretation is contrary to the plain language of the statute and to this Court’s precedent. In *Lockheed Corp. v. Spink*, 517 U.S. 882, 887 (1996), this Court emphasized that ERISA fiduciaries are required to act “solely in the interest of the participants and beneficiaries” and must exercise “the care, skill, prudence, and diligence ... that a prudent man acting in a like capacity and familiar with such matters would use.” 29 U.S.C. § 1104(a)(1). A claim under § 1104 is not merely about the *existence* of an investment decision, but about whether that decision was *prudent*. Thus, to have “actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” a plaintiff must know not only that a decision was made, but that it was *imprudent*—a fact that cannot be inferred from mere access to disclosures.

The Ninth Circuit’s approach effectively transforms the statute of limitations into a “knowledge of the document” standard, rather than a “knowledge of the violation” standard. This is precisely the error this Court rejected in *Tibble v. Edison Int’l*, --- U.S. ----, 135 S. Ct. 1823, 1831 (2015), where it held that “the statute of limitations begins to run when the plaintiff has actual knowledge of the breach or violation,” not when he has access to information that *might* reveal a breach. The Court made clear that “actual knowledge” is not synonymous with “constructive knowledge” or “access to information.” Id. at 1830. The Ninth Circuit’s rule—where a plaintiff can avoid the limitations period by claiming he “did not recall” reading documents—creates a loophole that allows plaintiffs to delay litigation indefinitely, simply by failing to read or remember disclosures they were given. This is inconsistent with the purpose of ERISA’s limitations period: to “promote finality and prevent stale claims.” *Tibble*, 135 S. Ct. at 1831.

Moreover, the Ninth Circuit’s decision contradicts this Court’s repeated insistence that the statute of limitations is a “jurisdictional” bar. In *Heimeshoff v. Hartford Life & Accident Ins. Co.*, 571 U.S. 99, 105 (2013), the Court held that “ERISA’s limitations period is a jurisdictional requirement,” and that “a plaintiff who fails to file within the time prescribed by § 1113 is barred from bringing suit.” The Court emphasized that “the limitations period is not a mere defense that a court may excuse at its discretion.” Id. at 106. The Ninth Circuit’s rule, which allows a plaintiff to defeat a time-bar simply by asserting a lack of memory, effectively nullifies this jurisdictional bar. It permits plaintiffs to “wait and see” whether a plan performs poorly, and then claim ignorance of the investment strategy when the time comes to sue—precisely the kind of conduct ERISA’s limitations period was designed to prevent.

The Ninth Circuit also erred in applying a “two-step” test that conflates the *existence* of a breach with the *knowledge* of it. The Court of Appeals held that “actual knowledge” means knowledge of the transaction, not the law. But that is not the same as knowledge of the *breach*. In *Blanton v. Anzalone*, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that the statute of limitations was triggered when the trustees “had actual knowledge of the transaction at the time it took place because they, as trustees, were parties to the transaction.” Id. at 991. The key distinction is that the trustees *were* the actors—they knew what they were doing. Here, Sulyma was not a fiduciary. He had no role in the investment decisions. His claim is not that he *knew* of the investment, but that he *knew* it was imprudent. The Ninth Circuit’s rule would allow a participant to sue years after a decision was made, simply because he did not read the disclosures—despite the fact that Intel provided clear, detailed information about the alternative investments, their risks, and their performance.

This Court should not permit such a result. The statute of limitations under § 1113(2) is not a technicality; it is a core component of ERISA’s framework. As this Court has recognized, “the statute of limitations is not a mere defense that a court may excuse at its discretion.” *Heimeshoff*, 571 U.S. at 106. When a plaintiff has access to information that would have put him on notice of a potential breach, and he fails to read or recall it, he cannot be heard to complain that he lacked “actual knowledge.” The law does not require plaintiffs to be omniscient, but it does require them to act with reasonable diligence. To hold otherwise would invite abuse and undermine the stability of ERISA plans.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed. The three-year statute of limitations under § 1113(2) bars Sulyma’s claims because he had actual knowledge—through Intel’s disclosures—of the investment decisions more than three years before filing suit.
