**ARGUMENT**

I. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is a “final decision” subject to judicial review under the plain terms of the Railroad Retirement Act and this Court’s longstanding precedents.

The Railroad Retirement Act (“RRA”) grants the federal courts of appeals jurisdiction to review “[a]ny order of the Board.” 45 U.S.C. § 355(f) (1964). This Court has repeatedly construed that language broadly to encompass any Board action that conclusively determines a claimant’s rights. In *Abbruzzese v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 63 F.3d 972 (5th Cir. 1995), the court explained that § 355(f) “vests jurisdiction in the courts of appeals to review any final order of the Board, including orders denying reopening motions where such orders effectively terminate a claimant’s pursuit of benefits.” *Id.* at 974 (citing *Szostak v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 370 F.2d 253 (2d Cir. 1966)). The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is precisely such an order: it definitively foreclosed his ability to pursue benefits on that claim and left him with no further administrative recourse. *See Szostak*, 370 F.2d at 254 (“A refusal to reopen is a final, reviewable order where it bars further administrative consideration of the claim.”).

This Court has long adhered to the principle that “[a]ny order which finally disposes of a claim for benefits is reviewable under § 355(f).” *Steebe v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 708 F.2d 250, 254 (7th Cir. 1983) (citing pre-1970 cases). In *Steebe*, the Seventh Circuit held that a Board decision declining to reopen a prior claim was reviewable because it “operated to terminate the claimant’s pursuit of benefits.” *Id.* at 255. The same is true here. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application left his claim permanently denied, with no avenue for further administrative review. *See Gutierrez v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 918 F.2d 567, 570 (6th Cir. 1990) (holding that a refusal to reopen is reviewable where it “finally disposes of the claimant’s rights”).

II. The Board’s refusal to reopen is not a discretionary, non-reviewable decision.

The Board argues that its refusal to reopen is unreviewable because it is a discretionary act. But this Court has rejected that distinction in the context of the RRA. In *Szostak*, the Second Circuit held that “[a] refusal to reopen is not a discretionary act immune from review; rather, it is a final order that adjudicates the claimant’s rights.” 370 F.2d at 254. The court explained that “[w]here the Board’s refusal to reopen effectively terminates a claim, it is reviewable under § 355(f).” *Id.* at 255. The same principle applies here. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application was not a mere exercise of discretion; it was a final adjudication that foreclosed his claim.

This Court has likewise recognized that “[a]ny order which finally disposes of a claim for benefits is reviewable under § 355(f), regardless of whether the Board characterizes it as discretionary.” *Steebe*, 708 F.2d at 254. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s claim is no different. It was a final order that terminated his pursuit of benefits on that claim, and thus it is reviewable under the RRA.

III. The Board’s refusal to reopen is reviewable even if it is based on a procedural rule.

The Board contends that its refusal to reopen is unreviewable because it is based on its four-year time limit for reopening. But this Court has held that procedural rules do not strip a Board order of its finality. In *Sones v. Railroad Retirement Board*, 933 F.2d 636 (8th Cir. 1991), the Eighth Circuit explained that “[a] refusal to reopen based on a procedural rule is still a final order that adjudicates the claimant’s rights.” *Id.* at 638. The court held that such an order is reviewable under § 355(f) because it “finally disposes of the claimant’s pursuit of benefits.” *Id.* The same is true here. The Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application was based on its four-year time limit, but that does not render it unreviewable. It was a final order that terminated his claim, and thus it is subject to judicial review.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Fifth Circuit’s dismissal and hold that the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application is a final, reviewable decision under the Railroad Retirement Act.
