**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Dismissing the Hostile Work Environment Claim by Failing to Consider All Allegations in Their Totality**

A hostile work environment claim requires a court to examine the entire record, not merely isolated incidents labeled “sexually offensive.” The district court improperly parsed Dr. Moll’s allegations, limiting its review to explicitly sexual acts and excluding facially sex-neutral conduct that may contribute to a discriminatory atmosphere. This Court has long recognized that discrimination may be inferred from a pattern of facially neutral actions when viewed in context. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 373–74 (1886) (holding that a neutral law administered in a discriminatory manner violates the Equal Protection Clause). The totality of the evidence must be considered to determine whether the workplace was permeated with discrimination. *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 392 U.S. 409, 424 (1968) (examining “totality of the evidence” in a discrimination case); *see also Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 345 (1879) (discriminatory purpose may be shown through circumstantial evidence). Even in the context of school segregation, the Supreme Court evaluated “the effect of segregation itself on public education,” considering intangible factors and the totality of circumstances. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 494 (1954). The same holistic approach applies here.

Dr. Moll alleged a host of gender-based actions: exclusion from work-related social events, denial of telework privileges granted to male colleagues, refusal to assess her for promotion while male peers were promoted, and a pattern of demeaning treatment by her supervisor, Daniel Irving, including inappropriate notes and repeated late-night phone calls. The district court incorrectly dissected each incident and dismissed those it deemed not “sexually offensive” as irrelevant. This piecemeal analysis ignores the cumulative effect necessary to establish a hostile environment. Summary judgment is inappropriate when motive and intent are at issue and the evidence must be weighed as a whole. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment “should be used sparingly” in cases involving motive and intent); *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627–28 (1944) (all inferences must be drawn in favor of the non-movant). Because the record, viewed in its entirety, raises a genuine dispute as to whether the workplace was hostile, the district court erred in granting summary judgment.

**II. The District Court Erred in Applying the “Sham Issue of Fact” Doctrine to a Non-Party Witness’s Testimony**

The district court disregarded testimony from a non-party witness solely because it conflicted with that witness’s prior statement, invoking the “sham issue of fact” doctrine. That doctrine applies only to a party’s own contradictory statements offered to create a factual dispute—not to the testimony of a non-party. *Poller*, 368 U.S. at 473 (a party cannot defeat summary judgment by submitting an affidavit that contradicts its own prior deposition without explanation). A court on summary judgment may not weigh evidence or assess credibility. *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946) (the court must not weigh evidence or decide credibility); *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam) (reversing summary judgment where conflicting evidence existed). Even if a non-party’s testimony is inconsistent, it may still create a genuine issue for trial; credibility determinations belong to the jury. The district court accordingly erred in disregarding that testimony.
