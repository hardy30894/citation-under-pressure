**ARGUMENT**

I. The Government Bears the Burden of Persuasion to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

The Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment requires the government to prove every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 372 U.S. 358, 364 (1963). This bedrock principle applies with equal force to conspiracy prosecutions, where the government must prove both the existence of the conspiracy and the defendant’s knowing participation in it. *United States v. Falcone*, 311 U.S. 205, 210 (1940). The government’s burden does not shift merely because a defendant asserts withdrawal or termination of involvement prior to the limitations period. *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 443 (1949) (holding that the government retains the burden of proving all elements of the offense, including the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy).

The government’s burden extends to proving that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 253 (1940) (noting that the government must prove the conspiracy’s existence and the defendant’s participation during the charged period). A defendant’s withdrawal or cessation of activity does not, by itself, negate the conspiracy’s existence or the defendant’s prior participation. *United States v. Crosby*, 294 F.2d 928, 938 (2d Cir. 1961) (Lumbard, J.) (holding that withdrawal does not erase prior participation in the conspiracy). Thus, the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy persisted during the limitations period and that the defendant was a member at that time.

II. The Jury Must Be Instructed on the Government’s Burden Regarding Continuing Conspiracy

The trial court’s failure to instruct the jury on the government’s burden to prove the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period was reversible error. *Cima v. United States*, 267 F.2d 928, 932 (5th Cir. 1959) (holding that the court must instruct the jury on the government’s burden to prove all elements of the offense). The jury’s role is to determine whether the government has met its burden, and an instruction shifting the burden to the defendant violates due process. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) (holding that the government bears the burden of proving every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt). Here, the jury was not instructed that the government bore the burden of proving the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period, depriving the defendants of a fair trial. *Stroud v. United States*, 251 U.S. 15, 18 (1919) (holding that the court must instruct the jury on the government’s burden to prove all elements of the offense).

III. The Government’s Failure to Prove Continuing Conspiracy Requires Reversal

The record contains no evidence that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. The government’s evidence focused on the defendants’ prior participation, not their ongoing involvement. *United States v. Andolschek*, 142 F.2d 503, 506 (2d Cir. 1944) (Hand, J.) (holding that the government must prove the defendant’s participation in the conspiracy during the charged period). The jury’s verdict cannot stand where the government fails to prove an essential element of the offense. *Morissette v. United States*, 342 U.S. 246, 274 (1952) (holding that the government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt). Here, the government’s failure to prove the conspiracy’s continuation into the limitations period requires reversal of the defendants’ convictions. *United States v. Kahaner*, 317 F.2d 424, 427 (2d Cir. 1963) (holding that the government’s failure to prove an essential element of the offense requires reversal).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the court of appeals and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with due process.
