# ARGUMENT

## I. SECTION 289'S TEXT, STRUCTURE, AND HISTORY REQUIRE THAT DAMAGES BE LIMITED TO PROFITS ATTRIBUTABLE TO THE INFRINGED COMPONENT

Section 289 of the Patent Act permits recovery of an infringer's "total profit" for design patent infringement. 35 U.S.C. § 289. But this Court's task is to determine what "total profit" means when a design patent claims only a component of a multicomponent product. The statute's text, structure, and purpose all confirm that the "total profit" recoverable is limited to profit attributable to the "article of manufacture" to which the patented design has been applied—not the infringer's entire product where that product contains many unpatented, unclaimed features.

Section 289 provides that whoever applies a patented design "to any article of manufacture" shall be liable "to the extent of his total profit." The phrase "article of manufacture" is the operative limiting term. As this Court has recognized in the analogous trademark context, statutory damages provisions targeting specific components must be read to reach only the value attributable to the infringing feature, not windfall profits on an entire product. *See generally* *Aro Mfg. Co. v. Convertible Top Replacement Co.*, 377 U.S. 476 (1964) (distinguishing between an entire "article of manufacture" and its component parts for purposes of patent infringement liability).

The 1887 Act, which is the direct predecessor to § 289, was itself a response to this Court's decision in *Dobson v. Hartford Carpet Co.*, 114 U.S. 439 (1885), and its companion cases, which had denied meaningful recovery to design patent holders because it was often impossible to show which portion of the profit on a carpet or oilcloth was due to the pattern as opposed to the fabric itself. Congress's remedy was not to declare that an infringer must disgorge all profits on an entire finished product regardless of the design's contribution; rather, Congress sought to ensure that design patent holders could recover the profit made on "the article of manufacture" bearing the design—recognizing that in the *Dobson* cases, the "article of manufacture" was the patterned carpet itself, not a larger multi-component assembly.

Here, the components at issue—the design of a screen's rounded corners, the shape of the phone's rim, and a colorful grid of icons—are far more analogous to the pattern on a carpet than to the carpet as a whole. Where a design patent claims only a component, the "article of manufacture" under § 289 is that component, and "total profit" must be calculated accordingly.

## II. THE FEDERAL CIRCUIT'S CATEGORICAL RULE CONFLATES THE INFRINGING PRODUCT WITH THE PATENTED ARTICLE OF MANUFACTURE

The Federal Circuit below did not undertake this textual analysis. Instead, in affirming the damages award, the court of appeals treated Samsung's argument as forfeited because Samsung "did not argue that there was a lack of substantial evidence to support the award." This procedural disposition sidesteps the antecedent legal question—whether the "total profit" available under § 289 could, as a matter of law, ever be limited to profits on a component rather than the entire smartphone.

That antecedent legal error infected the underlying judgment. The Federal Circuit's approach mirrors the rule it announced in *Nordock, Inc. v. Systems, Inc.*, 803 F.3d 1344 (Fed. Cir. 2015), and in prior panel decisions holding that unless an infringer can prove the different components of the product are "separate products, entirely unconnected," the entire product is deemed the relevant "article of manufacture" for § 289 purposes. This all-or-nothing rule improperly places a categorical, near-irrebuttable burden on defendants and treats the statutory inquiry as a matter of physical severability rather than what Congress actually asked courts to identify: the article to which the design has been "applied."

Nothing in the statutory text supports collapsing the "article of manufacture" analysis into a test of physical separability. A smartphone is unquestionably a single physical object, but that does not mean the "article of manufacture" to which Apple's asserted designs were "applied" is the entire operating smartphone, inclusive of its processor, memory, wireless radios, and all other unclaimed technology. The design patents here claimed only a black rectangular rounded-rectangle front face, a rim, and a grid of icons—discrete elements of the phone's overall design. The Federal Circuit's rule that any product lacking "physically distinct" sub-components must be treated as a single, indivisible "article of manufacture" reads the word "article" out of the statute and replaces it with "product."

## III. THIS COURT'S PRECEDENT ON APPORTIONMENT IN ANALOGOUS INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY CONTEXTS SUPPORTS AN ARTICLE-OF-MANUFACTURE INQUIRY THAT ACCOUNTS FOR APPORTIONMENT

This Court has long recognized that intellectual property damages must be tethered to the value the intellectual property actually contributed, not simply the profits of the product in which the intellectual property was embodied. In the utility patent context, this Court established the "entire market value rule," under which damages base must be limited to the smallest salable patent-practicing unit unless the patentee can show that the patented feature drove demand for the entire product. Although Congress separately addressed design patents in § 289 by eliminating any causation requirement between the design and demand, it did not eliminate the antecedent requirement to identify the correct "article of manufacture."

The proper reading of § 289 requires a two-step inquiry: first, identify the "article of manufacture" to which the infringed design has been applied; second, calculate the infringer's total profit on that article. Where, as here, a design patent covers only a component of a complex, multi-functional device with thousands of other component technologies, the "article of manufacture" is properly identified as that component, and any profits award must be limited accordingly.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should hold that where a design patent applies only to a component of a product, § 289 damages must be limited to the infringer's profit attributable to that component, vacate the judgment below, and remand for further proceedings consistent with this holding.
