**ARGUMENT**

**I.  The Board’s dismissal of Perry’s appeal for lack of jurisdiction is a procedural dismissal, not a jurisdictional one, and therefore falls under the *Kloeckner* exception.**

The D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit rests on a rigid distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals. However, the Board’s finding that Perry’s retirement and suspension were voluntary is a procedural determination regarding the *process* of the settlement, not a lack of statutory power. Consequently, the case should be reviewed in district court under the *Kloeckner* exception.

In *Kloeckner v. Solis*, the Supreme Court held that when the Board dismisses a mixed-case appeal on procedural grounds, judicial review resides in district court. 568 U.S. 41, 603 (2012). The Court specifically defined the issue as whether an employee seeking judicial review should proceed in the Federal Circuit or a district court when the MSPB has dismissed her mixed case on “procedural grounds.” Id. at 603. The Board’s dismissal of Perry’s appeal was based on a finding that the settlement agreement was voluntary. This finding is a procedural determination about the validity of the agreement, not a conclusion that the Board lacked the statutory authority to hear the case. Like a dismissal for untimeliness, a dismissal for lack of jurisdiction based on voluntariness is a procedural bar that prevents the Board from hearing the case, but it does not mean the Board was never empowered to do so.

**II.  The statutory definition of a “mixed case” in 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A) encompasses cases where the Board dismisses for lack of jurisdiction because the action was voluntary.**

The statutory definition of a mixed case requires that the employee “(A) has been affected by an action which [she] may appeal to the Merits Systems Protection Board.” 5 U.S.C. § 7702(a)(1)(A). In *Kloeckner*, the Court held that the employee was affected by an action “appealable to the MSPB” despite the Board’s dismissal for untimeliness. 568 U.S. at 604. The Court reasoned that the action was appealable because the Board *could* have heard the case if the procedural defect had been cured. Id.

The same logic applies here. Perry was “affected by an action” (the settlement and resulting suspension) that was appealable to the Board *if* it were involuntary. The Board’s finding that the action was voluntary is a procedural determination that the action was *not* appealable. Because the action was appealable in the abstract, the statutory language of § 7702(a)(1)(A) is satisfied. The Board’s jurisdictional dismissal is therefore a procedural bar, and the *Kloeckner* exception mandates review in district court.

**III.  The D.C. Circuit’s distinction in *Powell* is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *Kloeckner* and creates an impermissible forum shopping problem.**

The D.C. Circuit’s reliance on *Powell v. Dept of Def.* to transfer this case is misplaced. *Powell* predates *Kloeckner* and established that jurisdictional dismissals go to the Federal Circuit. 158 F.3d 597, 599 (D.C. Cir. 1998). However, *Kloeckner* effectively overruled the portion of *Powell* that would require a jurisdictional dismissal to be heard in the Federal Circuit. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Kloeckner* suggests that the distinction between procedural and jurisdictional dismissals is not as bright as the D.C. Circuit claims. By forcing jurisdictional dismissals into the Federal Circuit, the D.C. Circuit creates a forum shopping problem where the agency can manipulate the outcome by framing a finding of voluntariness as a jurisdictional bar rather than a procedural one. The Supreme Court’s focus on “procedural grounds” indicates a broader category of dismissals that should be reviewed in district court to ensure that discrimination claims are heard in a neutral forum. Therefore, the D.C. Circuit’s decision to transfer this case to the Federal Circuit is erroneous.
