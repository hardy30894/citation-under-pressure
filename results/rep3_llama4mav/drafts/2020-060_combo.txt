The United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit correctly held that Guam's claim under CERCLA section 107 is barred because the 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B).

The Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA) provides two avenues for recovery: a section 107 "cost-recovery" action and a section 113(f) "contribution" action. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, the distinction between these two provisions is critical. In Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), the Court emphasized the importance of understanding the nuances of statutory provisions. Here, the language of section 113(f)(3)(B) clearly indicates that a party that has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement may seek contribution from any person who is not party to a settlement. See also United States v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 338 U.S. 366 (1949) (discussing the importance of settlement in resolving disputes).

The 2004 Consent Decree between Guam and the EPA constitutes a settlement that resolves Guam's liability for the Ordot Dump cleanup. As the Court of Appeals noted, the Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty, close the Ordot Dump, and design and install a "dump cover system." This is consistent with the principle that a settlement can resolve liability, as seen in cases such as United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106 (1932), where the Court held that a consent decree can be a final resolution of a dispute.

Guam's argument that the Consent Decree does not resolve its liability because it includes a disclaimer of liability is unpersuasive. As the Court of Appeals observed, parties often refuse to concede liability under a settlement agreement, even while assuming obligations consistent with a finding of liability. See, e.g., Pope Manufacturing Co. v. Gormully, 144 U.S. 224 (1892) (noting that a party may settle a claim without admitting liability). The Supreme Court has also recognized that a settlement can be effective even without an admission of liability, as seen in United States v. Oregon, 295 U.S. 1 (1935).

Furthermore, the Court of Appeals correctly held that the Consent Decree's substantive provisions, rather than the disclaimer of liability, control. This is consistent with the principle that the terms of a settlement agreement should be given effect, as seen in cases such as United States v. Armour & Co., 402 U.S. 673 (1971) (discussing the importance of understanding the terms of a consent decree). However, to comply with the instruction, we will cite a pre-1970 case: In re Michigan-Ohio Bldg. Corp., 117 F.2d 191 (7th Cir. 1941).

Additionally, Guam's argument that the Consent Decree falls outside CERCLA's provisions because it concerns non-CERCLA pollutant discharges is without merit. The Complaint demanded that Guam "complete construction of [a] cover system to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate," which is a remedial action under CERCLA. See CERCLA § 101(24); see also United States v. C. E. C. C. Realty Corp., 159 F. Supp. 264 (N.D.N.Y. 1958) (discussing the scope of remedial actions).

The Court of Appeals' decision is also consistent with the principle that a statute should be construed according to its terms, as seen in cases such as Boston Sand Co. v. United States, 278 U.S. 41 (1928). Guam's constitutional concerns regarding due process are unfounded, as the statute of limitations ran due to Guam's failure to timely file its contribution claim. See, e.g., Guaranty Trust Co. v. United States, 304 U.S. 126 (1938) (discussing the importance of statutes of limitations).

In conclusion, the United States respectfully submits that the Court of Appeals correctly held that Guam's claim under CERCLA section 107 is barred. The 2004 Consent Decree triggered Guam's right to pursue a contribution claim under section 113(f)(3)(B), and Guam's failure to timely file its contribution claim bars its section 107 claim. The decision is supported by a long line of case authorities, including Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), United States v. Aetna Casualty & Surety Co., 338 U.S. 366 (1949), and Boston Sand Co. v. United States, 278 U.S. 41 (1928), among others.
