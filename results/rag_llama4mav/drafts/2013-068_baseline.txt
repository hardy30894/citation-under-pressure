The law, which creates a 35-foot buffer zone around the entrances, exits, and driveways of abortion clinics, is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time-place-manner regulation that protects the rights of prospective patients and clinic employees without offending the First Amendment rights of others.

The petitioners argue that the law restricts their ability to engage in "sidewalk counseling" and other forms of expression outside abortion clinics. However, the First Circuit correctly held that the law is content-neutral, as it does not regulate speech based on its content, but rather regulates the place and manner of speech to ensure public safety and protect the rights of clinic patients and employees. See Hill v. Colorado, 530 U.S. 703, 719-20 (2000) (upholding a similar Colorado statute regulating communicative activities within 100 feet of healthcare facility entrances).

However, the First Circuit correctly held that the law is narrowly tailored, as it exempts certain categories of individuals, including clinic employees, law enforcement, and individuals using the public sidewalk or street right-of-way for purposes other than accessing the clinic. See McCullen v. Coakley, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009) (upholding the law against a facial challenge).

These cases involved content-based and speaker-based speech restrictions, which are not at issue here. The Massachusetts law is a content-neutral regulation that is subject to intermediate scrutiny, and the First Circuit correctly held that it satisfies this standard. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 725-26 (applying intermediate scrutiny to a content-neutral regulation).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court's ruling in Hill v. Colorado is directly applicable to this case. The Colorado statute at issue in Hill was similar to the Massachusetts law, and the Court upheld it as a valid time-place-manner regulation. See Hill, 530 U.S. at 735-36. The petitioners argue that Hill should be limited or overruled, but there is no compelling reason to do so. The Hill decision was a careful and considered ruling that balanced the competing interests of public safety, patient access, and free speech.

See McCullen, 571 F.3d at 181-82. The petitioners have not shown that the law burdens their ability to communicate effectively with their intended audience.
