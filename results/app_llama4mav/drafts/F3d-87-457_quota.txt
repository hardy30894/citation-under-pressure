Argument

The Florida League of Professional Lobbyists, Inc. (the "League") challenges the constitutionality of Chapter 93-121, Laws of Florida, which regulates lobbying activities in the state. Specifically, the League contests the statute's disclosure provisions and the ban on contingency-fee lobbying.

I. The Disclosure Provisions are Unconstitutionally Overbroad

The League argues that the disclosure provisions of the statute are facially invalid due to their overbreadth. The statute requires lobbyists to disclose all lobbying expenditures, including those made by their principals, and to categorize these expenditures. Fla.Stats. § 11.045(3)(a). This requirement burdens the First Amendment rights of lobbyists and their principals, as it compels them to reveal information about their lobbying activities. See Buckley v. Valeo, 424 U.S. 1, 64 (1976) (noting that disclosure requirements can have a "chilling effect" on First Amendment activity).

The Supreme Court has held that a statute is facially invalid if it is "substantially overbroad." Broadrick v. Oklahoma, 413 U.S. 601, 615 (1973). To determine whether a statute is substantially overbroad, courts consider whether the statute's "legitimate sweep" is outweighed by its potential to chill protected speech. See Virginia v. Hicks, 539 U.S. 113, 118-19 (2003). Here, the statute's disclosure provisions are likely to chill protected speech, as they require lobbyists to disclose sensitive information about their activities. See McIntyre v. Ohio Elections Comm'n, 514 U.S. 334, 341-42 (1995) (holding that an Ohio statute prohibiting the distribution of anonymous campaign literature was unconstitutional).

Furthermore, the statute's failure to provide clear guidance on what constitutes "lobbying" or "lobbying expenditures" exacerbates the risk of overbreadth. See Smith v. Goguen, 415 U.S. 566, 572-73 (1974) (striking down a statute that was "void for vagueness"). The League's members are thus faced with the daunting task of determining what information to disclose, and risk civil sanctions if they err. See Abbott Lab. v. Gardner, 387 U.S. 136, 152-53 (1967) (holding that a challenge to a regulation was ripe where appellants faced a choice between complying with the regulation and risking "serious" civil penalties).

II. The Contingency-Fee Ban is Unconstitutional in Light of Recent Supreme Court Precedent

The League also challenges the statute's ban on contingency-fee lobbying, arguing that it is unconstitutional in light of recent Supreme Court decisions. While this Court has previously upheld similar bans, see, e.g., In re Primus, 436 U.S. 412, 438 n. 32 (1978) (citing Williamson v. Lee Optical Co., 348 U.S. 483 (1955)), more recent cases have cast doubt on the constitutionality of such provisions. See, e.g., Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 365-66 (2010) (striking down a ban on corporate independent expenditures); see also Lorillard Tobacco Co. v. Reilly, 533 U.S. 525, 564-65 (2001) (striking down a ban on outdoor advertising of tobacco products).

The Supreme Court has held that regulations that burden speech must be narrowly tailored to serve a compelling government interest. See FEC v. Nat'l Conservative PAC, 470 U.S. 480, 496-97 (1985). Here, the ban on contingency-fee lobbying is not narrowly tailored, as it prohibits all contingency-fee arrangements, regardless of their potential impact on the legislative process. See Edenfield v. Fane, 507 U.S. 761, 770-71 (1993) (striking down a ban on in-person solicitation by certified public accountants).

In light of these recent decisions, the League argues that the contingency-fee ban is unconstitutional. While this Court may be bound by earlier precedent, see, e.g., Rodriguez de Quijas v. Shearson/American Express, Inc., 490 U.S. 477, 484 (1989) (holding that lower courts are bound by Supreme Court precedent), the League urges this Court to reconsider the constitutionality of the ban in light of the evolving Supreme Court jurisprudence.

In conclusion, the League respectfully submits that Chapter 93-121, Laws of Florida, is unconstitutional due to its overbroad disclosure provisions and its ban on contingency-fee lobbying. This Court should reverse the district court's grant of summary judgment and hold that the statute is facially invalid.
