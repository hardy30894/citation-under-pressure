**ARGUMENT**

The Court should reject petitioners’ sweeping rule that a fee enhancement may never rest solely on superior performance and exceptional results. That position is inconsistent with this Court’s fee-shifting precedents, which hold that the lodestar is the “presumptively reasonable fee,” but do not make it irrebuttable. *Hensley v. Eckerhart*, 461 U.S. 424, 434 (1983); *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897 (1984); *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 564-66 (1986) (“Delaware Valley I”). In the “rare” and “exceptional” case, a district court may enhance the lodestar when the record contains specific evidence that counsel’s quality of service was superior to what the market rate would ordinarily capture and that the results were exceptional. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-68.

1. **This Court has never adopted a categorical bar on enhancements for superior performance and results.**

In *Blum*, the Court rejected an enhancement on the facts before it because the plaintiffs had not shown that quality and results were not already reflected in the lodestar. 465 U.S. at 898-900. But the Court also stated that “in some cases of exceptional success an enhancement award may be justified.” *Id.* at 901. That is not a prohibition; it is a recognition that the lodestar may undercompensate in an uncommon case. *Id.* Similarly, *Delaware Valley I* reaffirmed that “upward adjustments of the lodestar figure are still permissible” in “rare” and “exceptional” cases supported by “specific evidence” and “detailed findings.” 478 U.S. at 565.

Later, in *City of Burlington v. Dague*, the Court held that enhancements for contingency risk are not available under certain fee-shifting statutes because they would compensate for something outside the merits of the representation. 505 U.S. 557, 562-67 (1992). But *Dague* did not displace *Blum*’s and *Delaware Valley I*’s recognition that a lodestar may be adjusted when the lodestar itself fails to account for extraordinary performance in an exceptional case. *Id.* at 562; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901.

2. **Superior performance and exceptional results may justify enhancement when supported by record evidence.**

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the district court’s fee determination must be grounded in the evidentiary record and in reasoned findings. *Hensley*, 461 U.S. at 437; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-901; *Perdue v. Kenny A.*, 559 U.S. 542, 552-56 (2010). In *Perdue*, the Court clarified that enhancements are permissible only in “rare” and “exceptional” circumstances and only where the applicant produces “specific evidence” that the lodestar fee would not have been adequate to attract competent counsel. *Id.* at 552, 554. But *Perdue* did not create an absolute rule forbidding enhancements based on superior quality or results; it reaffirmed that such enhancements may be available when properly proven, while warning that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors.” *Id.* at 553-54 (quoting *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 483 U.S. 711, 727 (1987) (plurality opinion)).

The Court’s pre-*Perdue* cases are in the same vein. In *Hensley*, the Court instructed that the “degree of success obtained” is “the most critical factor” in determining the reasonableness of a fee. 461 U.S. at 436. In *Farrar v. Hobby*, the Court likewise held that results matter centrally to fee reasonableness. 506 U.S. 103, 114 (1992). And in *Riverside v. Rivera*, the Court recognized that fee awards under § 1988 are not mechanically tied to the monetary value of relief, but to vindication of important rights. 477 U.S. 561, 574-78 (1986) (plurality opinion). These decisions confirm that results are not irrelevant extras; they are part of the reasonableness inquiry.

3. **The Eleventh Circuit’s approach is consistent with this Court’s cases and the realities of district-court fee adjudication.**

The Eleventh Circuit correctly recognized that district courts, which “are intimately familiar with the case,” are best positioned to assess whether the lodestar fully reflects exceptional lawyering and results. *Norman v. Housing Authority of Montgomery*, 836 F.2d 1292, 1304 (11th Cir. 1988); *NAACP v. City of Evergreen*, 812 F.2d 1332, 1336-37 (11th Cir. 1987). Other circuits have likewise permitted enhancements in rare cases supported by specific evidence. *Quaratino v. Tiffany & Co.*, 166 F.3d 422, 425 (2d Cir. 1999); *Hyatt v. Apfel*, 195 F.3d 188, 192 (4th Cir. 1999); *Shipes v. Trinity Industries*, 987 F.2d 311, 320 (5th Cir. 1993); *Geier v. Sundquist*, 372 F.3d 784, 794-95 (6th Cir. 2004); *Forshee v. Waterloo Industries, Inc.*, 178 F.3d 527, 532 (8th Cir. 1999); *Van Gerwen v. Guarantee Mutual Life Co.*, 214 F.3d 1041, 1046 (9th Cir. 2000); *Roe v. Cheyenne Mountain Conference Resort, Inc.*, 124 F.3d 1221, 1233 n.8 (10th Cir. 1997).

4. **The award here falls within that narrow category.**

The district court found, based on its direct observation and a detailed record, that counsel achieved extraordinary systemic relief and performed at an exceptional level in a complex, prolonged institutional-reform case. Those are exactly the circumstances in which this Court’s precedents permit a court to conclude that the lodestar alone does not fully compensate the attorney’s fee reasonably necessary to attract similarly capable counsel. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899-901; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-68; *Perdue*, 559 U.S. at 553-56.

Because the Court should not impose a categorical ban where its precedents permit a narrowly cabined enhancement, the judgment below should be affirmed.
