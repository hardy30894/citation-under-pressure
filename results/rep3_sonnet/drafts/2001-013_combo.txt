**ARGUMENT**

**I. Section 4(b)(1) Reflects Congress's Traditional Insistence on a Single, Uniform Body of Law Governing Maritime Workers, and the Coast Guard, Not OSHA, Occupies That Field.**

The general maritime law has long been understood to require "harmony and uniformity" in the regulation of shipboard life, a principle this Court articulated in *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205 (1917), where the Court held that local regulation is invalid if it "works material prejudice to the characteristic features of the general maritime law or interferes with the proper harmony and uniformity of that law." *Id.* at 216. That same concern for uniformity animated *Panama R.R. Co. v. Johnson*, 264 U.S. 375 (1924), which recognized Congress's constitutional prerogative to revise maritime law only through legislation of general, not fragmented, application. Section 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act, 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1), carries this historic principle forward by withdrawing OSHA's authority wherever "another Federal agency" already "exercise[s] statutory authority" over the working conditions at issue. Congress did not intend, and this Court's precedents do not permit, two federal regulators to simultaneously govern the same vessel-based workforce.

**II. Seamen Aboard Vessels Have Always Occupied a Distinct Legal Category, Committed to a Single Regulatory and Remedial Scheme Rather Than Overlapping Regimes.**

This Court has repeatedly recognized that seamen occupy a unique status under federal law, separate from ordinary land-based employees, precisely because their working conditions are inseparable from the vessel itself. In *Swanson v. Marra Bros.*, 328 U.S. 1 (1946), the Court held that Congress structured the Jones Act and the Longshoremen's and Harbor Workers' Compensation Act as "mutually exclusive" systems turning on whether the worker is a "master or member of a crew of any vessel," *id.* at 7, precisely to avoid the confusion of dual coverage. The Court reaffirmed that exclusive, either-or allocation in *South Chicago Coal & Dock Co. v. Bassett*, 309 U.S. 251 (1940), *Norton v. Warner Co.*, 321 U.S. 565 (1944), and *Desper v. Starved Rock Ferry Co.*, 342 U.S. 187 (1952), each holding that once a worker qualifies as a member of a vessel's crew, he falls outside the compensation and regulatory scheme applicable to land-based maritime workers. These decisions confirm a consistent congressional (and judicial) practice: rather than layering multiple federal regimes atop one another for the same class of maritime worker, Congress and the courts have assigned crew members to a single, coherent body of law. Section 4(b)(1) simply extends that longstanding practice to the safety context, assigning seamen's working conditions exclusively to the Coast Guard rather than subjecting the same vessel to competing OSHA and Coast Guard directives.

**III. Petitioner's Employees Aboard the Drilling Barge Were Seamen, and the Nature of the Vessel Does Not Remove Them From the Coast Guard's Exclusive Sphere.**

The Fifth Circuit correctly recognized, consistent with its own settled precedent, that workers assigned to a special-purpose vessel such as a drilling barge qualify as seamen. *Producers Drilling Co. v. Gray*, 361 F.2d 432 (5th Cir. 1966), held that employees whose duties contribute to the operation and mission of such a vessel are seamen notwithstanding the specialized, non-transportation function of the craft. *Offshore Co. v. Robison*, 266 F.2d 769 (5th Cir. 1959), likewise established that a worker need not be a traditional "blue water" sailor to receive seaman status; it is enough that he is permanently assigned to a vessel and his duties contribute to its function. Once workers are properly classified as seamen serving aboard a vessel, their working conditions—wherever that vessel operates—fall within the historic and statutory competence of the Coast Guard, not OSHA.

Nor can the absence of "inspected vessel" status remove Rig 52 from that exclusive sphere. The Coast Guard's authority over vessel safety has never been confined to inspected vessels; Congress has long empowered the Coast Guard to promulgate safety standards for uninspected craft as well. This comprehensive statutory design mirrors the reasoning of *Kelly v. Washington ex rel. Foss Co.*, 302 U.S. 1 (1937), in which the Court examined whether a pervasive federal regulatory scheme governing vessel safety displaced overlapping local requirements, looking not to isolated gaps in coverage but to the overall scope and purpose of the federal scheme. The Coast Guard's regulatory reach over uninspected vessels—covering firefighting equipment, lifesaving gear, ventilation, and casualty investigation, as occurred here—demonstrates the very kind of comprehensive occupation of the field that *Kelly* recognized as sufficient to exclude duplicative regulation. Nothing in the Act's text conditions the § 4(b)(1) exemption on the degree of Coast Guard oversight; it asks only whether the Coast Guard "exercise[s] statutory authority" over the working conditions—authority it plainly possesses, and exercised here through its investigation and applicable safety regulations, regardless of Rig 52's inspection classification.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Fifth Circuit should be affirmed.
