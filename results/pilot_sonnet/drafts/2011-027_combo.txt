# ARGUMENT

## I. THE EQUAL-FOOTING TITLE TEST ASKS WHETHER A RIVER, VIEWED AS A CONTINUOUS HIGHWAY OF COMMERCE, WAS NAVIGABLE IN FACT AT STATEHOOD—NOT WHETHER EVERY ISOLATED STRETCH BENEATH A LATER-BUILT DAM INDEPENDENTLY SATISFIES THAT TEST.

When Montana entered the Union in 1889, it took title to the beds of its navigable rivers under the equal-footing doctrine first recognized in *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212 (1845), which held that the "shores of navigable waters, and the soils under them" belong to the States as "incident to their sovereignty." *Id.* at 230. This Court's task in defining navigability for title purposes has always been functional, not formalistic: a water is navigable if it is "used, or susceptible of being used, in [its] ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870). That inquiry examines the river as a continuous artery of transportation, because it is the river's *capacity to serve commerce*—not the topography of any single quarter-mile reach—that determines whether the State's sovereign interest attached at statehood. *See Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876) (title to soil beneath navigable waters passes to the States "as an incident of sovereignty").

This whole-river approach explains why courts applying the navigability-for-title test have long treated localized obstructions—falls, rapids, or short unnavigable reaches—as incidents of an otherwise navigable highway rather than as independent jurisdictional units. In *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), this Court confronted a river broken by rock-strewn shoals that made continuous navigation impossible without portage. The Court nonetheless held the river navigable, explaining that the interruption did not "destroy the navigable character of the river" where boats could be, and historically were, portaged around the obstruction to continue commerce on the waters above and below. *Id.* at 118, 123–24. The same logic governs here: PPL's dams sit on rivers—including the Missouri at the Great Falls, historically portaged by Lewis and Clark themselves—whose navigable character as continuous highways of trade is not erased by isolated stretches that required portage.

Where this Court has divided a river into discrete reaches, it has done so only at *natural* discontinuities in the river's physical character, not at every point where a modern dam happens to sit. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court separated the Colorado and Green Rivers into stretches for navigability purposes, but the boundaries it drew tracked canyons and rapids that render passage "impracticable," 283 U.S. at 87—naturally occurring breaks in the river's physical condition at statehood, not points selected by a twentieth-century licensee's engineering choices. Nothing in *Utah* supports carving a single continuous river into as many "segments" as there are dams built decades after admission to the Union.

## II. THE PRESUMPTION FAVORING STATE TITLE AND THE SUSCEPTIBILITY STANDARD CONFIRM THAT THE TRIAL COURT'S APPROACH WAS CORRECT.

Two further principles reinforce that the District Court properly assessed navigability by examining the character of these rivers as a whole rather than demanding stretch-specific proof keyed to modern dam footprints. First, this Court has repeatedly held that conveyances of sovereign title to submerged lands are not lightly inferred, and that doubts are resolved in favor of the State. *See United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49, 55 (1926) ("[T]he rule is settled that grants by the United States of its public lands bounded on streams and other waters, without any specific declaration of intention, are to be construed favorably to the [ownership passing to the State]."); *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14 (1935) (placing the burden of overcoming the presumption of state title on the party asserting otherwise). PPL, having built its dams on these rivers for nearly a century without ever paying compensation, bore the burden of proving non-navigability segment by segment; it did not carry that burden merely by pointing to the presence of its own facilities.

Second, navigability for title purposes has never demanded that a river be navigable in its "ordinary condition" without any need for portage, improvement, or seasonal variation; it is enough that the river was *susceptible* of use as a highway of commerce. *See United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407–08 (1940) (navigability may be shown by susceptibility to use "by reasonable improvement," and need not be defeated by discontinuities that a "negligible part" of the watercourse presents). The rivers at issue—used historically to float logs, transport furs, and carry commerce along their full lengths, as documented in the historical record properly admitted below—satisfy that standard when considered, as *The Daniel Ball* and *Economy Light* require, as continuous highways rather than as a patchwork of isolated dam sites.

Because the constitutional test asks whether the river as a whole was navigable in fact at statehood, and because the District Court's findings faithfully applied that standard, the judgment below should be affirmed.
