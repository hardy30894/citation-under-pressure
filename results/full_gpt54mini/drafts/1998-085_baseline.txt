**Argument**

Title VII does not require proof that an employer acted “egregiously” or “outrageously,” independent of its mental state, before punitive damages may be awarded. Congress supplied the governing standard in 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1): punitive damages are available when the defendant acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights” of the plaintiff. That text focuses on the employer’s state of mind, not on a freestanding threshold of outrageousness. When Congress uses statutory language that is “clear,” courts are not free to add extra-statutory elements. See Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 U.S. 244, 252-55 (1994); St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993).

This Court’s Title VII framework likewise confirms that discriminatory intent, not some heightened degree of brutality, is the relevant inquiry. Under McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792, 802-05 (1973), a plaintiff proves intentional discrimination by establishing a prima facie case and discrediting the employer’s explanation. As this Court explained below, once a factfinder rejects the employer’s stated reasons, it may infer discrimination; “no additional proof of discrimination is required.” St. Mary’s Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993). That principle would be undermined if punitive damages required an added showing that the conduct was independently “egregious” or “outrageous” beyond the statutory mental-state requirement.

The statutory structure reinforces that Congress intentionally separated liability from the punitive-damages trigger. Section 1981a(a) authorizes compensatory and punitive damages for intentional discrimination, and § 1981a(b)(1) narrows punitive recovery to cases of “malice or reckless indifference.” Congress also capped such awards by employer size, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3), demonstrating that it calibrated punishment through express statutory limits, not through an amorphous judicial requirement of outrageousness. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 252-55.

ADA’s proposed rule would rewrite the statute. It would make punitive damages available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases,” but Congress never used that phrase. The better reading is the one the courts of appeals have long adopted in analogous punitive-damages settings: the focus is on the defendant’s knowledge that it may be acting in violation of federal law, not on whether the facts are uniquely shocking. See, e.g., Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 56 (1983) (punitive damages available where the defendant’s conduct is motivated by evil motive or involves reckless or callous indifference to federally protected rights); Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30, 51-52 (1983) (punitive damages do not require proof of actual malice in every case). The statutory phrase Congress chose in § 1981a(b)(1) tracks that well-established formulation.

ADA relies on legislative history to impose an added “egregiousness” element, but legislative history cannot override enacted text. See Landgraf, 511 U.S. at 263. And the history here is at best mixed: Congress enacted the present language after considering competing formulations, including broader language in the House version. Where the final statute says “malice or reckless indifference,” courts should not graft on an independent “outrageousness” requirement that Congress omitted.

Respondent Kolstad need not show that ADA’s discrimination was shocking in the abstract. She must show only what § 1981a(b)(1) requires: that ADA acted with malice or reckless indifference to her federally protected rights. The jury could readily find that standard satisfied if ADA, through decisionmakers aware of Title VII, intentionally denied her a promotion because she is a woman. See McDonnell Douglas, 411 U.S. at 802-05; Hicks, 509 U.S. at 511. That is the proper inquiry on remand. The question is not whether ADA’s conduct was independently “egregious,” but whether the evidence proves the statutory mental state.
