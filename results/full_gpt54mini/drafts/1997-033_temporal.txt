Section 1407(a) authorizes transfer only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and it commands that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel” to the original district “at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). A district court cannot use 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a) to defeat that statutory command and keep the case for trial. The statutory text leaves no room for a transferee court to convert a pretrial assignment into a trial assignment.

This Court has repeatedly held that transfer statutes must be applied according to their terms, not judicial convenience. In United States v. Union Pacific Railroad Co., 98 U.S. 569 (1879), the Court explained that when Congress has prescribed the forum and the mode of proceeding, courts are not at liberty to substitute a different one. The same principle controls here: Congress specified both the purpose of § 1407 transfer and the end point of that transfer—remand. A transferee court’s self-assignment for trial rewrites the statute.

Nor can § 1404(a) be read to authorize what § 1407(a) forbids. Section 1404(a) permits transfer “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but it presupposes a venue transfer otherwise available under the venue laws. See Van Dusen v. Barrack, 376 U.S. 612, 616-17 (1964). It is a general venue provision; § 1407(a) is a specific multidistrict provision. Under settled interpretive principles, the specific governs the general where the two are in tension. See Rodgers v. United States, 185 U.S. 83, 87-88 (1902); Bulova Watch Co. v. United States, 181 U.S. 131, 136 (1901). Reading § 1404(a) to permit a transferee judge to keep an MDL case for trial would nullify § 1407(a)’s mandatory remand clause.

The legislative structure confirms that conclusion. Congress enacted § 1407 to permit centralization only “for pretrial proceedings,” not to create a roving trial court. See In re Petrol and Petroleum Products Antitrust Litigation, 367 F.2d 608, 613 (3d Cir. 1966) (describing § 1407 as a pretrial-coordination device). When Congress limits a procedural device to a defined purpose, courts may not enlarge it by implication. See United States v. Boisdore’s Heirs, 49 U.S. (8 How.) 113, 122 (1850). If a transferee judge may simply invoke § 1404(a) to retain the case, the statutory guarantee of remand becomes meaningless surplusage, contrary to the rule that every word of a statute should be given effect. See Market Co. v. Hoffman, 101 U.S. 112, 115-16 (1879).

The Ninth Circuit’s reasoning below cannot be squared with those principles. The panel relied on convenience and on its view that the parties had already spent time in Arizona. But convenience cannot override a mandatory statutory command. In Railroad Co. v. Grant, 98 U.S. 398, 401 (1879), the Court held that equitable considerations cannot justify a result contrary to statute. Congress balanced convenience against the structure of multidistrict litigation and chose remand. Courts must enforce that choice.

Milberg may argue that trial in Arizona would be efficient because discovery occurred there. But efficiency is not the statutory standard. In Gulf Oil Corp. v. Gilbert, 330 U.S. 501, 508-09 (1947), the Court recognized that forum decisions require weighing private and public interests, but it also stressed that the plaintiff’s chosen forum is entitled to substantial respect. Here, Congress went further and required remand. Efficiency cannot authorize a transferee court to keep a case that Congress said “shall be remanded.”

Because § 1407(a) required remand to the Northern District of Illinois, the Arizona district court lacked authority to assign the case to itself for trial under § 1404(a). The judgment should be reversed.
