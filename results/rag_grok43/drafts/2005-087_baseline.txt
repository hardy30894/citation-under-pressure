The Fourteenth Amendment does not compel Arizona to admit expert mental-health evidence for the purpose of negating mens rea when the defendant has already invoked the state’s insanity defense. Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790 (1952), establishes that a State may “characterize the insanity defense as it chooses” and may require the defendant to shoulder the burden of proving legal insanity. Id. at 799. The same decision confirms that the prosecution remains obligated to prove “beyond a reasonable doubt every element of the crime charged, including . . . intent,” yet it permits the State to channel evidence of mental abnormality through the affirmative defense rather than through a freestanding mens-rea rebuttal. Id. at 794-95.

Rivera v. Delaware, 429 U.S. 877 (1976), reaffirmed Leland’s allocation of proof and rejected the contention that evidence relevant to insanity must also be treated as automatically relevant to mens rea. The Arizona rule at issue here does no more than enforce that permissible allocation: expert testimony is received on the insanity claim, and the defendant bears the burden he elected to assume when he raised that claim. Jones v. United States, 463 U.S. 354 (1983), likewise recognizes that “a defendant could be required to prove his insanity by a higher standard than a preponderance of the evidence,” id. at 368 n.18, without offending due process.

Because the State may constitutionally confine mental-health evidence to the insanity defense it has defined, the trial court’s exclusion of the same evidence offered solely to negate intent did not deprive Clark of any Fourteenth Amendment right. The judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
