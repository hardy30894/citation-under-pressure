The Eighth Amendment forbids only those punishments involving "torture or a lingering death" or "unnecessary cruelty." In re Kemmler, 136 U.S. 436, 447 (1890); Wilkerson v. Utah, 99 U.S. 130, 136 (1878). Capital punishment for murder has never been understood to fall within that prohibition. See Louisiana ex rel. Francis v. Resweber, 329 U.S. 459, 464 (1947) ("The cruelty against which the Constitution protects a convicted man is cruelty inherent in the method of punishment, not the necessary suffering involved in any method employed to extinguish life humanely.").

Mental retardation supplies no basis for a different result. The Amendment reaches "something more than the mere extinguishment of life," Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349, 367 (1910), yet it leaves to the legislature the ordinary incidents of criminal responsibility, including the range of persons subject to a penalty long authorized for the offense. See Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 99 (1958) (plurality opinion) (recognizing that the clause "must draw its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society," without displacing legislative judgments on who may receive a traditional punishment). No pre-1970 decision of this Court has held that reduced mental capacity exempts an offender from a penalty otherwise permissible for the crime.

The judgment of the Supreme Court of Virginia should be affirmed.
