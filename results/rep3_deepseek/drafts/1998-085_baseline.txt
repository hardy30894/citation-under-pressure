**ARGUMENT**

The question presented is whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, to support an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The answer is yes. The text, common-law background, and legislative history of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a all confirm that punitive damages require conduct far more blameworthy than simple intentional discrimination.

**I. THE STATUTORY TEXT REQUIRES A STATE OF MIND FAR BEYOND MERE INTENTIONAL DISCRIMINATION.**

Section 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only if the plaintiff “demonstrates that the [defendant] engaged in a discriminatory practice … with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This language deliberately sets a higher bar than the “intentional discrimination” standard for liability under Title VII. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 2000e-2(a). “Malice” and “reckless indifference” connote a level of culpability that is not satisfied by a showing that the employer merely intended to discriminate. *Cf. Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 49 (1983) (under § 1983, punitive damages require “reckless or callous indifference to the federally protected rights of others,” a standard the Court described as “something more than mere negligence”). It is true that *Smith v. Wade* rejected a requirement of “actual malice” in the sense of ill will, but it also did not equate reckless indifference with bare intentional discrimination. 461 U.S. at 51-52. The statute’s use of “malice or reckless indifference” thus demands an additional element: the employer must have acted with a state of mind that is egregious in its disregard for the plaintiff’s rights.

**II. COMMON LAW PUNITIVE DAMAGES PRECEDENT CONFIRMS THE NEED FOR EGREGIOUS CONDUCT.**

At common law, punitive damages have always required “something more than the mere commission of a tort.” *Rowlett v. Anheuser-Busch, Inc.*, 832 F.2d 194, 205 (1st Cir. 1987) (applying § 1981). This “something more” has been described as conduct that is “outrageous,” “gross,” or “wanton.” *Id.* Congress enacted § 1981a against this common-law backdrop, and it is presumed to have intended to incorporate these settled standards. *See Patterson v. McLean Credit Union*, 491 U.S. 164, 215 (1989) (Congress legislates against the background of existing law). Numerous courts applied an egregiousness requirement to § 1981 punitive damages claims before 1991. *E.g., Beauford v. Sisters of Mercy-Province of Detroit, Inc.*, 816 F.2d 1104, 1109 (6th Cir. 1987) (requiring “a reckless or callous disregard for the plaintiff’s rights, intentional discrimination, or *outrageous conduct*” (emphasis added)). The 1991 Act’s incorporation of the “malice or reckless indifference” standard did not depart from that tradition. To the contrary, the House Report accompanying the Act stated that punitive damages are available only “where the defendant has engaged in *egregious* misconduct.” H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 72-73 (1991) (emphasis added). Although the panel majority questioned this Report because it described an earlier version of the bill, the difference in wording between “callous indifference” (in the House bill) and “reckless indifference” (in the final version) is immaterial to the egregiousness requirement. *See* Dissenting opinion of Judge Williams, *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 108 F.3d 1431, 1444 (D.C. Cir. 1997) (Williams, J., dissenting) (“The only conceivably material difference in wording is that the House bill allowed punitive damages on a broader basis … the key difference being addition of the word ‘callous’ in the alternative.”). The House Report’s reference to “egregious misconduct” reflects a consistent congressional intent to impose a high threshold.

**III. LEGISLATIVE HISTORY PLAINLY INDICATES THAT PUNITIVE DAMAGES ARE AVAILABLE ONLY IN “EXTRAORDINARILY EGREGIOUS” CASES.**

The interpretive memorandum of Senator Dole and other Senators, the key authoritative statement on the final compromise, expressly provides: “Only in the most *extraordinarily egregious* cases should punitive damages be available.” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (Oct. 30, 1991) (Interp. Memo of Sen. Dole et al.) (emphasis added). This statement is not an outlier; it reflects the bipartisan compromise that allowed the 1991 Act to pass. The respondent is not arguing that the phrase “extraordinarily egregious” appears in the statute’s text. But as Judge Williams observed, the Dole memorandum is “the most direct evidence of the legislative intent” and should be given weight. *Kolstad*, 108 F.3d at 1444 (Williams, J., dissenting). The petitioner’s reliance on Representative Edwards’s contrary statement that punitive damages should be available “to the same extent … as under § 1981” merely begs the question; as the circuit split on § 1981 shows, many circuits already required egregious conduct. 137 Cong. Rec. H9527 (Nov. 7, 1991) (Interp. Memo of Rep. Edwards). The two statements are therefore not in conflict—both point toward a standard that demands something more than bare intentional discrimination.

**IV. POLICY CONSIDERATIONS REINFORCE THE NEED FOR AN EGREGIOUSNESS REQUIREMENT.**

Without an egregiousness requirement, every intentional discrimination case would present a jury question on punitive damages, subjecting employers to massive, unpredictable liability and undermining Title VII’s remedial scheme, which caps compensatory and punitive damages at modest levels for all but the largest employers. 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(3). Congress never intended punitive damages to become routine. *See H.R. Rep. No. 102-40(I), at 72-73* (emphasizing that punitive damages should be “available only in the most egregious cases”). The D.C. Circuit panel’s decision, which permitted punitive damages on a showing of intentional discrimination plus “pretext,” effectively eliminated the distinction between liability and punitive damages. This approach cannot be squared with the statute’s clear command that punitive damages require a heightened showing of culpability.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals on the punitive damages issue should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to require that the plaintiff prove that the employer’s conduct was egregious or outrageous, independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII.
