**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to Petitioners Because They Are "Appointee[s] on the Policymaking Level" Under 29 U.S.C. § 630(f).**

The ADEA defines "employee" to exclude "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" (29 U.S.C. § 630(f)). Petitioners argue that judges do not make policy, but the First Circuit rejected this view, holding that "policymaking" is "indisputably a part of the function of judging" (*EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988)). The court noted that judges "establish either general standards or specific rules of decision" that have future implications (*Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55). This aligns with the "exercise of discretion" and "judgment" inherent in the policymaking exception. Furthermore, Missouri judges exercise significant supervisory power, adopting rules for the judiciary and resolving internal disputes (*Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55). Therefore, they fall squarely within the statutory exception.

**II. The Equal Protection Clause Does Not Prohibit Missouri's Mandatory Retirement Age.**

Even assuming the ADEA applies, the Equal Protection Clause does not bar the retirement age. Under rational basis review, a state may treat judges differently if it has a legitimate interest in ensuring judicial competence and efficiency (*Malmed v. Thornburgh*, 621 F.2d 565, 572 (3d Cir. 1980)). The Third Circuit noted that mandatory retirement "eliminates the unpleasantness of selectively removing aged ... judges" (*Malmed*, 621 F.2d at 572). The Seventh Circuit agreed that the work of judges makes "unique and exacting demands on faculties that age tends to erode" (*Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979)). These rationales provide a sufficient basis for Missouri's law.
