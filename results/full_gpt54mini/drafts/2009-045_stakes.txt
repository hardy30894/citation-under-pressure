**ARGUMENT**

Under this Court’s fee-shifting precedents, the answer is no: a reasonable attorneys’ fee may not be enhanced solely because counsel’s performance was excellent or the result was exceptional. The lodestar—reasonable hours multiplied by a reasonable rate—“is presumed to be the reasonable fee,” and it already subsumes the factors ordinarily associated with quality and results. *Pennsylvania v. Delaware Valley Citizens’ Council for Clean Air*, 478 U.S. 546, 564-66 (1986); *Blum v. Stenson*, 465 U.S. 886, 897-900 (1984).

In *Blum*, the Court squarely rejected an enhancement based on “the quality of representation” and “the results obtained” where those considerations were not supported by specific record evidence showing that the lodestar failed to account for them. 465 U.S. at 898-900. The Court explained that “the novelty and complexity of the issues,” “the special skill and experience of counsel,” and the “results obtained” are generally reflected in the reasonable hourly rate and number of hours, so “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance.” *Id.* at 898-900. The Court did note that in a “rare case,” a fee applicant might offer “specific evidence” that the lodestar does not fully capture superior service and exceptional success. *Id.* at 899. But that language does not create a quality-and-results enhancement untethered from something outside the lodestar; it merely recognizes that, in exceptional circumstances, the lodestar may not fully reflect market value. *Id.* at 899-900.

*Delaware Valley I* confirms the same point. There, the Court reaffirmed that the lodestar “includes most, if not all, of the relevant factors constituting a ‘reasonable’ attorney’s fee,” and that “it is unnecessary to enhance the fee for superior performance in order to serve the statutory purpose of enabling plaintiffs to secure legal assistance.” 478 U.S. at 565-66. The Court cautioned that “the overall quality of performance ordinarily should not be used to adjust the lodestar,” though it left open the possibility of adjustment in an extraordinary case supported by specific evidence. *Id.* at 566-68. The governing principle is therefore not that superior performance and superior results independently justify a premium; it is that the lodestar ordinarily already accounts for them. *Id.*; *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-900.

This Court’s decision in *City of Burlington v. Dague* removes any doubt about the risk of double counting. 505 U.S. 557 (1992). There, the Court held that enhancements for contingency are not permitted under federal fee-shifting statutes because the lodestar is designed to produce a fee that is reasonable without supplementation for factors already reflected in the calculation. *Id.* at 562-67. The Court emphasized that departures from the lodestar invite “windfalls” and undermine administrability. *Id.* at 562-63. That reasoning applies with equal force here: if superior quality and results are already baked into a reasonable hourly rate and the hours reasonably expended, a separate enhancement for those same attributes would compensate counsel twice. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 898-900; *Dague*, 505 U.S. at 562-67.

Petitioners rely on the suggestion in *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* that a fee enhancement may be warranted in a “rare” or “exceptional” case. But this Court has repeatedly treated such language as narrow and contingent on record evidence showing that the lodestar does not already provide full compensation. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899-900; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-68. In other words, the question is not whether counsel performed well or achieved excellent results; it is whether those features are not adequately reflected in the lodestar. Absent that showing, enhancement is impermissible. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899-900.

The rule urged by respondents below would collapse the distinction between the lodestar and an enhancement and make fee awards turn on subjective praise for counsel’s work. That is precisely what *Blum* and *Delaware Valley I* sought to avoid. The lodestar method “provides an objective basis on which to make an initial estimate of the value of a lawyer’s services,” and “a presumptively reasonable fee” promotes uniformity and judicial administrability. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 897; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565. Allowing an enhancement based solely on “quality” and “results” would reintroduce the very discretion the Court has consistently cabined.

Accordingly, the Court should hold that a federal fee-shifting statute does not permit a lodestar enhancement based solely on the quality of performance and the results obtained. At most, an enhancement may be considered only where the fee applicant adduces specific evidence that those factors were not already reflected in the lodestar and that the case is truly exceptional. *Blum*, 465 U.S. at 899-900; *Delaware Valley I*, 478 U.S. at 565-68; *Dague*, 505 U.S. at 562-67.
