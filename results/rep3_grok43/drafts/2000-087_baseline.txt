The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize indefinite detention of a removable alien beyond the 90-day removal period. The provision states only that certain aliens “may be detained beyond the removal period,” without specifying any temporal limit. When a statute is “silent on the issue of duration,” courts must determine whether Congress intended to authorize detention “indefinitely or only for a period reasonably necessary to secure removal.” Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 689 (2001) (quoting the governing text).

This construction follows from the settled rule that “where a statute is susceptible of two constructions, by one of which grave and doubtful constitutional questions arise and by the other of which such questions are avoided, our duty is to adopt the latter.” United States v. Delaware & Hudson Co., 213 U.S. 366, 407 (1909). Indefinite detention of admitted aliens raises serious due-process concerns because “freedom from imprisonment—from government custody, detention, or other forms of physical restraint—lies at the heart of the liberty that [the Due Process] Clause protects.” Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 690 (quoting Foucha v. Louisiana, 504 U.S. 71, 80 (1992)). The Fifth Amendment therefore forbids civil detention “unless there is a ‘sufficiently strong’ justification.” Id. at 691 (quoting Kansas v. Hendricks, 521 U.S. 346, 356 (1997)).

The statute’s purpose—to ensure the alien’s presence at the moment of removal—supplies the limiting principle. Once removal is no longer reasonably foreseeable, continued detention ceases to serve that purpose and becomes unauthorized. Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 699. The six-month presumptively reasonable period recognized in the governing precedent implements this limitation without encroaching on the Executive’s authority to effect removal or to impose supervised release. Id. at 701.

Petitioner satisfied that standard. After more than three years of post-removal confinement, no country had accepted him and none was likely to do so in the reasonably foreseeable future. The government offered no evidence rebutting that showing. Under these circumstances, § 1231(a)(6) does not permit further detention.

The judgment ordering release, subject to appropriate conditions of supervision, should be affirmed.
