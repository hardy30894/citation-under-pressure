### Argument

The judgment should be affirmed because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal does not entitle him to habeas relief when he already knew of the right and chose not to appeal. Habeas corpus is not a substitute for direct review and does not lie to correct every procedural omission. *Ex parte Watkins*, 28 U.S. (3 Pet.) 193, 202–03 (1830); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178 (1947); *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 181 (1947). As the Court held in *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962), “collateral relief is not available when all that is shown is a failure to comply with the formal requirements” of a rule. Petitioner therefore must show prejudice, not mere noncompliance.

He cannot. Rule 32(a)(2) serves to protect a defendant who might otherwise be unaware of the right to appeal. Where the defendant already knows of that right, the rule’s purpose has been satisfied despite the district court’s omission. The Court’s harmless-error decisions confirm that result. In *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), the Court declined to reverse for technical error that did not affect substantial rights. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760 (1946), the Court explained that an error warrants relief only if it had a substantial influence on the outcome. And in *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), the Court again asked whether the error had a real effect on the judgment. Here, the district court found after an evidentiary hearing that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at sentencing. On that finding, the omitted advisement did not cause him to lose an appeal he otherwise would have taken.

That conclusion is reinforced by the Court’s consistent distinction between prejudicial error and harmless irregularity. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 70 (1942), teaches that errors must be evaluated in context, not treated as automatically fatal. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), similarly limits relief to errors affecting substantial rights. *Riddle v. Dyche*, 262 U.S. 333, 335–36 (1923), rejects collateral disturbance of a judgment for defects that do not undermine the legality of the conviction or sentence. And *United States v. Mayer*, 235 U.S. 55, 67–68 (1914), confines postjudgment relief to serious defects going to the validity of the proceeding. An omitted appeal advisement is not such a defect when the defendant already knew of the right and elected not to exercise it.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would transform Rule 32 into an automatic-vacatur provision, untethered from prejudice. That would conflict with the Court’s longstanding insistence on finality. *Baldwin v. Traveling Men’s Assn.*, 283 U.S. 522, 525 (1931), recognizes the importance of repose in judgments, and *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. at 178, warns against using collateral review as an alternative to the ordinary appellate process. There is no basis for setting aside a sentence where the record shows the defendant knew of his appeal right and deliberately declined to appeal. To do so would elevate form over substance and expand habeas beyond its proper role.

The district court’s factual finding that Peguero knew of his appeal right is entitled to deference. *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). On that finding, there was no prejudice, no lost appeal, and no miscarriage of justice. Because habeas relief requires more than a formal omission, the judgment should be affirmed.
