ARGUMENT

I. THE VESSEL OWED SERBIN AN ACTIVE-CONTROL DUTY BECAUSE MOVING THE SNATCH BLOCKS WAS A TASK RESERVED EXCLUSIVELY TO THE SHIP’S CREW.

In Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos, 451 U.S. 156 (1981), the Supreme Court held that a vessel owner owes a longshoreman three distinct duties: a turnover duty, a duty arising from control over “areas or from equipment under the active control of the vessel,” id. at 167, and a duty to intervene when the vessel acquires actual knowledge of a hazard. The second of these—the active-control duty—recognizes that “the vessel is not free of all responsibility” once cargo operations begin, and that where the ship, rather than the stevedore, retains supervision over a piece of shipboard equipment, the vessel remains responsible for hazards arising from that equipment. Id. at 167, 170.

This Court applied that principle in Kirsch v. Plovidba, 971 F.2d 1026 (3d Cir. 1992), holding that a shipowner may be liable under the active-control duty where the instrumentality that caused injury—there, a defective winch—was an appliance the crew, not the stevedore, was responsible for operating and maintaining. The rule follows directly from Scindia: a vessel cannot delegate to itself the exclusive task of operating a piece of equipment and then disclaim responsibility when that equipment is left in a dangerous condition.

The record here compels the same conclusion. It is undisputed that the snatch blocks were part of the ship’s own block-and-pulley system for opening and closing the hatch covers, that only the crew operated the crane and cable that moved the blocks, and that “moving the snatch blocks is the responsibility of the crew.” Statement of Facts, supra. Serbin did not create the hazard; the crew did, by leaving the block down atop the cargo unit after opening the hatch cover. The block’s hinge, hardware, and stopper mechanism were fixed to the ship’s hatch structure—unlike ordinary portable snatch blocks—confirming that this was shipboard equipment within the vessel’s exclusive province, not stevedoring gear. Under Kirsch and Scindia, a genuine issue of material fact exists as to whether the area around the open No. 3 hatch, insofar as it concerned the snatch block, remained under the vessel’s active control notwithstanding the stevedore’s general presence there to unload cargo. Summary judgment was therefore inappropriate.

II. IN THE ALTERNATIVE, THE VESSEL’S DUTY TO INTERVENE WAS TRIGGERED BECAUSE THE CREW KNEW—OR SHOULD HAVE KNOWN—THAT THE BLOCK REMAINED IMPROPERLY DOWN.

Even if the active-control duty did not independently apply, the vessel’s duty to intervene supplies an alternative basis for reversal. Scindia teaches that a vessel must intervene where it has “actual knowledge” both of a hazard and that “the stevedore’s judgment” in continuing operations in its presence “is so obviously improvident that [the vessel], if it knew of the danger, should have realized the [continuing operation] presented an unreasonable risk of harm.” 451 U.S. at 175-76. The Supreme Court reaffirmed the vitality of this duty—and clarified that a hazard’s apparent obviousness to the longshoreman does not automatically discharge the vessel—in Howlett v. Birkdale Shipping Co., 512 U.S. 92 (1994).

Here, the block’s improper placement was the direct product of the crew’s own operation of the hatch-opening mechanism only hours earlier that morning. A jury could find that the crew knew, or in the exercise of reasonable care should have known, that the block remained down after the hatch cover had been opened, because the crew—and no one else—controlled the sequence of raising and lowering the blocks. Serbin’s testimony that he undertook to move the one-hundred-pound fixed block himself, rather than wait for the crew, because competitive pressures in the fruit trade made delay costly, is precisely the kind of “obviously improvident” continuation of work in the face of a known, uncorrected shipboard hazard that Scindia contemplated. 451 U.S. at 175-76. Whether the crew possessed the requisite actual knowledge, and whether Serbin’s decision to proceed was one the vessel should have anticipated and prevented, are quintessential jury questions.

III. GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT PRECLUDE SUMMARY JUDGMENT.

This Court has recognized that the fact-intensive nature of the Scindia inquiry ordinarily forecloses resolution on summary judgment. In Davis v. Portline Transportes Maritima Internacional, S.A., 16 F.3d 532 (3d Cir. 1994), this Court reversed a grant of summary judgment for a shipowner because disputed questions concerning the vessel’s knowledge of a defective condition and the stevedore’s ability to discover and correct it required resolution by a jury, not the court. The same is true here. Whether the snatch block remained within the vessel’s active control, whether the crew had actual knowledge that the block was left improperly positioned, and whether Serbin’s attempt to move it was an “obviously improvident” continuation of work that should have prompted the vessel to intervene, are all disputed factual questions that go to the heart of the Scindia duties. The district court erred in resolving them against Serbin on summary judgment.

CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed and the case remanded for trial.
