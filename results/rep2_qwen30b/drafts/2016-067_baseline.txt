**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a company that purchases defaulted debts and then attempts to collect them is a “debt collector” subject to the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA)—requires a straightforward application of the statute’s plain language. The Fourth Circuit correctly concluded that Santander, having purchased the defaulted loans from CitiFinancial Auto and thereafter collecting on them for its own account, was not acting as a “debt collector” under § 1692a(6) of the FDCPA. The petitioners’ argument—that default status alone transforms a purchaser into a debt collector—is legally unsound and contradicts both the text and structure of the Act.

### I. THE FDCPA REGULATES DEBT COLLECTORS, NOT CREDITORS COLLECTING THEIR OWN DEBTS

The FDCPA’s core purpose is to “eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors.” 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e). It does not regulate creditors collecting debts owed to themselves. *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (“The Act’s definition of the term ‘debt collector’ includes a person ‘who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] ... another’ ” (quoting § 1692a(6))). This distinction is fundamental: a creditor acts in its own interest; a debt collector acts on behalf of another. *See* S. Rep. No. 95-382, at 3 (1977), as reprinted in 1977 U.S.C.C.A.N. 1695, 1698 (“The Committee intends the term ‘debt collector,’ subject to the exclusions discussed below, to cover all third persons who regularly collect debts for others”). The statutory definitions confirm this dichotomy.

Under § 1692a(6), a “debt collector” includes any person who (1) uses interstate commerce or the mails in a business whose principal purpose is debt collection, (2) regularly collects debts owed to another, or (3) collects its own debts using a name other than its own. *Id.* The first two categories apply only when the entity collects for someone else. The third category applies only when a creditor uses a false identity. But none of these definitions apply here because Santander collected debts it had purchased—not for another party, but for itself.

### II. THE DEFAULT STATUS OF A DEBT DOES NOT DETERMINE WHETHER A PURCHASER IS A “DEBT COLLECTOR”

The petitioners’ central claim—that Santander became a “debt collector” simply because it acquired the debts after they were in default—rests on a flawed interpretation of the statute. They argue that because § 1692a(4) excludes from the definition of “creditor” any person who receives an assignment of a debt in default “solely for the purpose of facilitating collection for another,” such a person must be a “debt collector.” But this logic misapplies the statute’s structure. The exclusion in § 1692a(4) does not create a new category of debt collector—it merely clarifies that certain entities are not “creditors” when they act solely as collection agents. *See* Davidson v. Capital One Bank (USA), N.A., 797 F.3d 1309, 1314 (11th Cir. 2015) (“[W]here a person does not fall within subsection (F) or any one of the six statutory exclusions, he is not deemed a ‘debt collector’ as a matter of course. Instead, ... he must satisfy the Act’s substantive requirements”).

More critically, the petitioners ignore the threshold inquiry: whether Santander satisfies any of the three definitional categories in § 1692a(6). The complaint alleges that Santander purchased the loans from CitiFinancial Auto on December 1, 2011, and thereafter attempted to collect on them. There is no allegation that Santander collected on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto after that date. On the contrary, the purchase constituted a transfer of ownership. Once Santander acquired the debt, it became the obligee—the rightful holder of the claim. As such, it was acting as a creditor, not a debt collector.

### III. THE STATUTORY TEXT DEMANDS THAT COLLECTION ACTIVITY BE FOR ANOTHER’S BENEFIT

The phrase “debts owed or due another” in § 1692a(6)(2) refers to the current status of the debt at the time of collection, not its origin. *See* Davidson, 797 F.3d at 1318 (“Our inquiry under § 1692a(6) is not whether Capital One regularly collects on debts originally owed or due another and now owed to Capital One; our inquiry is whether Capital One regularly collects on debts owed or due another at the time of collection”). The petitioners’ argument that Santander collected debts “originally owed to another” fails because Congress did not intend the FDCPA to regulate every entity that once collected for another. Rather, the Act targets those who regularly collect for others *at the time of the conduct*. *Schlegel v. Wells Fargo Bank, N.A.*, 720 F.3d 1204, 1209 (9th Cir. 2013) (“The statute is not susceptible to the [plaintiffs’] interpretation that ‘owed or due another’ means ‘originally owed or due another’ ”).

Santander’s role changed upon purchase. Before December 1, 2011, it may have serviced the loans for CitiFinancial Auto—but that activity, if regular, would have made it a debt collector *in that context*. After the sale, however, it acted as a creditor collecting its own debt. The law does not require a company to remain a debt collector simply because it once performed collection services. *See* Bridge v. Ocwen Fed. Bank, FSB, 681 F.3d 355, 359 (6th Cir. 2012) (rejecting the notion that “default status alone determines whether a purchaser is a debt collector”).
