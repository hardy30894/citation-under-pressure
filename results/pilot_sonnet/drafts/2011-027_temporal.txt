# ARGUMENT

## I. The Navigability-for-Title Test Asks Whether a River, Considered as a Whole in Its Ordinary Condition, Was Used or Susceptible of Use as a Highway for Commerce.

Under the equal-footing doctrine, a State acquires title to the beds of rivers that were navigable at the time it entered the Union. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845) (holding that the "shores of navigable waters" and the soils under them belong to the several States "in their sovereign capacity"). This Court's controlling test for navigability-for-title purposes was set forth in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557 (1870): rivers are navigable in fact "when they are used, or are susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce." *Id.* at 563. That inquiry asks whether the watercourse, taken as a whole, functioned or could function as a channel of trade—not whether every discrete stretch, considered in isolation, independently satisfied the test. *See Barney v. Keokuk*, 94 U.S. 324, 338 (1876) (title to riverbeds vests in the States because "the primary object of the grant" of admission was full sovereignty over waters within their limits).

## II. This Court Has Never Required Trial Courts to Fragment a River Into Segments Defined by the Location of Later-Built Structures.

Nothing in this Court's precedents supports isolating a river's navigability at the precise, arbitrary points where a dam happens to sit decades or a century after statehood. In *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64 (1931), the Court examined stretches of the Colorado and Green Rivers, but it did so by reference to *natural* discontinuities in the river's character—canyons, rapids, and topographic barriers—not by reference to structures built long after 1896. The Court there explained that the true inquiry is whether "trade and travel" could be conducted on the river "in the customary modes of trade and travel on water." *Id.* at 82. Segmentation, to the extent it is appropriate at all, follows the river's own geography, not the modern footprint of a hydroelectric facility.

Likewise, in *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377 (1940), the Court held that a waterway's navigability is not defeated merely because portions require artificial aids or present occasional obstacles; the inquiry is whether the river as a "continuous highway" was suitable, or could be made suitable with reasonable improvements, for commercial use. *Id.* at 407–09. The Court emphasized that "[t]he power of the government over its waters ... is not dependent on ownership of the underlying lands," and that navigability is assessed by the river's *capacity* to serve trade, not by mechanically subdividing it into segments coextensive with obstructions. *Id.* at 426–27. Similarly, in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113 (1921), the Court considered the Desplaines River's navigability as an integrated whole notwithstanding shallow or obstructed stretches, holding that such conditions must be weighed against the river's overall usefulness as a highway of commerce. *Id.* at 122–24.

Reading the constitutional test to compel a segment-by-segment title determination keyed to the specific footprint of each modern dam would convert a doctrine rooted in sovereignty and public trust into a game of measurement dictated by where private industry later chose to build. Nothing in *The Daniel Ball*, *Utah*, or *Appalachian Electric* supports that approach.

## III. Post-Statehood Evidence Is Probative of Navigability at Statehood Because a River's Physical Character Persists Over Time.

Because direct eyewitness testimony from 1889 is unavailable, courts have long relied on the settled principle that a river's physical characteristics—its width, depth, gradient, and current—are geologically persistent, such that evidence of use before and after statehood illuminates the river's condition at the critical date. In *United States v. Holt State Bank*, 270 U.S. 49 (1926), the Court considered evidence of the navigability of Mud Lake without confining its analysis to a single moment, examining the lake's "natural and ordinary condition" as demonstrated by evidence spanning years. *Id.* at 56. Similarly, in *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574 (1922), the Court drew on historical accounts of use of the Red River from surrounding periods to establish the river's navigable character at the relevant time. *Id.* at 585–86.

This approach is not a relaxation of the constitutional test; it is a recognition that the test's factual predicate—whether a river was "used, or susceptible of being used" as a highway for commerce, *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. at 563—can be proven through the ordinary tools of historical reconstruction, including reports, journals, and government surveys describing river conditions close in time to statehood.

## IV. The Montana Supreme Court Properly Applied This Framework.

Consistent with this authority, the Montana Supreme Court evaluated the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers as continuous highways of commerce, considering historical evidence—including the Foley and related affidavits—bearing on their capacity for trade and travel at and around statehood. That approach faithfully applies the test of *The Daniel Ball*, *Utah*, and *Appalachian Electric Power Co.* The constitutional test does not require, and this Court's precedents do not support, an artificial requirement that trial courts isolate each modern dam site as its own discrete "stretch" divorced from the river's historical character as a whole.
