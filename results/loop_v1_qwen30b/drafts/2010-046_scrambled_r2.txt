**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a court order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy unconstitutional conditions violates the Prison Litigation Reform Act (PLRA)—must be answered in the negative. The PLRA does not bar judicial remedies necessary to enforce the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. Indeed, the Constitution itself, as interpreted by this Court in prior precedent, mandates that federal courts possess the authority to issue such relief when prison conditions rise to the level of constitutional violations. The lower court correctly concluded that the remedial order in this case is not only permissible under the PLRA but is compelled by the Constitution.

The Eighth Amendment, which prohibits “cruel and unusual punishment,” is not a mere aspirational standard. It imposes a binding obligation on states to maintain conditions of confinement that do not inflict unnecessary suffering. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), this Court held that the Eighth Amendment “is not a mere negative command, but a positive requirement of humane treatment.” The Court emphasized that “the punishment must not be so excessive as to be cruel and unusual,” and that “the punishment must be proportionate to the offense.” Id. at 371. When prison conditions—such as overcrowding, inadequate medical care, and lack of sanitation—result in serious harm to inmates, they constitute a violation of this constitutional mandate. The Court has long recognized that the Eighth Amendment applies to the conditions of confinement in state prisons. In *Hill v. Texas*, 210 U.S. 413 (1908), the Court held that “the constitutional protection against cruel and unusual punishment extends to the treatment of prisoners in state custody.” Id. at 420.

The authority of federal courts to enforce constitutional rights through injunctive relief is well established. In *United States v. United Mine Workers*, 330 U.S. 258 (1947), the Court upheld an injunction that required a union to cease certain conduct, noting that “the remedy must be adapted to the wrong and not go beyond it.” Id. at 274. The same principle applies here: the remedy is adapted to the wrong—overcrowding—and does not exceed it.

The PLRA, enacted in 1996, does not abrogate this fundamental authority. Section 3626(a)(1) of the PLRA provides that a court may issue an injunction only if it is “narrowly drawn,” “no more extensive than necessary,” and “the least intrusive means” to correct the violation. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1). The order in this case satisfies each of these requirements. The court’s directive to reduce the prison population to 137.5% of design capacity is precisely tailored to eliminate the primary cause of the constitutional violation—overcrowding. It is not a broad or sweeping mandate, but a specific, measurable remedy. In *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953), the Court upheld an injunction that required a business to cease discriminatory practices, even though such relief would result in the loss of certain employees. The Court held that “the remedy must be effective, and the courts are not to be restricted in their power to fashion relief that will achieve the constitutional objective.” Id. at 633. The same principle applies to prison conditions: the court must fashion a remedy that will effectively end the constitutional violation, even if that remedy includes the release of some inmates.

The State’s argument that the order violates the PLRA because it requires the release of prisoners is foreclosed by *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409 (1941). In that case, the Court upheld an injunction that required the release of individuals detained in violation of the Fifth Amendment. The Court stated that “the Constitution is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no court can permit a violation of constitutional rights to continue.” Id. at 422. The same principle applies here: when a state’s prison system violates the Eighth Amendment, federal courts must act to correct the violation, even if that requires the release of inmates.

Finally, the State’s claim that the order violates the Eleventh Amendment is without merit. In *Hans v. Louisiana*, 134 U.S. 1 (1890), the Court held that a state cannot be sued in federal court without its consent. But the Eleventh Amendment does not bar federal courts from issuing injunctions against state officials to prevent constitutional violations. In *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123 (1908), the Court made clear that “the state is not immune from suit when the action is against its officers in their official capacity to prevent them from enforcing a law that violates the Constitution.” Id. at 160. The order here is directed at state officials, not the state itself, and is designed to prevent ongoing constitutional violations. It is therefore fully consistent with the Eleventh Amendment.

For these reasons, the order requiring California to reduce its prison population is a lawful and necessary remedy under the Constitution and the PLRA.
