**ARGUMENT**

The plain text of 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge “any debt … for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by … actual fraud.” The phrase “any debt” is unqualified, and the modifier “to the extent obtained by” refers to the debt itself—not merely the value of the property or money fraudulently obtained. *See In re St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d 672, 678 (11th Cir. 1993). Thus, once a debt is “obtained by” fraud, the entire debt—including treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs—is nondischargeable.

The Third Circuit correctly held that the treble-damage award under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act is part of the debt “obtained by” fraud. The debtor’s fraudulent overcharges directly triggered the statutory penalty. As the court observed, the award was “codified and therefore entirely foreseeable by the debtor at the time he made the false representations.” *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 185 B.R. 180, 183 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1995), *aff’d*, 1996 WL 1186833 (D.N.J. Jan. 24, 1996). Because the debtor knew or should have known that his fraud would expose him to treble damages, those damages are a direct consequence of the fraud and are nondischargeable.

This interpretation aligns with the Bankruptcy Code’s purpose. The “fresh start” policy is reserved for “the honest but unfortunate debtor.” *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 286–87 (1991). A debtor who commits actual fraud forfeits that protection. *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 680. Allowing discharge of punitive damages would undermine the deterrent effect of state consumer protection laws and reward fraudulent conduct.

The contrary view—that only compensatory damages are nondischargeable—rests on a cramped reading of the statute. *See In re Levy*, 951 F.2d 196, 198 (9th Cir. 1991). But *Levy* erroneously focused on the “amount obtained” rather than the “debt” itself. Section 523(a)(2)(A) does not say “to the extent of money obtained”; it says “to the extent obtained by” fraud. The debt is the entire liability arising from the fraudulent transaction. *St. Laurent*, 991 F.2d at 678–79. Moreover, *Levy*’s approach would create an arbitrary distinction between compensatory and punitive damages, even though both flow from the same fraudulent conduct.

The debtor’s argument that he made no affirmative misrepresentation is equally unavailing. By charging rents exceeding the legal limit, he impliedly represented that the amounts were lawful. Silence coupled with an overcharge constitutes a false representation. *See In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991, 999 (Bankr. D.N.J. 1992). The bankruptcy court found that the debtor acted with reckless indifference to the truth—a standard that satisfies the scienter requirement under § 523(a)(2)(A). *In re Cohn*, 54 F.3d 1108, 1118–19 (3d Cir. 1995). Those findings are supported by the record: the debtor never consulted the rent control board, never read the ordinance, and failed to appeal the board’s decision.

Because the entire debt—including treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs—was “obtained by” actual fraud, it is nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
