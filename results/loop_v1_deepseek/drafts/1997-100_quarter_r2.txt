ARGUMENT

I. THE DOUBLE JEOPARDY CLAUSE PRECLUDES RETRIAL OF A PRIOR CONVICTION ALLEGATION ONCE A COURT HAS FINALLY DETERMINED THAT THE EVIDENCE IS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT IT.

The Fifth Amendment's Double Jeopardy Clause, made applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, protects a defendant from being "subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 794 (1969) ("we today find that the double jeopardy prohibition of the Fifth Amendment represents a fundamental ideal in our constitutional heritage, and that it should apply to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment"). This protection is not limited to proceedings on the substantive charges; it extends to any stage at which the State seeks to prove facts that increase the punishment for a crime. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) 163, 176 (1873) (holding that the Clause bars "a second punishment for the same offense" after sentence has been imposed). A prior conviction allegation is such a fact: it exposes the defendant to a doubled sentence and an additional one-year enhancement. When the State has failed to prove that allegation beyond a reasonable doubt, and an appellate court has finally reversed the true finding for insufficiency of the evidence, that determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal. Retrial would place the defendant in jeopardy a second time for the same issue, in direct violation of the Clause.

The United States Supreme Court has consistently held that once a defendant is acquitted—whether by jury or by a court on review of the evidence—the Double Jeopardy Clause absolutely bars any further prosecution for the same offense. *Kepner v. United States*, 195 U.S. 100, 130 (1904) ("the protection of the Constitution is not a matter of form, but of substance"); *Downum v. United States*, 372 U.S. 734, 736 (1963) (per curiam) ("the Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial following an acquittal"). An appellate court's reversal for insufficient evidence is an acquittal for double jeopardy purposes; it is a final determination that the State failed to prove its case. *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 130. Just as the State may not retry a defendant on a substantive charge after an appellate court has found the evidence insufficient, it may not retry a sentencing allegation after the same determination.

The Clause also protects against multiple punishments for the same offense. *Ex parte Lange*, 85 U.S. (18 Wall.) at 176. A prior conviction allegation is not a new crime, but it is a fact that, when proved, authorizes a greater punishment. If the State could relitigate that fact after an insufficiency finding, the defendant would be forced to endure the risk of receiving a sentence that is, in effect, a second punishment for conduct that has already been determined not to support enhanced consequences. *Id.* The Double Jeopardy Clause does not permit the State to "try again" to obtain a harsher sentence after it has once failed to prove the necessary facts.

The California Supreme Court erred in holding that double jeopardy protections do not apply to noncapital sentencing proceedings. The Clause draws no such distinction. *In re Nielsen*, 131 U.S. 176, 187 (1889) ("The constitutional prohibition is not to be confined to cases of life or limb, but extends to all cases where a second punishment is attempted to be inflicted for the same offense"). The same principle applies here: the State may not seek an enhanced punishment after it has been finally determined that the enhancement is unsupported.

The California Legislature has chosen to provide a trial-like proceeding for prior conviction allegations, including a jury trial, proof beyond a reasonable doubt, and the right to confront witnesses. Those procedural protections transform the sentencing hearing into a trial on the enhancement issue. When the State invokes that mechanism, it must accept the consequences of an adverse verdict. The Double Jeopardy Clause does not permit the State to have a second chance to prove a fact simply because the proceeding is labeled "sentencing" rather than "guilt." *Kepner*, 195 U.S. at 130.

Petitioner Monge was subjected to a full trial on the prior conviction allegation. The trial court found the allegation true, but the Court of Appeal reversed, holding that the evidence was insufficient to prove that Monge personally used a deadly weapon—a necessary element of the "serious felony" enhancement. That reversal was a final judgment of acquittal on the enhancement issue. The State may not retry that allegation. The Double Jeopardy Clause bars the door.

The judgment of the California Supreme Court should be reversed.
