**Argument**

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from the debtor’s estate because an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” within the meaning of 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12). Those provisions exempt “retirement funds to the extent that those funds are in a fund or account that is exempt from taxation” under the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The statutory text thus requires more than tax-favored status; it requires funds that retain the character of retirement savings. Inherited IRAs do not. As the Seventh Circuit explained, “an inherited IRA is a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” In re Clark, 714 F.3d 559, 562 (7th Cir. 2013).

This Court should follow In re Clark and reject the Fifth Circuit’s contrary view in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012). The ordinary meaning of “retirement funds” refers to money set aside for retirement use by the person who holds the account. That understanding fits the structure of the Code. A traditional IRA owned by a worker remains protected because it is held for the owner’s retirement, and the owner is subject to age-based withdrawal restrictions and penalty taxes. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 561-62. By contrast, an inherited IRA cannot receive new contributions, cannot be rolled over, and must begin mandatory distributions shortly after the original owner’s death. Id. at 562-63. Those features show that inherited IRAs are not retirement savings for the beneficiary’s later-life use.

The text of § 522 reinforces that conclusion. Congress protected “retirement funds,” not merely any funds that once were in a retirement account. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C), (d)(12). The Seventh Circuit correctly observed that when Ruth Heffron died, the IRA ceased to be anyone’s retirement fund in the relevant sense; it became a vehicle for limited tax deferral, but one that required distribution before Heidi’s retirement. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 563-64. If Congress had intended to exempt inherited IRAs, it could have said so expressly. Instead, it used a term tied to the function of the funds, not their origin.

The contrast with other Code exemptions confirms the point. Section 522 elsewhere uses ownership- and timing-based language, such as property in which “the debtor had, immediately before the commencement of the case, an interest.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(B). The Seventh Circuit explained that such language makes the exemption depend on how the debtor held the property at filing, not how someone else once held it. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 564-65. So too here: a debtor cannot transform nonexempt property into exempt property by pointing to a predecessor’s use. Id. at 565-66. An inherited IRA is no different from a house that was once the decedent’s home but is not the debtor’s homestead. It is the debtor’s present use, not the decedent’s past use, that matters. Id. at 566-67.

The policy arguments advanced by petitioners cannot overcome the statute’s text. Congress chose to protect retirement savings, but not all assets with a retirement-related pedigree. As the Seventh Circuit noted, “no legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987). Extending the exemption to inherited IRAs would convert a narrow bankruptcy protection into a sweeping shelter for assets that can be used for current consumption. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 567-68. That result would exceed the statutory design and distort the balance Congress struck between debtor relief and creditor recovery.

Finally, this Court should not adopt a rule that turns on the phrase “inherited IRA” rather than on the statutory function of the funds. The fact that the Internal Revenue Code labels the account an IRA does not mean it remains a retirement fund for the beneficiary. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 564. As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, inherited IRAs are not “retirement funds” protected by § 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12). Id. at 568.

For those reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
