# ARGUMENT

## I. THE PLAIN TEXT OF ANILCA § 103(c) DOES NOT STRIP NPS OF AUTHORITY TO REGULATE HOVERCRAFT USE ON WATERS WITHIN CONSERVATION SYSTEM UNITS

Section 103(c) of ANILCA provides that "[o]nly those lands within [conservation system unit] boundaries in Alaska which are public lands (as such term is defined in this Act) shall be deemed to be included as a portion of such unit." 16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). Petitioner reads this provision to mean that whenever inholdings of state, Native, or private land or water lie within a unit's boundaries, NPS loses all regulatory authority over conduct there. That reading cannot survive the statute's text.

Section 103(c) speaks only to which *lands* are "deemed to be included as a portion of" a conservation system unit for purposes of unit designation—it does not purport to limit NPS's regulatory authority over *activities*, such as hovercraft operation, that Congress has separately authorized the agency to regulate. The Ninth Circuit correctly recognized that the hovercraft ban set forth at 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e) is a regulation of general applicability that does not depend on land ownership status; it applies uniformly to "NPS-administered lands and waters," without regard to whether the submerged lands beneath a given waterway have passed to the State. Sturgeon v. Frost, 768 F.3d 1066, 1078 (9th Cir. 2014). As this Court has instructed, statutory analysis must begin with, and often ends with, the text Congress enacted. Connecticut Nat'l Bank v. Germain, 503 U.S. 249, 253-54 (1992) ("[C]ourts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there."). Nothing in § 103(c)'s text purports to override the Park Service's independent, congressionally delegated authority to regulate activities affecting park resources and visitor safety.

This reading is reinforced by ANILCA § 1319, which provides that "[n]othing in this Act shall be construed as . . . superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to . . . exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto." 16 U.S.C. § 3207. Congress thus made clear that ANILCA operates only as a limited, specifically enumerated modification of preexisting federal regulatory authority—not as the sweeping ouster of NPS jurisdiction that Petitioner posits. Where Congress intends implied repeals of prior regulatory schemes, it does not bury that intent in ambiguous cross-references; rather, "repeals by implication are not favored" and require "clear and manifest" congressional intent. Radzanower v. Touche Ross & Co., 426 U.S. 148, 154 (1976) (quoting Morton v. Mancari, 417 U.S. 535, 551 (1974)). Petitioner identifies no such clarity here.

## II. THE FEDERAL GOVERNMENT'S NAVIGATIONAL SERVITUDE AND COMMERCE CLAUSE AUTHORITY INDEPENDENTLY SUPPORT NPS'S REGULATION

Even if the submerged lands beneath the Nation River have passed to the State under the Submerged Lands Act, that transfer of title does not divest the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on navigable waters. As Justice Scalia observed in a related context, "[i]f title to submerged lands passed to Alaska, the Federal Government would still retain significant authority to regulate activities in the waters of Glacier Bay by virtue of its dominant navigational servitude, other aspects of the Commerce Clause, and even the treaty power." Alaska v. United States, 545 U.S. 75, 116 (2005) (Scalia, J., concurring in part and dissenting in part). This principle flows directly from this Court's longstanding recognition that the federal navigational servitude is "a servitude that exists by reason of the purposes served by the Commerce Clause and is a right vested in the United States." United States v. Rands, 389 U.S. 121, 123 (1967). That servitude is entirely independent of who holds title to the submerged lands.

The hovercraft ban falls comfortably within this authority. NPS's regulation protects the ecological integrity and visitor experience within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, a purpose squarely within the federal government's Commerce Clause and property-management powers, irrespective of state ownership of the riverbed.

## III. STURGEON HAS STANDING, BUT ALASKA'S CLAIMS WERE PROPERLY DISMISSED

The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon satisfied Article III's requirements. Consistent with Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus, 573 U.S. 149 (2014), Sturgeon alleged "an intention to engage in a course of conduct arguably affected with a constitutional interest, but proscribed by a statute, and there exists a credible threat of prosecution thereunder." Id. at 159 (quoting Babbitt v. Farm Workers Nat'l Union, 442 U.S. 289, 298 (1979)). NPS's verbal warning, its explicit statement that Sturgeon risked criminal liability, and the existence of an active regulation proscribing his conduct together establish a credible threat of enforcement sufficient for standing.

By contrast, Alaska's claims regarding the completed 2010 salmon study fail the redressability requirement. Injunctive relief cannot remedy costs already incurred for a study that has concluded. See Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife, 504 U.S. 555, 564 (1992) ("Past exposure to illegal conduct does not in itself show a present case or controversy regarding injunctive relief . . . if unaccompanied by any continuing, present adverse effects." (quoting Los Angeles v. Lyons, 461 U.S. 95, 102 (1983))). Nor may Alaska rely on generalized sovereign interests or an asserted "chilling effect" on its citizens, because "a state does not have standing to protect her citizens from the operation of federal statutes." Massachusetts v. EPA, 549 U.S. 497, 520 n.17 (2007); accord Alfred L. Snapp & Son, Inc. v. Puerto Rico ex rel. Barez, 458 U.S. 592, 607 (1982) (requiring a state to "articulate an interest apart from the interests of particular private parties").

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be affirmed.
