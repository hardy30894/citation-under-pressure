**Argument**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim as “cause” to excuse the default of another habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was properly presented to the state courts. That rule follows from settled exhaustion and procedural-default doctrine, which requires a petitioner to give the state courts a fair opportunity to pass on the very federal claim later offered as cause. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977); *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391 (1963).

The Court has repeatedly held that federal habeas review is barred when a state court judgment rests on an adequate and independent state ground, including a state procedural default. *Brown v. Allen*, 344 U.S. 443, 485-86 (1953); *Henry v. Mississippi*, 379 U.S. 443, 447-48 (1965); *Fay*, 372 U.S. at 438-39. The same principle applies when the petitioner seeks to use one defaulted claim to revive another. Cause and prejudice exist only where the external impediment asserted as cause is itself a federal claim that has been properly preserved or otherwise excused. Otherwise, the federal court would permit a petitioner to accomplish indirectly what he could not accomplish directly.

That conclusion is compelled by *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986). There, the Court recognized that attorney error may constitute cause only when it amounts to constitutionally ineffective assistance under *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668 (1984). But the Court also held that a petitioner must first give the state courts an opportunity to resolve the ineffectiveness claim before relying on it as cause. 477 U.S. at 489. In other words, a defaulted ineffectiveness claim cannot excuse another default unless the ineffectiveness claim has itself been exhausted and is not procedurally barred, or unless the petitioner establishes an exception to the default. That is the logic of *Carrier* and it controls here.

This Court’s prior cases are entirely consistent. In *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275-76 (1971), the Court held that exhaustion requires fair presentation of the same federal claim to the state courts. In *Darr v. Burford*, 339 U.S. 200, 204 (1950), the Court explained that comity requires the state judiciary to have the first chance to correct constitutional error. In *Ex parte Hawk*, 321 U.S. 114, 116-17 (1944), the Court likewise emphasized that federal habeas relief is unavailable unless state remedies have been exhausted. A petitioner may not evade those commands by labeling an unexhausted, defaulted contention as “cause.”

Nor does *Carnley v. Cochran*, 369 U.S. 506 (1962), help petitioner. That case involved the independent constitutional right to counsel and the State’s burden to show a valid waiver. Id. at 516-17. It did not authorize federal courts to disregard state procedural bars whenever a petitioner alleges attorney error. To the contrary, *Strickland* later made clear that ineffective assistance is a constitutional claim governed by demanding standards of deficient performance and prejudice, 466 U.S. at 687, 694, and *Carrier* then required that such a claim itself be fairly presented to state court before it may serve as cause, 477 U.S. at 489.

Allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to serve as cause would also undermine the finality and regularity interests recognized in *Sunal v. Large*, 332 U.S. 174, 178-79 (1947), and *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 465 (1938). It would permit a petitioner to bypass state rules of timeliness and presentation simply by embedding one forfeited claim inside another. That is not habeas “cause”; it is a device for nullifying procedural default doctrine.

Finally, petitioner cannot rely on a generalized fairness argument. The Court has consistently held that habeas relief is not a substitute for direct review and that procedural rules serve legitimate state interests in orderly administration and finality. *Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 88-91; *Stone v. Powell*, 428 U.S. 465, 493-94 (1976). If a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim could always excuse another default, the exhaustion requirement would be largely meaningless. The petitioner would need only assert counsel’s ineffectiveness in federal court, even after failing to present that issue properly to the state judiciary.

The correct rule is simple: a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance claim cannot excuse the default of another claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself has been properly exhausted and is not barred, or unless the petitioner independently establishes cause and prejudice for the default of the ineffectiveness claim. That rule follows directly from *Carrier*, *Strickland*, *Sykes*, *Picard*, and the Court’s longstanding habeas precedents. The judgment below should therefore be affirmed.
