**ARGUMENT**

Under the framework of *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), a new constitutional rule of criminal procedure generally does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review. But two exceptions exist: (1) rules that place “certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe,” and (2) rules that prohibit “a certain category of punishment for a class of defendants because of their status or offense.” *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 136 S. Ct. 718, 729-30 (2016) (quoting *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 307, 311). The first exception covers “substantive” rules, which apply retroactively because they “necessarily carry a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of ‘an act that the law does not make criminal.’” *Bousley v. United States*, 523 U.S. 614, 620 (1998) (quoting *Davis v. United States*, 417 U.S. 333, 346 (1974)). *Johnson* announced such a substantive rule and therefore must be applied retroactively.

**I. *Johnson* Held the Residual Clause Void for Vagueness**

In *Johnson v. United States*, 135 S. Ct. 2551 (2015), the Court struck down the residual clause of the Armed Career Criminal Act, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), as unconstitutionally vague in violation of the Due Process Clause. The Court reasoned that the clause “requires a court to picture the kind of conduct that the crime involves in the ordinary case, and to judge whether that abstraction presents a serious potential risk of physical injury.” *Id.* at 2557. That inquiry, the Court held, created “more unpredictability and arbitrariness than the Due Process Clause tolerates.” *Id.* at 2558. Thus, the residual clause was void: no conviction or sentence could rest upon it.

**II. The *Johnson* Rule Is Substantive, Not Procedural**

The *Johnson* rule is not a rule of criminal procedure. It does not, for example, alter the manner in which courts determine guilt or allocate decisionmaking. Cf. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 354 (2004) (holding that a rule requiring a jury to find aggravating factors is procedural). Rather, *Johnson* invalidated the very legal standard that defined a class of predicate offenses. By declaring the residual clause void, the Court determined that conduct falling only within that clause “did not—and could not—qualify as a violent felony.” A conviction or enhanced sentence obtained under that clause is therefore the product of a rule that “place[s] ‘a class of private conduct’ beyond the power of the State to punish.” *Montgomery*, 136 S. Ct. at 733.

This Court has long treated such rules as substantive. In *Bousley*, a defendant collaterally attacked a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c) after *Bailey v. United States*, 516 U.S. 137 (1995), clarified that “use” of a firearm required active employment. The Court held that *Bailey* announced a substantive rule for *Teague* purposes because it changed the definition of the offense, meaning the defendant may have been convicted of “an act that the law plainly does not make criminal.” 523 U.S. at 620-21. Similarly, *Johnson* changed the definition of “violent felony” by eliminating an entire category of predicates. Under the residual clause, “ordinary case” risk assessment determined whether a defendant’s prior conviction triggered a mandatory fifteen-year sentence. *Johnson*, 135 S. Ct. at 2557. After *Johnson*, those convictions cannot support ACCA enhancement. That is a substantive alteration of the scope of criminal punishment, not a mere procedural adjustment.

*Montgomery* confirms this conclusion. There, the Court held that *Miller v. Alabama*, 567 U.S. 460 (2012)—which forbade mandatory life-without-parole sentences for juveniles—applied retroactively because it “rendered unconstitutional” a “distinctive set of penalties” and thus announced a substantive rule. 136 S. Ct. at 734-37. *Johnson* is a fortiori: it rendered unconstitutional the very law that authorized a fifteen-year minimum sentence for thousands of defendants like Welch. The rule “necessarily carr[ies] a significant risk that a defendant stands convicted of an act that the law does not make criminal.” *Id.* at 733 (quoting *Bousley*, 523 U.S. at 620).

**III. Welch’s Sentence Was Imposed Under the Residual Clause**

The Eleventh Circuit held that Welch’s prior Florida robbery conviction qualified as a violent felony under the residual clause, reasoning that the robbery “otherwise involves conduct that presents a serious potential risk of physical injury.” *Welch v. United States*, 604 F. App’x 838, 845 (11th Cir. 2015). That is precisely the clause *Johnson* invalidated. Lacking the residual clause, Welch’s prior conviction does not satisfy ACCA’s elements clause, which requires “the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force,” because Florida’s robbery statute permitted conviction based on mere snatching—force insufficient to qualify under federal law. *See Johnson v. United States*, 559 U.S. 133, 140-41 (2010). Accordingly, Welch’s fifteen-year sentence rests solely on a void provision.

Because *Johnson*’s rule is substantive, it must be applied retroactively on collateral review. The decision below, which denied a certificate of appealability without recognizing this rule, cannot stand. This Court should reverse and remand for resentencing without the ACCA enhancement.
