The post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The Supreme Court has construed the statute to contain an implicit "reasonable time" limitation to avoid serious constitutional concerns, as seen in Zadvydas v. Davis, where the Court relied on the principles established in Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228 (1896), to limit detention to a period reasonably necessary to secure removal (Zadvydas v. Davis, 533 U.S. 678, 121 S.Ct. 2491, 2501, 150 L.Ed.2d 653 (2001), citing Wong Wing v. United States, 163 U.S. 228, 237 (1896)).

The Court's interpretation is consistent with the long-standing principle that detention must be reasonable and not punitive, as established in United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod, 264 U.S. 131 (1924), where the Court held that detention is permissible only if it is "part of a proceeding looking to deportation" and not "a penalty for an unlawful residence" (United States ex rel. Tisi v. Tod, 264 U.S. 131, 133 (1924)). Similarly, in Bilokumsky v. Tod, 263 U.S. 149 (1923), the Court emphasized that detention must be "reasonably necessary" to effectuate removal (Bilokumsky v. Tod, 263 U.S. 149, 157 (1923)).

The government's authority to detain aliens is not unlimited, as seen in the Court's decision in United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253 (1905), where the Court held that an alien's detention must be justified by a legitimate government interest (United States v. Ju Toy, 198 U.S. 253, 263 (1905)). In the context of deportation proceedings, the Court has consistently held that detention is permissible only if it is necessary to effectuate removal, as in Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698 (1893), where the Court upheld the government's authority to detain aliens pending deportation, but only if it is "in accordance with law" (Fong Yue Ting v. United States, 149 U.S. 698, 730 (1893)).

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that aliens have constitutional rights, including the right to due process, as established in Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356 (1886), where the Court held that aliens are entitled to equal protection under the law (Yick Wo v. Hopkins, 118 U.S. 356, 369 (1886)). In the context of detention, the Court has applied this principle to require that detention be reasonable and not arbitrary, as seen in Kwock Jan Fat v. White, 253 U.S. 454 (1920), where the Court held that an alien's detention must be justified by a legitimate government interest and must not be "unreasonable or arbitrary" (Kwock Jan Fat v. White, 253 U.S. 454, 457 (1920)).

The Supreme Court's decision in Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206 (1953), is distinguishable from the present case, as it involved an alien who had not effected entry into the United States, whereas Zadvydas is a resident alien (Shaughnessy v. United States ex rel. Mezei, 345 U.S. 206, 213 (1953)). The Court's reliance on cases involving excludable aliens, such as Mezei, is misplaced, as the constitutional concerns are different for resident aliens like Zadvydas.

In light of these authorities, it is clear that the post-removal-period statute does not authorize indefinite detention. The government's interest in detaining aliens is limited to the period reasonably necessary to secure removal, and detention beyond that period is not authorized by the statute. As the Court held in Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524 (1952), the government's authority to detain aliens is subject to the requirements of due process, and detention must be "reasonably necessary" to effectuate removal (Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 538 (1952)).

In conclusion, the post-removal-period statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1231(a)(6), does not authorize the Attorney General to detain a removable alien indefinitely beyond the 90-day removal period. The statute contains an implicit "reasonable time" limitation, and detention beyond that period is not authorized. The government's authority to detain aliens is limited by the Constitution and the statute, and detention must be reasonably necessary to effectuate removal.
