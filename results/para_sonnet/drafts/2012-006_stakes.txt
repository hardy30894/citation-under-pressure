# ARGUMENT

## I. Once a Defendant Introduces Evidence of Withdrawal, Due Process Requires the Government to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That the Defendant Remained a Conspirator Within the Limitations Period.

The Due Process Clause guarantees that no defendant may be convicted "except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Continued membership in a conspiracy within the five-year period preceding indictment is not a peripheral matter of mitigation—it is the fact that transforms otherwise time-barred conduct into a prosecutable offense. Where a defendant comes forward with evidence that he withdrew before the limitations window opened, the question whether he was still "in" the conspiracy during that window goes to the very existence of the offense charged, not to an affirmative defense that merely excuses or mitigates conduct already proven.

This Court has long distinguished between defenses that negate an element of the crime, which the government must disprove beyond a reasonable doubt, and defenses that concede the elements but seek exoneration on some other ground, which a State may constitutionally place on the defendant. *Compare Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), *with Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977). In *Mullaney*, the Court struck down a Maine rule presuming malice and requiring the defendant to prove heat-of-passion by a preponderance of the evidence, because malice—or its absence—was "a fact necessary to constitute the crime" and could not be shifted to the defendant consistent with *Winship*. 421 U.S. at 704. In *Patterson*, by contrast, the Court sustained a New York rule placing the burden of proving extreme emotional disturbance on the defendant, because that defense did not negate any element the State was required to prove—the State still had to establish, beyond a reasonable doubt, every element of the homicide itself. 432 U.S. at 206–07.

Withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the limitations period is squarely a *Mullaney*-type circumstance, not a *Patterson*-type circumstance. The crime charged here is not "conspiracy" in the abstract; it is conspiracy committed within the statutory window. When a defendant introduces evidence that he affirmatively withdrew before that window began, he is not raising a collateral excuse—he is disputing an essential temporal element of the very offense for which he stands accused. To permit the government to obtain a conviction without proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's membership persisted into the charged period would allow exactly what *Winship* forbids: conviction without proof beyond a reasonable doubt of a fact necessary to the crime.

This conclusion is reinforced by the settled principle that "criminal limitations statutes are 'to be liberally interpreted in favor of repose.'" *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 112, 115 (1970) (citation omitted). The limitations period exists precisely to protect defendants from having to answer for stale conduct, and its protective force is hollowed out if the government may secure a conviction while the jury remains in genuine doubt about whether the defendant was still a conspirator during the only period that matters for purposes of prosecution.

The framework this Court articulated in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422 (1978), confirms rather than undermines this rule. *Gypsum* recognized that a defendant asserting withdrawal must first come forward with evidence of "affirmative acts inconsistent with the purpose of the conspiracy" that were "communicated in a manner reasonably calculated to reach co-conspirators." *Id.* at 464–65. That is a burden of production, not persuasion. Once the defendant has satisfied that threshold showing, the ordinary constitutional allocation of the burden of persuasion reasserts itself: the government, which alone bears the burden of proving the elements of the crime, must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's membership continued into the charged period notwithstanding that evidence. Nothing in *Gypsum* suggests that the ultimate burden of persuading the jury on this element may be flipped onto the defendant merely because he was the one who introduced the triggering evidence.

The Second Circuit has correctly applied this framework. In *United States v. Salmonese*, 352 F.3d 608 (2d Cir. 2003), the court held that once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal, "the burden shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt" that the defendant did not withdraw before the limitations period began. *Id.* at 620. This approach honors both halves of the constitutional structure: the defendant must do the work of putting withdrawal at issue, but the government—not the defendant—must bear the ultimate risk of nonpersuasion on an element of the offense.

The decision below inverted this structure, permitting the jury to convict without ever requiring the government to prove, to the constitutionally required degree of certainty, that petitioner remained a member of the conspiracy during the only period in which his conduct was legally prosecutable. That result cannot be squared with *Winship* and *Mullaney*. This Court should hold that once a defendant produces evidence of withdrawal predating the limitations period, the burden of persuasion shifts to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's membership continued into that period.
