**ARGUMENT**

The government bears the burden of proving every element of a criminal offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895). When a defendant is charged with conspiracy, the government must prove not only the existence of an agreement but also that the defendant was a member of that conspiracy during the period alleged in the indictment. *United States v. Kissel*, 218 U.S. 601, 608 (1910) (conspiracy is a continuing offense; government must prove the agreement continued). Because the statute of limitations defines the temporal scope of the crime, the government must also prove that the conspiracy persisted within the limitations period. *United States v. Cook*, 84 U.S. (17 Wall.) 168, 170 (1872) (government must show offense committed within limitations period). The petitioner presented evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders separated before the limitations period began. This evidence called into question whether the conspiracy—and thus the petitioner’s membership—continued into the relevant period. The lower court erred by failing to place the burden of persuasion on the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the petitioner was a member of the conspiracy during the limitations period.

**I. The Government Must Prove Every Element of the Offense, Including the Conspiracy’s Duration**

The Due Process Clause requires the government to prove every fact necessary to constitute the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 474 (1895). This principle applies with full force to the temporal element of a continuing conspiracy. *Speiser v. Randall*, 357 U.S. 513, 525-26 (1958). A conspiracy is a continuing offense; the government must prove that the agreement remained in effect during the period charged. *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 253 (1940). Where the statute of limitations is at issue, the government must demonstrate that the conspiracy existed within the limitations window. *Cook*, 84 U.S. at 170. The petitioner’s evidence that the leaders split up before the limitations period created a reasonable doubt as to whether the conspiracy—and therefore his membership—continued into that period. The government failed to carry its burden to prove otherwise.

**II. Withdrawal Does Not Shift the Burden of Proof to the Defendant**

The lower court’s reasoning implicitly required the petitioner to prove his withdrawal from the conspiracy before the limitations period. This conflicts with the fundamental rule that the government bears the burden of persuasion on all elements. *Tot v. United States*, 319 U.S. 463, 467 (1943) (burden remains on government; presumptions may not shift it). Withdrawal is an affirmative defense only if the defendant must prove it; but here, the petitioner’s evidence of withdrawal merely negated the government’s required proof of continuing membership. *Deutch v. United States*, 367 U.S. 456, 471 (1961) (government must prove time element when material). The government cannot rely on the defendant’s silence to establish membership during the limitations period. *Coffin*, 156 U.S. at 453 (presumption of innocence). Once the petitioner produced evidence that the conspiracy terminated before the limitations period, the government was obliged to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy continued and that the petitioner remained a member. *Davis*, 160 U.S. at 474.

**III. The Lower Court’s Error Violated Due Process**

The court below effectively required the petitioner to disprove his membership during the limitations period. This impermissibly shifted the burden of persuasion. *Speiser*, 357 U.S. at 525-26. The government must prove the defendant’s membership in the conspiracy within the limitations period as an element of the offense. *Kissel*, 218 U.S. at 608. Evidence of withdrawal does not create a separate defense requiring the defendant to prove anything; it merely undermines the government’s proof of a continuing agreement. *Deutch*, 367 U.S. at 471. The petitioner’s evidence of the leaders’ separation was sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt. The government should have been required to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the conspiracy—and the petitioner’s participation—extended into the limitations period. *Socony-Vacuum*, 310 U.S. at 253.

**Conclusion**

The burden of persuasion on the question of whether the petitioner was a member of the conspiracy during the statute of limitations period rests with the government. The lower court’s contrary holding is inconsistent with the presumption of innocence and the government’s constitutional obligation to prove every element beyond a reasonable doubt. This Court should reverse.
