**ARGUMENT**

I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO APPOINTED STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY ARE EXEMPT AS “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL”

The Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621–634, protects employees aged 40 and over from age-based discrimination in employment decisions. However, Congress explicitly excluded certain categories of public officials from the definition of “employee,” including “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The question before this Court is whether Missouri’s appointed state court judges fall within that exemption. The answer is unequivocally yes.

The text of the ADEA’s exception is unambiguous: it excludes “any appointee on the policymaking level.” This language does not limit its application to executive or legislative branch officials. To the contrary, the phrase “on the policymaking level” describes a functional role, not a branch of government. As the First Circuit held in *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988), “policymaking” is “indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judicial decisions often “fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents.” This is not mere speculation—it is the very essence of common law development. As this Court recognized in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), “the Constitution is not a static document,” and its interpretation evolves through judicial decisionmaking. That evolution is policymaking in its most fundamental form.

The role of a state judge is not limited to mechanically applying settled law. As the Eighth Circuit correctly observed, “the reality of judging is that it is not always—perhaps not even usually—merely a matter of mechanically applying settled, unambiguous principles of law.” The judges in this case are not mere clerks; they are entrusted with interpreting constitutional provisions, resolving ambiguities in statutes, and shaping the law through precedent. In *Cruzan v. Harmon*, 760 S.W.2d 408 (Mo. 1988) (en banc), the Missouri Supreme Court confronted a profound moral and legal question about end-of-life care, ultimately holding that life-sustaining treatment may be withdrawn from a comatose patient without cognitive function. That decision was not a mechanical application of law—it was a policy determination with far-reaching consequences for medical ethics, family rights, and public health. Such decisions are quintessential policymaking.

Moreover, Missouri judges exercise significant administrative and supervisory authority over the state judiciary. Under Article V, Section 4 of the Missouri Constitution, the Supreme Court has the power to adopt rules governing the conduct of the bar and the operation of courts. It also resolves internal disputes within the judicial hierarchy, as demonstrated in *In re Rules of Circuit Court for Twenty-First Judicial Circuit*, 702 S.W.2d 457 (Mo. 1985), where the Court stepped in to restore order after a factional split. These responsibilities are not incidental—they are core to the functioning of the judiciary and require the exercise of judgment, discretion, and policy judgment. The Supreme Court’s authority to set standards for the entire state’s legal system is no less policymaking than the authority of a cabinet secretary to implement federal regulations.

The petitioners argue that a literal reading of the ADEA’s exception supports their position, claiming that judges “decide cases between litigants involving questions of law in which there are no interstices or lacunae to fill.” But this view is both factually and legally flawed. It ignores the dynamic nature of law and the reality that judges regularly confront novel legal questions, especially in areas like constitutional law, civil rights, and emerging technologies. To suggest that judges never make policy is to deny the foundational role of precedent in American jurisprudence.

The petitioners also contend that the exception applies only to executive and legislative appointees. But this narrow reading would render the exception meaningless in states with appointed judiciaries. If appointed judges were not “appointees on the policymaking level,” then no judicial officer in any state would qualify for the exemption—despite the fact that elected judges are explicitly excluded from the ADEA’s coverage. That result is implausible and contradicts the structure of the statute. As the First Circuit noted, “it is highly unlikely that Congress intended this result.” *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 680 F. Supp. 455, 460 (D. Mass. 1988).

Finally, the legislative history cited by the petitioners—specifically, the Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers—does not support their position. That statement refers to “cabinet officers” and “persons with comparable responsibilities at the local level,” but it does not define “policymaking level” in a way that excludes judges. Indeed, the EEOC itself has acknowledged that judges appointed under non-partisan plans are not “elected” and thus may fall within the exception. See EEOC Opinion Letter to Rep. Claude Pepper, April 7, 1987, reprinted in EEOC Compliance Manual (BNA) at N:1001, n.2. The EEOC’s position is consistent with the text and purpose of the ADEA.

Accordingly, the ADEA does not apply to appointed Missouri state judges because they are “appointees on the policymaking level” within the meaning of 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s dismissal of the ADEA claim.

II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION SURVIVES EQUAL PROTECTION REVIEW
