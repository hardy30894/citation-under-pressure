## Argument

Section 355(f) authorizes review only of a “final decision” of the Railroad Retirement Board made after the prescribed administrative process. 45 U.S.C. § 355(f). A refusal to reopen a long-final benefits determination is not such a decision. The Supreme Court’s pre-1970 precedents make clear that reopening is an extraordinary, discretionary remedy; when an agency declines to exercise that discretion, it does not enter a new merits adjudication that Congress made reviewable.

This Court should therefore dismiss the petition.

First, the governing review statute is limited to final agency action on the claim itself, not later collateral requests to revive closed proceedings. In **Shields v. Utah Idaho Central Railroad Co., 305 U.S. 177 (1938)**, the Court held that review under a similar administrative scheme depends on the agency’s completed adjudication of the matter committed to it, not on subsequent attempts to relitigate the same dispute. Likewise, **Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. United States, 284 U.S. 248 (1932)** recognized that where Congress channels review to final administrative determinations, courts may not expand jurisdiction beyond the decision Congress made reviewable.

Second, the Board’s refusal to reopen is not itself a new “decision” on benefits entitlement. In **Interstate Commerce Commission v. Atlantic Coast Line Railroad Co., 383 U.S. 576 (1966)**, the Court explained that judicial review under a special statutory review provision turns on whether the agency has taken the kind of action the statute makes reviewable; courts may not treat every agency refusal to reconsider as a fresh final order. That principle controls here. Salinas received a final decision on his 2006 application in 2007. His later reopening request did not produce a new entitlement determination; it only asked the Board to revisit an already final one.

Third, agency discretion to reopen is generally not subject to judicial compulsion absent express statutory authorization. In **United States ex rel. Accardi v. Shaughnessy, 347 U.S. 260 (1954)**, the Court enforced an agency’s own procedural rules, but the case presupposed a reviewable administrative action on the merits of the affected proceeding. It did not hold that a refusal to reopen a closed matter is independently reviewable. Here, Salinas identifies no statute authorizing courts to review the Board’s discretionary decision whether to reopen a stale claim.

Fourth, the Board’s regulations reinforce the point that reopening is a limited, discretionary mechanism, not part of the ordinary adjudicatory sequence. Courts had already recognized before 1970 that agencies may provide internal reconsideration rules without converting every refusal to reconsider into a judicially reviewable final order. See **Lane v. United States, 241 U.S. 201 (1916)**; **Bates & Guild Co. v. Payne, 194 U.S. 106 (1904)**. Those cases reflect the settled principle that administrative finality matters: once the prescribed process ends, later petitions to reopen do not automatically create a second opportunity for judicial review.

Fifth, the Supreme Court’s pre-1970 treatment of administrative finality in analogous statutory schemes confirms the jurisdictional bar. In **Federal Communications Commission v. Pottsville Broadcasting Co., 309 U.S. 134 (1940)**, the Court emphasized that the agency’s final authority must be respected within the statutory framework Congress enacted. And in **Rochester Telephone Corp. v. United States, 307 U.S. 125 (1939)**, the Court underscored that courts reviewing agency action must confine themselves to the agency decision actually authorized for review, not to collateral issues outside that decision. Salinas’s requested reopening falls on the wrong side of that line.

Sixth, the Court has long distinguished between reviewable final agency action and nonreviewable internal reconsideration. In **Chicago, Milwaukee, St. Paul & Pacific Railroad Co. v. United States, 294 U.S. 499 (1935)**, the Court treated finality as turning on the agency’s conclusive disposition of the controversy, not on every later administrative response to a dissatisfied party. A refusal to reopen is, at most, a decision not to disturb a completed disposition. It is not a fresh merits determination under the Act.

Seventh, Salinas’s position would undermine the finality Congress built into the benefits system. The Court has repeatedly recognized that statutory review schemes are not license for perpetual relitigation. See **Securities and Exchange Commission v. Chenery Corp., 318 U.S. 80 (1943)**; **Federal Power Commission v. Idaho Power Co., 344 U.S. 17 (1952)**. Those decisions reflect the broader rule that courts review the agency action actually taken, not the agency’s refusal to undo old action on demand.

Finally, this Court should not infer reviewability from silence. In **Switchmen’s Union of North America v. National Mediation Board, 320 U.S. 297 (1943)**, the Court refused to infer judicial review where Congress did not clearly provide it. The same restraint is required here. Congress authorized review of final Board decisions on claims; it did not authorize review of every refusal to reopen a final claim years later.

Because the Board’s refusal to reopen Salinas’s 2006 application was not a “final decision” within the meaning of 45 U.S.C. § 355(f), the petition should be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.
