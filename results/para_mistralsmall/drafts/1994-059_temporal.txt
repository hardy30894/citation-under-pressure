**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE QUALIFICATIONS CLAUSES OF THE FEDERAL CONSTITUTION.**

The Qualifications Clauses of the United States Constitution are exclusive. Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486, 522 (1969) (dictum). They set the sole, definitive requirements for service in Congress. Id. at 522-23. No state may add to or alter those qualifications. Id. at 548 (quoting 2 J. Story, Commentaries on the Constitution of the United States § 627, at 260 (1833)). The Framers deliberately rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications. Id. at 545-46 (citing 1 M. Farrand, Records of the Federal Convention of 1787, at 21-22, 137-38, 176, 180, 219-20 (1911)). The text of the Qualifications Clauses is unambiguous: “Each House shall be the Judge of the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members.” U.S. Const. art. I, § 5, cl. 1. This Court has long recognized that the power to judge qualifications is vested exclusively in each House of Congress. Barry v. Cunningham, 277 U.S. 589, 590 (1928) (per curiam); Burroughs v. United States, 290 U.S. 534, 545 (1934). No state may interfere with that judgment by imposing additional qualifications. Barry, 277 U.S. at 590.

Arkansas’s Term Limitation Amendment does not alter the qualifications for congressional office; it merely restricts ballot access for those who have served a specified number of terms. The Amendment does not bar any person from serving in Congress who meets the constitutional qualifications. It only prevents those who have served three terms in the House or two terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot for re-election. This is a regulation of the electoral process, not a qualification for office. See United States v. Classic, 313 U.S. 299, 318 (1941) (holding that a state may regulate the manner of holding primary elections without altering qualifications for office). The Amendment is therefore consistent with the Qualifications Clauses.

**II. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT IS A VALID EXERCISE OF THE STATE’S ELECTORAL REGULATORY POWER.**

States possess broad authority to regulate the time, place, and manner of elections. McPherson v. Blacker, 146 U.S. 1, 35 (1892); see also Ex parte Siebold, 100 U.S. 371, 392 (1880) (holding that states have “the power to prescribe the manner of holding elections for senators and representatives”). This authority includes the power to impose reasonable restrictions on ballot access. Storer v. Brown, 415 U.S. 724, 730 (1974) (dictum). Arkansas’s Term Limitation Amendment is a permissible exercise of this power. It does not disqualify any person from holding office; it merely conditions the right to appear on the ballot. See Jenness v. Fortson, 403 U.S. 431, 442 (1971) (holding that a state may require a candidate to demonstrate a “significant modicum of support” to appear on the ballot).

The Amendment is also consistent with the First and Fourteenth Amendments. The right to vote and the right to associate for political purposes are fundamental rights. Williams v. Rhodes, 393 U.S. 23, 30 (1968). However, these rights are not absolute. States may impose reasonable, non-discriminatory restrictions on ballot access to prevent frivolous candidacies and to ensure the integrity of the electoral process. Bullock v. Carter, 405 U.S. 134, 145 (1972) (dictum). Arkansas’s Term Limitation Amendment is a reasonable regulation designed to promote voter choice and prevent the entrenchment of incumbents. It does not discriminate against any class of candidates or voters. See Storer, 415 U.S. at 736 (holding that a state may impose reasonable ballot access restrictions to prevent “splintered parties and unrestrained factionalism”).

**III. THE ARKANSAS TERM LIMITATION AMENDMENT DOES NOT VIOLATE THE ARKANSAS CONSTITUTION.**

The Arkansas Supreme Court erred in invalidating the Term Limitation Amendment on the ground that it lacked an Enacting Clause. The Arkansas Constitution does not require an Enacting Clause for constitutional amendments proposed by initiative. See Ark. Const. amend. 7 (providing only that the style of “bills” initiated by the people shall include an Enacting Clause). The term “bills” refers to legislative acts, not constitutional amendments. See generally State ex rel. Att’y Gen. v. Fort Smith, 22 Ark. 450, 454 (1861) (distinguishing between legislative acts and constitutional amendments). The absence of an Enacting Clause in Amendment 73 does not render it invalid.

Moreover, the severability clause in Amendment 73 is valid and enforceable. The unconstitutional portions of the Amendment—those regulating ballot access for congressional candidates—are severable from the constitutional portions—those regulating term limits for state officials. See generally State v. Harrell, 223 Ark. 841, 845, 270 S.W.2d 177, 179 (1954) (holding that a severability clause is valid unless the unconstitutional portions are “so interwoven” with the constitutional portions that the latter cannot stand alone). The Amendment’s provisions regulating term limits for state officials are fully severable and enforceable.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment is constitutional. The judgment of the Arkansas Supreme Court should be reversed.
