**ARGUMENT**

The Tenth Amendment reserves to the States or the people powers not delegated to the United States. U.S. Const. amend. X. A private criminal defendant lacks standing to assert that a federal statute enacted pursuant to the Treaty Power violates this reservation.

In *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939), private utilities challenged federal power sales as an invasion of state authority under the Tenth Amendment. The Court held that the companies “absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” *Id.* at 144. That holding controls here. Bond, like the utilities, is a private party asserting a structural federalism claim without state participation.

The same principle appears in *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), where the Court explained that the Tenth Amendment “was intended to confirm the understanding of the people at the time the Constitution was adopted, that powers not granted to the United States were reserved to the States or to the people,” and that only a State can maintain an action to protect its sovereign interests. *Id.* at 483. An individual defendant may contest the elements of the offense or raise personal constitutional defenses, but may not litigate the allocation of power between the federal and state governments.

Bond’s reliance on cases permitting private parties to invoke federalism-based limits is misplaced. Those decisions either involved Commerce Clause challenges with an interstate-commerce element or did not address the distinct Treaty Power context. *See, e.g.*, *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995). When Congress acts under Article II, § 2 and the Necessary and Proper Clause to implement a valid treaty, *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), the statute rests on an enumerated federal power. The Tenth Amendment supplies no independent restraint that a private litigant may invoke. *See also* *Printz v. United States*, 521 U.S. 898, 918-19 (1997) (Tenth Amendment claims ordinarily raised by States).

Because Bond cannot establish the requisite injury to a legally protected interest of her own, the Third Circuit correctly held that she lacks standing to press the Tenth Amendment objection. The judgment should be affirmed.
