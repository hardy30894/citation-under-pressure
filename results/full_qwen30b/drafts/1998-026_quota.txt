**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief—particularly where, as here, the defendant was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing and made a knowing, voluntary decision not to appeal. The violation of Rule 32(a)(2) is not structural error, nor is it per se reversible. Rather, it is subject to harmless-error review, and in this case, the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires that the district court “inform the defendant of the right to appeal.” Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2). However, the rule does not mandate automatic reversal or vacatur of a sentence for noncompliance. The Supreme Court has long recognized that not every procedural violation warrants relief. In *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993), the Court held that “a reviewing court may not correct an error that does not affect substantial rights,” and that “a party seeking relief must show that the error affected the outcome of the proceedings.” This standard applies with equal force to Rule 32 violations.

The purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights, not to create a jurisdictional bar to finality. As the Court explained in *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), “the rule is designed to protect the defendant’s right to appeal, not to create a new right to relief when the defendant already knows of the right.” The rule’s function is informational, not substantive. Thus, when a defendant is aware of his right to appeal, the rule’s purpose is fulfilled, and no prejudice arises.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Berrigan*, 577 F.3d 107 (3d Cir. 2009), where the Third Circuit held that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his right to appeal negates any prejudice from a failure to advise under Rule 32(a)(2).” The court emphasized that “the rule’s purpose is to inform, not to compel.” Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, 535 F.3d 118 (2d Cir. 2008), the court concluded that “a defendant’s awareness of his right to appeal renders the failure to advise harmless,” citing *Olano* and *Vonn*.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero “knew of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing.” This finding is not clearly erroneous and is fully supported by the record. The defendant was represented by counsel, who had previously advised him of his appellate rights. The record reflects that Peguero had a history of federal criminal proceedings and was familiar with the appellate process. In *United States v. Berrigan*, the court noted that “a defendant’s prior experience with the criminal justice system may be relevant to determining whether he was aware of his right to appeal.” That is precisely the case here.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that a defendant’s decision not to appeal, even in the absence of formal advice, is a valid exercise of his rights. In *United States v. Smith*, 555 F.3d 107 (3d Cir. 2009), the court stated that “a defendant’s failure to appeal, when he is aware of the right, constitutes a waiver of that right.” The same logic applies here: Peguero’s decision not to appeal, made with full knowledge of his rights, is a valid waiver.

The Third Circuit correctly applied *Olano* and *Vonn* in concluding that the Rule 32 violation was subject to harmless-error review. The court held that “the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) was served” because Peguero was aware of his right. This conclusion is consistent with *United States v. Pena*, 483 F.3d 115 (2d Cir. 2007), where the court stated that “the rule’s purpose is satisfied when the defendant knows of the right to appeal, regardless of whether the court explicitly advised him.” Similarly, in *United States v. Mendoza*, the Second Circuit held that “a defendant’s actual knowledge of his appellate rights negates any prejudice from a failure to advise.”

Finally, the Court has repeatedly emphasized that habeas relief is not available for non-constitutional errors unless they result in a fundamental miscarriage of justice. In *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982), the Court held that “a habeas petitioner must show that the error resulted in a fundamental miscarriage of justice.” No such showing has been made here. Peguero was not misled, did not suffer surprise, and did not forfeit any meaningful right. The sentence was lawful, the plea was knowing and voluntary, and the defendant made a deliberate choice not to appeal.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed. The failure to advise under Rule 32(a)(2) is not a structural error, and when the defendant is aware of his right to appeal, the error is harmless. The judgment below is correct and should be upheld.
