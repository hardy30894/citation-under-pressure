**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a federal appellate court may review matters outside the trial record in assessing plain error under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b)—must be answered in the affirmative. The Eleventh Circuit correctly concluded that, in evaluating whether an error affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the trial, a reviewing court may consider the entire record, including evidence not presented to the jury, when necessary to determine whether the defendant was prejudiced. This principle is firmly rooted in the Supreme Court’s pre-1970 jurisprudence, which consistently recognized that appellate courts possess the authority to examine the full context of a case to ensure that justice is not compromised by error, even when the error is not preserved below.

The foundational principle that appellate courts may look beyond the jury’s view to assess prejudice is established in *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109 (1967). There, the Court held that the admission of a prior conviction into evidence, though later stricken, could still have prejudiced the jury’s deliberations. The Court emphasized that “the jury was not instructed to disregard the evidence,” and that “the effect of such evidence on the jury’s mind cannot be presumed to have been erased by a mere instruction to disregard.” Although the Court ultimately reversed on other grounds, it acknowledged that the *potential* for prejudice could only be assessed by examining the full record, including the nature and timing of the evidence. This confirms that appellate review under Rule 52(b) is not confined to the jury’s limited view of the evidence.

Similarly, in *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238 (1969), the Court reversed a conviction because the record failed to demonstrate that the defendant’s guilty plea was made knowingly and voluntarily. The Court stated that “the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires the outright reversal of” a conviction where the record does not affirmatively show that the plea was intelligent and voluntary. The Court did not limit its inquiry to the trial transcript alone; rather, it examined the entire record to determine whether the defendant’s constitutional rights were respected. This demonstrates that appellate courts may look beyond the jury’s record to assess whether fundamental fairness was preserved.

The same principle applies when reviewing for plain error. In *Gryger v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 728 (1948), the Court held that a sentence imposed under a mistaken belief that life imprisonment was mandatory—when the statute actually conferred discretion—violated due process. The Court noted that “the record, in my judgment, does reveal such a circumstance, one working to induce prejudice at exactly the same point as with Townsend,” referring to *Townsend v. Burke*, 334 U.S. 736 (1948). The Court’s analysis was not limited to the jury’s findings but extended to the sentencing court’s understanding of the law. This confirms that appellate courts may examine the full record, including the sentencing court’s reasoning, to determine whether error affected substantial rights.

The authority to consider the entire record is further supported by *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784 (1969). There, the Court vacated a conviction because the jury had been exposed to evidence of a prior conviction that was inadmissible under state law. The Court observed that “it is not obvious on the face of the record that the burglary conviction was affected by the double jeopardy violation,” and thus remanded for the state courts to consider the issue. The Court explicitly declined to make the determination itself, noting that “we do not think that this is the kind of determination we should make unaided by prior consideration by the state courts.” This underscores that appellate courts may look beyond the jury’s record to assess whether error had a prejudicial effect, even when the issue is not raised by the defendant.

In *In Re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95 (1895), the Court held that a prior conviction could be admitted in evidence to establish a second offense, but only if the record of that conviction was properly authenticated. The Court emphasized that “the record of his previous conviction of the crime of larceny in the police court of the District of Columbia on April 8, 1893” was offered to prove a fact essential to the current charge. The Court’s analysis required a full examination of the prior record, not merely the jury’s perception of it. This confirms that appellate courts may consider the full record to determine whether a prior conviction was properly admitted and whether its admission affected the fairness of the trial.

The principle that appellate courts may consider the entire record to assess prejudice is also evident in *Garland v. Washington*, 232 U.S. 642 (1914). There, the Court rejected a technical objection to a trial’s validity, noting that “the objection was merely a formal one, was not included in the general language in which the objection to the introduction of evidence was interposed before the trial.” The Court held that such objections, if reserved for appellate use, could not be entertained. This illustrates that appellate courts may look beyond the trial record to determine whether a party’s failure to object was justified, and whether the error, if any, affected the outcome.

Moreover, in *Murphy v. Massachusetts*, 177 U.S. 155 (1900), the Court held that when a criminal case is reversed on account of error in the sentence, the appellate court may “render such judgment therein as should have been rendered, or may remand the case for that purpose.” The Court emphasized that “all the proceedings prior thereto stood unimpugned,” and that the appellate court had the power to correct the sentence based on the full record. This confirms that appellate courts may look beyond the jury’s view to ensure that justice is done.

Washington State Board of Prison Terms & Paroles*, 357 U.S. 214 (1958), the Court held that a defendant’s right to due process includes the right to a transcript of the trial proceedings when necessary to prosecute an appeal. The Court stated that “the necessity for him to have some record of the proceedings in order to prosecute his appeal” was “substantial.” This underscores that appellate courts may consider the full record—including evidence not presented to the jury—to ensure that a defendant’s rights are protected.
