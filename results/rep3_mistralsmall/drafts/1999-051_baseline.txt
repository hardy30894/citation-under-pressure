**ARGUMENT**

**I. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim.**

Respondent respectfully submits that the Sixth Circuit erred in holding that a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim. This Court has long held that a prisoner’s failure to comply with a state procedural rule ordinarily bars federal habeas review unless the prisoner can demonstrate cause and prejudice. *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977). Cause to excuse a procedural default must ordinarily be something external to the defense, such as a factual or legal justification that cannot fairly be attributed to the prisoner’s own conduct. *Id.* at 87-88.

The Sixth Circuit’s rule—that an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim may excuse the default of another claim, even if the ineffective-assistance claim itself is defaulted—finds no support in this Court’s decisions. To the contrary, this Court has repeatedly emphasized that a prisoner’s own defaulted claims cannot be used to excuse another default. In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Court held that a prisoner’s defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel could not serve as cause to excuse the default of another claim, explaining that the prisoner’s own defaulted conduct could not constitute cause. *Id.* at 488. Similarly, in *Reed v. Ross*, 468 U.S. 1 (1984), the Court rejected the argument that a defaulted claim could serve as cause for another default, stating that a prisoner’s own procedural default cannot excuse another default. *Id.* at 11.

The Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule is also inconsistent with the principle that a prisoner’s defaulted claims are not cognizable in federal habeas proceedings. *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 750 (1991) (“[I]t is well settled that a state prisoner’s failure to raise a claim in state court procedurally bars federal habeas review of the claim.”). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would undermine this principle by permitting a prisoner to bootstrap a defaulted claim into federal court.

**II. Even if an ineffective-assistance claim is not defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to excuse another default unless it is first adjudicated on the merits in state court.**

Respondent further submits that, even if an ineffective-assistance claim is not procedurally defaulted, it cannot serve as cause to excuse another default unless it has been adjudicated on the merits in state court. This Court has held that a prisoner must first present a claim to the state courts before raising it in federal habeas proceedings. *Picard v. Connor*, 404 U.S. 270, 275 (1971). This exhaustion requirement ensures that state courts have the first opportunity to correct constitutional violations. *Id.* at 276.

The Sixth Circuit’s rule would allow a prisoner to bypass the exhaustion requirement by raising an ineffective-assistance claim in federal court to excuse another default, even if the ineffective-assistance claim has not been adjudicated on the merits in state court. This would contravene the exhaustion requirement and permit federal habeas review of claims that have not been presented to the state courts. *See Duncan v. Henry*, 513 U.S. 364, 365-66 (1995) (per curiam) (holding that a claim must be fairly presented to the state courts to satisfy the exhaustion requirement).

**III. The Sixth Circuit’s rule would encourage piecemeal litigation and undermine the finality of state court judgments.**

Finally, respondent submits that the Sixth Circuit’s rule would encourage piecemeal litigation and undermine the finality of state court judgments. This Court has long recognized the importance of finality in criminal proceedings. *See, e.g., Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288, 309 (1989) (plurality opinion) (“[A] state’s interest in the finality of its criminal judgments is a substantial one.”). Allowing a defaulted ineffective-assistance claim to excuse another default would permit prisoners to repeatedly challenge their convictions in federal court, delaying the resolution of their cases and undermining the finality of state court judgments.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, respondent respectfully submits that the Sixth Circuit’s decision should be reversed. A procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim cannot serve as cause to excuse the procedural default of another habeas claim, and the Sixth Circuit’s contrary rule should be rejected.
