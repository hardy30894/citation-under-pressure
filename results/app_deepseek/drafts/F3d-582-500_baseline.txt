**ARGUMENT**

**THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN GRANTING SUMMARY JUDGMENT ON ERDMAN’S FMLA CLAIMS BECAUSE GENUINE DISPUTES OF MATERIAL FACT EXIST AS TO WHETHER SHE ACCUMULATED THE REQUISITE 1,250 HOURS OF SERVICE.**

**A. Standard of Review**

This Court reviews a grant of summary judgment de novo, applying the same standard as the district court. *McTernan v. City of York*, 564 F.3d 636, 646 (3d Cir. 2009). Summary judgment is proper only when “there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(a). All facts must be viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party. *McTernan*, 564 F.3d at 646.

**B. Erdman Presented Sufficient Evidence to Create a Genuine Dispute Regarding Her Hours of Service.**

To be eligible for FMLA leave, an employee must have worked at least 1,250 hours of service during the previous 12-month period. 29 U.S.C. § 2611(2)(A)(ii). The FMLA defines “hours of service” by reference to the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c). Under the FLSA, “hours worked” includes all time during which an employee is “suffered or permitted to work,” even if not formally approved. *See* 29 U.S.C. § 203(g); *Tennessee Coal, Iron & R. Co. v. Muscoda Local No. 123*, 321 U.S. 590, 598 (1944) (holding that “work” includes “physical or mental exertion (whether burdensome or not) controlled or required by the employer and pursued necessarily and primarily for the benefit of the employer and his business”).

Erdman testified that she regularly worked extra hours outside the office, and her former supervisor, Patty Sarno, consistently authorized payment for those hours or permitted her to use them as “comp” time. (App. 34-36, 42-43). This evidence demonstrates that Nationwide “suffered or permitted” Erdman to perform overtime work. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 785.11 (“Work not requested but suffered or permitted is work time.”). The mere fact that Nationwide later sought to restrict unapproved overtime does not retroactively erase hours actually worked. *See Whalen v. J.P. Morgan Chase & Co.*, 569 F. Supp. 2d 327, 333 (W.D.N.Y. 2008) (holding that an employer cannot unilaterally exclude hours actually worked for FMLA eligibility purposes).

The record further shows that as late as January 2003, Erdman’s new supervisor, Stella Getgen, conceded the accuracy of Erdman’s “comp” time usage without objection. (App. 58-60). And when Getgen later admonished Erdman about unapproved overtime, she did so only after Erdman had already accumulated those hours. (App. 61-64). A reasonable jury could conclude that Erdman’s extra hours constituted compensable work time under the FLSA and thus counted toward the 1,250-hour threshold. *See, e.g., Reich v. New York City Transit Auth.*, 45 F.3d 646, 649 (2d Cir. 1995) (recognizing that employers may be estopped from denying that hours were worked after accepting the benefit of the employee’s labor).

**C. The District Court Improperly Resolved Factual Disputes at the Summary Judgment Stage.**

The district court, in granting summary judgment, appears to have credited Nationwide’s assertion that Erdman lacked authorization for overtime work and thus could not count those hours. However, the FMLA’s eligibility determination is a factual question concerning actual hours worked, not hours approved. *See* 29 C.F.R. § 825.110(c)(1) (stating that “hours of service” includes “any time that an employee is suffered or permitted to work”). Moreover, the court failed to consider Erdman’s evidence that Nationwide had a practice of compensating her for extra hours, which creates a material factual dispute. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986) (holding that credibility determinations and the weighing of evidence are improper at summary judgment).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should reverse the grant of summary judgment on Erdman’s FMLA claims and remand for further proceedings consistent with this Court’s opinion.
