**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Federal Courts Do Not Have Exclusive Jurisdiction Over State Malpractice Claims That Merely Require Application of Patent Law as an Incidental Element.**

The question presented is whether a state-law legal malpractice claim that requires a court to apply federal patent law necessarily “arises under” the patent laws for purposes of exclusive federal jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). It does not. This Court has long held that a case does not “arise under” federal law simply because a federal issue must be resolved. *Gully v. First Nat’l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 115 (1936). Rather, the federal question must be “a substantial one, and not merely a colorable one,” and the right to relief must “depend[] upon the construction or application of the Constitution or laws of the United States.” *Id.* at 112-13. A state malpractice claim that requires a court to determine whether a patent would have been valid under the experimental use exception does not satisfy this standard.

**A. The Patent Issue Is Not “Substantial” in the Context of a State Malpractice Claim.**

In *Gully*, this Court explained that a federal question is not “substantial” when it is merely “incidental” to the state-law claim. 299 U.S. at 117. Here, the patent issue—whether the experimental use exception would have saved Minton’s patent—is not the primary right being enforced. The malpractice claim is a state tort for professional negligence. The patent issue is merely one element of the “case within a case” that Minton must prove to establish causation. As this Court stated in *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912), “a suit to enforce a right which takes its origin in the laws of the United States is not necessarily, or for that reason alone, one arising under those laws.” The patent issue here is no more substantial than the federal tax issue in *Gully*, where this Court held that a state-law claim did not arise under federal law simply because it required interpretation of a federal tax statute. 299 U.S. at 117.

**B. The Patent Issue Is Not “Necessary” to the Malpractice Claim.**

Even if the patent issue were substantial, it is not “necessary” to Minton’s malpractice claim. A federal issue is “necessary” only if the state-law claim cannot be resolved without deciding it. *See Gully*, 299 U.S. at 112. Here, Minton could have prevailed on his malpractice claim by showing that Gunn’s negligence caused him to lose a settlement opportunity, without ever proving that the experimental use exception would have saved his patent. The patent issue is thus avoidable, not necessary. This Court has consistently held that federal jurisdiction does not lie when the federal issue is “merely collateral” to the state claim. *See Shulthis*, 225 U.S. at 569.

**C. Federal Jurisdiction Would Disturb the Balance Between State and Federal Courts.**

Finally, this Court has warned against expanding federal jurisdiction in a way that “would bring into the federal courts a vast number of cases” that Congress intended to remain in state courts. *Gully*, 299 U.S. at 117. Allowing federal jurisdiction over every state malpractice claim that touches on patent law would flood the federal courts with routine negligence cases. This would “disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities” that Congress carefully struck in § 1338(a). *See id.* at 114. The patent issue here is not the kind of “substantial” federal question that warrants exclusive federal jurisdiction.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed, and the case remanded for consideration on the merits.
