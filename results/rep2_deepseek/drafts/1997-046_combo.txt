**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Circuit erred in holding that minimal due process attaches to Ohio’s clemency proceedings and in remanding for speculative Fifth Amendment analysis. Both conclusions lack support in precedent.

**I. Woodard Has No Protected Life or Liberty Interest in Executive Clemency.**

The pardon power is an act of grace, not a legal right. United States v. Wilson, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833). “A pardon is an act of grace, by which the sovereign forgives a party.” Knote v. United States, 95 U.S. 149, 153 (1877). The Constitution confides this discretion to the executive, and it is not subject to judicial control. Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. 87, 120 (1925); see also Ex parte Wells, 59 U.S. (18 How.) 307, 310 (1855) (executive may attach conditions to pardons as it thinks proper). Because clemency may be withheld for any reason, a prisoner possesses a mere hope, not a protected interest. Due process applies only to deprivations of life, liberty, or property, and clemency does not deprive a prisoner of anything—it simply declines to bestow mercy. See Flemming v. Nestor, 363 U.S. 603, 610-11 (1960) (statutory benefits are not constitutionally protected interests); Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union v. McElroy, 367 U.S. 886, 898 (1961) (government may deny a privilege without a hearing).

The court of appeals nevertheless found that Ohio made clemency “an integral part” of its adjudicatory process, thereby triggering minimal due process. That reasoning is backwards. The pardon power has always been separate from the judicial system. Ex parte Grossman, 267 U.S. at 120. A state’s decision to provide a clemency proceeding does not transform a gratuity into a right. Wilson, 32 U.S. at 160. Because Ohio pardons may be granted or denied for any reason, no law or regulation creates a legitimate expectation of a particular outcome. Without such an expectation, there is no liberty interest to protect.

**II. The Voluntary Clemency Interview Does Not Violate the Fifth Amendment.**

The Fifth Amendment prohibits compelled self-incrimination. It does not prohibit a prisoner from voluntarily speaking to a parole board. Ohio’s regulation gives the prisoner the option of requesting a personal interview, but does not require him to do so, and does not require him to answer any question at that interview. The prisoner who participates may choose to say as much or as little as he wishes. That is the very antithesis of compulsion.

The privilege against self-incrimination is personal and may be waived. Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. 591, 597 (1896). A witness who voluntarily chooses to speak foregoes the protection. United States v. Sullivan, 274 U.S. 259, 263 (1927). A voluntary interview with a parole board is no different from any other voluntary statement. If Woodard fears that an answer might incriminate him, he may decline to answer, or he may decline the interview entirely. Ohio does not penalize silence; the regulation does not even authorize an adverse inference.

The “unconstitutional conditions” doctrine does not help Woodard. That doctrine forbids a state from conditioning a privilege on the relinquishment of a constitutional right. Frost & Frost Trucking Co. v. Railroad Commission, 271 U.S. 583, 594 (1926). But Ohio has not conditioned clemency on anything. A prisoner may submit affidavits, briefs, and other materials through counsel; he may request an interview and still assert the privilege; or he may refuse the interview altogether. There is no condition, and thus no violation.

Finally, any claimed risk of incrimination is speculative. The privilege may be asserted only in the face of a realistic danger of prosecution. Brown v. Walker, 161 U.S. at 599. Woodard’s convictions and death sentence are final; he identifies no pending or threatened charge to which his statements could relate. A hypothetical risk is not a constitutional injury.

**Conclusion**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed. Woodard has no due process interest in clemency, and Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not violate the Fifth Amendment.
