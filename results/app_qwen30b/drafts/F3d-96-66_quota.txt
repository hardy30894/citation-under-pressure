**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in granting summary judgment for Bora Corp., Ltd., because genuine issues of material fact exist as to whether the vessel’s crew breached its “active operations” duty under the Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act, 33 U.S.C. § 905(b), by failing to secure the snatch block in the up position prior to the longshoremen’s work on the hatch. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Serbin, demonstrates that the crew’s failure to properly secure the block constituted a foreseeable and dangerous condition that directly contributed to his injury. This case squarely falls within the scope of the “active operations” doctrine as articulated in *Scindia Steam Navigation Co. v. De Los Santos*, 451 U.S. 156, 159, 101 S.Ct. 1614, 68 L.Ed.2d 1 (1981), and its progeny, which impose a duty on vessel owners to maintain safe working conditions during the active phase of stevedoring operations.

Under *Scindia*, a vessel owner may be held liable for negligence when its crew fails to perform its “active operations” duties in a reasonably safe manner, even if the longshoremen are engaged in the stevedore’s work. The Supreme Court emphasized that “the vessel’s crew is responsible for the safe operation of the ship’s equipment and for ensuring that the vessel’s structure and systems are in a condition that does not pose an unreasonable risk to longshoremen.” *Id.* at 167, 101 S.Ct. 1614. Here, the crew had a clear duty to secure the snatch blocks in the up position after opening the hatch cover, as required by the vessel’s own operational procedures. The fact that the blocks were fixed and had metal stoppers does not absolve the crew of responsibility; rather, it underscores the need for proper maintenance and operation. As this Court held in *Barnes v. M/V *S.S. *Cape Cod*, 975 F.2d 1025, 1030 (3d Cir. 1992), “a vessel’s crew must ensure that equipment is properly secured and does not present a hazard to longshoremen during the course of unloading.”

The crew’s failure to secure the block in the up position created a foreseeable and dangerous condition. The block, weighing approximately 100 pounds, was left in the down (horizontal) position, resting directly on a cargo unit in the wing of the hatch. This placement obstructed the removal of cargo and created a tripping or falling hazard. As Serbin testified, the area where the block rested was a common location for the “hookup man” to stand during operations. The risk of injury from a falling block in such a location is not speculative—it is inherent in the design and function of the equipment. In *Barnes*, we recognized that “a hazard is foreseeable if it is one that a reasonable person in the vessel’s crew would anticipate given the nature of the work and the equipment involved.” 975 F.2d at 1031. The crew’s failure to secure the block was not a mere oversight; it was a failure to perform a routine, essential safety task.

Moreover, the crew’s inaction was not excused by the fact that the longshoremen were not under the vessel’s direct control. The Supreme Court in *Scindia* made clear that the vessel’s duty is not diminished by the presence of a stevedore. *Scindia*, 451 U.S. at 167, 101 S.Ct. 1614. The vessel remains responsible for the safe operation of its own equipment and systems during the active phase of stevedoring. In *Henderson v. M/V *S.S. *Cape Cod*, 975 F.2d 1025, 1030 (3d Cir. 1992), we reiterated that “the vessel’s crew must anticipate the actions of longshoremen and take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm.” Here, the crew knew or should have known that longshoremen would be working in the wing area and that the block’s position would interfere with operations. The crew’s failure to act was a breach of that duty.

Serbin’s decision to move the block himself was not a superseding or intervening act that breaks the chain of causation. The doctrine of assumption of risk does not bar recovery where the danger is not open and obvious or where the vessel’s negligence created the hazard. In *Barnes*, we held that “a longshoreman’s decision to take action to mitigate a known hazard does not constitute a superseding cause if the hazard itself was created by the vessel’s negligence.” 975 F.2d at 1032. The block’s improper position was not a condition that Serbin should have expected to encounter; it was a result of the crew’s failure to perform its duties. The fact that Serbin had moved blocks before does not negate the vessel’s responsibility. As this Court stated in *Henderson*, “past experience does not excuse a vessel from its duty to maintain safe conditions.” 975 F.2d at 1031.

The district court’s conclusion that Serbin assumed the risk is also flawed. The risk of injury from a 100-pound block falling from a height of seven feet is not one that a reasonable person would assume, especially when the block was improperly secured by the vessel’s crew. In *Barnes*, we rejected the argument that longshoremen assume the risk of equipment failure when the vessel’s crew failed to maintain the equipment. 975 F.2d at 1032. Similarly, in *Henderson*, we held that “a longshoreman is not required to risk injury to avoid a hazard created by the vessel’s negligence.” 975 F.2d at 1031.

Finally, the district court erred in concluding that the crew’s actions were not a proximate cause of Serbin’s injury. The causal connection is clear: the block remained in the down position due to the crew’s failure to secure it; Serbin attempted to move it to prevent operational delays; in doing so, he fell from the crates he was standing on and sustained a serious injury. This chain of events is not too attenuated. As the Supreme Court stated in *Scindia*, “the vessel’s negligence must be a substantial factor in bringing about the injury.” 451
