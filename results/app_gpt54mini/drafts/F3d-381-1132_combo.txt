**ARGUMENT**

The Court of Federal Claims erred in treating the government’s conduct as noncompensable as a matter of law. A physical taking occurs when the sovereign authorizes a permanent, continuous, or recurring occupation of private property by itself or by third parties, even if the invasion is accomplished through a formal property interest that the government has exceeded. *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 261–62 (1946); *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748–49 (1947); *Portsmouth Harbor Land & Hotel Co. v. United States*, 260 U.S. 327, 329–31 (1922). Here, the government did not merely use the easement as acquired in 1953; it constructed and maintained a fiber-optic communications network far beyond the needs of the electric-transmission easement, then dedicated the surplus capacity to commercial telecommunications users. That is an occupation of Tuthill’s property interest, not simply an internal governmental management decision.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the Fifth Amendment focuses on the reality of the burden imposed on the landowner, not on the government’s label for its conduct. In *Pumpelly v. Green Bay Co.*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 166, 177–78 (1872), the Court held that government-induced flooding that permanently invaded property was a taking because it destroyed the owner’s practical enjoyment of the land. The same principle governs when the government physically appropriates an owner’s right to exclude others. *United States v. Cress*, 243 U.S. 316, 328–30 (1917). The Court later explained that even partial and nonexclusive physical invasions may be takings when they are authorized and enduring. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–62; *Griggs v. Allegheny County*, 369 U.S. 84, 87–90 (1962).

The government’s overbuilt fiber network and commercial leasing are not protected by the original easement simply because some cable was permitted in 1953. An easement is a limited property interest, and the holder exceeds it when it burdens the servient estate beyond the granted terms. *Gilman v. City of Philadelphia*, 70 U.S. (3 Wall.) 713, 724–25 (1866); *Northern Transportation Co. v. Chicago*, 99 U.S. 635, 642–43 (1879). Where the government occupies or uses property outside the scope of its authority, the owner may recover in compensation if the invasion is physical and permanent. *Cress*, 243 U.S. at 328–30; *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–62. The Court of Federal Claims therefore erred in concluding that the “scope of the easement is not relevant.” It is central. If the government’s present use exceeds the servitude purchased in 1953, the excess is not an acquired property right; it is an uncompensated burden on Tuthill’s land.

Nor does the fact that the government installed the fiber itself defeat the taking claim. The question is not whether the cables were originally placed on the easement corridor, but whether the government has since converted the corridor into a commercial communications facility for the benefit of third parties. The Supreme Court has long held that government occupation for a public or quasi-public use is compensable even when the occupation is beneficial or efficient. *Monongahela Navigation Co. v. United States*, 148 U.S. 312, 327–28 (1893); *United States v. Lynah*, 188 U.S. 445, 470–71 (1903). The leasing of unused fibers to private telecommunications companies confirms that the government is exploiting an asset beyond the transmission needs that supposedly justified the easement. That additional burden is not erased by the government’s decision to reserve some capacity for possible future need. A taking turns on the actual present invasion and its legal effect, not on speculative future utility. *Dickinson*, 331 U.S. at 748–49.

The government may argue that Tuthill has alleged only misuse of an easement, sounding in trespass rather than takings. But the Supreme Court has never held that a physical taking is unavailable whenever the government’s entry can also be described as exceeding a land interest. To the contrary, repeated and authorized invasions can constitute takings even when they resemble an easement-like burden. *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–62; *Griggs*, 369 U.S. at 87–90. The distinction is not semantic. A trespass remedy addresses unauthorized entry; the Takings Clause addresses a government-imposed, permanent occupation of private property. *Pumpelly*, 80 U.S. at 177–78; *Lynah*, 188 U.S. at 470–71.

Finally, the government’s contention that no “additional physical intrusion” has occurred because the fibers already occupy the corridor misses the constitutional injury. The taking is not the physical laying of the cable alone; it is the permanent dedication of part of the corridor’s capacity to commercial users, pursuant to the government’s ongoing authorization. When the sovereign authorizes and maintains a continuous occupation that burdens the owner’s right to exclude and the practical use of the easement area, compensation is required. *Cress*, 243 U.S. at 328–30; *Causby*, 328 U.S. at 261–62; *Griggs*, 369 U.S. at 87–90. The judgment should therefore be reversed and the case remanded for determination of just compensation.
