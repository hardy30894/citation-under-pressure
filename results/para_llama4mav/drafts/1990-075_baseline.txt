The Controlled Substances Act (CSA) does not unconstitutionally delegate legislative power to the Attorney General. The non-delegation doctrine, as reaffirmed by the Supreme Court in Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361 (1989), allows Congress to delegate authority to a coordinate branch when it lays "down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to [exercise the delegated authority] is directed to conform." Id. at 372 (quoting J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The CSA provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, listing specific factors to consider when scheduling substances, including the substance's potential for abuse, its pharmacological effect, and the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(c). The Attorney General must also obtain a scientific and medical evaluation from the Secretary of Health and Human Services before scheduling a substance. 21 U.S.C. § 811(b).

The temporary scheduling provision, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h), is similarly guided by intelligible principles. The Attorney General may temporarily schedule a substance only if it poses "an imminent hazard to the public safety" and after considering specific factors related to the substance's history, current pattern, and significance of abuse, as well as the risk it poses to public health. 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3). These guidelines provide a sufficient check on the Attorney General's authority, ensuring that the delegation is constitutional.

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the Drug Enforcement Agency (DEA) is also authorized. The CSA explicitly allows the Attorney General to delegate his functions to other officials. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). The Attorney General has done so through regulation, assigning the DEA Administrator the functions vested in the Attorney General by the CSA. 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b). This sub-delegation is consistent with the statutory text and is a reasonable exercise of the Attorney General's authority.

The Toubys' argument that the delegation is unconstitutional because it allows the Attorney General to create crimes is unpersuasive. While the Supreme Court has invalidated delegations that lack sufficient guidance, see Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), the CSA's delegation is distinguishable. The statute provides clear guidelines and constraints on the Attorney General's authority, ensuring that the delegation is constitutional.

In conclusion, the CSA's delegation of authority to the Attorney General is constitutional, and the Attorney General's sub-delegation to the DEA is authorized. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.
