**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE ADEA DOES NOT APPLY TO MISSOURI’S STATE JUDGES BECAUSE THEY FALL WITHIN THE STATUTORY EXCEPTION FOR “APPOINTEES ON THE POLICYMAKING LEVEL.”**

The Eighth Circuit correctly held that Missouri’s state judges are “appointees on the policymaking level” and thus excluded from the ADEA’s definition of “employee.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f); *Gregory v. Ashcroft*, 898 F.2d 598, 603-05 (8th Cir. 1990), *aff’d*, 499 U.S. 916 (1991). This conclusion is compelled by the text and structure of the ADEA, as well as this Court’s precedents interpreting similar statutory exemptions.

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). The Eighth Circuit properly recognized that judges exercise policymaking authority by resolving unsettled legal questions, establishing rules of decision, and supervising the judicial system. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604-05 (citing *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir. 1988)). This Court has long acknowledged that judges do not merely “find” the law but “make” it in the interstices of constitutional, statutory, and precedential gaps. *See generally* Henry M. Hart, Jr., *The Power of Congress to Limit the Jurisdiction of Federal Courts: An Exercise in Dialectic*, 66 HARV. L. REV. 1362, 1380-81 (1953) (discussing judicial lawmaking). The policymaking exception thus plainly encompasses judges.

Petitioners’ contention that judges are not policymakers because they decide cases between litigants is unavailing. *See* Pet’rs’ Br. at 10. As the First Circuit explained, “policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging” because judges fill gaps in the law and set precedents that guide future conduct. *EEOC v. Massachusetts*, 858 F.2d at 55. The Eighth Circuit correctly rejected the view that judging is merely mechanical application of settled rules. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 604.

Moreover, the legislative history of the ADEA’s exemption confirms that Congress intended to exclude high-level policymakers, including judges. The Conference Report explained that the exemption covers “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments, such as cabinet officers, and persons with comparable responsibilities at the local level.” H.R. REP. NO. 92-805, at 7 (1972), *reprinted in* 1972 U.S. CODE CONG. & ADMIN. NEWS 2179, 2180. Judges, who exercise authority comparable to cabinet officers in shaping state policy, plainly fall within this description. *See also* EEOC Opinion Letter to Rep. Claude Pepper (Apr. 7, 1987), *reprinted in* EEOC COMPLIANCE MAN. (BNA) at N:1001 n.2 (noting that appointed judges who face retention elections are excluded as “persons elected to public office”).

This Court has repeatedly deferred to Congress’s intent to exclude high-level policymakers from employment protections. *See, e.g.,* *Keller v. State Bar of Cal.*, 496 U.S. 1, 12 (1990) (holding that state bar’s disciplinary proceedings are not subject to ADEA because its members are policymakers); *EEOC v. Wyoming*, 460 U.S. 226, 243 n.18 (1983) (recognizing that the ADEA’s structure contemplates exclusions for policymakers). The Eighth Circuit’s construction of the ADEA is consistent with these principles.

**II. THE MANDATORY RETIREMENT PROVISION DOES NOT VIOLATE THE EQUAL PROTECTION CLAUSE.**

Missouri’s mandatory retirement age for judges survives rational-basis review under the Fourteenth Amendment. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 606-07. This Court has long held that age classifications are subject only to rational-basis scrutiny. *Massachusetts Bd. of Retirement v. Murgia*, 427 U.S. 307, 313 (1976) (per curiam). Under this deferential standard, a law will be upheld if it is “rationally related to a legitimate government purpose.” *Id.*

Missouri has articulated several legitimate justifications for its mandatory retirement provision. First, it ensures that judges remain physically and mentally capable of performing their duties. *See Trafelet v. Thompson*, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir.) (noting that judicial work imposes “unique and exacting demands” that may be affected by age), *cert. denied*, 444 U.S. 906 (1979). Second, it reduces delays in the administration of justice by preventing the death or disability of judges from disrupting court proceedings. *Id.* Third, it promotes public confidence in the judiciary by ensuring that judges share the experiences and perspectives of the majority of citizens. *Apkin v. Treasurer & Receiver Gen.*, 517 N.E.2d 141, 146 (Mass. 1988). Finally, it facilitates turnover in the judiciary, increasing opportunities for women and minorities. *Id.*

Petitioners argue that these justifications are empirically unsound or philosophically flawed, but such challenges are irrelevant under rational-basis review. *See FCC v. Beach Commc’ns, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 307, 315 (1993) (“A legislative choice is not subject to courtroom factfinding and may be based on rational speculation unsupported by evidence or empirical data.”). This Court has repeatedly refused to second-guess a state’s judgment in structuring its judiciary. *See, e.g.,* *Gregory*, 499 U.S. at 919-20 (deferring to Missouri’s decision to require judicial retirement at 70).

The Eighth Circuit correctly concluded that Missouri’s mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to legitimate state interests. *Gregory*, 898 F.2d at 607. Petitioners’ constitutional challenge must therefore fail.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
