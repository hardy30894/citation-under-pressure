# ARGUMENT SECTION — BRIEF FOR RESPONDENT

## I. Section 811(h) Satisfies the Intelligible-Principle Standard That Governs Congressional Delegations.

Article I vests “all legislative powers” in Congress, but that provision has never been read to forbid Congress from enlisting the Executive's assistance in administering a complex regulatory scheme. This Court articulated the controlling test more than sixty years ago in the context of tariff rate-setting: “If Congress shall lay down by legislative act an intelligible principle to which the person or body authorized to fix such rates is directed to conform, such legislative action is not a forbidden delegation of legislative power.” *J.W. Hampton, Jr., & Co. v. United States*, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928). That principle has since been applied well beyond rate-fixing to delegations across the regulatory landscape. *See Yakus v. United States*, 321 U.S. 414, 423-27 (1944); *National Broadcasting Co. v. United States*, 319 U.S. 190, 225-26 (1943). Section 811(h) meets the standard many times over.

Congress did not hand the Attorney General a blank check. It specified the factors he must weigh — the substance's history and current pattern of abuse, the scope and significance of that abuse, and the risk to public health, 21 U.S.C. § 811(h)(1), (3) (incorporating § 811(c)(4)-(6)) — required a finding that scheduling is necessary “to avoid an imminent hazard to the public safety,” § 811(h)(1), mandated notice to and consultation with the Secretary of Health and Human Services, § 811(h)(4), and capped the duration of any temporary schedule at one year, extendable by only six months, § 811(h)(2). That is precisely the kind of governing criterion whose *absence* doomed the statutes struck down in the mid-1930s. In *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, 293 U.S. 388, 415 (1935), the Court found that the statute did not declare any policy as to the transportation of the excess production, did not qualify the President's authority, and established no criterion to guide his decision. And in *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 295 U.S. 495, 521-22 (1935), the Court likewise found the delegation unconstrained by any adequate legislative standard. Those decisions turned on the total absence of standards, not on the mere fact that criminal consequences might follow from the delegatee's action; Section 811(h) supplies exactly the standards those statutes lacked.

The Court has consistently sustained delegations far less confined than this one when Congress faced a fast-moving regulatory problem. In *Yakus*, 321 U.S. at 423-27, the Court upheld the Price Administrator's authority to fix “fair and equitable” wartime prices, recognizing that the rapidity of change in the regulated subject justified broad executive discretion. The same is true here: clandestine chemists can synthesize new “designer drugs” faster than the ordinary scheduling process can respond, which is why Congress authorized *temporary* action pending completion of full notice-and-comment proceedings. Likewise, in *National Broadcasting Co.*, 319 U.S. at 225-26, the Court held that the “public interest, convenience, or necessity” standard adequately guided the licensing of broadcast stations, and in *American Power & Light Co. v. SEC*, 329 U.S. 90, 105 (1946), it approved the similarly open-ended “fair and equitable” standard for utility reorganizations. Section 811(h)'s multi-factor, imminent-hazard standard is considerably more specific than either.

Nor is the absence of exhaustive technical detail fatal. In *Fahey v. Mallonee*, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947), the Court upheld a conservatorship statute containing scant express standards because the field possessed “well-known and generally acceptable standards” on which the delegatee could draw. The same is true of the pharmacological and law-enforcement criteria Congress specified in § 811(c). And in *Lichter v. United States*, 334 U.S. 742, 778-86 (1948), the Court sustained the Renegotiation Act's authorization to recapture “excessive profits” — a standard with no fixed numerical content — because Congress reasonably relied on the delegatee's expertise to fill in details Congress itself could not practically anticipate. The same practical necessity justifies Congress's choice here.

Finally, judicial review of the *constitutionality* of the delegation remains available, satisfying the concern this Court identified in *Carlson v. Landon*, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952). Although § 811(h)(6) bars review of the substantive correctness of a particular scheduling order, it does not — and could not — foreclose a defendant's right to challenge the delegation itself. That structural check preserves the “integrity and maintenance of the system of government ordained by the Constitution” that this Court has long protected. *Field v. Clark*, 143 U.S. 649, 692 (1892).

## II. Congress Authorized the Attorney General to Redelegate Temporary-Scheduling Authority to the DEA.

Petitioners' subdelegation argument fails for a simpler reason: Congress expressly authorized it. Section 871(a) of Title 21 permits the Attorney General to delegate his functions under the subchapter to officers and employees of the Department of Justice. 21 U.S.C. § 871(a). That express statutory grant distinguishes this case from *Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland*, 315 U.S. 357 (1942), where this Court invalidated a subdelegation precisely because the enabling statute contained no comparable authorization permitting the Administrator to assign his personal duties to regional officers. Where Congress has supplied that authorization, as it did here, subdelegation to an agency built to administer the very statute at issue is unremarkable. *Cf. United States v. Shreveport Grain & Elevator Co.*, 287 U.S. 77, 85 (1932) (upholding delegated authority to an executive department to set enforceable standards carrying criminal consequences). The Attorney General's 1987 regulation assigning temporary-scheduling functions to the DEA Administrator, 28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b) (1989), falls squarely within the authority Congress granted in § 871(a), and the delegation to the DEA was therefore proper.
