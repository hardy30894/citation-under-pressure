## ARGUMENT

### I. The District Court Erred in Designating Furlow as an Armed Career Criminal and Career Offender Because His Prior Convictions Do Not Qualify as Predicate Offenses Under the ACCA and the Guidelines.

The ACCA and the Guidelines career offender provision both require that a prior conviction meet specific definitions to serve as a predicate. The district court erroneously concluded that Furlow’s Georgia first-degree arson conviction and South Carolina crack cocaine distribution conviction satisfied those definitions. When the proper legal standards—grounded in principles of strict construction and common law—are applied, neither conviction qualifies.

#### A. Furlow’s Georgia First-Degree Arson Conviction Is Not a “Violent Felony” or “Crime of Violence” Because the Georgia Statute Is Broader Than Generic Arson.

The ACCA defines “violent felony” to include “arson.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii). The Guidelines’ identical definition likewise includes “arson.” USSG § 4B1.2(a)(2). Where a federal enhancement statute incorporates a common law term, that term must be given its settled common law meaning. *United States v. Sweet*, 145 U.S. 1, 8 (1892) (holding that, under a federal arson statute, “the crime of arson at common law is the malicious burning of the dwelling house of another”). At common law, arson was confined to the burning of a *dwelling house*. The Georgia first-degree arson statute, by contrast, criminalizes the burning of *any* building, regardless of whether it is a dwelling. J.A. ___. This broader definition means that a conviction under Georgia law does not necessarily establish that the defendant committed generic arson. The government cannot rely on a conviction that sweeps more broadly than the federal definition. *See United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (Marshall, C.J.) (“The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself.”). Because the district court failed to apply the categorical approach required by common law principles, it erred in counting the Georgia arson conviction as a predicate.

#### B. Furlow’s South Carolina Conviction for Distribution of Crack Cocaine Is Not a “Serious Drug Offense” or “Controlled Substance Offense” Because the State Statute May Cover Conduct Outside the Federal Definitions.

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as a state-law offense “involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance” with a maximum penalty of ten years or more. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” similarly. USSG § 4B1.2(b). The South Carolina statute under which Furlow was convicted, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-375, criminalizes “distributing” crack cocaine. But the term “distribute” under state law may include any transfer, including a gratuitous gift or a small share without a commercial purpose. The federal definitions, however, require a commercial or quasi-commercial element—an intent to “manufacture, import, export, distribute, or dispense” in the context of drug trafficking. When a state statute is ambiguous or broader than the federal definition, the rule of lenity requires that the ambiguity be resolved in the defendant’s favor. *United States v. Gradwell*, 243 U.S. 476, 485 (1917) (holding that penal statutes “must be construed strictly, and any doubt resolved in favor of the accused”). The government failed to prove that Furlow’s conviction necessarily involved conduct matching the federal definition. *See United States v. Lacher*, 134 U.S. 624, 628 (1890) (applying strict construction to criminal statute). Therefore, the South Carolina drug conviction cannot serve as a predicate.

#### C. Without These Two Convictions, Furlow Lacks the Requisite Number of Predicate Offenses for Both the ACCA and the Career Offender Provision.

The ACCA requires three prior “violent felony” or “serious drug offense” convictions. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). The career offender provision requires two prior “crime of violence” or “controlled substance offense” convictions. USSG § 4B1.1(a). The government’s information listed only four prior convictions: two Georgia drug convictions from 2003, the Georgia arson from 2008, and the South Carolina drug conviction from 2016. Even assuming the two 2003 Georgia drug convictions qualify (which Furlow does not concede), the removal of either the Georgia arson or the South Carolina drug conviction leaves Furlow with only two qualifying predicates for the ACCA—insufficient for the three required—and only one for the career offender provision—insufficient for the two required. The district court thus erred in applying both enhancements.

Because the sentence was imposed based on an erroneous application of the ACCA and the Guidelines, it must be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing.
