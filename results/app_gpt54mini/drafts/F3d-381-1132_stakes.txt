**ARGUMENT**

### I. The Government’s use of Tuthill’s easement to install and lease excess fiber-optic capacity exceeded the easement’s scope and effected a per se physical taking.

The Fifth Amendment requires just compensation when the government physically appropriates or occupies private property. *Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.*, 458 U.S. 419, 426–35 (1982); *United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 261–67 (1946). A permanent physical occupation is “the most serious form of invasion of an owner’s property interests,” because it destroys the owner’s right to exclude. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 435–38. That principle applies whether the invasion is by the government itself or by a third party the government authorizes to occupy the property. *Kaiser Aetna v. United States*, 444 U.S. 164, 179–80 (1979); *Nollan v. California Coastal Comm’n*, 483 U.S. 825, 831–32 (1987).

Here, the easement granted BPA only the right to “enter and erect, operate, maintain, repair, rebuild, and patrol” “electrical power transmission lines and appurtenant signal lines ... necessary in connection therewith.” The undisputed record shows that BPA installed far more fiber capacity than it presently needs for its power-transmission functions and now leases unused fibers to a telecommunications company. That is not merely a modernized signal line; it is a commercial communications system carrying third-party traffic for profit.

When the government uses an easement for a materially different and greater burden than the one authorized, it exceeds the property interest it acquired. A use outside the scope of an easement is a wrongful invasion of the servient estate. *Preseault v. United States*, 100 F.3d 1525, 1534–36 (Fed. Cir. 1996) (en banc); *Gould v. United States*, 67 F.3d 925, 930–31 (Fed. Cir. 1995). And when the government’s conduct imposes a new, permanent physical burden on land, the Takings Clause is implicated even if the intrusion occurs through the government’s easement rights. *Loretto*, 458 U.S. at 436; *United States v. Dickinson*, 331 U.S. 745, 748–49 (1947).

The court below held that “the scope of the easement is not relevant ... because it cannot give rise to a physical taking.” That reasoning conflicts with controlling precedent. Scope is precisely what determines whether the government is acting within the property interest it purchased or whether it has imposed a new burden for which compensation is due. The Federal Circuit has repeatedly recognized that the Takings Clause protects the landowner when the government’s use of an easement goes beyond what was acquired. *Preseault*, 100 F.3d at 1534–36; *Cienega Gardens v. United States*, 331 F.3d 1319, 1338–40 (Fed. Cir. 2003).

### II. Leasing excess capacity to third parties is a separate governmental occupation, not a mere incidental use of the easement.

The government argues that once the fiber was installed, later leasing of unused strands caused no additional burden. But a physical taking does not depend on whether the burden is large or whether the owner’s present-day use is immediately impaired. In *Loretto*, the Court held that even a cable installation occupying only a small portion of the building was a per se taking because it authorized a permanent physical occupation. 458 U.S. at 426–35. Likewise, *Kaiser Aetna* held that forcing a private marina to admit public access destroyed the owner’s exclusion right and was compensable. 444 U.S. at 179–80.

Here, BPA did more than place its own communications equipment on the line. It transformed the easement into a revenue-generating telecommunications asset by permitting unrelated private parties to use fibers far beyond BPA’s present operational needs. That private use is not necessary “in connection” with transmission-line maintenance. A government may not expand a limited property right into an ongoing commercial occupation and then invoke the original easement as a shield against compensation. *Nollan*, 483 U.S. at 831–32; *Preseault*, 100 F.3d at 1534–36.

The government’s reliance on projected future needs cannot justify present third-party commercial leasing. The Constitution requires a taking analysis based on the burden actually imposed, not speculative future utility to the condemnor. *United States v. General Motors Corp.*, 323 U.S. 373, 379–80 (1945). BPA’s own admission that it now needs only a fraction of the fibers on Tuthill’s land confirms that the excess capacity presently serves non-easement purposes.

### III. The proper remedy is just compensation for the physical occupation, not relegation to a tort theory.

A property owner is not required to recharacterize a physical taking as trespass to obtain a constitutional remedy. The Takings Clause supplies its own cause of action when the government physically invades private property. *First English Evangelical Lutheran Church of Glendale v. County of Los Angeles*, 482 U.S. 304, 315–19 (1987). The Court of Federal Claims’ suggestion that Tuthill “may have avenues of relief ... but not on a takings theory” misapprehends the claim. Where the government exceeds an easement and permanently occupies additional property rights, compensation is required. *Preseault*, 100 F.3d at 1534–36; *Cienega Gardens*, 331 F.3d at 1338–40.

Because the record shows that BPA uses Tuthill’s servient estate not just for its own power-transmission needs but also as a commercial fiber-optic platform for third parties, the government has imposed a permanent physical occupation beyond the scope of the 1953 easement. Under *Loretto*, *Kaiser Aetna*, and *Preseault*, that is a per se taking. The judgment should be reversed.
