**ARGUMENT**

The text, structure, and purpose of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act confirm that its workers' compensation provision extends only to injuries occurring on the outer Continental Shelf itself.

**I. The Plain Text of OCSLA Imposes a Geographic Situs Requirement**

Section 1333(b) extends Longshore Act coverage to disability or death "resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The phrase "operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf" unambiguously locates the relevant operations on the Shelf. But the provision's coverage is limited to injuries "occurring as the result of" those operations. The natural reading of this language incorporates two distinct requirements: the injury must (1) result from Shelf operations, and (2) occur where those operations are conducted—i.e., on the Shelf itself.

This interpretation is reinforced by the rest of OCSLA. Section 1333(a)(1) extends federal jurisdiction to "the subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon." 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(1). Section 1333(a)(2)(A) applies state law as surrogate federal law to the same geographic areas. The Act's repeated emphasis on locale demonstrates that "Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA's coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale." *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986). Reading § 1333(b) to ignore the situs of injury would sever the compensation provision from the statute's overall geographic framework.

**II. *Tallentire* Confirms the Situs-of-Injury Requirement**

The Ninth Circuit dismissed the Supreme Court's statement in *Tallentire* as "unconsidered dictum." But the Court expressly noted that § 1333(b) "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." *Id.* at 219 n.2. The Court did not say that § 1333(b) eliminated the situs requirement; it said the provision *added* a status requirement to the situs requirement that already applied. This statement was not casual. The Court cited § 1333(b) to distinguish it from the choice-of-law provision at issue, demonstrating the Court's considered view that both provisions incorporate geographic limits.

The Ninth Circuit's contrary reading would render *Tallentire*'s footnote meaningless. If § 1333(b) imposed no situs requirement, the Court would have said so rather than noting that the provision "superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement." *Id.*

**III. The Fifth Circuit's Situs-of-Injury Test Is Correct**

The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, correctly held that OCSLA requires the injury to occur on the outer Continental Shelf. *Mills v. Director, OWCP*, 877 F.2d 356, 362 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc). The court reasoned that "the location of the injury is determinative of OCSLA coverage" and that "§ 1333(b) incorporates both a situs and a status requirement." *Id.* at 361. This interpretation respects OCSLA's text and structure while avoiding the boundless coverage that would result from a purely causal test.

A "but for" test, by contrast, would produce absurd results. Any injury that would not have occurred "but for" a worker's employment on the Shelf—including injuries sustained commuting to work, at home, or on vacation—would be covered. *See Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805, 809-11 (3d Cir. 1988) (applying but-for test to cover a car accident on the way to meet a helicopter). Congress could not have intended such limitless coverage.

The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test is equally problematic. It lacks any statutory anchor and invites inconsistent, case-by-case determinations. The plain text provides a clearer, more administrable rule: the injury must occur on the Shelf.

**IV. Valladolid's Onshore Injury Is Not Covered**

Juan Valladolid was killed at Pacific Operations's onshore facility, La Conchita, located on the California coast. That facility is not on the outer Continental Shelf. Under any proper reading of § 1333(b), his death does not qualify for OCSLA benefits.

The Benefits Review Board and the administrative law judge correctly applied the situs-of-injury test. The Ninth Circuit erred by reversing their decisions and remanding for application of its novel "substantial nexus" standard. The judgment should be reversed, and the denial of benefits reinstated.
