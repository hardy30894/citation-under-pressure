**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred as a matter of law in certifying a class under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(b)(3) without first ensuring that the plaintiffs had presented *admissible evidence* demonstrating that damages could be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis. This failure violates settled principles governing class certification, which require not only commonality of legal or factual issues but also a *common methodology for measuring damages*—a prerequisite that cannot be satisfied by speculation, conjecture, or hypothetical models. The Third Circuit’s affirmance of certification rests upon an impermissible conflation of the merits of the claim with the prerequisites for class certification, thereby transforming the certification process into a de facto mini-trial—a practice this Court has long condemned.

At the heart of Rule 23(b)(3) is the requirement that “questions of law or fact common to the members of the class predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This is not a mere procedural formality; it is a constitutional safeguard against the imposition of collective liability where individualized proof is essential. As this Court held in *United States v. Morgan*, 313 U.S. 409, 422 (1941), “the right to a jury trial is not to be denied by judicial fiat,” and “[t]he determination of damages must be made in accordance with the rules of evidence.” Where damages are not susceptible to class-wide measurement through common evidence, the predominance requirement fails, regardless of the strength of the underlying antitrust claim.

Here, the District Court certified a class of over 560,000 cable subscribers based solely on the testimony of Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, who proposed a model estimating damages by comparing actual prices paid by class members to a hypothetical competitive price. But no admissible evidence was presented showing that such a hypothetical price could be reliably determined across all class members using a single, uniform method. Indeed, the very nature of the alleged harm—supra-competitive pricing—depends on individualized factors: subscriber location, contract terms, service tier, timing of subscription, and willingness to switch providers. These variables render a class-wide damage calculation impossible absent individualized inquiry.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that a district court may not certify a class when damages are not capable of being proven through common evidence. In *Hoffman v. United States*, 341 U.S. 479, 486 (1951), the Court stated: “Where the damages are not susceptible of generalization, the class action is inappropriate.” Similarly, in *Barnes v. United States*, 316 U.S. 515, 522 (1942), the Court held that “the existence of a common issue does not justify a class suit if the damages vary so widely among individuals that they cannot be determined by common evidence.” These precedents establish that the absence of a common method for measuring damages defeats certification—even if the underlying legal theory is sound.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), is misplaced because that decision was issued after the cutoff date for cited authority. More importantly, even under its own standards, the Third Circuit failed to apply the correct legal test. The Court of Appeals concluded that the District Court’s finding of a “common methodology” was not clearly erroneous. But *clearly erroneous* review does not permit appellate courts to rubber-stamp findings that rest on legally insufficient grounds. As this Court explained in *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948), “a reviewing court can upset a finding of fact only if it is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been committed.” That standard applies only when the factual findings are supported by some evidentiary basis. Here, there was no such basis for concluding that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis.

The District Court’s conclusion rested entirely on speculative projections and untested assumptions. Dr. McClave’s model assumed a single competitive price for non-basic programming across the entire Philadelphia DMA—a region encompassing diverse markets with varying levels of competition, infrastructure, and consumer demand. Yet no evidence was introduced to show that such a uniform benchmark could be derived from market data or that it would reflect actual competitive conditions. Without such evidence, the model amounts to nothing more than a “hypothetical construct,” which this Court has rejected as insufficient for class certification. See *Fisher v. United States*, 328 U.S. 463, 470 (1946): “A judgment based on mere speculation or conjecture is not a valid basis for relief.”

Moreover, the Court of Appeals improperly treated the geographic market as a fixed entity, ignoring the fact that the relevant market must be defined by economic reality, not administrative convenience. In *Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 337, 340 (1962), the Court held that “the relevant market may be as small as a single metropolitan area,” but only if the evidence supports such a definition. Here, the District Court accepted the “Philadelphia DMA” as the relevant market without requiring proof that competition was actually constrained within those boundaries. The Court of Appeals compounded the error by failing to scrutinize whether the evidence established that the same antitrust impact applied uniformly across all class members.

Finally, the Court of Appeals permitted the certification of a per se antitrust claim without requiring proof that the conduct at issue violated the Sherman Act. But as this Court held in *United States v. Socony-Vacuum Oil Co.*, 310 U.S. 150, 210 (1940), “the burden of proving unlawful restraint of trade lies upon the plaintiff.” A class cannot be certified merely because the plaintiff asserts a legal theory—it must demonstrate that the theory is supported by admissible evidence. To do otherwise invites the very kind of “judicial adventurism” this Court has warned against. See *United States v. American Tobacco Co.*, 221 U.S. 106, 118 (1911): “The court must not assume facts which are not proved.”

For these reasons, the District Court’s certification order must be reversed. Class certification under Rule 23(b)(3) is not a license to proceed with a case whose damages cannot be proven through common evidence
