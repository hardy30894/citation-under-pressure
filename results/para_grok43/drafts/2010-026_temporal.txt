The Freedom of Information Act commands that every agency record be disclosed unless it falls squarely within one of the nine exemptions. Those exemptions are to be read narrowly so that the statutory policy of full agency disclosure is not frustrated. 

Exemption 2 protects only matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2). The phrase “internal personnel rules and practices” reaches no farther than rules governing the conditions of employment—such as leave, pay, and working hours—and does not extend to operational or technical data merely because an agency finds the data useful in carrying out its functions. Any broader reading would convert the exemption into a general shield for sensitive internal information, contrary to the statute’s text and the rule that exemptions must be confined to their literal terms.

The “predominantly internal” test employed below cannot be reconciled with this limitation. That test substitutes a judicial balancing of an agency’s asserted need for secrecy against the public interest in disclosure for the categorical approach Congress enacted. Such balancing finds no support in the statutory language and would permit agencies to withhold records that Congress did not place beyond public reach.

The additional requirement that disclosure “present a risk of circumventing agency regulation” likewise exceeds the exemption’s scope. Exemption 2 contains no language authorizing withholding on the ground that release might impair enforcement of substantive rules. Where Congress wished to protect information whose disclosure could aid circumvention, it did so in other exemptions with explicit language. The absence of any such language in Exemption 2 confirms that the risk-of-circumvention rationale has no place there.

Because the ESQD documents at issue are neither personnel rules nor practices, they fall outside Exemption 2. The judgment of the court of appeals must therefore be reversed.
