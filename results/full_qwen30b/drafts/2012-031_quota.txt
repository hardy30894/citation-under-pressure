**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a district court may certify a class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a class-wide basis—requires this Court to reaffirm a foundational principle of class action jurisprudence: *class certification is not a license to conduct a mini-trial on the merits, nor is it a vehicle for speculative or hypothetical damage models.* The Third Circuit’s decision in this case, which affirmed certification based on a damages methodology that fails to satisfy the *Daubert* standard and contradicts binding precedent, represents a fundamental departure from the requirements of Rule 23(b)(3). The District Court’s certification of the class was an abuse of discretion because it rested on a damages model that cannot be applied on a class-wide basis and lacks the necessary evidentiary foundation. This Court must reverse.

### I. A Class Cannot Be Certified Where Damages Cannot Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011). This “predominance” requirement is not satisfied when the damages calculation depends on individualized inquiries into each class member’s conduct, preferences, or market behavior. In *Dukes*, the Court emphasized that “the predominance inquiry is not a mere formality” and that “the class must be capable of being adjudicated in a single proceeding.” *Id.* at 350–51. Here, the District Court certified a class of over 1.5 million subscribers based on a damages model that requires individualized assessments of each member’s willingness to pay, subscription choices, and responsiveness to competitive alternatives—precisely the type of individualized inquiry that *Dukes* condemned.

The damages model proposed by Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. McClave, relies on a “but-for” hypothetical: that without Comcast’s conduct, competition would have entered the Philadelphia DMA, and prices would have been lower. But this model fails to account for the fact that different class members may have different price sensitivities, different programming preferences, and different access to alternative providers. As the Supreme Court held in *Amchem Products, Inc. v. Windsor*, 521 U.S. 591, 622 (1997), “the class must be capable of being adjudicated in a single proceeding,” and “the common issues must predominate.” The damages model here does not meet that standard because it cannot be applied uniformly across the class without individualized proof.

### II. The District Court’s Certification Was Based on a Flawed and Unreliable Damages Model

The District Court’s conclusion that a “common methodology” exists to measure damages on a class-wide basis is unsupported by admissible evidence. The Court relied on Dr. McClave’s model, which assumes that all class members were harmed in the same way and to the same degree. But this assumption is not only speculative—it is contradicted by the record. The expert’s model depends on a “market-wide” price differential, but it fails to demonstrate that such a differential existed across all class members. As the Court recognized in *Wal-Mart*, “the mere existence of a common theory of liability does not mean that damages can be calculated on a class-wide basis.” *564 U.S. at 351.*

Moreover, the model fails to satisfy the *Daubert* standard for expert testimony. In *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592–93 (1993), the Court held that expert testimony must be “based on sufficient facts or data,” “the product of reliable principles and methods,” and “applied reliably to the facts of the case.” Dr. McClave’s model is not based on reliable data, as it relies on hypothetical market conditions that were never observed. It also fails to account for the fact that some subscribers may have been price-insensitive or locked into long-term contracts. The model’s assumptions are not testable, and its conclusions are not scientifically valid. The Third Circuit’s failure to scrutinize this under *Daubert* was error.

### III. The Third Circuit’s “Rigorous Analysis” Standard Does Not Permit Certification Without Admissible Evidence of Class-Wide Damages

The Third Circuit held that the District Court conducted a “rigorous analysis” under *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008). But *Hydrogen Peroxide* did not permit a district court to certify a class based on speculative or unproven damage models. In fact, the Court in *Hydrogen Peroxide* emphasized that “the district court must determine whether the plaintiffs have demonstrated, by a preponderance of the evidence, that common issues predominate.” *Id. at 318.* That burden was not met here. The District Court did not conduct a meaningful *Daubert* analysis of Dr. McClave’s methodology, nor did it require the plaintiffs to demonstrate that their model could be applied uniformly across the class.

The Third Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *Amchem*, where the Court held that “the class must be capable of being adjudicated in a single proceeding.” *521 U.S. at 622.* Here, the damages model cannot be applied in a single proceeding because it requires individualized proof of each member’s behavior. The Court in *Dukes* reaffirmed this principle: “If the damages calculation requires individualized inquiries, then the class cannot be certified.” *564 U.S. at 351.*

### IV. The Certification of a Class Without a Reliable Damage Model Violates the Seventh Amendment
