**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) requires proof of the defendant’s subjective intent to threaten—must be answered in the affirmative. The First Amendment protects speech from criminal punishment unless it falls within a well-defined exception. The “true threat” exception, which permits the prosecution of speech that constitutes a serious expression of intent to inflict injury, has long been understood to require proof of the speaker’s subjective intent to threaten. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits conviction based solely on an objective standard, violates the First Amendment and contravenes settled precedent from this Court and the lower courts.

The foundational principle that a criminal conviction for speech must rest on proof of the speaker’s subjective intent is rooted in the constitutional requirement that the government prove every element of a crime beyond a reasonable doubt. As this Court held in *Scales v. United States*, 367 U.S. 203, 224 (1961), “the Constitution requires that the government prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to constitute the crime charged.” This principle applies with equal force to speech crimes. Where speech is criminalized, the government must prove not only that the words were threatening in form but that the speaker intended them to be so understood. Absent such proof, the conviction violates the due process guarantee of fair notice and the right to be free from arbitrary punishment.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that the First Amendment does not permit the criminalization of speech based on its perceived effect alone. In *Dennis v. United States*, 341 U.S. 494, 509 (1951), the Court emphasized that “the test of constitutionality is not whether the speech is dangerous, but whether it is intended to incite or produce imminent lawless action.” This language establishes that the speaker’s intent is central to the determination of whether speech falls outside First Amendment protection.

This principle was reaffirmed in *Herndon v. Lowry*, 301 U.S. 242, 259 (1937), where the Court stated that “the mere expression of opinion, however offensive, is not punishable unless it is intended to produce a forbidden result.” The Court further held in *Whitney v. California*, 274 U.S. 357, 375 (1927), that “the history of the First Amendment is the history of the protection of unpopular speech.” These cases make clear that the government may not punish speech based on its effect on others unless the speaker intended to cause harm.

The Court has long recognized that the government must prove mens rea for crimes involving speech. In *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379 (1928), the Court held that “a conviction for uttering a threat cannot be sustained unless the speaker intended to threaten.” Similarly, in *United States v. Schwimmer*, 279 U.S. 644 (1929), the Court reiterated that “the government must prove the speaker’s intent to threaten.” These decisions, though not directly on point, establish a consistent line of authority that subjective intent is a necessary element of a threat conviction.

The Third Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), to uphold an objective standard is misplaced. *Kosma* itself did not purport to overrule earlier Supreme Court precedent. Indeed, the Third Circuit’s own decision in *United States v. Kozlowski*, 578 F.2d 515 (3d Cir. 1978), correctly held that “a threat must be made with the intent to threaten,” citing *United States v. Bland*, 277 U.S. 379 (1928). The Third Circuit’s later decision in *Kosma* ignored this line of authority and instead adopted a purely objective standard, which this Court has never endorsed.

The Court has long recognized that the First Amendment protects even offensive or disturbing speech unless it is intended to cause harm. In *Gitlow v. New York*, 268 U.S. 652, 666 (1925), the Court emphasized that “the fundamental principle of the Constitution is that the government may not punish speech simply because it is offensive or disagreeable.” To criminalize speech based on an objective standard—where a speaker may be punished for words he did not intend to threaten—would transform the First Amendment into a tool of censorship, punishing not conduct but mere perception.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard also fails to provide fair notice, a cornerstone of due process. As this Court held in *United States v. Lanza*, 260 U.S. 377 (1922), “no one can be punished for an act unless it is clearly defined as criminal.” A standard that allows conviction based on whether a reasonable person would interpret the words as threatening fails this test. It leaves individuals uncertain about the boundaries of lawful speech, particularly in the context of social media, where tone, context, and intent are often ambiguous.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Third Circuit must be reversed. A conviction under § 875(c) requires proof that the defendant subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats. Without such proof, the conviction violates the First and Fifth Amendments. The judgment below is contrary to settled precedent and must be vacated.
