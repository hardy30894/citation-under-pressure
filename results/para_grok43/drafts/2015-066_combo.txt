ARGUMENT

The Sixth Amendment does not constrain tribal court proceedings. Talton v. Mayes, 163 U.S. 376, 384 (1896) (holding that the Fifth Amendment grand-jury requirement “is not operative upon the Indian tribes”). Because the Bill of Rights “does not apply to Indian tribes,” Worcester v. Georgia, 31 U.S. (6 Pet.) 515, 559 (1832), a conviction obtained in tribal court without appointed counsel cannot be said to violate the Sixth Amendment. See also United States v. Kagama, 118 U.S. 375, 381-82 (1886); Ex parte Crow Dog, 109 U.S. 556, 568-69 (1883).

Section 117(a) therefore employs only valid predicates. The statute “penalizes only the last offense committed by the defendant.” Oyler v. Boles, 368 U.S. 448, 451 (1962). Recidivist legislation “imposes a higher punishment for the same offence” upon a showing of prior convictions, but “the prior conviction is not an element of the offense.” Graham v. West Virginia, 224 U.S. 616, 623 (1912). The government need only prove the fact of the earlier judgment; it need not relitigate the constitutional sufficiency of the proceedings that produced it. See Moore v. Missouri, 159 U.S. 673, 677 (1895); McDonald v. Massachusetts, 180 U.S. 311, 313 (1901).

Even when the prior judgment was rendered without counsel, its use as a sentencing factor does not offend the Sixth Amendment. Betts v. Brady, 316 U.S. 455, 462 (1942), recognized that the right to appointed counsel is not absolute in every prosecution. Where, as here, the tribal conviction is “valid when rendered,” Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 U.S. 458, 468 (1938), its subsequent consideration by a federal court does not transform it into a new constitutional violation. Powell v. Alabama, 287 U.S. 45, 71 (1932), required counsel only in capital cases presenting “special circumstances”; nothing in that decision precludes reliance on an otherwise lawful judgment for recidivist purposes.

The same principle governs the use of foreign or military judgments. In re Ross, 140 U.S. 453, 464 (1891), upheld a conviction obtained abroad without American constitutional protections when the judgment was later given effect in a domestic proceeding. Tribal courts stand on analogous footing: they exercise authority independent of the Constitution, yet their judgments remain competent evidence of prior offenses.

Because each predicate conviction satisfied the law applicable at the time and place of its rendition, and because Section 117(a) punishes only the present federal offense, the Sixth Amendment is not implicated. The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed.
