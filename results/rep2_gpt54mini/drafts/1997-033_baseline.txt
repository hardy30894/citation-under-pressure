**Argument**

The question presented is one of statutory command, not convenience. Section 1407(a) authorizes transfer only “for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings,” and then directs that “[e]ach action so transferred shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings.” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a). Congress thus limited multidistrict transfer to pretrial management and reserved trial in the originating district. In *Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach*, 523 U.S. 26 (1998), this Court held that a transferee court conducting MDL proceedings may not invoke § 1404(a) to assign a transferred case to itself for trial. Id. at 35-40.

First, the text of § 1407(a) forecloses self-assignment. This Court explained that the statutory command that transferred cases “shall be remanded” is “on its face” mandatory, not permissive. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 35. The Court rejected the argument that a transferee court may use § 1404(a) to accomplish what § 1407(a) requires the Panel to do, because “[t]here is no indication that the Panel should be in the business of transferring cases to itself for trial.” Id. at 39. The Court further held that “the Panel has no authority to assign a transferee action to itself for trial.” Id. at 40.

Second, § 1404(a) cannot be used to defeat § 1407(a)’s remand requirement. Section 1404(a) authorizes a district court to transfer an action “[f]or the convenience of parties and witnesses, in the interest of justice,” but this Court in *Lexecon* held that the statute does not permit a transferee court to “effectively nullify” § 1407(a)’s remand command. 523 U.S. at 37-38. The Court reasoned that “the transferee court’s authority is limited to pretrial proceedings,” and once those proceedings end, “the case goes back to the transferor court.” Id. at 34-35. Permitting a transferee court to invoke § 1404(a) to keep the case for trial would create an end run around Congress’s express allocation of authority to the MDL Panel. Id. at 40.

Third, the contrary rule adopted below rested on policy concerns, but this Court has repeatedly held that policy cannot override text. In *Lexecon*, the Court acknowledged the “practical considerations” favoring trial in the transferee court, but rejected them because “our task is to apply the statute as written.” 523 U.S. at 35. The Ninth Circuit’s view below—echoing the now-abrogated practice approved in *Pfizer, Inc. v. Lord*, 447 F.2d 122, 124-25 (2d Cir. 1971), and *In re Fine Paper Antitrust Litigation*, 685 F.2d 810, 820 (3d Cir. 1982)—cannot survive this Court’s decision in *Lexecon*. See also *In re Air Crash Disaster Near Hanover, New Hampshire*, 342 F. Supp. 907, 909 (D.N.H. 1971) (recognizing that nothing in §§ 1407(a) or 1404(a) “directly allows” the transferee judge to transfer cases to itself for trial).

Finally, the structural logic of § 1407 confirms petitioner’s position. Multidistrict transfer exists to coordinate common pretrial proceedings, not to alter the parties’ forum rights at trial. As this Court explained, Congress “carefully crafted” § 1407 to allow centralized pretrial management while preserving trial in the original district. *Lexecon*, 523 U.S. at 40. If a transferee court could retain a case for trial merely by invoking § 1404(a), the mandatory remand language would be stripped of effect. Statutes should not be read to render their own commands superfluous. *Id.* at 35-37.

Because the Arizona District Court lacked authority to assign the case to itself for trial, the judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to return the action to the Northern District of Illinois under § 1407(a).
