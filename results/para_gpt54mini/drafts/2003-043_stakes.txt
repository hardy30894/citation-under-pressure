## Argument

The government need not make a separate assessment against each individual partner to obtain the benefit of the ten-year collection period under 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). Section 6502(a) turns on whether “the assessment of any tax” was timely made, and nothing in the statute requires the IRS to duplicate an assessment against every person who may ultimately be liable for the underlying debt. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a). When the IRS timely assessed the partnership’s employment-tax deficiency, it preserved its right to collect that assessed tax for ten years. The court of appeals erred in holding that the government lost that extended collection period merely because it did not separately assess the individual partners.

This Court has long recognized that a tax assessment is the administrative act that fixes the government’s claim and allows collection to proceed. See Bull v. United States, 295 U.S. 247, 259-60 (1935). An assessment “has the force of a judgment for collection purposes.” United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. 114, 122 (2004). It is the tax debt that is assessed, not each procedural avenue by which the government may later collect from those who are liable for it. See id. at 121-22. That principle is dispositive here. The partnership’s employment taxes were timely assessed; therefore the government had ten years to collect that assessed tax. 26 U.S.C. § 6502(a); Galletti, 541 U.S. at 121-22.

Respondents’ contrary position conflates assessment with collection. Assessment determines and records the amount of the tax debt; collection enforces payment from those liable for that debt. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122. A taxpayer may be liable for a tax without having been separately assessed, because liability and assessment are distinct concepts. See id. at 122-23. The Code defines “taxpayer” broadly, but it does not say the IRS must assess every liable person before collecting an already assessed tax. 26 U.S.C. §§ 6203, 7701(a)(14), (a)(1). The Ninth Circuit’s requirement of individualized assessments adds a requirement that Congress did not enact. See United States v. Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123-24.

Nor does the statute of limitations for assessment, 26 U.S.C. § 6501(a), help respondents. Section 6501 governs the time to assess tax; section 6502 governs the time to collect after assessment. This Court has repeatedly treated those provisions as distinct. See Commissioner v. Lundy, 516 U.S. 235, 241-42 (1996). Once the IRS timely assessed the partnership’s tax, § 6502(a) gave it ten years to collect the debt. The fact that the partners themselves were not separately named in an assessment does not shorten that period. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-25.

Respondents rely on the Ninth Circuit’s view that California partnership law requires a separate judgment before partnership creditors may reach a partner’s personal assets. But state collection law cannot override the federal rule that a timely assessment of the tax debt permits collection within ten years. See United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc., 440 U.S. 715, 726 (1979). The question under § 6502(a) is whether the tax was assessed, not whether the government later must take an additional collection step under state law to reach a particular asset. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 123-24. The federal assessment here was enough to trigger the federal ten-year collection period.

Respondents’ reliance on partnership formalities is also inconsistent with the federal tax scheme. Partnerships are taxed through their taxable events and filings, but the IRS is not required to assess a partnership debt separately against each partner in order to preserve collection rights. See United States v. Updike, 281 U.S. 489, 495 (1930) (assessment fixes the government’s right to proceed). The government may pursue those who are liable for the assessed tax so long as it does so within the statutory period measured from the assessment. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 122-25.

Finally, the bankruptcy court’s disallowance cannot stand if the IRS held an enforceable claim when the petitions were filed. A claim is disallowed only if it is unenforceable against the debtor under applicable law. 11 U.S.C. § 502(b)(1). Because the partnership’s tax was timely assessed and the ten-year collection period had not expired, the IRS’s claim was enforceable. See Galletti, 541 U.S. at 124-25. The court of appeals’ contrary rule improperly deprived the government of the benefit Congress provided in § 6502(a).

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed.
