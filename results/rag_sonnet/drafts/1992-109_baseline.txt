# ARGUMENT

## THE DUE PROCESS CLAUSE DOES NOT REQUIRE A HEIGHTENED "REASONED CHOICE" STANDARD FOR COMPETENCY TO PLEAD GUILTY OR WAIVE COUNSEL

### A. The Competency Standard for Waiving Constitutional Rights Is the Same Standard That Governs Competency to Stand Trial

The Ninth Circuit erred in holding that due process demands a heightened competency standard—the capacity for "reasoned choice"—before a defendant may plead guilty or waive counsel. This Court has never adopted such a bifurcated approach, and the Nevada courts properly applied the competency standard this Court articulated in *Dusky v. United States*, 362 U.S. 402 (1960) (per curiam).

*Dusky* established that a defendant is competent to stand trial if he possesses "sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding" and has "a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him." 362 U.S. at 402. This Court reaffirmed that standard in *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162 (1975), explaining that competency requires "the capacity to understand the nature and object of the proceedings against him, to consult with counsel, and to assist in preparing his defense." *Id.* at 171.

Nothing in this Court's precedents suggests that a defendant who is competent to stand trial under *Dusky* somehow becomes incompetent merely because he chooses to plead guilty rather than proceed to trial, or because he chooses to represent himself rather than accept counsel. The decision to plead guilty and the decision to waive counsel are not more mentally demanding than the decision to stand trial; if anything, they may be less demanding, since the defendant need not track the intricacies of an ongoing adversarial proceeding.

### B. This Court's Precedents Confirm That Competency to Plead Guilty Requires Only the Dusky Standard

In *North Carolina v. Alford*, 400 U.S. 25 (1970), this Court addressed the validity of a guilty plea entered by a defendant who maintained his innocence, and the state court had found the plea "willingly, knowingly, and understandingly" made. The inquiry focused on whether the plea was voluntary and intelligent—not on some elevated competency threshold distinct from trial competency.

Similarly, in *Henderson v. Morgan*, 426 U.S. 637 (1976), this Court examined whether a guilty plea was valid where the defendant was not informed of an element of the offense. The Court's analysis centered on whether the defendant understood "the nature and object of the proceedings against him"—the same understanding required under *Dusky*—not on any heightened capacity for "reasoned choice."

This Court's treatment of waiver of counsel likewise confirms no heightened standard applies. In *McMann v. Richardson*, 397 U.S. 759 (1970), the Court evaluated the voluntariness of guilty pleas entered on the advice of counsel using ordinary competency and voluntariness principles, without suggesting that a separate, more demanding mental-capacity test governed the defendant's choice to plead guilty.

### C. The Ninth Circuit's "Reasoned Choice" Standard Lacks Any Grounding in This Court's Due Process Jurisprudence

The Ninth Circuit relied exclusively on its own precedents—*Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211 (9th Cir. 1973), and its progeny—to manufacture a standard requiring "the capacity for 'reasoned choice' among the alternatives available to him." Pet. App. 14. But those decisions are circuit-court inventions untethered to this Court's due process analysis. The court below acknowledged that "[t]he legal standard used to determine a defendant's competency to stand trial is different from the standard used to determine competency to waive constitutional rights," Pet. App. 14, yet cited no decision of this Court establishing that distinction.

To the contrary, this Court's capital cases demonstrate that ordinary competency findings suffice even where a defendant waives significant rights, including the right to pursue further appeals or to present mitigating evidence. In *Demosthenes v. Baal*, 495 U.S. 731 (1990), psychiatric examinations found the defendant "competent to stand trial," and on that basis the state courts accepted his guilty plea and rejected his claim "that he was incompetent to enter a guilty plea." This Court did not require a separate "reasoned choice" inquiry distinct from trial competency.

The procedural history surrounding *Lenhard v. Wolff*, 444 U.S. 807 (1979), and 443 U.S. 1306 (1979), is instructive as well. There, a capital defendant was "found competent to plead guilty and represent himself after an evidentiary hearing," and separately found to have made "a knowing and intelligent waiver" of further litigation rights. The competency determination and the knowing-and-intelligent-waiver determination were treated as related but analytically distinct inquiries—competency being the *Dusky* threshold, and knowing/intelligent waiver being the separate voluntariness inquiry this Court has long applied to waivers of constitutional rights generally. See *Brookhart v. Janis*, 384 U.S. 1, 4 (1966) (waiver requires "an intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right or privilege" (quoting *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938))).

### D. The Two-Step Inquiry—Competency Plus Voluntariness—Adequately Protects Due Process

The proper framework requires only two inquiries, both already grounded in this Court's precedent: first, whether the defendant is competent under *Dusky*; second, whether the waiver of rights was knowing, intelligent, and voluntary under *Johnson v. Zerbst* and its progeny, including *Brookhart*, 384 U.S. at 4, and *Arsenault v. Massachusetts*, 393 U.S. 5 (1968) (per curiam). This two-part framework, not a freestanding "reasoned choice" competency super-standard, fully vindicates due process.

The Nevada trial court in this case satisfied both inquiries: it ordered psychiatric examinations establishing Moran's *Dusky* competency, and it conducted a colloquy addressing the voluntariness of his waivers. No more was constitutionally required.

### CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed.
