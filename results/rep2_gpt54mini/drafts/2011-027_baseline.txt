## Argument

### I. The Court should reaffirm that navigability for title is fixed at the moment of statehood.

The equal-footing doctrine vests a new State with title to the beds of waters that were navigable when the State entered the Union. *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 228-29 (1845); *Shively v. Bowlby*, 152 U.S. 1, 57-58 (1894). The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that “[t]he test for navigability is whether the rivers were navigable in fact when the state was admitted to the Union.” *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 75 (1931); see also *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. 1, 14-15 (1935) (title turns on navigability “at the time of statehood”). That fixed temporal inquiry is not optional. It is the constitutional rule for determining sovereign title.

Montana’s contrary approach would improperly sever title from statehood and permit later conditions to define what the State received in 1889. The Supreme Court has rejected that approach. In *United States v. Utah*, the Court explained that the relevant question is the “natural and ordinary condition” of the river “at the time of statehood,” not its later condition. 283 U.S. at 75-76. And in *Parker v. Mut. Life Ins. Co. of N.Y.*, 331 U.S. 581, 609 (1947), the Court reiterated that title is determined by historical facts existing when the State was admitted. A trial court therefore must determine navigability as of statehood, stretch by stretch, before it can conclude that a State acquired title to a particular riverbed.

### II. Navigability for title is an “ordinary” federal question that requires evidence tied to the statehood date and to the relevant segment of river.

The Court’s decisions in *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1870), and *The Montello*, 87 U.S. (20 Wall.) 430, 441-42 (1874), define navigability in fact as susceptibility to use as a highway of commerce. But when the question is title, the inquiry is narrower: the river must have been navigable in its ordinary condition at statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75; *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14-15.

That principle has particular force because navigability is not a yes-or-no proposition for an entire river system. The Court has instructed that “[t]he issue of navigability is determined on a segment-by-segment basis.” *PPL Montana, LLC v. Montana*, 565 U.S. 576, 601 (2012); see also *United States v. Appalachian Elec. Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 407-08 (1940) (recognizing that navigability may vary by reach). A trial court must therefore identify the specific stretch at issue and ask whether that stretch, in its condition at statehood, was capable of commercial use. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 604-05.

Montana’s rule, by contrast, would allow later improvements, dams, channel changes, or modern recreational use to blur the constitutional line. The Court has rejected reliance on such later evidence to establish title. In *United States v. Utah*, the Court held that post-statehood conditions may aid the inquiry only insofar as they shed light on pre-statehood conditions; they cannot replace the required historical showing. 283 U.S. at 75-76. Likewise, *Oklahoma v. Texas*, 258 U.S. 574, 586-87 (1922), treated later evidence as relevant only to the extent it illuminates the river’s condition at statehood.

### III. The Montana Supreme Court’s approach conflicts with settled title doctrine because it permits title to rest on non-historical navigability evidence.

The State argued below that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers are navigable, but the constitutional inquiry is not present-day navigability. It is whether the relevant reaches were navigable in fact in 1889. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75; *United States v. Oregon*, 295 U.S. at 14-15. The Court has emphasized that the equal-footing doctrine concerns “title to lands under navigable waters,” which is fixed when statehood occurs. *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551-52 (1981).

The State may not rely on broad declarations about a river’s general character. The Court has made clear that title depends on the “particular segment” in dispute. *PPL Montana*, 565 U.S. at 604. Nor may it rely on evidence of current or later navigation unrelated to statehood. *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75-76. Because the Montana court’s analysis did not confine itself to the constitutional moment of statehood, its title ruling cannot stand.

### IV. Reversal is required because the statehood-based test protects settled federal interests and prevents expansion of state title beyond what the Constitution conferred.

The equal-footing doctrine ensures that every State enters the Union with the same sovereign rights as the original States, no more and no less. *Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 228-29; *Shively*, 152 U.S. at 57-58. Allowing a State to prove title by reference to later navigability would expand state ownership beyond the riverbeds actually conveyed at admission. That would upset the federal-state balance the Court has preserved in cases like *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, and *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544.

The proper rule is straightforward: a court deciding navigability for title must determine whether the relevant river segment was navigable in fact at the time of statehood. Because the Montana Supreme Court did not apply that constitutional test with the required historical precision, its judgment should be reversed.
