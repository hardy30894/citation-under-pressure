# ARGUMENT

## I. The Three-Judge Court's Population Order Satisfies the PLRA's Narrow-Tailoring Requirement

The Prison Litigation Reform Act permits exactly the relief the three-judge court ordered. Congress specified that "[p]rospective relief in any civil action with respect to prison conditions shall extend no further than necessary to correct the violation of the Federal right," and that such relief must be "narrowly drawn" and "the least intrusive means necessary to correct the violation." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A). This standard codifies the settled equitable principle that "the nature of the violation determines the scope of the remedy." *Milliken v. Bradley*, 433 U.S. 267, 280 (1977). The three-judge court applied precisely that principle here.

The court's extensive findings—following years of failed remedial efforts, including the receivership documented in the record below—establish that overcrowding is the primary cause of the unconstitutional denial of medical and mental health care in California's prisons. Petitioners do not dispute the constitutional violation; they dispute only the remedy. But once a court has found an ongoing violation of the Eighth Amendment's guarantee that prison officials must "provide medical care for those whom it is punishing by incarceration," *Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 103 (1976), and must "provide humane conditions of confinement," *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 832 (1994), the remedy must be measured against the cause actually found, not against a preferred alternative that has already failed. A population limit tied to design capacity—here, 137.5 percent—is narrowly drawn precisely because it corresponds to the numerical relationship between population and capacity that the court found, based on extensive expert testimony, to be driving the constitutional deficiencies.

This Court has long recognized that structural remedies for ongoing institutional violations may require systemic relief, including relief that affects populations beyond the named plaintiffs, where the violation itself is systemic. *See Hutto v. Finney*, 437 U.S. 678, 687 (1978) (federal courts have "wide discretion" to remedy Eighth Amendment violations even where that discretion touches core state prison administration). The PLRA did not abolish that discretion; it channeled it. Section 3626(a)(1)(A) requires narrow tailoring, not the elimination of systemic remedies for systemic violations. Where, as here, the violation is caused by crowding that pervades the entire system, a remedy confined to individual cells or individual plaintiffs would not "correct the violation," 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(1)(A), and would therefore fail the very narrow-tailoring test petitioners invoke.

## II. The Order Satisfies § 3626(a)(3)'s Specific Prerequisites for Population Relief

Congress imposed additional, specific prerequisites before a court may order a population limit. A three-judge court may enter such relief only if it finds "by clear and convincing evidence that—(i) crowding is the primary cause of the violation of a Federal right; and (ii) no other relief will remedy the violation of the Federal right." 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E). The record satisfies both.

First, the district court did not leap to a population order. As the Ninth Circuit's own recitation of this litigation's history confirms, the court initially proceeded by consent decree, then by stipulated remedial orders in 2002 and 2004, then by six days of evidentiary hearings in 2005 documenting continued noncompliance, and finally by appointment of a Receiver in 2006 to take over medical operations entirely. Even that extraordinary intervention proved insufficient, as the subsequent contempt proceedings over funding for the Receiver's Turnaround Plan of Action demonstrate. This is not a case in which the court skipped over less intrusive remedies. It is a case in which less intrusive remedies were tried, in escalating form, over nearly a decade, and failed. That history is precisely what § 3626(a)(3)(A)'s companion requirement—that the court have "previously entered an order for less intrusive relief that has failed to remedy the deprivation"—contemplates.

Second, the finding that "no other relief will remedy the violation" is amply supported. The three-judge court heard extensive expert testimony establishing that the deficiencies in care were a direct function of a population far exceeding what the prisons were designed, staffed, and equipped to serve safely. That evidentiary predicate mirrors the kind of factual record this Court has required before approving systemic prospective relief. *Cf. Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail*, 502 U.S. 367, 383 (1992) (remedial orders in institutional reform litigation must rest on the factual circumstances actually found by the court). Nothing in the PLRA requires a court to ignore its own factual findings, made after adversarial proceedings, in favor of a legislative presumption that some unspecified alternative remedy must exist.

Petitioners' argument reduces to a preference for a different remedy, not a demonstration that the remedy ordered exceeds what the PLRA permits. But *Rhodes v. Chapman*, 452 U.S. 337 (1981), on which petitioners rely for the proposition that overcrowding alone is not per se unconstitutional, is not to the contrary: it addresses whether crowding *establishes* a violation, not what a court may do once crowding is *found*, after full litigation, to be the primary cause of an established violation. Having proved that cause through years of litigation and repeated failed remedies, respondents are entitled to a remedy commensurate with that finding. The three-judge court's order is that remedy, and it satisfies every requirement Congress imposed in the PLRA.
