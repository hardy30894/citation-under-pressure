The judgment below should be reversed because § 4(b)(1) of the Occupational Safety and Health Act does not displace OSHA jurisdiction absent actual federal regulation of the specific occupational hazard at issue. The Act withdraws OSHA only where another federal agency “exercise[s] statutory authority to prescribe or enforce standards or regulations affecting occupational safety or health.” 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). That text requires more than a general maritime mandate or a limited set of vessel-safety rules. It requires exercised authority over the working condition in question. Where Congress has spoken in narrow preemptive terms, courts do not enlarge the exception by implication. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917); Chelentis v. Luckenbach S.S. Co., 247 U.S. 372 (1918); Knickerbocker Ice Co. v. Stewart, 253 U.S. 149 (1920); Washington v. W.C. Dawson & Co., 264 U.S. 219 (1924).

Here, the Coast Guard did not “exercise” comprehensive authority over the drilling hazards that caused the citation. The record shows only that Rig 52 was an uninspected vessel subject to minimal Coast Guard rules. That is not the same thing as a federal agency’s prescribing or enforcing standards for the industrial dangers of oil-well drilling aboard the barge. This Court has long recognized that maritime regulation is not all-or-nothing. Federal authority may exist in a maritime setting without extinguishing all other regulation; the question is the scope of the actual regulatory scheme. See Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924); Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932); Alaska Packers Ass’n v. Industrial Accident Comm’n, 294 U.S. 532 (1935). The Fifth Circuit’s contrary approach conflated general Coast Guard jurisdiction over vessels with actual regulation of occupational safety and health.

That error is especially clear because the Coast Guard’s authority over uninspected vessels is limited and equipment-focused. Congress authorized specific safety rules for uninspected vessels, but that selective regulation does not amount to a comprehensive occupational code for every workplace aboard such a vessel. See 46 U.S.C. § 4102. Section 4(b)(1) does not say that OSHA is displaced whenever another agency has some authority touching the same general setting. It says OSHA yields only where another agency has exercised authority to regulate the relevant safety matter. 29 U.S.C. § 653(b)(1). The distinction matters because an uninspected drilling barge is a workplace as well as a vessel, and the hazards OSHA cited were workplace hazards, not merely nautical equipment issues. See International Stevedoring Co. v. Haverty, 272 U.S. 50 (1926); Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924).

The Fifth Circuit also erred in treating the vessel’s uninspected status as irrelevant to § 4(b)(1) analysis. The uninspected-vessel distinction shows the opposite: Congress and the Coast Guard recognized that some vessels receive detailed federal supervision while others do not. The existence of some rules for uninspected vessels cannot be transformed into exclusive preemption of all OSHA standards on those vessels. This Court has rejected that kind of overreading before. In cases involving maritime regulation, the Court has looked to the actual reach of the governing federal scheme, not to abstract notions of maritime supremacy. See Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen, 244 U.S. 205 (1917); Chelentis v. Luckenbach S.S. Co., 247 U.S. 372 (1918); Alaska Packers Ass’n v. Industrial Accident Comm’n, 294 U.S. 532 (1935).

Nor does any pre-1970 decision support the proposition that a limited Coast Guard inspection regime bars all OSHA enforcement aboard uninspected vessels. To the contrary, this Court’s maritime cases caution against inferring exclusivity absent clear congressional direction. In Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22 (1932), the Court upheld a federal compensation system for maritime workers despite the maritime setting, demonstrating that workplace protection and maritime governance can coexist. Likewise, Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375 (1924), recognized that Congress may regulate maritime matters through targeted legislation; such legislation displaces other rules only to the extent Congress has actually done so.

Because the Coast Guard had not exercised authority to prescribe or enforce occupational safety standards governing the drilling hazards cited here, OSHA retained jurisdiction under § 4(b)(1). The court of appeals’ broad exemption for uninspected vessels cannot be reconciled with the statute’s text or with this Court’s decisions requiring courts to respect the actual scope of federal regulation rather than speculative exclusivity. The judgment should therefore be reversed.
