**ARGUMENT**

This case is not moot. The Constitution requires a live “case or controversy,” and that requirement is satisfied where a plaintiff seeks redress for a completed past wrong. *See* *Chafin v. Chafin*, 568 U.S. 165, 172 (2013) (reaffirming that a case becomes moot only when it is “impossible for a court to grant any effectual relief whatever”). Although the school revised its policy during litigation, that voluntary change does not extinguish the judicial power to decide whether the prior policy violated the Constitution or to award appropriate retrospective relief. *See* *City of Mesquite v. Aladdin’s Castle, Inc.*, 455 U.S. 283, 289 (1982) (voluntary cessation does not moot a case absent assurance the challenged conduct cannot reasonably be expected to recur). But even putting voluntary cessation aside, a nominal-damages claim preserves Article III jurisdiction because nominal damages are themselves a form of judicial relief for the invasion of a legal right. *See* *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247, 266 (1978); *Memphis Community School District v. Stachura*, 477 U.S. 299, 308 n.11 (1986).

The lower court erred by treating the policy revision as if it erased the prior constitutional injury. This Court has long recognized that “[t]he possibility of a judicial declaration that the past conduct was illegal and an award of nominal damages” supplies a live controversy. *See* *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266. That principle is rooted in the historic function of nominal damages to vindicate “the absolute rights of individuals.” *See* *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 162–63 (1803); *Baker v. Carr*, 369 U.S. 186, 204 (1962) (holding that a claim is justiciable where it seeks “a determination of the validity of the challenged statutory scheme”). A plaintiff alleging that government officials enforced an unconstitutional restriction on speech has suffered a completed injury when the restriction was applied, even if the government later alters the policy. *See* *Monroe v. Pape*, 365 U.S. 167, 187 (1961) (recognizing § 1983 as a federal remedy for constitutional deprivations by state actors).

The mootness doctrine is especially ill-suited to this case because the challenged policy was rescinded only after suit was filed. The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned that a defendant “cannot moot a case merely by voluntarily ceasing the challenged conduct.” *See* *United States v. W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629, 632–33 (1953). Otherwise, a government defendant could “return to his old ways” once the case is dismissed. *See* *Id.* at 632. Here, the school’s revision does not negate the alleged past suppression of speech or the legal consequences flowing from that conduct. The question whether officials violated the First Amendment by stopping a student from speaking and distributing literature remains one a court can answer, and nominal damages provide the traditional remedy for that completed violation. *See* *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266.

Nor does the absence of ongoing injunctive relief eliminate jurisdiction. The mootness inquiry asks whether there remains “any effectual relief whatever” that the court may grant. *See* *Chafin*, 568 U.S. at 172. A judgment awarding nominal damages is effectual relief. The Supreme Court has described nominal damages as more than a symbolic gesture; they are the legal acknowledgment that a right has been invaded. *See* *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266; *Stachura*, 477 U.S. at 308 n.11. Where, as here, the complaint seeks compensation for a completed constitutional wrong, the controversy is not extinguished merely because the defendant has changed its conduct going forward. *See* *W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632–33.

Finally, the respondent should prevail because dismissing constitutional claims as moot whenever a policy is revised would invite precisely the manipulation Article III forbids. The Supreme Court has long refused to permit public officials to evade review by altering challenged practices after litigation begins. *See* *W.T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632–33; *City of Mesquite*, 455 U.S. at 289. That concern is acute in free-speech cases, where the constitutional injury is the suppression itself. Once that injury has occurred, nominal damages keep the dispute alive and allow the courts to declare whether the prior policy was unlawful. *See* *Carey*, 435 U.S. at 266.

For these reasons, the judgment dismissing the case as moot should be reversed.
