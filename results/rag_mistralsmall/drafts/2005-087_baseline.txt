**ARGUMENT**

**I. ARIZONA’S LIMITATION ON THE USE OF EXPERT EVIDENCE TO THE INSANITY DEFENSE DOES NOT VIOLATE DUE PROCESS.**

The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause does not entitle a defendant to present expert evidence of mental illness to negate mens rea independently of an insanity defense. *Leland v. Oregon*, 343 U.S. 790, 798 (1952) ("The jury might have found appellant to have been mentally incapable of the premeditation and deliberation required to support a first degree murder verdict or of the intent necessary to find him guilty of either first or second degree murder, and yet not have found him to have been legally insane."). The Due Process Clause requires only that the State prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt; it does not mandate that a defendant be permitted to introduce evidence of mental illness for a purpose other than that recognized by state law. *Rivera v. Delaware*, 429 U.S. 877, 881 (1976) ("[E]vidence relevant to insanity as defined by state law may also be relevant to whether the required mens rea was present, [but] the existence or nonexistence of legal insanity bears no necessary relationship to the existence or nonexistence of the required mental elements of the crime.").

Arizona’s rule is consistent with this principle. The state permits expert testimony on mental illness solely to support an insanity defense, and the Due Process Clause does not compel Arizona to allow such evidence to be used for a different purpose. *Moore v. Duckworth*, 443 U.S. 713, 715 (1979) ("[A] state prisoner is entitled to a determination whether the record evidence could support a finding of guilt beyond a reasonable doubt."). Clark’s proposed use of the evidence—to negate mens rea—is not constitutionally compelled; it is a matter of state evidentiary policy. *Lynch v. Overholser*, 369 U.S. 705, 712 (1962) ("[T]he Government has established that [a defendant] committed a criminal act constitutes only strong evidence that his continued liberty could imperil ‘the preservation of public peace.’").

**II. ARIZONA’S INSANITY DEFENSE SCHEME COMPLIES WITH DUE PROCESS.**

Arizona’s insanity defense, which requires the defendant to prove insanity by clear and convincing evidence, does not violate due process. *Jones v. United States*, 463 U.S. 354, 366 (1983) ("A defendant could be required to prove his insanity by a higher standard than a preponderance of the evidence.") (citing *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 799). The Due Process Clause does not mandate a particular burden of proof for an insanity defense, and states are free to allocate burdens as they see fit. *Davis v. United States*, 160 U.S. 469, 488 (1895) ("The burden of proof, in every criminal case, is on the Commonwealth to prove all the material allegations in the indictment.").

Clark’s reliance on *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), is misplaced. *Mullaney* invalidated a state scheme that shifted the burden of proving malice to the defendant. Unlike malice, insanity is not an element of the offense; it is an affirmative defense that the state may place within the defendant’s burden to prove. *Leland*, 343 U.S. at 798. Arizona’s scheme does not offend due process because it does not shift the burden of proving an element of the crime to the defendant. *Rivera*, 429 U.S. at 881.

**III. THE TRIAL COURT’S RULING DID NOT DEPRIVE CLARK OF A FAIR TRIAL.**

The trial court’s exclusion of expert evidence on mens rea did not violate Clark’s right to present a defense. Due process does not guarantee a defendant the right to present irrelevant or inadmissible evidence. *Granviel v. Texas*, 495 U.S. 963, 967 (1990) (upholding a state’s statutory scheme for expert testimony in insanity cases). Arizona’s rule is a reasonable limitation on the scope of expert testimony, designed to prevent confusion of the jury and to ensure that the insanity defense is not improperly expanded. *Blackburn v. Alabama*, 361 U.S. 199, 206 (1960) (recognizing the state’s interest in regulating expert testimony in insanity cases).

Clark’s argument that the exclusion of this evidence violated due process is foreclosed by *Leland* and *Rivera*. The Due Process Clause does not require states to permit expert evidence to negate mens rea independently of an insanity defense. The trial court’s ruling was a permissible exercise of Arizona’s evidentiary authority, and it did not deprive Clark of a fair trial.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Supreme Court should be affirmed.
