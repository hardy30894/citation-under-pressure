ARGUMENT

I. The First Circuit Correctly Held That Massachusetts’ 35-Foot Buffer Zone Is a Constitutional Time-Place-Manner Regulation.

The Massachusetts buffer zone is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored regulation that leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. It therefore satisfies the First Amendment standards for time-place-manner restrictions in a public forum. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989); *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45 (1983).

A. The Statute Is Content-Neutral.

The law applies to all persons regardless of viewpoint. It prohibits *anyone* from knowingly entering or remaining within 35 feet of a reproductive health care facility’s entrance, exit, or driveway. Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(b). Unlike the ordinance struck down in *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552, 565 (2011)—which “disfavor[ed]” speech based on “the content of [the] message and the identity of the speaker”—the Massachusetts law draws no distinctions based on what a speaker says or whom the speaker represents. The exemptions are functionally necessary: patients, employees, and emergency personnel cannot be blocked from access. The law is “justified without reference to the content of the regulated speech.” *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

B. The Statute Serves a Significant Government Interest.

Massachusetts has a compelling interest in “ensuring the safe passage of persons wishing to enter healthcare facilities” and protecting public safety. *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 715 (2000). The record documents “congestion, obstruction, and harassment” outside clinics. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F. Supp. 2d 133, 145 (D. Mass. 2010). The 35-foot zone is a modest, targeted response to these problems. It is “narrowly tailored to serve [a] significant governmental interest” without excessively burdening speech. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 791.

C. The Statute Leaves Open Ample Alternative Channels.

The First Circuit correctly found that petitioners retain “a panoply of alternative channels of communication.” *McCullen v. Coakley*, 759 F. Supp. 2d at 213. They may speak, pray, and display signs from outside the buffer zone; use amplified sound; distribute literature; and engage in “sidewalk counseling” at conversational distances from patients who are not entering the facility. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 727 (upholding Colorado’s 100-foot buffer because “the statute [does not] prevent[ ] leafleting or oral speech” from the perimeter). The 35-foot zone is smaller than the 100-foot zone upheld in *Hill*, and the First Circuit found that “the possibilities for effective communication are not so limited as to render the Act unconstitutional.” *McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 181.

II. *Hill v. Colorado* Controls and Should Not Be Overruled.

*Hill* upheld a Colorado statute that prohibited “knowingly approach[ing]” within eight feet of another person, without consent, for the purpose of counseling or protest, within 100 feet of a health care facility. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 707–08. The Court held that the law was content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and left open ample alternative channels. *Id.* at 715–30. The Massachusetts law is even less restrictive: it creates a fixed 35-foot zone, not a floating bubble, and does not prohibit speech *per se*—only entry into a clearly marked area. *Hill* is directly on point and remains good law. *See also Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768 (1994) (upholding a 36-foot buffer zone as a content-neutral injunction).

Petitioners ask this Court to overrule *Hill*—a decision now 14 years old. Such a step would require “special justification,” *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827 (1991), that petitioners cannot provide. No subsequent decision has undermined *Hill’s* reasoning. *Sorrell* and *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310 (2010), involved different contexts—commercial speech and campaign finance—and did not question *Hill*’s holding that buffer zones around health care facilities are permissible. *Hill* remains the governing precedent for fixed buffer zones.

III. The As-Applied Challenges Fail.

Petitioners’ as-applied claims are essentially a facial challenge in disguise. The First Circuit correctly held that the law of the case doctrine barred relitigation of the facial validity. *McCullen*, 759 F. Supp. 2d at 136–41. Even on the merits, the evidence at trial showed that petitioners continue to communicate effectively: they have persuaded dozens of women to forgo abortions, and they use signs, prayers, and literature to reach their audience. The buffer zone does not “substantially impair[ ]” their ability to convey their message. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802.

CONCLUSION

The decision of the First Circuit should be affirmed.
