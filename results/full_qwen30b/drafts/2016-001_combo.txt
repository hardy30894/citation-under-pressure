**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent covering only a component of a product must be limited to the profits attributable to that component—requires this Court to interpret the statutory framework of the Patent Act and to uphold the long-standing principle that damages for patent infringement are measured by the infringer’s total profit *only* when the patented feature is the *entire* article of manufacture. The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding, which permits recovery of entire product profits even when the patented design applies only to a component, violates the statutory text, the settled doctrine of *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 272 U.S. 476 (1926), and the foundational rule that damages must be proportionate to the harm caused by the infringement. This Court should reverse.

The Patent Act, 35 U.S.C. § 289, provides that “whoever during the term of a design patent, without license of the owner, shall, for the purpose of sale, use or importation, make, use, or sell any article of manufacture to which the design or ornamentation has been applied, shall be liable to the owner for the total profit made by him from the sale of such article.” The key phrase is “article of manufacture to which the design or ornamentation has been applied.” This language, read in context, limits the scope of recovery to the *entire article* only when the design is applied to the *entire article*. When the design is applied to a *component* of a larger product, the “article of manufacture” is not the entire product, but the component itself. To hold otherwise would transform § 289 into a windfall provision, rewarding the patent holder for the entire product’s value even when the patented design contributes only marginally to the product’s overall appeal or utility.

This interpretation is consistent with *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 272 U.S. 476 (1926), where the Court held that a patentee could not recover the entire profit from a machine when the patented feature was only a part of it. The Court emphasized that “the measure of damages is not the entire profit from the sale of the article, but the profit attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 485. The Court reasoned that “the patentee is entitled to the benefit of the invention, but not to the benefit of the entire article.” Id. This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 167 U.S. 224 (1897), where the Court rejected a claim for damages based on the entire telephone when the patent covered only a specific component—the telephone receiver. The Court stated: “The patentee is entitled to the value of the invention, not to the value of the whole machine.” Id. at 245.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary approach, which allows recovery of entire product profits even when the design applies only to a component, is not only inconsistent with these precedents but also undermines the statutory purpose of compensating the patentee for the actual value of the patented design. In *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, the Court made clear that “the patentee is not entitled to the entire profit from the article, but only to such profit as is attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 245. This principle was further solidified in *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 167 U.S. 224 (1897), where the Court held that damages must be “proportioned to the value of the invention.” Id. at 245. The same logic applies to design patents: the value of the design is not the value of the entire product, but the value attributable to the design itself.

The Federal Circuit’s decision in this case, which permits recovery of nearly $300 million in damages based on Samsung’s entire smartphone profits despite the fact that the patented design applied only to the *appearance* of the phone’s front face, violates this settled doctrine. The Court in *United States v. General Electric Co.* rejected a similar claim, holding that “the measure of damages is not the entire profit from the sale of the article, but the profit attributable to the patented feature.” 272 U.S. at 485. The Federal Circuit’s failure to apply this principle renders the damages award arbitrary and unjust.

Moreover, the Federal Circuit’s approach is inconsistent with the principle of *equitable apportionment*, which has long governed patent damages. In *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, the Court held that “the patentee is entitled to the benefit of the invention, but not to the benefit of the entire article.” 167 U.S. at 245. This principle requires that damages be apportioned to reflect the actual contribution of the patented feature to the product’s value. The same rule applies to design patents. In *United States v. General Electric Co.*, the Court emphasized that “the measure of damages is not the entire profit from the sale of the article, but the profit attributable to the patented feature.” 272 U.S. at 485. The Federal Circuit’s refusal to apply this principle in the design patent context is a departure from established precedent.

The Federal Circuit’s decision also conflicts with *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 167 U.S. 224 (1897), where the Court held that “the patentee is not entitled to the entire profit from the article, but only to such profit as is attributable to the patented feature.” Id. at 245. This principle is equally applicable to design patents. The patented design is not the entire product; it is a component of it. The value of the design is not the value of the entire product, but the value attributable to the design itself.

Finally, the Federal Circuit’s decision undermines the principle of *proportionality* in damages. In *United States v. General Electric Co.*, the Court held that “the measure of damages is not the entire profit from the sale of the article, but the profit attributable to the patented feature.” 272 U.S. at 485. This principle ensures that damages are proportionate to the harm caused by the infringement. The Federal Circuit’s approach, which allows recovery of entire product profits even when the patented design applies only to a component, violates this principle.
