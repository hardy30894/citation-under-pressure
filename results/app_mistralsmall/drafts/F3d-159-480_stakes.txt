**ARGUMENT**

**I. CONGRESS LACKED CONSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY TO ENACT 18 U.S.C. § 1951**

Appellant challenges the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 1951 (the Hobbs Act), arguing that Congress exceeded its enumerated powers by enacting the statute. The Hobbs Act criminalizes interference with interstate commerce "by robbery or extortion." 18 U.S.C. § 1951(a). Appellant contends that the statute impermissibly regulates purely intrastate activity that has no substantial effect on interstate commerce, in violation of the Commerce Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, § 8, cl. 3.

This Court has consistently upheld the Hobbs Act’s constitutionality under the Commerce Clause. *United States v. Sullivan*, 919 F.2d 1403, 1422 (10th Cir. 1990) (citing *Perez v. United States*, 402 U.S. 146, 150 (1971)). In *Perez*, the Supreme Court held that Congress may regulate intrastate activity where it is part of a "class of activities" that, in the aggregate, substantially affects interstate commerce. *Id.* at 154. The Hobbs Act’s jurisdictional element—requiring proof that the robbery or extortion affected interstate commerce—satisfies this standard. *Sullivan*, 919 F.2d at 1422.

Appellant’s reliance on *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), is misplaced. *Lopez* invalidated the Gun-Free School Zones Act because it lacked a jurisdictional nexus to interstate commerce and did not regulate an economic activity. *Id.* at 561-62. Unlike the statute in *Lopez*, the Hobbs Act contains an express jurisdictional element and targets economic activity—robbery and extortion—that, by their nature, implicate interstate commerce. *See Sullivan*, 919 F.2d at 1422-23.

Moreover, this Court has repeatedly upheld the Hobbs Act’s application to intrastate robberies where the victim’s business engaged in interstate commerce. *United States v. Wright*, 932 F.2d 868, 871 (10th Cir. 1991) (upholding Hobbs Act conviction where victim’s business purchased goods from out-of-state suppliers). Here, the government presented evidence that Mr. Goodcents purchased goods from out-of-state suppliers, satisfying the Hobbs Act’s jurisdictional requirement. *See* R. Vol. III at 120 (testimony of Steve Peterson). Thus, the statute was constitutionally applied to Appellant’s conduct.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN EXCLUDING APPELLANT’S TARDY ALIBI DEFENSE**

Appellant sought to introduce an alibi defense based on a watch repair receipt and testimony from a jewelry store employee. The district court excluded this evidence as untimely under Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1. This ruling was erroneous.

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 12.1 requires a defendant to disclose an alibi defense "at the time provided by the court." Fed. R. Crim. P. 12.1(a). However, the rule does not mandate exclusion of untimely alibi evidence. *United States v. Cook*, 608 F.2d 1175, 1185 (9th Cir. 1979) (holding that exclusion is not mandatory where the defendant acted in good faith). Here, Appellant’s counsel sought to introduce the alibi evidence promptly upon discovering it, and the government suffered no prejudice. *See* R. Vol. II at 89-90. The district court abused its discretion in excluding the evidence without considering these factors. *United States v. Cook*, 608 F.2d at 1185.

**III. THE EVIDENCE WAS INSUFFICIENT TO SUPPORT APPELLANT’S CONVICTION**

Appellant challenges the sufficiency of the evidence supporting his convictions for conspiracy, Hobbs Act robbery, and felony murder. A conviction must be upheld if, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, "any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt." *Jackson v. Virginia*, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979).

Here, the government presented substantial evidence linking Appellant to the robbery, including testimony from accomplices and an eyewitness. *See* R. Vol. III at 50-60. While the eyewitness identification was weak, the jury was entitled to credit the accomplice testimony, which was corroborated by physical evidence and Appellant’s own admissions. *See id.* at 100-110. Thus, the evidence was sufficient to support the convictions.

**IV. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN INSTRUCTING THE JURY ON FELONY MURDER**

Appellant contends that the district court’s felony murder instruction misstated the law by omitting the requirement that the killing be a "direct causal result" of the felony. The instruction tracked the language of 18 U.S.C. § 1111, which defines murder as the "unlawful killing of a human being with malice aforethought." The statute does not require a "direct causal result" instruction, and this Court has upheld similar instructions. *United States v. One 1972 Chevrolet Blazer*, 812 F.2d 1331, 1335 (10th Cir. 1987).

**V. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN REFUSING TO INSTRUCT ON LESSER-INCLUDED OFFENSES**

Appellant requested instructions on lesser-included offenses, including simple assault and misdemeanor theft. The district court refused, concluding that the evidence did not support such instructions. This ruling was correct. A lesser-included offense instruction is required only if the evidence "would permit a jury rationally to find [the defendant] guilty of the lesser offense and acquit him of the greater." *Schmuck v. United States*, 489 U.S. 705, 716 n.8 (1989). Here, the evidence overwhelmingly supported the charged offenses, and no rational jury could have acquitted Appellant of the greater crimes while convicting him of lesser offenses. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Appellant’s convictions should be reversed.
