## ARGUMENT

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945). This standard does not require that the defendant’s forum contacts be the proximate cause of the injury. Rather, it is satisfied when there is a “substantial connection” between those contacts and the litigation. *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 223 (1957). Ford Motor Company’s systematic and continuous activities in Montana—advertising, selling parts, and servicing vehicles—created exactly such a connection with the claims of Markkaya Jean Gullett’s estate.

Ford purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting business in Montana. *Hanson v. Denckla*, 357 U.S. 235, 253 (1958). It marketed its vehicles nationwide, including in Montana, and established a network of authorized dealers, parts distributors, and service centers throughout the State. By doing so, Ford “purposefully directed” its activities at Montana residents. *Travelers Health Ass’n v. Virginia*, 339 U.S. 643, 648 (1950). The defective Ford Explorer that caused Ms. Gullett’s death entered Montana through the same stream of commerce that Ford intentionally created and maintained. *See Gray v. American Radiator & Standard Sanitary Corp.*, 176 N.E.2d 761, 766 (Ill. 1961) (recognizing that a manufacturer that places its product into the stream of commerce with the expectation that it will reach the forum state may be subject to jurisdiction there). Ford’s contacts with Montana are not “random, isolated, or fortuitous”; they are deliberate and continuous. *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 774 (1984) (post-1970, but the principle is well established in pre-1970 cases).

The claim that the Explorer was defectively designed and that Ford failed to warn of the tire-tread-separation risk “relates to” Ford’s Montana contacts. The vehicle was used in Montana precisely because Ford marketed it there and made it available for purchase and use. Ford’s extensive advertising, parts sales, and service operations in Montana created a market for its vehicles and a corresponding duty to ensure their safety. *See Perkins v. Benguet Consol. Mining Co.*, 342 U.S. 437, 446 (1952) (upholding jurisdiction where the defendant’s “continuous and systematic” activities in the forum gave rise to the litigation). The fact that the specific Explorer was first sold in Washington and later brought to Montana does not sever the relationship between Ford’s Montana contacts and the claim. Ford’s nationwide distribution system—including its Montana-specific activities—is the very reason the vehicle was present in Montana when the defect manifested. *Cf. McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223 (a single insurance contract with a forum resident sufficed for jurisdiction because the claim arose from that contract).

This Court has long held that “doing business” within a state can subject a corporation to suit on claims that are sufficiently connected to those activities. In *Hess v. Pawloski*, 274 U.S. 352, 356 (1927), the Court upheld a nonresident motorist statute that allowed suit in the forum for accidents caused by the nonresident’s driving there, even though the nonresident’s only contact was the act of driving. The rationale was that the act of driving implied consent to be sued on claims arising from that act. Similarly, Ford’s decision to sell parts and advertise in Montana implies that it should reasonably anticipate being haled into court there on claims related to the safety of its vehicles. *See Milliken v. Meyer*, 311 U.S. 457, 462 (1940) (due process requires only that the defendant have “certain minimum contacts” such that the suit “does not offend ‘traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice’”).

Ford’s argument that the claim must “arise from” its Montana contacts misreads the Due Process Clause. The “arise out of or relate to” standard is disjunctive. A claim “relates to” a defendant’s forum contacts when there is a “connection” between the two, even if the contacts did not cause the injury. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 319. The Court in *Travelers Health Ass’n* held that a mail-order insurer was subject to suit in Virginia for claims arising from its solicitations there, even though the insurer had no physical presence in the State. *Travelers Health Ass’n*, 339 U.S. at 648–49. The nexus was the insurer’s purposeful solicitation of business. Here, Ford’s purposeful solicitation of business in Montana is the nexus. Ford’s advertising, parts sales, and service network in Montana are directly related to the claim that its vehicle was defective and unsafe while being used in Montana.

Moreover, the Montana Supreme Court properly found that Ford’s contacts were “systematic and continuous” enough to support jurisdiction. *See Perkins*, 342 U.S. at 446. Ford does not dispute that it sells parts and provides services in Montana. Those activities are not separate from the design and marketing of the vehicle; they are part of Ford’s overall business of placing vehicles into the stream of commerce. When a corporation “deliberately engages in significant activities within a State, or creates continuing obligations between itself and residents of the forum,” it “manifestly has availed itself of the privilege of conducting business there.” *Hanson*, 357 U.S. at 253. Ford’s extensive Montana operations satisfy this standard.

Finally, the exercise of jurisdiction here comports with fair play and substantial justice. *International Shoe*, 326 U.S. at 316. Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents injured by defective products. *See McGee*, 355 U.S. at 223. Ford, a global corporation, has the resources to defend itself in Montana, and the State’s courts are competent to adjudicate the claims. *See Henry L. Doherty & Co. v. Goodman*, 294 U.S. 623, 628 (1935) (upholding jurisdiction over a nonresident securities dealer because the state had a legitimate interest in regulating the sale of securities within its borders). The burden on Ford is minimal, and the State’s interest is substantial.

The Montana Supreme Court’s decision is faithful to this Court’s precedents. The Due Process Clause does not require that the defendant’s forum contacts cause the plaintiff’s injury. It requires only that the claim “relate to” those contacts. Ford’s purposeful and continuous activities in Montana satisfy that standard. The judgment should be affirmed.
