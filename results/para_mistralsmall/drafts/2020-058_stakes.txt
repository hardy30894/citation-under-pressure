**ARGUMENT**

I. **A GRANT OF TPS CONSTITUTES AN “ADMISSION” UNDER 8 U.S.C. § 1255 BECAUSE IT IS A LAWFUL ENTRY FOLLOWING INSPECTION AND AUTHORIZATION**

The plain text of 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)(A) defines “admission” as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” *See INS v. Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. 421, 432 (1987) (statutory terms are construed according to their plain meaning unless otherwise defined). The Third Circuit’s contrary interpretation in *Sanchez v. Mayorkas*, 8 F.4th 495 (3d Cir. 2021), erroneously divorces the concept of “lawful entry” from the statutory definition of “admission.” *See id.* at 503-04. This misreading ignores that TPS recipients, like all noncitizens granted TPS, undergo individualized adjudication by USCIS, including biographic and biometric background checks, before being authorized to remain in the United States. *See 8 C.F.R. § 244.7*. Such authorization constitutes “inspection and authorization by an immigration officer” within the meaning of § 1101(a)(13)(A).

Moreover, § 1254a(f)(4) expressly provides that a TPS recipient “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant.” *See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4)*. The Third Circuit’s holding that this provision does not also confer “admission” under § 1255(a) is inconsistent with the statutory framework. *See Sanchez*, 8 F.4th at 504-05. As the Ninth Circuit correctly recognized in *Ramirez v. Brown*, 852 F.3d 954 (9th Cir. 2017), when Congress intended to distinguish between “status” and “admission,” it did so explicitly. *See id.* at 960-61. Here, Congress did not. Instead, it provided that TPS recipients are “considered” as lawful nonimmigrants—language that necessarily includes the legal fiction of admission for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255.

II. **CONGRESSIONAL SILENCE ON THE ADMISSION REQUIREMENT FOR TPS RECIPIENTS REFLECTS INTENT TO WAIVE IT**

The Third Circuit erroneously relied on the canon that “where Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another section of the same Act, it is generally presumed that Congress acts intentionally and purposely in the disparate inclusion or exclusion.” *Sanchez*, 8 F.4th at 506 (quoting *Cardoza-Fonseca*, 480 U.S. at 432). This presumption does not apply where, as here, Congress has spoken directly to the legal consequences of TPS through § 1254a(f)(4). *See Chevron, U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Res. Def. Council, Inc.*, 467 U.S. 837, 842-43 (1984) (agency interpretations are owed deference where statute is ambiguous). The Third Circuit’s reliance on *Cardoza-Fonseca* was misplaced because § 1254a(f)(4) does not merely omit the admission requirement—it affirmatively provides an alternative path to lawful status that must be read harmoniously with § 1255.

The Eleventh Circuit’s decision in *Serrano v. Att’y Gen.*, 655 F.3d 1260 (11th Cir. 2011), is not controlling here, but its reasoning is persuasive. The *Serrano* court held that TPS does not waive the admission requirement for § 1255(a) purposes, but it did not address whether TPS itself constitutes an admission. *See id.* at 1265. This Court should go further and hold that TPS recipients, by operation of § 1254a(f)(4), are deemed admitted for purposes of adjustment of status.

III. **THE GOVERNMENT’S INTERPRETATION WOULD RENDER § 1254a(f)(4) SUPERFLUOUS AND DEFEAT CONGRESSIONAL PURPOSE**

The Third Circuit’s holding that TPS does not confer admission renders § 1254a(f)(4) meaningless. *See Sanchez*, 8 F.4th at 507. If TPS recipients are not “admitted” for purposes of § 1255, then the provision that they “shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant” has no practical effect. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(f)(4)*. This violates the rule that statutes should be construed to give effect to all their provisions. *See Hibbs v. Winn*, 542 U.S. 88, 101 (2004).

Furthermore, the Third Circuit’s interpretation undermines the humanitarian purpose of TPS. *See Sanchez*, 8 F.4th at 508. TPS was designed to provide temporary protection to nationals of countries suffering from armed conflict or natural disaster. *See 8 U.S.C. § 1254a(b)(1)*. The Third Circuit’s holding forces TPS recipients who entered without inspection to leave the United States and reenter legally—a dangerous and often impossible requirement—before they can adjust status. *See id.* This result is inconsistent with Congress’s intent to provide relief, not additional barriers. *See INS v. St. Cyr*, 533 U.S. 289, 308 (2001) (courts should avoid interpretations that render statutory schemes “harsh and absurd”).

IV. **THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT TPS CONSTITUTES AN ADMISSION FOR § 1255 PURPOSES**

The District Court’s well-reasoned opinion in *Sanchez v. Johnson*, 2018 WL 6427894 (D.N.J. 2018), correctly held that a grant of TPS meets § 1255(a)’s requirement of being “inspected and admitted or paroled.” *See id.* at *5-6*. The court recognized that § 1254a(f)(4) places TPS recipients in lawful nonimmigrant status, which is functionally equivalent to admission for adjustment purposes. *See id.* at *4*. This Court should affirm the District Court’s judgment and hold that TPS recipients who entered without inspection are eligible for adjustment of status under § 1255.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Petitioners respectfully submit that a grant of TPS constitutes an “admission” under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. The Third Circuit’s contrary holding should be reversed, and the District Court’s judgment should be reinstated.
