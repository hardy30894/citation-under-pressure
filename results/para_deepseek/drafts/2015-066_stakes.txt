**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Sixth Amendment Prohibits the Government from Using an Uncounseled Conviction to Prove Guilt in a Subsequent Federal Prosecution.**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right… to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence.” The right to counsel is “fundamental and essential to a fair trial.” *Gideon v. Wainwright*, 372 U.S. 335, 341 (1963). It exists to ensure a reliable outcome. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984). Applying these principles, this Court held in *Burgett v. Texas*, 389 U.S. 109, 115 (1967), that “[t]o permit a conviction obtained in violation of *Gideon v. Wainwright* to be used against a person either to support guilt or enhance punishment for another offense is to erode the principle of that case.” *Burgett* thus draws a clear line: an uncounseled conviction may not serve as a building block of guilt. This Court has enforced that principle across contexts, holding that uncounseled convictions may not even be used to impeach a defendant’s credibility. *Loper v. Beto*, 405 U.S. 473, 483 (1972). The reason is always the same: a conviction obtained without counsel is unreliable.

**II. *Nichols v. United States* Is Distinguishable and Does Not Sanction the Use of Uncounseled Convictions as Elements of an Offense.**

The government relies on *Nichols v. United States*, 511 U.S. 738 (1994). But *Nichols* is narrow. There, this Court held only that an uncounseled misdemeanor conviction, valid under *Scott v. Illinois* because no prison term was imposed, is valid for calculating criminal history points under the Sentencing Guidelines. *Id.* at 746. Two features separate *Nichols* from this case.

*First*, *Nichols* involved sentencing, not guilt. The prior conviction was used to compute criminal history points, not to establish an element of the offense. The Court emphasized that reliability concerns were “accommodated” because the Guidelines allowed a defendant to challenge the reliability of prior convictions and because sentencing employs a preponderance standard, not proof beyond a reasonable doubt. *Id.* at 747-48. Section 117(a) offers no such accommodation. The predicate convictions are elements of the offense that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). A jury has no authority to discount a predicate conviction as unreliable; if it exists, the element is satisfied. Nothing in *Nichols* “purports to sanction the use of an uncounseled conviction for Winship purposes.”

*Second*, the prior conviction in *Nichols* was constitutionally valid under *Scott* because the defendant was not imprisoned. By contrast, Bryant’s tribal court convictions resulted in terms of imprisonment. Had those convictions been obtained in state or federal court, they would be void, for “absent a knowing and intelligent waiver, no person may be imprisoned for any offense… unless he was represented by counsel.” *Scott v. Illinois*, 440 U.S. 367, 373-74 (1979) (citing *Argersinger v. Hamlin*, 407 U.S. 25, 37 (1972)). *Nichols* merely permits use of convictions that are valid under *Scott*; it does not bless convictions that would be invalid in any forum bound by the Constitution.

**III. The Federal Government Cannot Circumvent the Sixth Amendment by Relying on Convictions from a Forum Not Bound by It.**

The government argues that the Sixth Amendment does not apply to tribal courts, so Bryant’s convictions are “valid.” See *Talton v. Mayes*, 163 U.S. 376 (1896). That may be true of the tribal proceedings themselves, but it does not answer the constitutional question here. This is a federal prosecution, and the federal government is fully bound by the Sixth Amendment. The question is whether the government may use convictions from a proceeding in which the defendant had no counsel to establish an essential element of a federal felony carrying up to ten years’ imprisonment.

The accident of the forum does not make Bryant’s prior convictions reliable. He stood before the tribal court alone—no lawyer to investigate, to test the evidence, to cross-examine witnesses, or to preserve objections. The judgments the government now uses to transform a misdemeanor into a felony are untested and unreliable. When the government imports those judgments into a federal prosecution, it accomplishes precisely what *Burgett* forbids: it uses convictions obtained without counsel “to support guilt.” The fact that the Sixth Amendment did not bind the tribal court is not a reason to permit what the Sixth Amendment forbids here; it is the very reason these convictions are unreliable.

The government’s position would allow Congress to nullify the Sixth Amendment through forum choice. Congress could designate any uncounseled conviction—from a foreign tribunal, an administrative proceeding, or a tribal court—as a predicate element of a federal felony, and the government could obtain a felony conviction without ever proving guilt in a proceeding that satisfied the Constitution. That result is irreconcilable with *Burgett* and with this Court’s insistence that the right to counsel is “fundamental and essential to a fair trial.” *Gideon*, 372 U.S. at 341.

**IV. Congress Knows How to Condition Federal Consequences on the Provision of Counsel.**

Finally, Congress is free to authorize federal consequences based on tribal court convictions—but it must do so in a manner consistent with the Sixth Amendment. Congress has demonstrated that it knows how. In the Tribal Law and Order Act of 2010, Congress expanded tribal court sentencing authority but conditioned that expansion on a requirement that tribal courts provide a right to effective assistance of counsel at least equal to that guaranteed by the United States Constitution. 25 U.S.C. § 1302(c). Likewise, in the Violence Against Women Reauthorization Act of 2013, Congress conditioned the exercise of special domestic violence jurisdiction on the provision of such counsel. 25 U.S.C. § 1304(d). Congress therefore knows precisely how to ensure reliability when tribal convictions carry federal consequences. Section 117(a) contains no such protection, and this Court should not read it to authorize a violation of the Sixth Amendment.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
