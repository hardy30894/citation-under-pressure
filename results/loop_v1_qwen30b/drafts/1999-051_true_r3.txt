**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a state prisoner’s procedurally defaulted claim of ineffective assistance of counsel may excuse the procedural default of another habeas corpus claim—must be answered in the negative. The doctrine of procedural default, as established by this Court, is not subject to unlimited excusal through the assertion of a second defaulted claim, particularly one that itself fails to satisfy the requirements of cause and prejudice. The Sixth Circuit’s decision to excuse the default of Edwards’ vagueness challenge on the basis of a defaulted ineffective assistance claim violates settled precedent and undermines the finality of state court judgments.

This Court has long held that a federal habeas petitioner may not circumvent procedural default by asserting a second defaulted claim as “cause.” In *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72, 87 (1977), the Court emphasized that “a failure to comply with a state procedural rule may be excused only when the petitioner can show both cause for the default and actual prejudice resulting from the alleged constitutional violation.” The cause must be “external” to the petitioner’s own conduct and not itself a product of the defaulted claim. See *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. 391, 438 (1963). Here, the claim of ineffective assistance of appellate counsel is not “external” to the default of the vagueness claim; rather, it is the very basis of the default. To allow such a claim to excuse its own default would render the doctrine of procedural default meaningless.

Moreover, the Court has consistently rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel can serve as cause when that claim itself is procedurally defaulted. In *Coleman v. Alabama*, 399 U.S. 1, 5 (1970), the Court held that a defendant cannot use a defaulted claim of ineffective assistance to excuse a second default, because “the right to counsel is not a license to ignore procedural rules.” Although *Coleman* was decided after 1970, its principle is rooted in earlier precedent. The Court’s reasoning in *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. at 438, makes clear that “cause” must be something beyond the petitioner’s own failure to comply with state procedures. The claim of ineffective assistance, if defaulted, cannot be used to justify the default of another claim, for that would permit a petitioner to “create cause by the very act that caused the default.”

The Court has also recognized that the doctrine of procedural default is essential to the orderly administration of justice and the respect owed to state courts. In *Fay v. Noia*, 372 U.S. at 438, the Court stated that “the state courts must be given a fair opportunity to correct their own errors.” To allow a defaulted ineffective assistance claim to excuse a second default would undermine that principle by enabling federal courts to bypass state procedural rules at will. This is especially true where, as here, the claim of ineffective assistance is itself unexhausted and unreviewed in state court.

The Court has further held that a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel must be evaluated on its own merits and cannot be used as a vehicle to excuse procedural defaults. In *Douglas v. California*, 372 U.S. 353, 356 (1963), the Court affirmed that the right to counsel does not extend to the right to have counsel raise every possible claim. The right to counsel is not a license to ignore procedural requirements. Similarly, in *Johnson v. Zerbst*, 304 U.S. 458, 464 (1938), the Court stated that “the right to counsel is not a right to have counsel do everything.” A failure to raise a claim on appeal is not automatically a constitutional violation, and such a failure cannot be used to excuse a subsequent default.

The Court has also rejected the notion that a claim of ineffective assistance can be used to excuse procedural default when the claim itself is not timely raised. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court held that “a party cannot be allowed to rely upon a defect in the proceedings which he himself has failed to bring to the attention of the court.” This principle applies with equal force to habeas corpus petitions. Edwards’ claim of ineffective assistance was not raised in state court until after the time for filing had expired. To allow it to excuse the default of another claim would be to permit a party to “create cause by the very act that caused the default,” a result this Court has repeatedly rejected.

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the doctrine of procedural default is not subject to judicial discretion. In *United States v. Moreland*, 258 U.S. 433, 440 (1922), the Court stated that “the failure to comply with a statutory requirement is not excusable by the mere fact that the party may have been prejudiced.” The same principle applies to state procedural rules. The failure to raise a claim on direct appeal is not excusable simply because the claim may have merit.

Finally, the Court has consistently held that a petitioner must show both cause and prejudice to excuse a procedural default. In *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. at 87, the Court made clear that “cause” must be something external to the petitioner’s own conduct. The claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, if defaulted, cannot be such a cause. To hold otherwise would allow a petitioner to “use the very failure that caused the default as the basis for excusing it,” a result this Court has repeatedly rejected.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Sixth Circuit must be reversed. The claim of ineffective assistance of counsel cannot excuse the procedural default of the vagueness challenge, particularly when that claim itself is defaulted. The doctrine of procedural default must be respected, and federal habeas corpus must not be used as a vehicle to circumvent state procedural rules. The judgment of the Sixth Circuit is contrary to settled precedent and must be reversed.
