The question presented is whether an employer's conduct must be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII of the 1964 Civil Rights Act. The plain language of the statute, 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1), requires only that the employer engage in a discriminatory practice "with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual." 

The legislative history of the Civil Rights Act of 1991, which introduced punitive damages to Title VII, indicates that Congress intended to adopt the standard for punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1981. Under § 1981, courts have long held that punitive damages are available when a defendant acts with a culpable state of mind. 

In the context of tort law, courts have long recognized that punitive damages serve to deter and punish egregious conduct. As the Supreme Court noted in Milwaukee & St. P. Ry. Co. v. Arms, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875), "if the defendant did the act with a bad motive, or so recklessly as to imply a disregard of social obligations, ... the jury may give exemplary damages." The Court further explained in Philadelphia, W. & B.R. Co. v. Quigley, 62 U.S. 202 (1858), that malice is a key factor in determining the availability of punitive damages.

The Restatement of Torts, which reflects the common law, provides guidance on the standard for punitive damages. This standard is consistent with the language of § 1981a(b)(1) and the principles established in cases such as Barry v. Edmunds, 116 U.S. 550 (1886), which discusses the role of juries in awarding damages, and Scott v. Donald, 166 U.S. 193 (1897).

In Krodel v. Young, 748 F.2d 701 (D.C.Cir.1984), the Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit held that an employer's preselection of a job candidate, in violation of its own procedures, is relevant to the question of discriminatory intent. The court stated that such conduct "may be considered by the trier of fact as evidence of discriminatory intent." Krodel, 748 F.2d at 709.

The ADA's conduct, including preselecting Spangler for the promotion and ignoring Kolstad's efforts to discuss the position, demonstrates a level of malice or reckless indifference that warrants consideration of punitive damages. The District Court should have allowed the jury to consider this claim, as the evidence presented at trial was sufficient to support a finding of punitive damages under the standard established by Congress in § 1981a(b)(1).

In conclusion, the plain language of § 1981a(b)(1), combined with the legislative history and relevant case law, including Rood v. Wallace, 109 F. 924 (8th Cir. 1901), supports the conclusion that an employer's conduct does not need to be "egregious" or "outrageous," independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings on Kolstad's punitive damages claim.
