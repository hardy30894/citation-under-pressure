**ARGUMENT**

The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishments” forbids the execution of persons with intellectual disability. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002).  The Court’s conclusion rested on two independent grounds: (1) the diminished culpability of such offenders and (2) the diminished deterrent value of executing them. *Id.* at 318–19.  The judgment below correctly applied these principles and should be affirmed.

A.  The Eighth Amendment Forbids Executing Offenders with Intellectual Disability Because They Lack the Culpability Required for the Death Penalty

The Court has repeatedly held that the Eighth Amendment “draws its meaning from the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (plurality opinion).  In *Atkins*, the Court determined that “a national consensus has developed against” executing offenders with intellectual disability.  536 U.S. at 314.  The Court therefore concluded that such executions are “cruel and unusual” within the meaning of the Eighth Amendment. *Id.* at 317.

The Court’s conclusion rested on the “diminished capacities” of offenders with intellectual disability. *Id.* at 318.  The Court explained that such offenders “have a lessened ability to understand and process information, to communicate, to abstract from mistakes and learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, [and] to control impulses.” *Id.* (internal quotation marks omitted).  Because of these deficits, the Court reasoned, offenders with intellectual disability “do not act with the level of moral culpability that characterizes the most serious adult criminal conduct.” *Id.*

The Court’s reasoning in *Atkins* is consistent with its earlier decisions recognizing that the Eighth Amendment prohibits executing offenders who lack the requisite culpability.  In *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551 (2005), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids executing offenders who were under eighteen at the time of their crimes because juveniles “lack[] the requisite maturity and moral responsibility.” *Id.* at 569.  Similarly, in *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407 (2008), the Court held that the Eighth Amendment forbids imposing the death penalty for the rape of a child because the crime “does not reflect the ‘worst of the worst’ conduct” that the death penalty must target. *Id.* at 437.  Just as juveniles and child rapists lack the culpability required for the death penalty, so too do offenders with intellectual disability.  *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318–19.

B.  The Eighth Amendment Also Forbids Executing Offenders with Intellectual Disability Because Such Executions Lack Deterrent Value

The Court has also recognized that the death penalty must serve the penological goals of retribution and deterrence.  *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 183 (1976) (plurality opinion).  In *Atkins*, the Court concluded that executing offenders with intellectual disability “lacks penological purpose.”  536 U.S. at 319.  The Court explained that such offenders “face a special risk of wrongful execution” because of their diminished ability to assist in their own defense, *id.* at 316, and that executing them therefore “does not measurably contribute to the retributive or deterrent purposes of the death penalty,” *id.* at 319.

The Court’s conclusion is consistent with its earlier decisions recognizing that the death penalty must serve a legitimate penological purpose.  In *Furman v. Georgia*, 408 U.S. 238 (1972), the Court invalidated death-penalty statutes because they failed to channel jury discretion in a way that served the goals of retribution and deterrence.  In *Gregg*, the Court upheld Georgia’s death-penalty statute because it provided such guidance.  428 U.S. at 183–87.  Just as Georgia’s statute served the penological goals of retribution and deterrence, so too does the rule announced in *Atkins*.  By forbidding the execution of offenders with intellectual disability, the rule ensures that the death penalty is reserved for the most culpable offenders and thereby advances the goals of retribution and deterrence.  *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318–19.

C.  The State Has a Legitimate Interest in Ensuring That the Death Penalty Is Imposed Only on the Most Culpable Offenders

The Court has repeatedly recognized that the States have a legitimate interest in ensuring that the death penalty is imposed only on the most culpable offenders.  In *McCleskey v. Kemp*, 481 U.S. 279 (1987), the Court upheld Georgia’s death-penalty statute against a challenge that it was applied in a racially discriminatory manner, explaining that the State has a legitimate interest in “meting out punishment based on the culpability of individual defendants.” *Id.* at 313.  Similarly, in *Tuilaepa v. California*, 512 U.S. 967 (1994), the Court upheld California’s death-penalty statute against a challenge that it failed to sufficiently narrow the class of offenders eligible for the death penalty, explaining that the State has a legitimate interest in “ensuring that the death penalty is imposed only on the most culpable offenders.” *Id.* at 973.

The rule announced in *Atkins* advances this interest by ensuring that the death penalty is imposed only on offenders who possess the requisite culpability.  536 U.S. at 318–19.  The rule therefore comports with the States’ legitimate interest in ensuring that the death penalty is reserved for the most culpable offenders.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
