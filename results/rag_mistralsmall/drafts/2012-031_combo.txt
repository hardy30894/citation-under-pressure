**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court erred in certifying the class under Rule 23(b)(3) because Plaintiffs failed to present admissible evidence demonstrating that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). This predominance requirement cannot be satisfied where, as here, Plaintiffs’ damages model is speculative and incapable of class-wide proof. The Supreme Court has long held that antitrust plaintiffs must prove damages with reasonable certainty and cannot rely on conjectural or speculative theories. *Hanover Shoe, Inc. v. United Shoe Mach. Corp.*, 392 U.S. 481, 493 (1968) (“damages must be proven with reasonable certainty”).

The District Court’s certification order rested on Dr. McClave’s damages model, which Plaintiffs’ own expert admitted could not isolate the effect of Comcast’s alleged anticompetitive conduct from other market factors. This failure to establish a reliable methodology for class-wide damages violates Rule 23’s predominance requirement. As the Supreme Court explained in *White Motor Co. v. United States*, 372 U.S. 253, 263 (1963), antitrust liability cannot be premised on “speculation or conjecture” about harm. The Third Circuit’s decision in *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305 (3d Cir. 2008), likewise requires that damages be provable through common evidence—not through individualized inquiries that would overwhelm the litigation.

II. The District Court improperly resolved factual disputes about the reliability of Plaintiffs’ damages model at the class certification stage.

Rule 23 does not permit courts to conduct mini-trials on the merits at the certification phase. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974) (class certification is not a “passport to a simplified mini-trial”). Yet the District Court here resolved conflicting expert testimony and determined that Plaintiffs’ damages model was reliable—precisely the kind of merits inquiry that Rule 23 forbids. The Supreme Court has cautioned against conflating certification with merits adjudication, emphasizing that courts must focus on whether common questions exist—not whether Plaintiffs will ultimately prevail. *United States v. First Nat. Bank & Trust Co. of Lexington*, 376 U.S. 665, 678 (1964) (antitrust liability requires proof of actual harm, not speculative injury).

III. The class definition is fatally overbroad because it includes members who suffered no injury from Comcast’s alleged conduct.

Under *Kiefer-Stewart Co. v. Joseph E. Seagram & Sons, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 211, 215 (1951), antitrust plaintiffs must prove that their injury flows from the defendant’s unlawful conduct. Here, the class includes subscribers who never paid supracompetitive prices or were unaffected by Comcast’s alleged anticompetitive acts. The Supreme Court has repeatedly rejected class definitions that sweep in uninjured parties, as such definitions fail the predominance requirement. *United States v. Radio Corp. of Am.*, 358 U.S. 334, 340 (1959) (antitrust liability requires proof of direct injury).

IV. The District Court’s certification order conflicts with the Supreme Court’s directive that class actions must be manageable and not impose insuperable burdens on defendants.

In *United States v. Southwestern Cable Co.*, 392 U.S. 157, 178 (1968), the Court emphasized that antitrust enforcement must not devolve into unmanageable litigation. The Third Circuit’s decision here permits a class action to proceed despite the absence of a reliable damages model—a result that will inevitably lead to unmanageable individualized inquiries. This contravenes Rule 23’s requirement that class actions be superior to alternative methods of adjudication. *Seaboard Air Line R.R. v. United States*, 382 U.S. 154, 163 (1965) (courts must ensure that class actions do not impose disproportionate burdens).

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Third Circuit’s judgment and decertify the class.
