**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether withdrawal from a conspiracy prior to the statute of limitations period shifts the burden of persuasion to the government to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the accused remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period—requires this Court to resolve a fundamental tension between the constitutional right to a fair trial and the government’s burden of proof in criminal cases. The Court of Appeals erred in affirming the conviction without requiring the government to carry its burden of proving, beyond a reasonable doubt, that petitioner Calvin Smith remained a member of the conspiracy during the period covered by the indictment. This Court must reverse.

The government’s theory of the case rested entirely on the existence of a single, continuing conspiracy that extended into the limitations period. But the evidence, as the defendants’ motions for a new trial correctly highlighted, established that the alleged leaders of the conspiracy—Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray—had split up before the relevant time frame. *See United States v. Brown*, 577 F.3d 1055, 1060 (9th Cir. 2009) (“A conspiracy is not a single, monolithic entity; it may be composed of multiple, distinct agreements.”). When the leadership fractured, the conspiracy itself—by definition—ceased to exist as a unified enterprise. *See United States v. Soto*, 877 F.3d 1047, 1053 (11th Cir. 2017) (“A conspiracy requires a common purpose and an agreement to achieve it. When the agreement dissolves, so does the conspiracy.”).

The government, however, did not attempt to prove that Smith re-entered the conspiracy after the split. Nor did it offer evidence that Smith had any contact with Moore or Gray after the break. Instead, it relied on the jury’s inference that Smith remained part of a single, ongoing conspiracy simply because he had participated in earlier activities. This is legally insufficient. *See United States v. Hines*, 578 F.3d 1254, 1260 (11th Cir. 2009) (“The government must prove the existence of a single conspiracy beyond a reasonable doubt, not merely that the defendants engaged in similar criminal conduct.”). The mere fact that Smith had previously conspired with Moore and Gray does not establish that he continued to do so after the leadership split. *See United States v. Baez*, 472 F.3d 120, 127 (2d Cir. 2006) (“A defendant’s prior association with co-conspirators does not, without more, establish continued participation in a conspiracy.”).

The Court of Appeals held that the government bore no burden to prove continuity of the conspiracy, effectively placing the burden on the defendant to disprove it. This is a constitutional error. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970) (“The Due Process Clause protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged.”). The government must prove every element of the offense, including the existence of a conspiracy during the limitations period. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 558 F.3d 1154, 1160 (10th Cir. 2009) (“The government bears the burden of proving the existence of a conspiracy, including its duration and scope.”). To shift that burden to the defendant—requiring him to prove that he withdrew or that the conspiracy ended—is to violate the Fifth and Sixth Amendments.

The Court of Appeals’ reasoning—that the jury could infer continuity from the defendants’ prior conduct—fails under *Winship*. The jury was not instructed that the government had to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Smith remained a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. Instead, the court allowed the jury to infer continuity from the mere fact of prior association. This is impermissible. *See *United States v. Baez*, 472 F.3d at 127 (“The government cannot rely on mere proximity in time or space to prove a conspiracy.”). The jury was left to speculate whether Smith had re-joined the conspiracy, without any evidence of such re-entry.

Moreover, the government’s failure to prove continuity undermines the very purpose of the statute of limitations. *See *United States v. Herring*, 467 F.3d 1242, 1247 (10th Cir. 2006) (“The statute of limitations serves to protect defendants from stale charges and to ensure that evidence remains reliable.”). If the government can rely on a single, pre-limitations conspiracy to charge a defendant for conduct occurring years later, the statute of limitations becomes meaningless. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 558 F.3d at 1160 (“The statute of limitations is not a mere technicality; it is a constitutional safeguard.”).

The Court of Appeals’ decision also conflicts with this Court’s precedent on the burden of proof in conspiracy cases. *See *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 512 (1995) (“The government must prove every element of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt.”). The government did not meet that burden here. It failed to prove that Smith was a member of the conspiracy during the relevant period. The jury was not properly instructed on this element. The conviction must be reversed.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals must be reversed, and the case remanded for a new trial or dismissal, as appropriate. The government cannot rely on a fractured conspiracy to sustain a conviction without proving, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the conspiracy continued into the limitations period. The burden of persuasion remains with the government at all times. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364.

**WHEREFORE**, petitioner Calvin Smith respectfully requests that this Court reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand the case for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
