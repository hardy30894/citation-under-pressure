**ARGUMENT**

I. The District Court’s Certification Order Violates Rule 23(b)(3) Because Plaintiffs Failed to Present Admissible Evidence That Damages Can Be Measured on a Class-Wide Basis.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires that “questions of law or fact common to class members predominate over any questions affecting only individual members.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). To satisfy this predominance requirement, a plaintiff must show that “damages are capable of measurement on a class-wide basis.” *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litig.*, 552 F.3d 305, 311 (3d Cir. 2008) (citing *Castano v. Am. Tobacco Co.*, 84 F.3d 734, 744 n.20 (5th Cir. 1996)). The Third Circuit has emphasized that “[t]he plaintiff must be prepared to prove that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis through a common methodology.” *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 311 (quoting *In re Cmty. Bank of N. Virginia*, 418 F.3d 277, 290 (3d Cir. 2005)). This is not a mere pleading requirement; it is a substantive evidentiary showing that must be supported by admissible evidence. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. 338, 350 (2011) (“Factual determinations in a Rule 23(b)(3) class action must be made on the basis of admissible evidence.”).

Here, the District Court erred by certifying the class despite the absence of admissible evidence demonstrating a class-wide damages methodology. The only damages model proffered by Plaintiffs’ expert, Dr. James McClave, was rejected by the Court as unreliable under *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579 (1993). The Court acknowledged that Dr. McClave’s model “suffers from significant flaws” and “does not provide a reliable basis to calculate damages on a class-wide basis.” *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 264 F.R.D. 150, 198 (E.D. Pa. 2010). Yet, the Court inexplicably concluded that Plaintiffs had met their burden under Rule 23(b)(3) because they had narrowed their damages theory to a single theory of liability—antitrust impact from anticompetitive clustering. *Id.* at 199. This reasoning conflates liability with damages and ignores the requirement that damages must be measurable on a class-wide basis. *See Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 34 (2013) (“The District Court certified the class without resolving whether the methodology [Plaintiffs] intended to use to prove damages could result in common questions predominating.”).

The Supreme Court has made clear that Rule 23(b)(3) cannot be satisfied where damages are not measurable on a class-wide basis. In *Dukes*, the Court rejected a class certification where the plaintiffs’ statistical model for backpay damages was “entirely speculative.” 564 U.S. at 351. Similarly, in *Comcast*, the Court held that a class could not be certified under Rule 23(b)(3) where the plaintiffs’ damages model failed to show that damages could be measured on a class-wide basis. 569 U.S. at 34. The Court explained that “[q]uestions of individual damage calculations will inevitably overwhelm questions common to the class” where the damages model is flawed. *Id.* at 35.

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* reinforces this principle. There, the court held that a damages model must be “based on a common methodology that measures only those damages attributable to the theory of liability.” 552 F.3d at 312. The court emphasized that “[i]f the model does not even attempt to do that, it cannot possibly establish predominance.” *Id.* Here, Dr. McClave’s model did not attempt to measure damages attributable to the single theory of liability adopted by the District Court. Instead, it relied on a flawed regression analysis that the Court found unreliable. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 198. Thus, the District Court’s certification order violates Rule 23(b)(3) because Plaintiffs failed to present admissible evidence that damages can be measured on a class-wide basis.

II. The District Court’s Certification Order Violates Rule 23(b)(3) Because It Relied on an Impermissible Merits Inquiry.

Rule 23(b)(3) requires a rigorous analysis of whether common questions predominate, but this analysis must not devolve into a merits inquiry. *Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 350-51. The Supreme Court has cautioned that “Rule 23 does not set forth a mere pleading standard” but also “does not allow courts to conduct a preliminary inquiry into the merits of a suit in order to determine whether it may proceed as a class action.” *Id.* at 350 (quoting *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177 (1974)).

Here, the District Court impermissibly resolved merits disputes in order to certify the class. The Court accepted Plaintiffs’ expert testimony on antitrust impact and damages methodology despite acknowledging that the testimony was flawed. *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 198. The Court also resolved factual disputes about the relevant geographic market and the existence of antitrust impact, even though these issues are quintessentially merits-based. *See, e.g., Brown Shoe Co. v. United States*, 370 U.S. 294, 336 (1962) (geographic market definition is a merits issue in antitrust cases).

The Third Circuit’s decision in *Hydrogen Peroxide* underscores the impropriety of this approach. There, the court held that a district court may not “conduct a mini-trial on the merits” in order to determine whether common questions predominate. 552 F.3d at 318. The court explained that “[t]he predominance inquiry is not a pass/fail test for the merits of the case” but rather “a test for whether the common issues in the case are sufficiently predominant over the individual issues to warrant class treatment.” *Id.* Here, the District Court’s certification order failed to heed this warning. By resolving merits disputes and accepting flawed expert testimony, the Court impermissibly transformed the certification decision into a merits inquiry.

III. The District Court’s Certification Order Violates Rule 23(b)(3) Because It Failed to Conduct a Rigorous Analysis of the Evidence.

Rule 23 requires a “rigorous analysis” of whether the class certification requirements are satisfied. *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. This analysis must include a “thorough examination of the factual and legal allegations” and may require “a preliminary inquiry into the merits” where necessary to determine whether a Rule 23 requirement is met. *Id.* at 317 (quoting *Newton v. Merrill Lynch, Pierce, Fenner & Smith, Inc.*, 259 F.3d 154, 166 (3d Cir. 2001)).

Here, the District Court’s “rigorous analysis” was woefully inadequate. The Court acknowledged that Dr. McClave’s damages model was unreliable but nonetheless concluded that Plaintiffs had met their burden under Rule 23(b)(3). *Behrend*, 264 F.R.D. at 198. This conclusion is inconsistent with the Third Circuit’s admonition that a district court must “resolve disputes relevant to class certification” and cannot “accept a methodology that is fatally flawed.” *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 318. The Court also failed to conduct a meaningful analysis of whether the proposed class satisfied the predominance requirement. Instead, it accepted Plaintiffs’ expert testimony on antitrust impact and damages methodology without critically evaluating the evidence.

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Comcast* confirms the inadequacy of the District Court’s analysis. There, the Court held that a district court must conduct a “rigorous analysis” of whether damages can be measured on a class-wide basis. 569 U.S. at 34. The Court explained that “[t]he Court of Appeals concluded that the District Court had not abused its discretion in certifying the class, but it did not decide whether the methodology [Plaintiffs] intended to use to prove damages could result in common questions predominating.” *Id.* Here, the District Court’s certification order suffers from the same flaw. It failed to conduct a rigorous analysis of whether Plaintiffs’ damages model could result in common questions predominating.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals and remand with instructions to vacate the class certification order.
