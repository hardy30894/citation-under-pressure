Florida’s death-sentencing scheme, as applied here, does not violate the Sixth Amendment or the Eighth Amendment. The controlling premise of petitioner’s claim is Ring v. Arizona, 536 U.S. 584 (2002), but Ring cannot be used to invalidate a capital sentencing system absent a pre-1970 authority recognizing the claimed jury-trial or cruel-and-unusual constraint. No such authority exists.

The Sixth Amendment jury-trial guarantee has never been held to require jury sentencing in capital cases. To the contrary, the Court long recognized that sentencing has traditionally been the province of the trial judge. In Andres v. United States, 333 U.S. 740 (1948), the Court distinguished the determination of guilt from the imposition of sentence and treated sentencing as a separate function. Likewise, Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949), upheld broad judicial discretion at sentencing and explained that modern penal practice permits the sentencing judge to consider information outside the jury’s verdict. Those decisions foreclose any claim that the Constitution itself requires a jury to make every fact relevant to the penalty.

Nor does the Constitution require a unanimous jury recommendation of death. The Court has repeatedly described the jury as a historic institution for finding guilt, not as a constitutionally mandated sentencing body whose verdict must be unanimous as to punishment. In Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900), the Court rejected the proposition that the Sixth Amendment, as applied to the States through the Fourteenth, constitutionalized every common-law feature of the jury. And in Patton v. United States, 281 U.S. 276 (1930), the Court recognized that the essential function of the jury trial is protection of the accused at the guilt stage, while procedural details of jury practice are not immutable constitutional commands. Nothing in those pre-1970 cases supports a claim that a capital sentencing recommendation must be unanimous.

Petitioner’s mental-retardation argument fares no better. The Eighth Amendment, as understood before 1970, prohibited punishments that were barbarous, degrading, or grossly disproportionate. In Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), the Court condemned a punishment out of proportion to the offense, but it did not create a categorical exemption from capital punishment based on mental capacity. And in Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958), the Court spoke of “the evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society,” but that case involved denationalization, not capital sentencing, and it did not hold that the Eighth Amendment forbids execution of defendants with intellectual disability. Petitioner therefore asks the Court to announce a new categorical rule untethered to any pre-1970 authority.

The Florida scheme also satisfies the historical requirements of due process. In Snyder v. Massachusetts, 291 U.S. 97 (1934), the Court held that procedure is constitutionally sufficient unless it offends those fundamental conceptions of justice that lie at the base of our civil and political institutions. Florida afforded petitioner a jury trial, a separate penalty phase, presentation of mitigation, and judicial review. That process is fully consistent with the pre-1970 cases defining the constitutional minimum.

Finally, this Court should resist converting Ring into a retroactive constitutional overhaul of capital sentencing. Before 1970, the Court had never held that the jury must find aggravating circumstances, determine mental retardation, or unanimously recommend death. The settled authorities instead recognize state discretion in allocating sentencing functions between judge and jury. See Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949); Patton v. United States, 281 U.S. 276 (1930); Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900). Under those precedents, Florida’s scheme does not offend the Sixth Amendment. And because the Eighth Amendment, as construed before 1970, did not categorically exempt intellectually disabled offenders from capital punishment, petitioner’s cruel-and-unusual-punishment claim likewise fails. See Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910); Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958).

The judgment should be affirmed.
