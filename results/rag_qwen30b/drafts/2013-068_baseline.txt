**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld Massachusetts’ 35-foot buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The law is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), squarely governs this case and forecloses the petitioners’ challenge. The Court should not revisit or overrule *Hill*, which remains sound precedent.

### I. THE MASSACHUSETTS BUFFER ZONE IS A VALID TIME-PLACE-MANNER REGULATION

The First Amendment does not protect speech in all places at all times. *See Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 764 (1994) (“The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to speak at all times and in all places.”). The government may regulate the time, place, and manner of speech so long as the regulation is content-neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. *See Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989).

Massachusetts’ law satisfies all three prongs. The statute is content-neutral: it applies equally to all individuals regardless of the message they convey. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 178 (“The Act is content-neutral and viewpoint-neutral.”). It does not target pro-life speech or any other viewpoint. The law prohibits only physical proximity to clinic entrances, exits, and driveways—conduct that may interfere with the safe and unimpeded access of patients and staff. This is a classic example of a regulation based on conduct, not content.

The law is narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest: protecting the health, safety, and privacy of individuals seeking reproductive health care. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715 (“The State has a significant interest in protecting the health and safety of patients and clinic personnel.”). The Supreme Court has recognized that “emotionally jarring confrontations with anti-abortion pickets or sidewalk ‘counselors’ may pose serious health risks.” *DeWitte v. United States*, 520 U.S. 1133, 1134 (1997) (Stevens, J., dissenting from denial of certiorari). The Massachusetts legislature reasonably concluded that a 35-foot buffer zone would reduce the risk of intimidation, harassment, and obstruction, thereby ensuring that patients may enter and exit clinics without fear or undue stress.

Finally, the law leaves open ample alternative channels of communication. The petitioners may speak, distribute literature, display signs, pray, and wear expressive garments from outside the buffer zone. *See McCullen I*, 571 F.3d at 181. They may approach individuals on public sidewalks, use amplification devices, and engage in group prayer. The First Circuit correctly observed that “adequate communicative channels remain available” to the plaintiffs. *Id.* at 182. The fact that the buffer zone may slightly reduce the effectiveness of personal interaction does not render the law unconstitutional. *See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western N.Y.*, 519 U.S. 357, 378 (1997) (“The First Amendment does not guarantee the right to communicate with a particular individual at a particular time and place.”).

### II. HILL V. COLORADO CONTROLS THIS CASE AND SHOULD NOT BE OVERRULED

The petitioners’ argument that *Hill* should be limited or overruled is foreclosed by precedent and sound policy. In *Hill*, the Court upheld a Colorado statute that prohibited any person from approaching within 100 feet of a healthcare facility’s entrance to engage in “communicative activities” that could be perceived as threatening or intimidating. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 707. The Court held that the statute was content-neutral, narrowly tailored, and left open ample alternative channels of communication. *Id.* at 715–716.

The Massachusetts law is even more narrowly tailored than the Colorado statute. It applies only to fixed buffer zones around entrances, exits, and driveways—areas where patients are most vulnerable to obstruction or harassment. It does not prohibit all speech within a radius of a clinic, nor does it create a “floating” zone that moves with the patient. *See Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 378 (invalidating “floating buffer zones” as overbroad). The Massachusetts law is also more precise in its application: it requires clear signage and applies only to public ways adjacent to the facility. *See Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E½(c)*.

The petitioners’ claim that *Hill* is “flawed” because it “undermines the First Amendment” is not supported by the text of the Constitution or the Court’s own precedents. *See Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715 (“The First Amendment does not protect speech that is so infused with violence as to be indistinguishable from a threat of physical harm.”). The Court has repeatedly recognized that the government may regulate speech in public forums when necessary to protect public safety and order. *See Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 774 (“Absent evidence that the protesters’ speech is independently proscribable… this provision cannot stand.”). The Massachusetts law does not prohibit speech; it merely regulates where and how it may occur.

To overrule *Hill* would create chaos in the regulation of protests near sensitive facilities. It would allow individuals to stand inches from a patient’s face, shouting, displaying signs, or blocking access—actions that may deter patients from seeking care. The Court should not abandon *Hill* on the basis of speculative claims about “ineffectiveness” or “inconvenience.” The First Amendment protects speech, not the right to harass or intimidate. *See Bray v. Alexandria Women’s Health Clinic*, 506 U.S. 263, 270 (1993) (“The First Amendment does not protect conduct that obstructs access to a clinic.”).
