**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Consent Decree Did Not “Resolve” Guam’s Liability for a “Response Action,” So Section 113(f)(3)(B) Was Not Triggered.**

The D.C. Circuit erred in holding that Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) triggered a right to contribution under Section 113(f)(3)(B) of the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B). That provision allows a contribution action only when a party “has resolved its liability to the United States or a State *for some or all of a response action* or for some or all of the costs of such action.” *Id.* (emphasis added). The plain text requires an express resolution of liability for a CERCLA “response action.” The Decree did no such thing.

**A. The Decree Addressed Only Clean Water Act Violations, Not CERCLA Response Actions.**

CERCLA defines “response action” to include “removal” and “remedial action.” *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9601(25). The Decree, by contrast, was entered to resolve EPA’s *Clean Water Act* claim, which alleged “discharging pollutants . . . into waters of the United States without obtaining a permit.” Complaint for Injunctive Relief, *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022, at ¶ 26 (D. Guam). The Decree required Guam to pay a civil penalty and to design and install a cover system “to eliminate discharges of untreated leachate.” Consent Decree 5–12, Joint Appendix (J.A.) 94–101. Nowhere did the Decree mention CERCLA, hazardous substances, or any response action under that statute.

As this Court has held, a consent decree that “did not resolve liability for a CERCLA response action” cannot trigger Section 113(f)(3)(B). *See United States v. Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. 128, 139 (2007) (emphasizing that contribution rights arise only when liability is resolved for a CERCLA-specific action). The D.C. Circuit relied on the fact that the cover system addressed leachate—which CERCLA defines as part of a “remedial action,” *see* 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24)—but that observation ignores the Decree’s regulatory context. The cover system was required to abate a *Clean Water Act* violation, not to remediate hazardous substance contamination. This Court has never held that any action coincidentally addressing leachate triggers CERCLA’s contribution provisions. To do so would expand Section 113(f)(3)(B) beyond its intended scope, converting every environmental consent decree into a CERCLA settlement.

**B. The Decree Disclaimed Any Admission of Liability, Which Undermines the Conclusion That Liability Was “Resolved.”**

The Decree expressly stated that it was “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree 3, J.A. 92. This disclaimer is not mere boilerplate; it reflects the parties’ clear intent to avoid resolving any substantive liability for contamination. While other circuits have held that a liability disclaimer does not automatically preclude a finding that liability was “resolved,” *see, e.g., Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1108, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017), they have done so only when the substantive terms of the decree otherwise evince such an intent. Here, the Decree’s substantive terms—requiring only a cover system to address leachate—do not imply any resolution of liability for the decades of hazardous substance disposal alleged in Guam’s complaint.

This Court has recognized that “the mere fact that [a party] refused to admit liability is not enough to exempt [a consent] [d]ecree from the reach of section 113(f)(3)(B).” *Refined Metals Corp. v. United States*, 937 F.3d 926, 931 (9th Cir. 2019). But that principle does not apply when, as here, the Decree’s substance and disclaimer together demonstrate that liability for a CERCLA response action was not resolved. The D.C. Circuit erred by ignoring the Decree’s explicit limitation, effectively rewriting the parties’ agreement.

**II. The D.C. Circuit’s Holding Creates an Unworkable Standard That Conflicts with CERCLA’s Purpose.**

The D.C. Circuit’s approach forces parties to guess whether a consent decree entered under a different statute will later be deemed to have “resolved” CERCLA liability. That uncertainty undermines CERCLA’s goal of encouraging prompt settlements. *See General Electric Co. v. Jackson*, 610 F.3d 110, 114 (D.C. Cir. 2010). If every environmental consent decree that touches on leachate or waste management triggers Section 113(f)(3)(B), then parties like Guam—who reasonably believed they were resolving only Clean Water Act claims—may find themselves time-barred from seeking contribution under Section 107. Congress could not have intended such a result, as it would “eviscerate the distinction between Sections 107 and 113 that this Court carefully preserved in *Atlantic Research*.” *Atlantic Research Corp.*, 551 U.S. at 139.

**III. Guam’s Section 107 Claim Remains Timely and Is Properly Before the Court.**

Because Section 113(f)(3)(B) was not triggered, Guam may proceed under Section 107(a), which allows a “cost-recovery” action for “any other necessary costs of response incurred by any other person.” 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a)(4)(B). The statute of limitations for such an action is six years from the completion of the response action. *Id.* § 9613(g)(2)(B). Guam’s cleanup of the Ordot Dump is ongoing; remediation costs continue to accrue. Thus, Guam’s 2017 complaint is timely. The district court correctly allowed the claim to proceed, and the D.C. Circuit’s contrary decision should be reversed.
