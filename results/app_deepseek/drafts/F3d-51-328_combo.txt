**ARGUMENT**

**I. The District Court Erred in Granting a Preliminary Injunction Because the Fair Housing Act Does Not Require a Private Landlord to Provide a Parking Space to a Tenant with a Disability in Derogation of a Neutral, First-Come, First-Served Policy.**

The Fair Housing Amendments Act of 1988 prohibits discrimination in housing on the basis of disability. 42 U.S.C. § 3604(f). However, this statute does not create an affirmative obligation to provide a specific accommodation that would fundamentally alter the nature of a housing provider’s operations or impose an undue hardship. The district court’s order requiring Cadman Towers to bypass its established waiting list for parking spaces constitutes such a fundamental alteration.

This Court has long recognized that the right to control one’s property, including the allocation of limited resources, is a fundamental aspect of ownership. *See, e.g., United States v. Causby*, 328 U.S. 256, 266 (1946) (recognizing the “ancient concept” that property rights include the right to exclude others). A cooperative’s decision to allocate scarce parking spaces on a first-come, first-served basis is a rational, nondiscriminatory policy. *Cf. Railway Express Agency, Inc. v. New York*, 336 U.S. 106, 110 (1949) (upholding a classification that is “reasonable” and not “arbitrary”). The policy applies equally to all residents, regardless of disability. Shapiro’s request for an immediate, reserved space is not a request for equal access; it is a demand for preferential treatment.

**II. The District Court’s Order Impermissibly Imposes an Affirmative Duty to Accommodate That Exceeds the Requirements of the Statute.**

The duty to make “reasonable accommodations” under § 3604(f)(3)(B) does not require a housing provider to create a new benefit or privilege that is not otherwise available to other tenants. *See, e.g., Southeastern Community College v. Davis*, 442 U.S. 397, 410 (1979) (interpreting analogous Rehabilitation Act provision and holding that an accommodation is not reasonable if it imposes “undue financial and administrative burdens”). Here, Cadman Towers has a finite number of parking spaces. Granting Shapiro a space immediately would require displacing another tenant who has waited years for a space, or reducing the number of spaces available to all tenants. This is precisely the type of fundamental alteration that the statute does not require.

The Supreme Court has consistently held that statutes prohibiting discrimination do not require the abandonment of neutral, legitimate policies. *See, e.g., Griggs v. Duke Power Co.*, 401 U.S. 424, 431 (1971) (holding that employment practices that are “neutral on their face” are not per se discriminatory). The waiting list is a neutral policy. Shapiro’s disability does not entitle her to a “right” to a parking space that no other tenant possesses. *Cf. Moose Lodge No. 107 v. Irvis*, 407 U.S. 163, 171 (1972) (holding that a private club’s refusal to serve a guest does not violate the Fourteenth Amendment because the guest has no right to service).

**III. The District Court’s Injunction Violates the Cooperative’s Property Rights and the Rights of Other Tenants.**

The district court’s order effectively transfers a property interest—a parking space—from the cooperative to Shapiro without compensation. This is a taking of property without due process of law. *See, e.g., Penn Central Transp. Co. v. New York City*, 438 U.S. 104, 124 (1978) (discussing regulatory takings). Moreover, the order disregards the rights of the other 300 tenants at 101 Clark Street who have patiently waited for a parking space. *See, e.g., Shelley v. Kraemer*, 334 U.S. 1, 13 (1948) (recognizing that judicial enforcement of a restrictive covenant implicates state action and must respect the rights of all parties). The cooperative’s board has a fiduciary duty to all shareholders, not just to Shapiro. *See, e.g., Pepper v. Litton*, 308 U.S. 295, 306 (1939) (discussing fiduciary duties of corporate directors).

**IV. The District Court Erred in Finding That Shapiro Would Suffer Irreparable Harm Absent an Injunction.**

Shapiro has managed to live in the building for two years without a parking space, using public transportation and street parking. Her claimed harm—inconvenience and occasional urinary accidents—does not rise to the level of irreparable injury necessary to justify a mandatory preliminary injunction. *See, e.g., Sampson v. Murray*, 415 U.S. 61, 90 (1974) (holding that temporary inconvenience does not constitute irreparable harm). The district court’s finding of irreparable harm is clearly erroneous.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the order of the district court granting a preliminary injunction should be reversed.
