**Argument**

The Eighth Amendment does not permit the execution of mentally retarded offenders. In *Penry v. Lynaugh*, 492 U.S. 302 (1989), the Court held that there was then insufficient evidence of a national consensus to bar such executions categorically, but it expressly left open the possibility that “evolving standards of decency” might later compel a different result. Id. at 334. That time has now arrived. The Eighth Amendment’s prohibition on “cruel and unusual punishments” is not static; it draws its meaning from the “evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society.” *Trop v. Dulles*, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958). Where objective indicia of national consensus demonstrate that a punishment has become unacceptable, the Court gives those standards constitutional force. *Gregg v. Georgia*, 428 U.S. 153, 173 (1976); *Coker v. Georgia*, 433 U.S. 584, 592 (1977).

Those indicia now point overwhelmingly in one direction. Since *Penry*, a clear legislative trend has emerged against executing persons with mental retardation. State enactments, death-penalty statutes, and executive practices have increasingly rejected the penalty in this class of cases. The Court has relied on such developments in recognizing categorical Eighth Amendment limits before. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304 (2002), the Court held that the execution of mentally retarded offenders violates the Eighth Amendment because a national consensus had crystallized and because the penological justifications for the death penalty do not apply with sufficient force to this class of offenders. Id. at 321-24.

The punishment also fails the Court’s independent judgment. The death penalty is reserved for those whose culpability is so grave that society’s “conscience” permits the severest sanction. *Gregg*, 428 U.S. at 183. But the characteristics of mental retardation diminish personal culpability in ways that directly undermine retribution and deterrence. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 318-21. As *Penry* recognized, persons with mental retardation “have diminished capacities to understand and process information, to communicate, to abstract from mistakes and learn from experience, to engage in logical reasoning, to control impulses, and to understand the reactions of others.” Id. at 318. Those traits do not immunize such offenders from punishment, but they do mean death is a disproportionate response under the Eighth Amendment. See *Ford v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 399, 409-10 (1986) (Eighth Amendment bars punishments inconsistent with evolving standards of decency and basic moral limits); *Roper v. Simmons*, 543 U.S. 551, 571-75 (2005) (categorically barring death for juveniles because diminished culpability reduces the force of retribution and deterrence).

Virginia’s contrary position also cannot be sustained by the fact that a mentally retarded defendant may be eligible for individualized mitigation at sentencing. The Court rejected that very argument in *Atkins*, explaining that the constitutional problem is not merely the risk of an erroneous sentence in a particular case, but the categorical mismatch between the death penalty and offenders whose intellectual disability substantially reduces blameworthiness. 536 U.S. at 306, 321. The same logic underlies the Court’s categorical exclusions in *Coker*, 433 U.S. at 592-97, and *Kennedy v. Louisiana*, 554 U.S. 407, 419-38 (2008): where a class of offenders or offenses falls outside the rationale for capital punishment, the Eighth Amendment forbids the death penalty altogether.

Nor is there any administrability problem sufficient to defeat the constitutional rule. Courts regularly make individualized findings in capital cases, including mental competence and mitigation determinations. See *Ford*, 477 U.S. at 426; *Panetti v. Quarterman*, 551 U.S. 930, 957-60 (2007). The relevant inquiry is whether the defendant is mentally retarded as defined by accepted clinical and legal standards, not whether his disability is obvious in every case. *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 317-18. That some borderline cases may arise does not justify executing those plainly within the protected class.

Finally, stare decisis does not require adherence to *Penry*’s earlier holding. *Penry* itself rested on the absence of a national consensus in 1989. 492 U.S. at 334. When objective evidence and contemporary judgment have shifted, this Court does not freeze the Eighth Amendment in time. *Trop*, 356 U.S. at 101; *Atkins*, 536 U.S. at 311-13. The Constitution forbids “cruel and unusual punishments,” not punishments that happen to have been accepted at the moment of ratification or in an earlier era. Because executing mentally retarded persons is inconsistent with contemporary standards of decency and serves neither retribution nor deterrence in a constitutionally sufficient way, the judgment below should be affirmed as to the Eighth Amendment bar.
