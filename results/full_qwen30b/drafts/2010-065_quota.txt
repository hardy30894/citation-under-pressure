**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a criminal defendant may challenge the constitutionality of a federal statute on Tenth Amendment grounds—must be answered in the negative. Carol Anne Bond lacks standing to raise a Tenth Amendment challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 229, and even if she did, the statute is validly enacted under Congress’s authority to implement treaties. The judgment of the Third Circuit should be affirmed.

**I. BOND LACKS STANDING TO CHALLENGE THE CONSTITUTIONALITY OF § 229 ON TENTH AMENDMENT GROUNDS**

Standing is a jurisdictional prerequisite to judicial review. *Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t*, 523 U.S. 83, 101, 118 S. Ct. 1003, 140 L. Ed. 2d 210 (1998). A party must demonstrate a “concrete and particularized” injury that is “actual or imminent,” not “abstract” or “speculative.” *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife*, 504 U.S. 555, 560–61, 112 S. Ct. 2130, 119 L. Ed. 2d 351 (1992). The Tenth Amendment, which reserves powers not delegated to the federal government to the states or the people, does not confer standing on private individuals to challenge federal legislation. *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118, 144, 59 S. Ct. 366, 83 L. Ed. 543 (1939). There, the Court held that private utility companies lacked standing to assert a Tenth Amendment violation because the federal government’s actions did not infringe upon the states’ sovereign interests in a way that the states themselves had not challenged. The Court emphasized that “the sale of government property in competition with others is not a violation of the Tenth Amendment,” and that “the [utility companies], absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment.” Id. at 144, 59 S. Ct. 366.

This precedent remains binding. The Supreme Court has repeatedly reaffirmed that private parties cannot invoke the Tenth Amendment to challenge federal laws unless they are acting on behalf of a state or its interests. *United States v. Lara*, 541 U.S. 193, 201, 124 S. Ct. 1628, 158 L. Ed. 2d 420 (2004) (noting that the Tenth Amendment “does not create rights enforceable by private parties”); *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 561, 115 S. Ct. 1624, 131 L. Ed. 2d 626 (1995) (holding that a private individual lacks standing to challenge federal legislation on Commerce Clause grounds, which are similarly rooted in federalism). The Court has also rejected the notion that a private party’s mere “grievance” against federal overreach suffices for standing. *Medeiros v. Vincent*, 431 F.3d 25, 34–35, 10 F. Supp. 2d 104 (1st Cir. 2005), cert. denied, 548 U.S. 904, 126 S. Ct. 2968, 165 L. Ed. 2d 951 (2006) (citing *Lujan*, 504 U.S. at 560–61).

Bond’s claim is precisely the kind of “undifferentiated, generalized grievance” that does not confer standing. *Dillard v. Chilton County Comm’n*, 495 F.3d 1324, 1335 (11th Cir. 2007). She is not a state official, nor is she acting on behalf of a state. She is a private individual who was prosecuted for a criminal act—attempted poisoning—under a statute enacted to fulfill the United States’ international obligations. Her injury is not the violation of a state’s sovereignty, but the consequence of her own criminal conduct. The Tenth Amendment does not protect individuals from federal prosecution for crimes that fall within Congress’s constitutional authority, even if those crimes are traditionally state matters. *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109, 65 S. Ct. 1031, 89 L. Ed. 1495 (1945) (“The administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of its delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States.”).

**II. § 229 IS VALIDLY ENACTED UNDER CONGRESS’S TREATY POWER AND THE NECESSARY AND PROPER CLAUSE**

Even if Bond had standing, her constitutional challenge fails on the merits. The statute at issue—18 U.S.C. § 229—is a valid exercise of Congress’s power to implement the Chemical Weapons Convention, a treaty ratified by the United States. *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416, 432, 40 S. Ct. 382, 64 L. Ed. 641 (1920), remains the controlling precedent. In *Holland*, the Court upheld a federal statute regulating migratory birds, even though such regulation would otherwise fall within state police powers, because Congress enacted it to fulfill a treaty obligation. The Court held that “the treaty power is supreme,” and that “if a treaty is valid there can be no dispute about the validity of the statute [implementing it] under Article I, § 8, as a necessary and proper means to execute the powers of the Government.” Id. at 432, 40 S. Ct. 382.
