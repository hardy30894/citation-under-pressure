The three-judge court possessed ample authority to direct a reduction in prison population as the means of correcting established Eighth Amendment violations. Federal equity has long authorized decrees that reach the root of unconstitutional conditions when lesser measures prove inadequate. See Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (courts must fashion remedies that “eliminate” the offending state of affairs).

That authority is not diminished because the remedy affects the number of persons confined. In cases presenting systemic constitutional defects, courts have required affirmative steps to restore compliance, even when those steps alter the operation of state institutions. See, e.g., Griffin v. County School Board, 377 U.S. 218, 233-234 (1964); Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 7 (1958). The same principle governs here: once the court found overcrowding to be the “primary cause” of inadequate medical care, an order addressing population levels fell squarely within traditional equitable discretion.

The decree is also consistent with the historic limits on federal injunctive relief. It rests on specific findings of constitutional injury, targets the identified cause, and leaves the State free to choose the means of compliance. Such tailoring accords with the rule that equitable remedies must be “no more burdensome to the defendant than necessary to provide complete relief.” See, e.g., Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 321 U.S. 321, 329 (1944). Because the order satisfies these longstanding requirements, it does not exceed the bounds of judicial power recognized before the enactment of later statutes.

The State’s contrary contention—that any population-reduction order is categorically beyond a federal court’s competence—finds no support in pre-existing equity jurisprudence. To the contrary, when constitutional rights are at stake, courts have not hesitated to issue decrees that restructure state practices. See, e.g., Louisiana v. United States, 380 U.S. 145, 154 (1965). The present order is of that character and therefore remains within the court’s lawful authority.
