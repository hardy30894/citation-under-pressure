The district court's order directing a reduction in prison population falls squarely within the equitable authority of federal courts to remedy ongoing constitutional violations. As this Court has long recognized, once a violation of federal rights is established, "the court of equity has power to afford complete relief." *Osborn v. Bank of the United States*, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 738, 839 (1824). That power includes structural remedies necessary to eliminate the conditions producing the violation. *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954).

The Constitution does not leave state officials free to persist in unconstitutional practices merely because compliance requires difficult administrative measures. *Ex parte Young*, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908). When state institutions inflict cruel and unusual punishment through overcrowding, federal courts may compel the measures required to restore constitutional compliance. *Cooper v. Aaron*, 358 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1958). Such orders are not advisory; they bind the parties and may be enforced through the court's contempt power. *Gompers v. Buck's Stove & Range Co.*, 221 U.S. 418, 450 (1911).

The scope of relief is measured by the nature and persistence of the violation. Where violations are "deeply rooted and long standing," the remedy may extend to altering the size and operation of the institution itself. *Griffin v. County School Board*, 377 U.S. 218, 233-34 (1964). Courts have repeatedly approved orders that restructure state systems to eliminate constitutional defects, even when those orders require substantial expenditures or changes in state policy. *Green v. County School Board*, 391 U.S. 430, 437-38 (1968). The same principle governs here: the population cap is the least intrusive means of ending the proven Eighth Amendment violations.

Nor does the order exceed traditional limits on federal judicial power over state officers. This Court has upheld decrees directing state compliance with federal law even when the decrees require affirmative action by state officials. *Virginia v. West Virginia*, 246 U.S. 565, 604 (1918). The decree here is no different in kind; it simply requires the State to cease maintaining prisons in a condition that violates the Constitution.

Finally, the order rests on the inherent authority of courts to protect constitutional rights against state infringement. *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 163 (1803); *In re Debs*, 158 U.S. 564, 599 (1895). That authority is not diminished because the remedy affects prison administration. The district court therefore acted within its established equitable jurisdiction.
