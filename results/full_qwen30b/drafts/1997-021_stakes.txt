**ARGUMENT**

I. THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS BOARD’S “GOOD-FAITH REASONABLE DOUBT” STANDARD FOR EMPLOYEE POLLS IS IRRATIONAL, CONTRARY TO THE NATIONAL LABOR RELATIONS ACT, AND UNREASONABLE IN PRACTICE.

The National Labor Relations Board’s requirement that an employer possess “objective indications raising a reasonable doubt” about a union’s majority status before conducting a poll of employees is not only inconsistent with the statutory framework of the National Labor Relations Act (NLRA), but also renders the poll—a tool explicitly recognized as a legitimate means of testing union support—functionally useless. The Board’s standard, as applied in this case, transforms what should be a neutral, fact-finding mechanism into a legal trap, discouraging employers from seeking clarity about employee sentiment and thereby undermining the very stability the NLRA seeks to promote.

The NLRA does not mandate that employers must rely on Board-supervised elections to determine union support. Indeed, the Act contemplates that employers may take steps to assess employee sentiment, including through polls, so long as those steps are conducted fairly and without coercion. See *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37–41 (1987) (recognizing the rebuttable presumption of continuing majority status for an incumbent union, but affirming that an employer may challenge that presumption through objective evidence). The Board’s own precedent acknowledges that polling is a “useful and legitimate tool” for employers with “sincere doubts” about a union’s support. *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1063 (1989). Yet the Board’s current standard—requiring the same level of objective evidence to justify a poll as to justify withdrawal of recognition—renders that tool meaningless. As the Board itself concedes, polling is “potentially disruptive and unsettling,” *id.* at 1061, and thus should be permitted only when necessary. But if an employer must already possess sufficient evidence to justify withdrawal of recognition before polling, then the poll serves no purpose other than to confirm what the employer already knows. This is not a rational or workable standard.

The Board’s insistence on a single, rigid evidentiary threshold for both withdrawal of recognition and polling creates a perverse incentive: employers are better off refusing to bargain altogether than to conduct a poll, because the latter requires a higher burden of proof. This is precisely the anomaly the Board sought to avoid, yet it has failed to resolve it. The Board’s own reasoning in *Texas Petrochemicals* reveals the contradiction: it states that RM elections are the preferred method for testing union support, *id.* at 1061, yet applies the same evidentiary standard to polling, which lacks the procedural safeguards of an election. If the Board truly believes elections are superior, then polling should be permitted under a lower standard—so that employers may use polls as a preliminary step before seeking a formal election. But the Board has not done so. Instead, it has created a regime in which the only way to avoid an election is to meet the same high standard required to withdraw recognition, thereby eliminating the utility of polling altogether.

This irrational result is compounded by the Board’s failure to recognize that the presumption of continuing majority status is rebuttable not only by objective evidence of a loss of support, but also by the passage of time and the emergence of a new bargaining unit. In *Fall River Dyeing*, the Supreme Court held that the presumption is rebuttable after one year, *482 U.S. at 41*, and that the presumption is weaker when a successor employer inherits a unit that is a subset of the previously certified unit. Here, the new employer, Allentown Mack, inherited only 32 of the original 45 employees, and the unit had been restructured significantly. The service department was reduced from 32 to 23 mechanics, and the parts department from 11 to 7. The Board’s refusal to acknowledge the diminished factual basis for the presumption in this context is arbitrary and capricious.

Moreover, the Board’s standard is not only irrational but also inconsistent with the decisions of three courts of appeals that have rejected it. The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits have all held that an employer may conduct a poll if it has “substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support,” even if that evidence is insufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. *A.W. Thompson, Inc.*, 651 F.2d 1139, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). These courts recognized that the purpose of a poll is to test the union’s support, not to replace the Board’s election process. They understood that an employer may have a “sincere doubt” about the union’s majority status even if that doubt does not rise to the level of a legal basis for withdrawal. The Board’s refusal to follow this well-reasoned precedent is not only contrary to the law, but also undermines the predictability and fairness of labor relations.

The Board’s decision here is not only legally unsound but also unsupported by substantial evidence.
