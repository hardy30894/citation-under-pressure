Arizona’s rule does not violate due process because the Constitution leaves states wide latitude to define the relevance of mental-condition evidence and to channel such evidence into an insanity defense. The Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant a free-standing right to introduce psychiatric testimony to negate mens rea whenever state law limits that use. See Leland v. Oregon, 343 U.S. 790, 798-99 (1952); Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977).

In Leland, this Court upheld Oregon’s requirement that a defendant prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt and rejected the claim that due process forbids such allocation of the burden. 343 U.S. at 798-99. Leland is especially important because the defendant there, like Clark here, sought constitutional protection for a mental-state defense. The Court made clear that the Constitution does not compel one particular insanity regime. Id. That principle forecloses Clark’s attempt to constitutionalize a secondary, non-insanity use of expert mental-condition evidence.

Patterson confirms the point. There, the Court sustained New York’s decision to place the burden of proving extreme emotional disturbance on the defendant and emphasized that the State may define the elements of the offense and the affirmative defenses available to a defendant, so long as it still requires the prosecution to prove the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. 432 U.S. at 201-02, 210-11. Arizona’s rule operates comfortably within that framework. Arizona required the State to prove intent, but it did not permit Clark to introduce expert psychiatric evidence for the separate purpose of negating intent outside the insanity defense. A state’s decision to structure the admissibility and legal effect of mental-condition evidence is precisely the sort of judgment Patterson leaves to the States. Id.

This Court’s precedent also recognizes that states may exclude or limit evidence when they have legitimate reasons grounded in the administration of criminal justice. In Montana v. Egelhoff, 518 U.S. 37, 56 (1996) (plurality opinion), the Court upheld a statute barring consideration of voluntary intoxication on the question of mens rea and explained that states have broad authority to define the reach of defenses and evidentiary rules relating to mental state. Justice Ginsburg’s controlling concurrence likewise emphasized that the State may determine the relevance of evidence that would otherwise bear on culpability. Id. at 58-59 (Ginsburg, J., concurring in the judgment). If due process permits a State to exclude intoxication evidence that may bear directly on intent, it necessarily permits Arizona to confine psychiatric evidence to an insanity defense.

The same principle appears in Clark v. Arizona, 548 U.S. 735 (2006), where this Court upheld Arizona’s evidentiary rule limiting “observation evidence,” “mental disease evidence,” and “capacity evidence.” Id. at 747-48. The Court explained that “the Constitution does not require [a] state to allow the use of mental-disease evidence to negate mens rea.” Id. at 772. It further recognized that Arizona had legitimate reasons to avoid jury confusion and to prevent the mental-disease evidence from becoming a backdoor substitute for an insanity defense. Id. at 769-72. That reasoning controls here. Clark asked the trial court to let him use expert testimony not merely to prove insanity, but also to suggest he lacked intent. Arizona was entitled to conclude that such an inquiry would invite the jury to confuse the cognitive standards for insanity with the separate issue whether the prosecution proved the elements of the offense. See id. at 774-79.

Nor does Crane v. Kentucky, 476 U.S. 683 (1986), help Clark. Crane held that a defendant may present evidence bearing on the credibility and voluntariness of a confession, but it did not announce a general due process right to present whatever evidence the defendant thinks relevant. Id. at 690-91. To the contrary, the Court reiterated that the States retain authority to apply ordinary rules governing evidence. Id. at 690. Arizona’s rule is an ordinary, neutral evidentiary limitation directed to the special dangers of psychiatric proof. See Clark, 548 U.S. at 770-71.

Finally, Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319 (2006), supports Arizona, not Clark. Holmes invalidated an arbitrary rule that turned on the perceived strength of the prosecution’s case, not on a principled evidentiary judgment. Id. at 331. Arizona’s rule is different: it reflects a considered choice to channel mental-disease evidence into the insanity defense and to avoid unreliable and misleading psychiatric mini-trials on mens rea. Clark, 548 U.S. at 769-72. That is a rational and constitutionally permissible evidentiary line.

Because due process does not require Arizona to admit expert mental-state evidence for the limited purpose of negating intent apart from insanity, the judgment should be affirmed.
