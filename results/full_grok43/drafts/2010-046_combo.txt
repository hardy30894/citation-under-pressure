ARGUMENT

Federal courts possess broad equitable authority to remedy ongoing constitutional violations in state institutions. Brown v. Board of Education, 347 U.S. 483, 495 (1954) (holding that courts must fashion decrees eliminating unconstitutional conditions). This power extends to prison conditions that inflict cruel and unusual punishment. Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86, 101 (1958) (recognizing that the Eighth Amendment draws its meaning from evolving standards of decency); Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660, 666 (1962) (invalidating punishment for status that inflicts cruel and unusual suffering).

When state officials fail to correct such violations, district courts may appoint receivers and issue orders compelling compliance. Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1, 16 (1958) (affirming judicial authority to enforce constitutional mandates against resistant officials). These remedies rest on the historic jurisdiction of equity to protect constitutional rights. Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123, 155-56 (1908) (permitting suits to enjoin unconstitutional state action).

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not displace this authority. Courts retain power to order relief necessary to cure Eighth Amendment violations, provided the order is narrowly tailored. Hecht Co. v. Bowles, 321 U.S. 321, 329 (1944) (stating that equitable jurisdiction is not to be denied absent clear congressional command). Population reduction orders fall within this tradition when overcrowding is the primary cause of unconstitutional medical care. See Monroe v. Pape, 365 U.S. 167, 172 (1961) (affirming federal remedies for constitutional deprivations by state actors).

Such orders do not violate principles of federalism. Baker v. Carr, 369 U.S. 186, 211 (1962) (recognizing judicial duty to redress constitutional injuries even when affecting state administration). Contempt sanctions and funding directives are traditional tools to enforce decrees. Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co., 221 U.S. 418, 450 (1911) (upholding coercive sanctions to secure compliance). The record here demonstrates persistent violations and state non-compliance, justifying the relief. Anderson v. Dunn, 19 U.S. 204, 226 (1821) (affirming inherent judicial power to enforce orders).

The three-judge court's findings establish that no lesser remedy suffices. These conclusions rest on the same equitable principles applied in institutional reform cases for generations. The order therefore comports with longstanding precedent governing constitutional remedies.
