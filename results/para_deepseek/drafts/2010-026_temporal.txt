## ARGUMENT

The Ninth Circuit erred by expanding FOIA Exemption 2 beyond its statutory text and congressional intent. The court’s “predominantly internal” standard finds no support in the plain language of 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(2) or in any precedent predating 1970. This Court should reverse.

### I. Exemption 2’s Plain Language Requires a Strict, Textual Interpretation

FOIA mandates “full agency disclosure unless information is exempted under clearly delineated statutory language.” The nine exemptions are “explicitly exclusive” and must be “narrowly construed.” Exemption 2 covers matters “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency.” The word “solely” is critical: it limits the exemption to matters that are exclusively about internal personnel rules and practices—not matters that merely relate to them in part or are “predominantly” internal.

Congress intended Exemption 2 to cover only trivial, housekeeping matters such as “rules and practices as to personnel’s use of parking facilities, lunch hours, sick leave, etc.” S. Rep. No. 813, 89th Cong., 1st Sess. 8 (1965). The Senate Report expressly stated that the exemption was designed for “minor internal matters” that are “of no genuine public interest.” Id. The House Report similarly described the exemption as covering “internal personnel rules and practices” that are “of a routine nature.” H.R. Rep. No. 1497, 89th Cong., 2d Sess. 10 (1966). Nothing in the legislative history suggests that Congress intended Exemption 2 to shield safety data about explosive ordnance storage that directly affects surrounding communities.

The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard rewrites the statute. By replacing “solely” with “predominantly,” the court eliminated Congress’s deliberate limitation. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in *Crooker v. Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco & Firearms*, 670 F.2d 1051 (D.C. Cir. 1981)—decided after 1970 and thus not controlling here—cannot justify this judicial amendment. The statute means what it says: only matters that are *exclusively* about internal personnel rules and practices qualify.

### II. The Requested Documents Do Not Relate “Solely” to Internal Personnel Rules and Practices

The ESQD arc maps and related data are technical safety calculations used to design ammunition storage facilities and to inform the public about blast zones. These documents serve multiple purposes: they guide construction, ensure safe ordnance storage, and—critically—affect the safety of residents near Naval Magazine Indian Island. The Navy itself uses these maps to communicate with local emergency responders and to plan for potential accidents. Such documents are not “solely” about internal personnel rules; they have direct external consequences for public safety.

The Ninth Circuit acknowledged that the ESQD data is “designed to be a long term planning tool for the Navy.” But planning tools that govern the storage of explosives and affect surrounding communities are not “internal personnel rules and practices.” They are operational safety regulations that implicate the public interest. Congress did not intend Exemption 2 to shield information that bears on the safety of persons living near military installations.

### III. The Risk of Circumvention Test Does Not Expand the Exemption’s Scope

The Ninth Circuit held that documents may be withheld under Exemption 2 if they are “predominantly internal” and their disclosure would “risk circumvention of agency regulation.” But the risk of circumvention test—first articulated in *Department of the Air Force v. Rose*, 425 U.S. 352 (1976)—is a post-1970 gloss on the statute. Even if that test were applicable, it cannot transform documents that are not “internal personnel rules and practices” into exempt material. The threshold question is whether the documents fall within the statutory category. If they do not, no amount of circumvention risk can save them.

The Navy’s concerns about security are legitimate, but they are addressed by other FOIA exemptions—such as Exemption 1 for classified national security information or Exemption 7 for law enforcement records. The Navy chose not to classify these documents under Exemption 1, and it failed to demonstrate that they were compiled for law enforcement purposes under Exemption 7. Congress carefully calibrated these exemptions to protect sensitive information. Expanding Exemption 2 to cover what other exemptions do not upsets that balance.

### IV. The Ninth Circuit’s Standard Contravenes FOIA’s Purpose

FOIA was enacted to “pierce the veil of administrative secrecy and to open agency action to the light of public scrutiny.” *Soucie v. David*, 448 F.2d 1067, 1080 (D.C. Cir. 1971) (Wilkey, J., dissenting) (citing legislative history). The Ninth Circuit’s “predominantly internal” standard invites agencies to withhold any document that has some internal use, so long as disclosure might help someone circumvent a regulation. That standard is boundless. It would allow agencies to shield everything from employee handbooks to safety manuals, contrary to Congress’s intent that exemptions be “narrowly construed.”

The documents at issue here are not trivial parking rules. They are technical safety data that the public needs to assess the risks posed by Navy operations near Puget Sound. The Ninth Circuit’s decision undermines FOIA’s core purpose of ensuring an informed citizenry.

### Conclusion

The judgment of the Ninth Circuit should be reversed, and the case remanded with instructions to order disclosure of the requested documents.
