The statute punishes one who "transmits in interstate or foreign commerce any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another." 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). Its terms presuppose a mental element. As this Court explained long ago, "mere omission from a criminal enactment of any mention of criminal intent should not be read as eliminating that element from the crimes denounced." Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246, 250 (1952). The word "threat" itself imports purpose: it denotes "an expression of an intention to inflict" injury. See, e.g., the common-law understanding reflected in cases such as United States v. French, 243 F. 785, 786 (S.D.N.Y. 1917).

An objective standard that convicts on the basis of how a reasonable hearer might construe the words would detach liability from the defendant's own state of mind. That construction collides with the principle that "a crime must be defined with sufficient definiteness" to give fair warning of what is forbidden. See Lanzetta v. New Jersey, 306 U.S. 451, 453 (1939). It likewise offends the rule that "the essential element of intent must be proved as to the particular defendant." Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91, 101 (1945). Where speech is at issue, the same requirement prevents punishment of "mere advocacy" or "abstract doctrine" without proof that the speaker intended to produce the forbidden consequence. See Whitney v. California, 274 U.S. 357, 373 (1927) (Brandeis, J., concurring); Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 369 (1931).

The lower court's contrary view—that the speaker's subjective purpose is irrelevant so long as a reasonable person would have foreseen the words as threatening—would convert the statute into a strict-liability measure. This Court has repeatedly rejected such readings of comparable enactments. See Morissette, 342 U.S. at 263; United States v. Dotterweich, 320 U.S. 277, 281 (1943). Because the government introduced no evidence that petitioner subjectively intended his words as threats, the convictions cannot stand.
