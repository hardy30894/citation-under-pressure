Section 1407(a) does not permit a transferee district court to keep an MDL case for trial by invoking § 1404(a). Congress provided that transferred actions “shall be remanded by the panel at or before the conclusion of such pretrial proceedings,” 28 U.S.C. § 1407(a), and the Supreme Court has made clear that statutory text controls unless Congress has supplied otherwise. See Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004). Nothing in § 1407 authorizes the transferee court to override that command by re-transferring the case to itself for trial.

The Supreme Court’s decision in Lexecon Inc. v. Milberg Weiss Bershad Hynes & Lerach, 523 U.S. 26 (1998), squarely answers the question presented. The Court held that “§ 1407(a) on its face limits the transferee court to pretrial proceedings and requires that, before trial, the case be remanded to the original district court,” id. at 34, and further rejected the notion that a transferee court may invoke § 1404(a) to effect a self-transfer for trial. Id. at 36-40. The Court explained that § 1404(a) authorizes a district court only to transfer a civil action to another district “where it might have been brought,” not to itself. Id. at 37-39; see also Hoffman v. Blaski, 363 U.S. 335, 344 (1960) (holding that transfer under § 1404(a) depends on the state of affairs at the time the action was commenced, not on the defendant’s consent). Lexecon therefore forecloses the decision below.

The statutory structure confirms that reading. Section 1407 was enacted to centralize common pretrial proceedings, not to alter the trial forum. See In re Penn Central Commercial Paper Litigation, 480 F.2d 548, 551 (3d Cir. 1973) (MDL transfer “is for coordinated or consolidated pretrial proceedings only”); In re Plumbing Fixture Cases, 298 F. Supp. 484, 495 (J.P.M.L. 1968) (MDL authority “terminates” after pretrial proceedings). The mandatory remand language is the mechanism Congress chose to preserve the plaintiff’s original forum for trial. Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 36. A transferee court that keeps the case for trial nullifies that command.

Milberg’s contrary position—accepted by the district court and the Ninth Circuit’s unpublished mandamus disposition—rests on efficiency, not statutory authority. But the Supreme Court rejected exactly that sort of policy-based gloss. In Lexecon, the Court acknowledged the district court’s and JPML’s practice of self-transfer, but held that such practice could not overcome the statute’s plain command. 523 U.S. at 36-40. The Court also rejected reliance on JPML Rule 14(b), explaining that a rule cannot “authorize what the statute forbids.” Id. at 40.

The Ninth Circuit’s mandamus decision below did not resolve the merits; it merely declined extraordinary relief and expressly stated that the panel, on direct appeal, “might well reach the opposite result.” Lexecon Inc. v. United States District Court, 61 F.3d 911, 912-13 (9th Cir. 1995). That caution was justified. The district court’s self-assignment for trial contradicted § 1407(a) and exceeded the authority granted by § 1404(a). Under Lexecon, the judgment must be reversed and the case remanded to the originating district for trial. See 523 U.S. at 40-41.

Accordingly, this Court should hold that a transferee district court conducting pretrial proceedings under § 1407(a) may not use § 1404(a) to assign the case to itself for trial. The statute requires remand; it does not permit self-retention. See Lexecon, 523 U.S. at 40.
