Milford violated the Good News Club’s free speech rights when it excluded the Club from after-hours use of school facilities because the Club wished to teach moral and character development from a Christian perspective. A public forum once opened to private speakers may not be administered to favor one viewpoint over another. Hague v. Committee for Industrial Organization, 307 U.S. 496, 515-16 (1939). The State may not “select which issues are worth discussing or debating.” Police Dept. of Chicago v. Mosley, 408 U.S. 92, 96 (1972). The underlying principle is older still: government may not suppress expression because of the ideas it conveys. Stromberg v. California, 283 U.S. 359, 368-69 (1931); Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 303-04 (1940); West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette, 319 U.S. 624, 642 (1943).

Milford’s policy did not exclude all discussion of morals, character, or youth development. It excluded only the Club’s Christian treatment of those subjects. That is viewpoint discrimination, not permissible subject-matter restriction. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; De Jonge v. Oregon, 299 U.S. 353, 364-65 (1937). The Free Speech Clause does not permit officials to insist that a permissible topic be presented only from a secular standpoint. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04; Murdock v. Pennsylvania, 319 U.S. 105, 110-11 (1943). Nor may the government discriminate against religious instruction merely because it is religious. Follett v. McCormick, 321 U.S. 573, 577-78 (1944); Niemotko v. Maryland, 340 U.S. 268, 272-73 (1951).

The Club’s activities were classic private speech. Its meetings involved prayer, Bible stories, songs, memory verses, and exhortation to live according to Christian teaching. But the Constitution protects religious expression as speech. Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04; Murdock, 319 U.S. at 110-11; Follett, 321 U.S. at 577-78. Milford could not avoid the First Amendment by relabeling that speech as “worship” and then excluding it from a forum that otherwise allowed community groups to gather for expressive purposes. Hague, 307 U.S. at 515-16; Niemotko, 340 U.S. at 272-73. The school’s selective exclusion burdened the Club because it spoke from a faith-based perspective, which is exactly what the First Amendment forbids. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; Mosley, 408 U.S. at 95-96.

Milford’s Establishment Clause defense is equally unsound. The Clause prevents government from composing religious exercises or sponsoring devotional activity, but it does not authorize hostility to private religious speech. Everson v. Board of Education, 330 U.S. 1, 15-18 (1947), held that the State must remain neutral between religion and nonreligion. Zorach v. Clauson, 343 U.S. 306, 313-15 (1952), reaffirmed that government need not display “callous indifference to religious groups.” Engel v. Vitale, 370 U.S. 421, 425-32 (1962), and School District of Abington Township v. Schempp, 374 U.S. 203, 222-23 (1963), prohibited government-sponsored prayer and Bible reading in public schools, but those cases do not say that private religious speakers may be excluded from an open forum. The Establishment Clause requires neutrality, not censorship. Everson, 330 U.S. at 17-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314.

Milford’s reliance on Establishment Clause concerns is therefore misplaced. The constitutional problem in Engel, Schempp, and McCollum v. Board of Education, 333 U.S. 203, 212-13 (1948), was governmental promotion of religion, not equal access for private religious expression. Equal treatment does not establish religion; it honors both clauses of the First Amendment. Everson, 330 U.S. at 15-18; Zorach, 343 U.S. at 314-15. If Milford feared confusion, the proper response was neutral administration of its facilities policy, not suppression of the Club’s viewpoint. Barnette, 319 U.S. at 642; Cantwell, 310 U.S. at 303-04.

For those reasons, Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club violated the Free Speech Clause, and the Establishment Clause does not justify that discrimination. The judgment should be reversed.
