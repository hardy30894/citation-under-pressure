**ARGUMENT**

**I. Roussos's Offense of Conviction Is Not a "Crime of Violence" Within the Meaning of 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), and the BOP's Program Statement Cannot Convert It Into One.**

Section 3621(e)(2)(B) permits early release for prisoners "convicted of a nonviolent offense." 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B). The implementing regulation borrows the definition of "crime of violence" from 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3), which asks whether the *offense*—not the offender's conduct at large—"has as an element the use, attempted use, or threatened use of physical force," or "by its nature, involves a substantial risk" of such force. 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)(3). Roussos's offense of conviction, conspiracy to distribute narcotics under 21 U.S.C. § 846, has no such element. Narcotics conspiracy can be, and often is, committed without any resort to force.

The proper inquiry looks to the generic character of the offense, not to case-specific facts that happen to surface during sentencing. This Court's predecessors have long insisted that federal statutes defining categories of criminal conduct be applied according to the generic nature of the offense rather than the idiosyncratic facts of a particular defendant's case. *See Nardello v. United States*, 393 U.S. 286, 295 (1969) (looking to the "generic" definition of the crime rather than the label a jurisdiction happens to attach to it). The same logic applies here: whether narcotics conspiracy is a "crime of violence" must be answered by reference to the elements Congress and the Sentencing Commission attached to that offense, not by reference to what a particular defendant's residence happened to contain.

The two-level firearm enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 2D1.1 did not add an element to Roussos's offense of conviction; it merely permitted the sentencing court to consider an aggravating fact in fixing punishment within the range already authorized by the statute of conviction. Courts have traditionally treated sentencing facts as distinct from the elements of the offense charged, precisely because a sentencing judge may take into account a wide range of information about the defendant without thereby redefining the crime for which he stands convicted. *See Williams v. New York*, 337 U.S. 241, 246–47 (1949) (a sentencing judge's consideration of information beyond the record of conviction reflects the "wide discretion" traditionally allowed in sentencing, not a redefinition of the offense itself). The BOP's Program Statement erroneously treats a sentencing enhancement as if it amended the elements of § 846—an offense that, standing alone, plainly falls outside § 924(c)(3)'s definition.

**II. The Program Statement Is Invalid Because It Conflicts with the Plain Terms of the Statute It Purports to Implement.**

An administrative agency's authority to issue interpretive guidance does not include the authority to rewrite the statute it administers. As the Supreme Court explained in *Manhattan General Equipment Co. v. Commissioner*, 297 U.S. 129 (1936), "[t]he power of an administrative officer or board to administer a federal statute and to prescribe rules and regulations to that end is not the power to make law . . . but the power to adopt regulations to carry into effect the will of Congress as expressed by the statute." *Id.* at 134. A regulation—or, as here, a Program Statement functioning as one—"which does not do this, but operates to create a rule out of harmony with the statute, is a mere nullity." *Id.* Because § 924(c)(3) defines "crime of violence" by reference to the elements or inherent nature of the offense, the BOP's Program Statement, which instead looks to whether the sentencing court applied an unrelated enhancement, is "out of harmony" with the statute Congress wrote.

Nor is the BOP's interpretation entitled to controlling weight merely because it appears in an internal Program Statement. Even under the more deferential approach to agency interpretations, weight is warranted only in proportion to "the thoroughness evident in its consideration, the validity of its reasoning, [and] its consistency with earlier and later pronouncements." *Skidmore v. Swift & Co.*, 323 U.S. 134, 140 (1944). The Program Statement offers no reasoned basis for equating a sentencing enhancement for firearm possession with an element of the underlying narcotics offense; it simply assumes, without analysis, that any weapon found anywhere in connection with the offense converts the conviction into a violent one. Such an unreasoned leap cannot receive the deference *Skidmore* reserves for genuinely persuasive agency reasoning.

**III. Any Doubt About the Statute's Reach Must Be Resolved in Roussos's Favor Under the Rule of Lenity.**

Even if the statute and regulation left room for doubt, that doubt must be resolved for Roussos, not against him. "The rule that penal laws are to be construed strictly, is perhaps not much less old than construction itself." *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820). Where "choice has to be made between two readings of what conduct Congress has made a crime," courts must, "before choos[ing] the harsher alternative, . . . require that Congress should have spoken in language that is clear and definite." *Bell v. United States*, 349 U.S. 81, 83 (1955). The same principle governs statutes that determine the consequences flowing from a criminal conviction, including eligibility for early release. *See Ladner v. United States*, 358 U.S. 169, 178 (1958). Congress never defined narcotics conspiracy, without more, as a violent offense; the BOP may not do so by administrative fiat, and any residual ambiguity must be resolved in Roussos's favor.

**CONCLUSION**

For these reasons, the order denying the writ should be reversed, and the BOP directed to consider Roussos eligible for early release under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(e)(2)(B).
