**ARGUMENT**

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires a trial court to apply a heightened “reasoned choice” standard when determining whether a defendant is competent to waive counsel or enter a guilty plea. The Ninth Circuit correctly held that the state court failed to satisfy this constitutional obligation, and its reversal must be affirmed. The record in this case reveals substantial evidence of Moran’s mental instability at the time he waived his rights—evidence that should have triggered a good faith doubt about his competence and compelled a hearing under the “reasoned choice” standard. The Supreme Court of Nevada’s reliance on psychiatric evaluations conducted solely to assess competency to stand trial, and its failure to apply the higher standard required for waiver of constitutional rights, violated due process.

The Constitution demands more than a mere rational understanding of the proceedings when a defendant seeks to relinquish fundamental rights. In *Drope v. Missouri*, 420 U.S. 162, 172 (1975), the Court held that a defendant is competent to stand trial only if he has “a rational and factual understanding of the proceedings against him” and can “assist counsel.” But that standard is insufficient when a defendant seeks to waive counsel or plead guilty. As this Court has repeatedly emphasized, such waivers involve “a far more extensive surrender of constitutional rights” than mere participation in trial. *Boykin v. Alabama*, 395 U.S. 238, 243 (1969). Therefore, a defendant must possess not just a basic understanding of the charges and penalties, but the capacity for “reasoned choice” among the alternatives available to him—such as retaining counsel, presenting mitigating evidence, or contesting the charges. *Harding v. Lewis*, 834 F.2d 853, 856 (9th Cir. 1987); *Chavez v. United States*, 656 F.2d 512, 518 (9th Cir. 1981); *Evans v. Raines*, 800 F.2d 884, 887 (9th Cir. 1986).

The record here demonstrates that Moran lacked the mental capacity for such a reasoned choice. He had recently attempted suicide, slashing his wrists and shooting himself in the abdomen. He was still recovering from that trauma when he appeared before the court. His mental state was further compromised by the fact that he was taking four different medications—*Inderal*, *Dilantin*, *Phenobarbitol*, and *Vistaril*—all of which affect the central nervous system and can impair judgment, memory, and decision-making. Although Moran acknowledged he was on medication, the court made no inquiry into the nature or effects of the drugs. This failure to investigate a known risk factor for diminished capacity is fatal to the validity of the waiver.

Moreover, Moran’s conduct at the plea hearing was not that of a rational actor making a deliberate choice. His responses to the court’s questions were largely monosyllabic, and he offered no explanation for his decision to discharge counsel and waive all defenses. Most troubling, he explicitly stated that he wanted to prevent any mitigating evidence from being presented at sentencing. This is not the behavior of someone exercising reasoned judgment; it is the conduct of a man in profound psychological distress, seeking to avoid the pain of public scrutiny and the possibility of mercy. As the Ninth Circuit observed, such a decision “cannot be reconciled with a rational and informed choice.” *Moran v. Godinez*, 972 F.2d 263, 269 (9th Cir. 1992).

The state court’s postconviction ruling, which relied solely on psychiatric evaluations conducted to determine competency to stand trial, cannot cure this constitutional defect. The evaluations by Dr. O’Gorman and Dr. Jurasky were not designed to assess the higher standard of “reasoned choice.” They focused on whether Moran could understand the charges and assist counsel—precisely the lower standard that does not govern waivers of counsel or guilty pleas. *Drope*, 420 U.S. at 171. The fact that both psychiatrists concluded Moran was competent to stand trial says nothing about his ability to make a reasoned choice among the alternatives available to him. Indeed, Dr. O’Gorman himself noted Moran’s “difficulty with his memory” and his “depression,” which may have undermined his ability to engage in meaningful decision-making. These observations, while relevant to competency to stand trial, are inadequate to support a finding of competence under the higher standard.

The Supreme Court of Nevada’s failure to apply the correct legal standard renders its finding of competence legally insufficient. Under *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 315 (1963), a federal court must assume that a state court applied the correct legal standard unless there is clear evidence to the contrary. Here, the state court’s order applied the standard for competency to stand trial in a single sentence, and made no mention of the “reasoned choice” standard required for waiver of constitutional rights. This is not a mere procedural oversight—it is a fundamental legal error. The state court did not even attempt to evaluate whether Moran could understand the consequences of waiving counsel, the risks of a death sentence without mitigation, or the strategic implications of his decision. Without such an inquiry, no valid finding of competence can stand.

The Due Process Clause does not permit a trial court to ignore clear signs of mental impairment when a defendant seeks to surrender his most fundamental rights. When a court has a good faith doubt about a defendant’s competence to waive counsel or plead guilty, it must initiate a hearing to determine whether the defendant can make a reasoned choice. *Sieling v. Eyman*, 478 F.2d 211, 214 (9th Cir. 1973); *Evans v. Raines*, 705 F.2d 1479, 1480 (9th Cir. 1973). The record here provides ample basis for such a doubt. The state court’s failure to act violated Moran’s constitutional rights.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Ninth Circuit must be affirmed. The Due Process Clause requires a heightened “reasoned choice” standard for waivers of counsel and guilty pleas. The state court failed to apply that standard, relied on irrelevant evidence, and ignored clear signs of mental instability. The judgment below is correct, and the writ of habeas corpus should issue.
