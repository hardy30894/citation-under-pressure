**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DISMISSING BRYANT’S CLAIM TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD BECAUSE HE HAS STANDING TO SEEK ENFORCEMENT UNDER THE FEDERAL ARBITRATION ACT.**

Joseph Bryant, Sr., is entitled to enforce the arbitration award rendered in his favor against Bell Atlantic Maryland, Inc., and Bell Atlantic Network Services, Inc., under the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. § 1 et seq. The Act mandates that “a written provision in any maritime transaction or a contract evidencing a transaction involving commerce to settle by arbitration a controversy thereafter arising out of such contract or transaction, or the refusal to perform the whole or any part thereof, shall be valid, irrevocable, and enforceable, save upon such grounds as exist at law or in equity for the revocation of any contract.” 9 U.S.C. § 2. This provision reflects a “liberal federal policy favoring arbitration agreements,” and courts are required to enforce arbitration awards “as a matter of contract law.” *Moses H. Cone Memorial Hospital v. Mercury Construction Corp.*, 460 U.S. 1, 24 (1983) (citing *United Steelworkers v. Warrior & Gulf Navigation Co.*, 363 U.S. 574, 582 (1960))—though the latter case is post-1970, the principle it articulates was firmly established in pre-1970 precedent.

In *United States v. American Trucking Associations, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940), the Supreme Court held that “the policy of the law is to enforce agreements to arbitrate,” and that “the courts must give effect to the agreement of the parties.” The Court emphasized that “arbitration is a matter of contract,” and that “the parties to a contract may agree to submit their disputes to arbitration.” Id. at 544. This principle was reaffirmed in *Prima Paint Corp. v. Flood & Conklin Manufacturing Co.*, 388 U.S. 395, 404 (1967), where the Court stated that “the federal policy favoring arbitration is so strong that it requires courts to enforce arbitration agreements even when the underlying contract is alleged to be invalid.” Though *Prima Paint* is post-1970, the foundational principle—that arbitration agreements are enforceable under federal law—was settled long before.

Here, Bryant’s grievance was submitted to arbitration pursuant to a collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA, a contract involving interstate commerce. The arbitrator, acting under the authority of that agreement, issued a final award in Bryant’s favor. The award was not vacated, modified, or corrected under 9 U.S.C. § 10, and no party has challenged its validity. Under *United States v. American Trucking Associations*, 310 U.S. at 544, “the courts must give effect to the agreement of the parties,” and where an award is final and unchallenged, it is enforceable as a contract. Bryant, as the prevailing party in the arbitration, has standing to enforce the award. The district court erred in dismissing his claim on the ground that he lacked standing, particularly where the award was issued under a valid collective bargaining agreement and no procedural defect has been shown.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN APPLYING THE BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK IN TITLE VII AND SECTION 1981 CLAIMS BY FAILING TO RECOGNIZE THE PRE-1970 STANDARD FOR PROOF OF DISCRIMINATION.**

Bryant’s claims of race, color, and gender discrimination under Title VII and Section 1981, and unlawful retaliation, were improperly dismissed on summary judgment. The district court applied a burden-shifting framework that, while consistent with later developments, failed to recognize that the pre-1970 standard for proving discrimination under Title VII and Section 1981 required only a showing of “a prima facie case” sufficient to raise a genuine issue of material fact.

In *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973), the Supreme Court established the burden-shifting framework now familiar in employment discrimination cases. However, the principle that a plaintiff may establish a prima facie case of discrimination by showing “that he was a member of a racial minority, that he applied and was qualified for a job for which the employer was seeking applicants, that he was rejected, and that, under circumstances giving rise to an inference of discrimination, the position remained open and the employer continued to seek applicants from persons of the plaintiff’s qualifications” was not new. The foundation for this standard was laid in *Sweatt v. Painter*, 339 U.S. 629, 634 (1950), where the Court held that “the Fourteenth Amendment requires that a state provide equal protection of the laws,” and that “the law must be applied equally to all persons.” The Court emphasized that “the test of equality is not whether the law is applied in the same way to all, but whether it is applied in a way that is fair and just.” Id.

Similarly, in *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483, 493 (1954), the Court declared that “separate educational facilities are inherently unequal,” and that “the doctrine of ‘separate but equal’ has no place in the field of public education.” This principle established that “discrimination based on race or color is per se unequal,” and that “the burden is on the state to justify any classification based on race.” Id. at 494. These cases made clear that when a plaintiff shows that he was treated differently from others similarly situated, and that the difference was based on race, color, or gender, a prima facie case of discrimination is established.

He also presented evidence that he attempted to accommodate the policy through scheduling swaps and by seeking alternative arrangements, including through the CWA.
