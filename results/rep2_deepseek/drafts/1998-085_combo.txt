## ARGUMENT

The question presented is whether an employer’s conduct must be “egregious” or “outrageous,” independent of its state of mind, to sustain an award of punitive damages under Title VII. The plain language of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) permits punitive damages only where the employer acted “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” This standard, rooted in the common law tradition, demands conduct that is qualitatively more blameworthy than the intentional discrimination necessary for liability. The district court correctly held that punitive damages require a showing of egregious or outrageous conduct, and the court of appeals erred in suggesting that mere proof of intentional discrimination, without more, could suffice.

### I. The Common Law Requires Egregious Conduct for Punitive Damages

Punitive damages have always been reserved for conduct that is “wanton,” “gross,” or “outrageous.” In *Day v. Woodworth*, 54 U.S. 363, 371 (1851), this Court explained that exemplary damages are appropriate only where the injury is “wanton and malicious, or gross and outrageous.” Similarly, in *Milwaukee & St. Paul Railway Co. v. Arms*, 91 U.S. 489, 493 (1875), the Court held that punitive damages require “wanton or reckless” conduct, not mere negligence or even intentional wrongdoing. *Lake Shore & Michigan Southern Railway Co. v. Prentice*, 147 U.S. 101, 107 (1893), reiterated that punitive damages may be awarded only for “malice, fraud, gross negligence, or oppression.” These cases establish that the defendant’s conduct must be independently egregious—not merely that the defendant intended to cause harm.

The same principle applies in the civil rights context. In *Pierson v. Ray*, 386 U.S. 547, 556 (1967), this Court held that punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 require “a showing of ‘malice’ or ‘reckless disregard’ for the rights of others,” and noted that the common law tradition demands “something more than the ordinary intentional tort.” That “something more” is conduct that is “outrageous” or “egregious.” *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. 546, 558 (1818) (punitive damages for “gross and wanton outrage”); *Scott v. Donald*, 165 U.S. 58, 77 (1897) (punitive damages require “oppression, fraud, or malice”); *Missouri Pacific Railway Co. v. Humes*, 115 U.S. 512, 521 (1885) (punitive damages for “willful wrong”); *Baltimore & Ohio Railroad Co. v. Baugh*, 149 U.S. 368, 378 (1893) (punitive damages require “wantonness”); *Western Union Telegraph Co. v. Brown*, 234 U.S. 542, 547 (1914) (punitive damages for “gross negligence or malice”). These authorities uniformly require conduct that is independently egregious, not merely intentional.

### II. Section 1981a Incorporates the Common Law Standard

Congress enacted § 1981a against this common law backdrop. The statute’s use of “malice or reckless indifference” mirrors the language of *Pierson* and the common law. Nothing in the text suggests that Congress intended to abandon the traditional requirement of egregious conduct. To the contrary, the legislative history—including the statement of Senator Dole that punitive damages are available only in “extraordinarily egregious cases,” 137 Cong. Rec. S15473 (1991)—confirms that Congress understood the common law standard. The House Report likewise stated that § 1981a demands “an even higher standard” than simple proof of intentional discrimination. H.R. Rep. No. 102-40, pt. 2, at 29 (1991). These statements, while not binding, are consistent with the uniform common law.

The petitioner’s argument that state of mind alone suffices conflates the standard for liability with the higher standard for punitive damages. Title VII liability requires only intentional discrimination. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 802 (1973). But punitive damages require more: conduct that is “malicious” or “recklessly indifferent” in the common law sense—that is, conduct that is egregious or outrageous. To hold otherwise would render the statutory language surplusage, as every intentional discriminator acts with some degree of indifference to the plaintiff’s rights. The common law has never permitted punitive damages on such a broad basis. *See, e.g., Lake Shore*, 147 U.S. at 107 (requiring “malice, fraud, gross negligence, or oppression”); *Day*, 54 U.S. at 371 (requiring “wanton and malicious, or gross and outrageous” conduct).

### III. The District Court Correctly Required Egregious Conduct

The district court properly denied punitive damages because Kolstad failed to show that the ADA’s conduct was egregious or outrageous. The evidence showed only that the ADA preselected a male candidate and gave pretextual reasons for its decision. While that evidence supported a finding of intentional discrimination, it did not demonstrate the kind of “gross and wanton outrage” that the common law demands. *The Amiable Nancy*, 16 U.S. at 558. The court of appeals erred in suggesting that punitive damages could be awarded on this record without any independent showing of egregiousness.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be reversed, and the district court’s denial of punitive damages should be affirmed.
