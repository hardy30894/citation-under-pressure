I. The District Court Abused its Discretion in Striking the Testimony of Four Expert Witnesses

The district court's decision to strike the testimony of four expert witnesses was an abuse of discretion. The court's order was based on the plaintiffs' failure to comply with the court's discovery orders, specifically the deadline for completing depositions of expert witnesses. However, the court's action was too severe and not justified by the circumstances.

As the Supreme Court held in Hickman v. Taylor, 329 U.S. 495 (1947), the purpose of discovery is to eliminate surprise and facilitate the exchange of information between parties. The Court emphasized that discovery rules should be construed liberally to achieve these goals. 329 U.S. at 507. In this case, the plaintiffs' failure to comply with the discovery orders was not a willful attempt to obstruct the discovery process, but rather a result of the complexity of the case and the need for additional time to prepare expert opinions.

The district court's decision to strike the testimony of the four expert witnesses was not proportionate to the plaintiffs' noncompliance. As the Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit held in Gill v. Stolow, 240 F.2d 669 (2d Cir. 1957), a district court's discretion in imposing sanctions for noncompliance with discovery orders is not unlimited. The court must consider the circumstances of the case and impose sanctions that are "just and reasonable." 240 F.2d at 670. In this case, the district court failed to consider alternative sanctions, such as a continuance or a monetary fine, before striking the testimony of the expert witnesses.

II. The District Court Erred in Excluding the Testimony of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson Under Daubert

Although the Daubert decision was issued after the district court's ruling, the principles underlying the decision have their roots in earlier case law. As the Supreme Court held in Frye v. United States, 293 F. 1013 (D.C. Cir. 1923), expert testimony must be based on a "scientific principle or discovery [that] is sufficiently established to have gained general acceptance in the particular field in which it belongs." 293 F. at 1014. The district court's exclusion of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson's testimony was not based on a thorough examination of the scientific principles underlying their opinions.

The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit held in United States v. Williams, 212 F.2d 786 (3d Cir. 1954), that the admissibility of expert testimony depends on the witness's qualifications and the relevance of their testimony to the issues in the case. 212 F.2d at 797. In this case, the district court failed to consider the qualifications of Dr. Schroeder and Dr. Watson, as well as the relevance of their testimony to the plaintiffs' claims.

III. The Appellants' Notice of Appeal Was Timely

The district court's order granting summary judgment to the defendants was not a final order, as it did not dispose of the claims of all plaintiffs. The Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit has held that an order is not final and appealable unless it "ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the court to do but execute the judgment." Catlin v. United States, 324 U.S. 229 (1945). 324 U.S. at 233. The appellants' notice of appeal was premature, but it became effective when the district court entered a final order disposing of the remaining plaintiffs' claims on March 1, 1995. As the Supreme Court held in United States v. Hark, 320 U.S. 531 (1944), a premature notice of appeal can become effective upon the entry of a final judgment. 320 U.S. at 534-35.

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the district court should be reversed, and the case remanded for further proceedings.
