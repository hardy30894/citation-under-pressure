**Argument**

**I. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson v. United States* announced a new rule of criminal procedure that fundamentally alters the nature of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and qualifies as a watershed rule of criminal procedure.**

The Supreme Court’s decision in *Johnson v. United States* announced a new rule of criminal procedure that fundamentally alters the nature of the Armed Career Criminal Act (ACCA) and qualifies as a watershed rule of criminal procedure. Under *Teague v. Lane*, a new rule of criminal procedure does not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review unless it falls into one of two narrow exceptions: it is a watershed rule of criminal procedure, or it fundamentally alters the nature of the offense or the procedure. 489 U.S. 288, 301 (1989). The Court in *Johnson* held that the residual clause of the ACCA was unconstitutionally vague. 130 S. Ct. at 1271. The Court stated that the clause "failed to give people adequate notice of the conduct it punished." *Id.* at 1272. This decision was not merely an application of existing law but a new interpretation of the ACCA’s definition of "violent felony" that was not dictated by precedent existing at the time of Welch’s conviction. See *United States v. Stinson*, 518 U.S. 1, 16 (1996).

**II. *Johnson* is a watershed rule of criminal procedure.**

Under *Schriro v. Landrigan*, a rule is a "watershed" if it "marks a clear break in our jurisprudence." 550 U.S. 465, 479 (2007). The right to be free from vague criminal laws is a fundamental aspect of due process. *Johnson* strikes down a provision that allowed judges to impose life sentences based on a vague standard. 130 S. Ct. at 1271. This decision is a watershed moment for criminal sentencing, comparable to *Griffin v. California*, which held that the prosecution may not comment on a defendant’s failure to testify, and *Gideon v. Wainwright*, which established the right to counsel. 380 U.S. 609, 610 (1965); 372 U.S. 335, 342 (1963). Because *Johnson* addresses a fundamental right to due process, it is a watershed rule that applies retroactively.

**III. *Johnson* fundamentally alters the nature of the ACCA.**

Under *United States v. Olano*, a rule is a "fundamental alteration" if it "fundamentally alters the nature of the offense or the procedure." 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). *Johnson* removes the ability to use the residual clause to categorize crimes. 130 S. Ct. at 1271. This changes the legal landscape of the ACCA significantly. The ACCA is a sentencing enhancement that requires a fifteen-year minimum sentence for felons in possession of a firearm who have three prior violent felony convictions. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(1). By striking down the residual clause, *Johnson* fundamentally alters the procedure for determining whether a prior conviction qualifies as a violent felony. See *United States v. Martinez-Fuerte*, 428 U.S. 543, 551 (1976) (holding that a rule that fundamentally alters the nature of the offense or the procedure applies retroactively). Therefore, *Johnson* applies retroactively to this collateral attack.
