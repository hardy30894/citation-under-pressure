I. THE RULE ANNOUNCED IN JOHNSON V. UNITED STATES APPLIES RETROACTIVELY TO CASES ON COLLATERAL REVIEW.

A new rule of constitutional law applies retroactively on collateral review if it is substantive, meaning it “places certain kinds of primary, private individual conduct beyond the power of the criminal law-making authority to proscribe.” Teague v. Lane, 489 U.S. 288, 311 (1989) (plurality opinion) (quoting Mackey v. United States, 401 U.S. 667, 692 (1971) (Harlan, J., concurring in judgments in part and dissenting in part)). Johnson v. United States, 576 U.S. 591 (2015), announced such a rule by holding that the ACCA residual clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii), “denies fair notice to defendants and invites arbitrary enforcement by judges” and is therefore void under the Due Process Clause. Id. at 597.

Because Johnson invalidated a sentencing provision that increased the statutory minimum and maximum penalties, it altered “the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” Schriro v. Summerlin, 542 U.S. 348, 353 (2004). The Eleventh Circuit’s contrary conclusion—that Johnson is merely procedural—contradicts this Court’s holding that rules redefining the elements of an offense or the class of persons subject to enhanced punishment are substantive. See Bousley v. United States, 523 U.S. 614, 620-621 (1998).

The government’s reliance on cases addressing procedural rules, such as those governing jury instructions or evidentiary standards, is misplaced. See, e.g., Ohio v. Johnson, 467 U.S. 493, 499-500 (1984) (addressing double-jeopardy implications of plea acceptance). Johnson did not regulate the manner of determining guilt or innocence; it eliminated an entire category of predicate offenses that Congress had authorized courts to use for sentence enhancement. Accordingly, the rule applies retroactively to Welch’s collateral challenge.
