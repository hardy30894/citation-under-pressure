# ARGUMENT

## I. The Double Jeopardy Clause Bars Retrial Once a Reviewing Court Determines the Evidence Was Insufficient to Support a Trial-Like Sentencing Finding

The Fifth Amendment provides that no person shall "be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb," a guarantee applicable to the States through the Fourteenth Amendment. *Benton v. Maryland*, 395 U.S. 784, 796 (1969). This Court has long held that when a reviewing court sets aside a conviction because the evidence was legally insufficient, the Double Jeopardy Clause forbids a second try; such a reversal "means that the government's case was so lacking that it should not have even been submitted to the jury," and is therefore "in effect . . . a determination that the government has failed to prove its case." *Burks v. United States*, 437 U.S. 1, 16-17 (1978). The State may not "make repeated attempts to convict an individual for an alleged offense, thereby subjecting him to embarrassment, expense and ordeal." *Green v. United States*, 355 U.S. 184, 187 (1957).

This protection is not confined to the guilt phase. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), this Court held that where a capital sentencing proceeding has "the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence," *id.* at 439, an acquittal-equivalent finding at that proceeding triggers the same double jeopardy bar on retrial that would apply to a substantive offense. The Court reaffirmed that principle in *Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), asking whether the proceeding possessed characteristics "that make it resemble a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause." *Id.* at 209-210. Nothing in the text of the Fifth Amendment, nor in the reasoning of *Bullington*, ties this rule to the presence of a capital charge. The relevant inquiry is functional: does the proceeding look like a trial? If so, the same interests in finality, and the same concern that the State not be permitted to hone its case through successive attempts, apply with full force. *See Pearce*, 395 U.S. at 717.

## II. California's Prior-Conviction Proceeding Bears Every Hallmark of a Trial on Guilt or Innocence

California has voluntarily clothed the prior-conviction determination with the very procedural safeguards that define a trial. A defendant enjoys a statutory right to a jury under Penal Code section 1025. The prosecution must prove the prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The ordinary rules of evidence apply. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224 (1996). And the defendant possesses the right to be "confronted with witnesses against him [and] to cross-examine" them. *Id.* at 228 n.6 (quoting *Specht v. Patterson*, 386 U.S. 605, 610 (1967)). These are precisely the "hallmarks" this Court identified in *Bullington*: a burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt, evidentiary formality, and adversarial procedure functionally indistinguishable from a trial of guilt. 451 U.S. at 439. Having chosen to structure this proceeding as a trial in substance, California cannot now disclaim the double jeopardy consequences that attend a trial in form. The State cannot have the procedural benefits of a trial-like adjudication while denying the defendant the constitutional protection that historically accompanies it.

## III. No Decision of This Court Confines *Bullington* to Capital Cases

The California Supreme Court read *Caspari v. Bohlen*, 510 U.S. 383 (1994), and *Pennsylvania v. Goldhammer*, 474 U.S. 28 (1985) (per curiam), as foreclosing extension of *Bullington* outside capital sentencing. Neither decision so holds. *Caspari* arose on habeas review under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), and this Court expressly limited its inquiry to whether application of *Bullington* to noncapital sentencing was "dictated by precedent" for purposes of retroactivity analysis; it observed only that "*Bullington* and *Rumsey* were capital cases," not that the double jeopardy analysis is confined to them. 510 U.S. at 392. *Goldhammer* likewise resolved a narrower procedural question and did not purport to limit *Bullington*'s reach. Neither case forecloses the functional inquiry *Rumsey* demands: whether the proceeding "resemble[s] a trial." 467 U.S. at 209-210.

## IV. The Court of Appeal Correctly Barred Retrial

The Court of Appeal held the evidence insufficient to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that Monge personally used a deadly weapon or personally inflicted great bodily injury as required for the "serious felony" enhancement. That determination is the functional equivalent of an acquittal on the enhancement allegation. Under *Burks*, 437 U.S. at 16-17, and *Bullington*, 451 U.S. at 446, the State forfeited its single opportunity to prove that allegation and may not be given a second chance to "supply evidence which it failed to muster in the first proceeding." *Burks*, 437 U.S. at 17.

## CONCLUSION

The judgment of the Supreme Court of California should be reversed.
