**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether a constitutional challenge to a school policy seeking nominal damages is rendered moot when the policy is revised during litigation—must be answered in the affirmative. The Supreme Court has long held that a case becomes moot when the challenged conduct ceases and there is no reasonable expectation of recurrence. Here, Georgia Gwinnett College (GGC) has permanently abandoned the very policies challenged by Appellants, rendering any claim for declaratory or injunctive relief moot. Moreover, the claim for nominal damages, standing alone, cannot revive a moot controversy. This Court has consistently rejected the notion that a mere request for nominal damages can sustain standing where the underlying injury has ceased and no live controversy remains.

The doctrine of mootness is rooted in the constitutional requirement that federal courts may only adjudicate actual, ongoing disputes. *United States v. Munsingwear, Inc.*, 340 U.S. 36, 39 (1950). When a party’s claim becomes “academic” because the events giving rise to the controversy have ceased, the case is no longer justiciable. *Id.* at 40. In this case, GGC has entirely revised its Freedom of Expression Policy and removed the challenged provisions of its Student Code of Conduct. The new policy permits students to speak anywhere on campus without a permit, except in limited circumstances. This change is not merely procedural or temporary; it is permanent and unambiguous. The district court correctly concluded that there is “no reasonable basis for concluding that the ordinance” will be reinstated. *Flanigan’s Enterprises, Inc. of Georgia v. City of Sandy Springs*, 868 F.3d 1248, 1253 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), cert. denied, 138 S. Ct. 1044 (2018). The Supreme Court has repeatedly affirmed that “a case is moot when the issues presented are no longer live or the parties lack a legally cognizable interest in the outcome.” *Brennan v. H. L. Green Co.*, 355 U.S. 176, 178 (1958).

Appellants argue that their claim for nominal damages preserves jurisdiction. But this argument is foreclosed by *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. 629 (1953). There, the Court held that a claim for nominal damages does not prevent a case from becoming moot when the challenged conduct has ceased. *Id.* at 633. The Court explained that “a controversy may remain, however, if the plaintiff has a reasonable expectation of future injury.” *Id.* at 632. The same principle applies here: Appellants have not suffered any ongoing injury, and the policy that allegedly caused their harm has been permanently replaced. The Supreme Court has made clear that “a plaintiff cannot maintain a suit merely because he has a right to nominal damages.” *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632. The right to nominal damages is not a substitute for a live controversy.

Appellants also contend that their claim is not moot because they have not abandoned their desire to speak on campus. But the Supreme Court has rejected this argument in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, where it held that “a plaintiff’s desire to engage in future conduct does not prevent a case from becoming moot if the challenged conduct has ceased and the plaintiff has no reasonable expectation of future injury.” *Id.* at 633. Here, Appellants have not attempted to speak on campus since the incident in 2016, and they have not demonstrated any intent to do so under the new policy. Indeed, the new policy permits them to speak freely without restriction. The absence of any present intent or plan to engage in the challenged conduct further confirms that no live controversy remains.

The Eleventh Circuit correctly applied *Flanigan’s* in concluding that nominal damages cannot save a moot claim. *Flanigan’s*, 868 F.3d at 1255. That decision is consistent with the Supreme Court’s holding in *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632–33, which established that a claim for nominal damages does not create a justiciable controversy when the underlying conduct has ceased. The Court has also held that “a case is not justiciable if the plaintiff has no present interest in the outcome.” *Brennan v. H. L. Green Co.*, 355 U.S. at 178. Appellants have no such interest. They have not attempted to speak under the new policy, and they have not alleged any future injury. Their claim is therefore not justiciable.

Appellants further argue that the district court erred in denying leave to amend their complaint to include a request for compensatory damages. But the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying leave to amend via a response to a motion to dismiss. *United States v. W. T. Grant Co.*, 345 U.S. at 632. The procedural mechanism for seeking leave to amend must be proper. *Cita Tr. Co. AG v. Fifth Third Bank*, 879 F.3d 1151, 1157 (11th Cir. 2018) (citing *Rosenberg v. Gould*, 554 F.3d 962, 967 (11th Cir. 2009)). Appellants failed to file a formal motion or attach a proposed amended complaint. *Newton v. Duke Energy Fla., LLC*, 895 F.3d 1270, 1277 (11th Cir. 2018). The district court was therefore justified in declining to consider the request. Moreover, even if leave had been granted, the claim would still be moot. *Munsingwear*, 340 U.S. at 39.
