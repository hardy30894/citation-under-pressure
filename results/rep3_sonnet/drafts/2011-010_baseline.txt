# ARGUMENT

## THE OCSLA EXTENDS LONGSHORE ACT COVERAGE TO ANY INJURY CAUSED BY OPERATIONS ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF, REGARDLESS OF WHERE THE INJURY OCCURS

### A. The Statutory Text Contains No Situs-of-Injury Requirement

Section 1333(b) of the OCSLA extends Longshore Act benefits for "disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources" of the shelf. 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). This language imposes a situs requirement on the *operations*—they must be "conducted on the outer Continental Shelf"—but imposes no comparable geographic limitation on the *injury*. The statute asks only whether the injury resulted "as the result of" such operations.

Congress knows how to draft a situs-of-injury requirement when it wants one. The Longshore Act itself requires that injury occur "upon the navigable waters of the United States." 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). Congress used no such language in § 1333(b). Where "Congress includes particular language in one section of a statute but omits it in another," courts should not read that limitation into the section from which it is absent. This textual contrast confirms that § 1333(b) is causally, not geographically, limited as to the situs of injury.

### B. Curtis Correctly Construed the Statute's Causal Language

The Third Circuit's decision in *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), correctly held that § 1333(b) requires only that the claimant satisfy a "but for" test establishing that the injury occurred "as the result of" operations on the shelf. *Id.* at 809-11. There, an employee injured in a car accident en route to a helicopter that would ferry him to an offshore platform qualified for benefits notwithstanding that his injury occurred onshore. *Id.* at 806, 811. The court reasoned that the statute's plain text ties eligibility to the *operations'* location, not the *injury's* location.

This reading harmonizes with the Act's remedial purpose. The Longshore Act "must be liberally construed in conformance with its purpose, and in a way which avoids harsh and incongruous results." *Voris v. Eikel*, 346 U.S. 328, 333 (1953). A situs-of-injury rule produces precisely such incongruous results: it would deny benefits to a platform worker crushed while performing support functions onshore, while granting benefits to an identically situated co-worker injured a few feet farther out, on a boat above shelf waters. Nothing in the statutory purpose—compensating those injured because of shelf extraction activities—supports so arbitrary a line.

### C. Tallentire's Footnote Is Unconsidered Dictum That Does Not Control

Petitioner relies heavily on a footnote in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), remarking that § 1333(b) "makes compensation for the death or injury of an 'employee' resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under" the Longshore Act. *Id.* at 219 n.2. But the Court expressly stated that "because this case does not involve a suit by an injured employee against his employer pursuant to § 1333(b), this provision has no bearing on this case." *Id.* The issue actually before the Court was the choice-of-law provision in § 1333(a)(2)(A), not the benefits provision in § 1333(b). *Id.* at 209, 219.

This Court has long recognized that "general expressions, in every opinion, are to be taken in connection with the case in which those expressions are used," because "their possible bearing on all other cases is seldom completely investigated." *Cohens v. Virginia*, 19 U.S. (6 Wheat.) 264, 399-400 (1821); *see Humphrey's Executor v. United States*, 295 U.S. 602, 627 (1935). The *Tallentire* footnote was neither briefed nor analyzed; it announces a conclusion without reasoning and addresses a provision the Court itself said had "no bearing" on the case. Such unconsidered dictum is not "entitled to special deference." *District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570, 625 n.25 (2008) (declining to follow prior dictum lacking analysis).

Moreover, the footnote's reference to a "situs requirement" does not resolve whether that requirement attaches to the *operations* or to the *injury*. Section 1333(b) unquestionably requires operations sited on the shelf; *Tallentire*'s passing remark is fully satisfied by that reading and says nothing about where the resulting injury must occur.

### D. The Fifth Circuit's Contrary Rule in Mills Rests on a Misapplication of Herb's Welding I

The Fifth Circuit's en banc decision in *Mills v. Director, OWCP*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989), grafted a situs-of-injury requirement onto § 1333(b), reasoning in part from this Court's decision in *Herb's Welding, Inc. v. Gray*, 470 U.S. 414 (1985). But *Herb's Welding I* addressed only whether a platform worker in *state* waters satisfied the Longshore Act's independent "maritime employment" status requirement; it did not construe § 1333(b) at all. *Id.* at 415-16. The Court's observation there that OCSLA coverage contains a "geographic limitation," *id.* at 422, refers to the undisputed requirement that the *operations* be on the shelf—not to any requirement regarding the situs of the *injury*. *Mills* thus imported a limitation the statutory text does not contain, producing the anomalous result that a welder injured while onshore constructing a platform destined for the shelf, and whose injury was unquestionably caused by shelf-directed operations, is nonetheless denied compensation. *Mills*, 877 F.2d at 357, 362.

### E. The "Substantial Nexus" Standard Properly Implements the Statute's Causal Requirement

The Ninth Circuit's "substantial nexus" test gives effect to § 1333(b)'s requirement that the injury result "as the result of" shelf operations without either eliminating meaningful limits or importing an artificial geographic bar unsupported by text. It ensures that only injuries causally connected to extractive operations on the shelf—not simply any injury suffered by anyone who once worked offshore—qualify for coverage, while refusing to deny compensation based on the fortuity of where within the causal chain the injury happened to occur.

### CONCLUSION

For these reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
