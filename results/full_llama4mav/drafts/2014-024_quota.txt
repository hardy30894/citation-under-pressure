The Third Circuit's decision affirming Anthony Elonis' conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) for transmitting threats in interstate commerce should be reversed because the court erred in holding that a conviction under this statute does not require proof of the defendant's subjective intent to threaten. The "true threats" exception to First Amendment protection, which is at issue here, has been interpreted by the Supreme Court to require a showing of intent to threaten.

In Virginia v. Black, 538 U.S. 343 (2003), the Supreme Court held that a "true threat" is a statement that a speaker "means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence" to a particular individual or group. Id. at 359. The Court emphasized that the speaker's intent is crucial in determining whether a statement constitutes a true threat. Id. at 360. This requirement of subjective intent is consistent with the Court's long-standing jurisprudence on the First Amendment, which prioritizes the protection of speech, even speech that is unpopular or disturbing. See, e.g., Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444 (1969); Watts v. United States, 394 U.S. 705 (1969).

The Third Circuit's reliance on United States v. Kosma, 951 F.2d 549 (3d Cir. 1991), which held that a statement is a true threat when a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat, is misplaced. Kosma was decided before Black, and its objective intent standard is no longer good law in light of the Supreme Court's subsequent decision. See United States v. Bagdasarian, 652 F.3d 1113, 1117 (9th Cir. 2011) ("Virginia v. Black requires that the speaker subjectively intend to threaten to violate § 875(c).").

Moreover, the Third Circuit's decision is in tension with the Supreme Court's decision in Elonis v. United States, which emphasized the importance of mens rea in federal criminal statutes. See Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952). The Court has long recognized that the mental state of the defendant is a critical element in determining criminal liability. See, e.g., Staples v. United States, 511 U.S. 600 (1994).

Requiring a subjective intent to threaten is also consistent with the Court's jurisprudence on other First Amendment issues. For example, in Hess v. Indiana, 414 U.S. 105 (1973), the Court held that a speaker's words must be considered in context to determine whether they constitute incitement to imminent lawless action. Similarly, in NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co., 458 U.S. 886 (1982), the Court emphasized the importance of considering the context and intent behind a speaker's words in determining whether they are protected by the First Amendment.

The Third Circuit's decision, if left standing, would have significant implications for the scope of the "true threats" exception and the protection of speech under the First Amendment. It would allow for the prosecution of individuals who make statements that are not intended to be threatening, but are nonetheless perceived as such by others. This would be inconsistent with the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on the First Amendment, which prioritizes the protection of speech, even speech that is unpopular or disturbing.

In conclusion, the Third Circuit's decision should be reversed because it failed to require proof of Elonis' subjective intent to threaten. The "true threats" exception to First Amendment protection requires a showing of intent to threaten, as established by the Supreme Court in Virginia v. Black. The Third Circuit's reliance on an objective intent standard is no longer good law, and its decision is in tension with the Supreme Court's jurisprudence on the First Amendment and mens rea in federal criminal statutes.
