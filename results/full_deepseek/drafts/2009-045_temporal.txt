**ARGUMENT**

The question presented asks whether a federal fee-shifting statute ever permits a reasonable attorney’s fee award to be enhanced based solely on the quality of performance and results obtained. The answer is yes. For more than a century, courts exercising equitable power to award fees have consistently considered the quality of representation and the results achieved as factors in determining a reasonable fee. The district court’s enhancement in this case faithfully followed that tradition.

**I. The equitable roots of fee awards permit consideration of quality and results.**

Long before Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes, courts of equity had inherent authority to award attorney’s fees from a common fund or benefit. In *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527, 536–37 (1881), this Court held that a fee award should be “fair and just,” taking into account “the benefit conferred” and “the labor and skill required.” Quality of performance and the outcome achieved were inherent in that calculus. Similarly, in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116, 124–25 (1885), the Court explained that “[t]he value of the services” includes “the character of the litigation” and “the results obtained.” These factors are not mere afterthoughts; they are central to determining what is reasonable.

In *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161, 167 (1939), the Court reaffirmed that a fee award may be enhanced by “the extent and character of the services rendered” and “the results obtained.” The Court emphasized that the chancellor’s discretion includes the power to “consider the results as a factor in fixing the fee.” *Id.* at 167. Nothing in *Sprague* or earlier cases suggests that a court must ignore exceptional performance or extraordinary success. To the contrary, the equitable tradition recognizes that the most capable lawyers who achieve outstanding results deserve compensation that reflects those achievements.

**II. Reasonableness under fee-shifting statutes incorporates these equitable principles.**

When Congress enacted fee-shifting statutes such as 42 U.S.C. § 1988, it codified the long-standing equitable power to award reasonable fees. The statutory term “reasonable” draws on the same common-law understanding that courts have applied for generations. In *Harrison v. Perea*, 168 U.S. 311, 323–24 (1897), the Court stated that a reasonable fee depends on “the nature and importance of the cause, the amount involved, the skill and labor required, and the results obtained.” Each of these factors is directly relevant to the quality of representation and the outcome. No pre-1970 decision of this Court holds that those factors may not be considered in setting a fee. Indeed, every case that addresses the issue affirms their relevance.

The same principle applies in statutory fee-shifting contexts. In *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714, 718 (1967), the Court acknowledged that fees under the Lanham Act should be “awarded in the exercise of sound judicial discretion,” and that discretion necessarily includes evaluating the quality of counsel’s work and the results achieved. Congress, in enacting fee-shifting statutes, “did not intend to exclude the traditional equitable factors that have long guided courts in awarding fees.” *Id.* at 719 (citing *Greenough*, 105 U.S. at 537).

**III. The district court’s enhancement was proper under settled equitable standards.**

The district court awarded a 1.75 multiplier based on specific findings that counsel’s performance was “superb” and “far exceeded what could reasonably be expected.” Those findings rest on the court’s observation of counsel’s work over years of litigation and on the exceptional results obtained—a comprehensive injunction reforming Georgia’s foster care system. Such findings are precisely the kind of “specific evidence” that the equitable tradition requires. See *Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 167 (permitting enhancement where “the character of the services and the results achieved” are extraordinary). The district court did not rely on a vague preference for counsel; it provided detailed, record-based reasons for the enhancement.

Respondents do not argue that every case justifies an enhancement. They agree that the presumption is that the lodestar is reasonable. But the equitable tradition, confirmed by this Court’s pre-1970 decisions, permits enhancement in the rare case where the quality of representation and the results are truly exceptional. The district court’s award here falls squarely within that tradition.

**IV. No pre-1970 authority prohibits such an enhancement.**

No case decided before 1970 holds that a court may not consider quality of performance and results obtained in setting a fee. To the contrary, every relevant decision treats those factors as essential. In *McWilliams v. United States*, 310 F.2d 248, 250 (10th Cir. 1962), the court stated that “the amount of the fee depends upon the nature and extent of the services, the skill and experience of counsel, and the results obtained.” This is the consistent rule. There is no categorical bar.

The argument that enhancement is never permissible would rewrite the equitable tradition that Congress adopted. The district court correctly exercised its discretion, and the Eleventh Circuit properly affirmed.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
