The federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over Minton's state-law legal malpractice claim because it necessarily depends on the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over cases "arising under" federal patent law. The Supreme Court has established a test to determine whether a state-law claim "arises under" federal patent law: the claim must necessarily raise a stated federal issue that is actually disputed and substantial, and federal jurisdiction must not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities. Grable & Sons Metal Prods., Inc. v. Darue Eng'g & Mfg., 545 U.S. 308, 314 (2005).

Minton's legal malpractice claim meets this test. To prevail on his claim, Minton must prove that Gunn's negligence proximately caused his injuries, which requires him to establish that he would have won his underlying patent infringement suit but for Gunn's negligence. Alexander v. Turtur & Assocs., 146 S.W.3d 113, 117 (Tex. 2004). This, in turn, requires the resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law, namely, whether the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar applies to Minton's patent. The experimental use exception is a critical issue in Minton's malpractice claim, and its resolution is necessary to determine whether Gunn's negligence caused Minton's losses.

The Federal Circuit has held that federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over state-law legal malpractice claims that require the application of federal patent law. Air Measurement Techs., Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P., 504 F.3d 1262, 1273 (Fed. Cir. 2007); Immunocept, LLC v. Fulbright & Jaworski, LLP, 504 F.3d 1281 (Fed. Cir. 2007). While the respondent argues that the Federal Circuit's approach is flawed, the Supreme Court's decision in Grable provides a framework for analyzing whether a state-law claim "arises under" federal law.

Applying the Grable test, Minton's malpractice claim necessarily raises a disputed and substantial federal issue, namely, the application of the experimental use exception to the on-sale bar. The resolution of this issue is necessary to determine Minton's entitlement to relief, and it is a substantial question of federal patent law. Furthermore, federal jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim will not disturb the balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities, as patent law is a complex and technical area that is best left to the expertise of federal courts.

In contrast, the dissenting opinion below and some other courts have taken a narrower view of "arising under" jurisdiction, holding that state-law claims that merely implicate patent law do not necessarily "arise under" federal patent law. See, e.g., Singh v. Duane Morris LLP, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir. 2008); New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner, 702 N.W.2d 336 (Neb. 2005). However, the Supreme Court's decision in Grable and the Federal Circuit's decisions in Air Measurement and Immunocept support a broader interpretation of "arising under" jurisdiction.

Therefore, this Court should reverse the court of appeals' judgment and dismiss Minton's appeal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, as the federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over his state-law legal malpractice claim.
