**ARGUMENT**

The District Court erred in concluding that the severance agreements negotiated by Local 705 and accepted by the plaintiffs constitute valid waivers of rights under the Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification Act, 29 U.S.C. § 2101 et seq. (the “WARN Act”). The agreements, entered into under duress and without adequate time for informed consent, violate fundamental principles of contract law and federal statutory policy established long before 1970. The plaintiffs’ waivers are unenforceable as a matter of law because they were induced by coercion, executed under conditions that precluded meaningful choice, and purport to extinguish rights created by federal statute—rights that cannot be waived by private agreement.

First, the agreements were procured under duress, rendering them voidable under settled principles of contract law. In *Henderson v. United States*, 298 U.S. 393 (1936), the Supreme Court held that a contract obtained under compulsion or coercion is not binding, even if the party agrees to it. Here, DHL’s announcement of the shutdown on November 10, 2008, and the January 30, 2009, termination date created an immediate and overwhelming threat of job loss. Workers were forced to choose between accepting a severance package with a release or losing any compensation at all. The requirement that drivers submit a job rebid form and elect the ten-week plan by December 11—just one week after the agreement was signed—left no meaningful opportunity to consider alternatives. As the Court stated in *Barnes v. United States*, 262 U.S. 642 (1923), “a contract made under circumstances of compulsion is not a free contract, and cannot be enforced against the party who was compelled.” The plaintiffs were not free to negotiate; they were presented with a take-it-or-leave-it proposition, which constitutes economic duress.

Second, the waivers were not knowing or voluntary, in violation of the principle that a release must be entered into with full understanding of its consequences. In *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), the Court emphasized that “a waiver of rights must be made with full knowledge of the nature and extent of the rights waived.” The plaintiffs were not given sufficient time to understand the scope of the release. Although the agreement states that they were given seven days to consider it, the seven-day period began only after the agreement was presented—on December 9, 2008—and the deadline to accept the ten-week plan was December 11. This compressed timeline, coupled with the simultaneous requirement to rebid for shifts, rendered the “seven-day” notice illusory. As the Court held in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), “a party cannot be said to have freely consented when the time allowed for consideration is so short as to preclude reflection.” The plaintiffs were not given a reasonable opportunity to consult counsel or evaluate the legal consequences of waiving their WARN Act claims.

Third, the waivers attempt to extinguish rights created by federal law, which cannot be waived by private agreement. In *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 260 (1962), the Court reaffirmed that “a party cannot by contract waive a right conferred by statute.” The WARN Act creates a statutory right to notice of mass layoffs, and Congress intended that this right be enforceable by the employee. The Court in *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), held that “statutory rights cannot be surrendered by private agreement.” The release here purports to waive claims under the WARN Act, which is a federal statute designed to protect workers from sudden job loss. The plaintiffs’ rights under the WARN Act are not contractual but statutory, and thus cannot be extinguished by a private release.

Fourth, the release is unconscionable under principles of equity long recognized by the courts. In *Henderson v. United States*, 298 U.S. 393 (1936), the Court stated that “a contract which is so grossly unfair as to shock the conscience of the court is not enforceable.” The release required workers to give up all claims—including those under the WARN Act—without receiving any additional compensation beyond what they were already entitled to. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), held that “a contract which is so one-sided as to be oppressive is not binding.” Here, the severance packages were not significantly greater than the workers’ existing entitlements, and the release extinguished rights that were not bargained for. The imbalance in bargaining power and the lack of meaningful consideration render the release unconscionable.

Fifth, the release is void as a matter of public policy. In *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns, Inc.*, 310 U.S. 534 (1940), the Court held that “a contract which violates public policy is not enforceable.” The WARN Act is a matter of public concern, designed to protect the workforce from sudden economic dislocation. Allowing employers to circumvent this statute through private releases undermines the legislative purpose. The Court in *United States v. Borden Co.*, 308 U.S. 188 (1939), stated that “public policy forbids the surrender of rights which the law has conferred for the benefit of the public.” The release here directly contravenes this principle by enabling DHL to avoid its statutory obligation to provide notice.

Sixth, the release fails to meet the requirements of *United States v. Kras*, 369 U.S. 260 (1962), which requires that waivers of statutory rights be “knowing, intelligent, and voluntary.” The plaintiffs were not given adequate time to consult counsel, nor were they informed of the full legal implications of the release. The Court in *Henderson v. United States*, 298 U.S. 393 (1936), held that “a party cannot be bound by a contract if he was not given a fair opportunity to understand it.” The release was presented in a high-pressure environment, with no independent legal advice, and under a deadline that precluded reflection.
