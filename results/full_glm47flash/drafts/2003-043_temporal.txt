**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE IRS MUST MAKE INDIVIDUALIZED ASSESSMENTS AGAINST PARTNERS TO EXTEND THE STATUTE OF LIMITATIONS.**

The Internal Revenue Code defines a "taxpayer" as "any person subject to any internal revenue tax." 26 U.S.C. § 7701(a)(14). A "person" includes "an individual... partnership." Id. § 7701(a)(1). While a partnership is a distinct taxpayer, an individual partner is a separate taxpayer. Consequently, the IRS must assess the tax liability against the individual partner to trigger the statutory collection period.

The Ninth Circuit has consistently held that an assessment against a partnership does not satisfy the statutory requirement of assessing the individual partner. In *United States v. Bowers*, 311 F.2d 416 (9th Cir. 1963), the court held that "the assessment of a tax against a partnership does not constitute an assessment against the individual partners." This principle is further illustrated in *United States v. Coson*, 286 F.2d 453 (9th Cir. 1961). There, the court noted that an assessment must identify the taxpayer. The district court held that the IRS’s attempts to collect the unpaid taxes from Coson were improper because "the assessment at issue named only the partnership... Relying on the fact that Coson had never been assessed individually... the district court held that the IRS’s attempts to collect the unpaid taxes from Coson were improper."

Here, the IRS assessed the unpaid taxes solely against the Partnership. It failed to assess the individual partners. Therefore, the assessment against the Partnership did not constitute an assessment against the individual partners, and the ten-year collection period did not begin to run for them.

**II. THE TEN-YEAR COLLECTION PERIOD EXTENDS ONLY TO THE ENTITY ASSESSED.**

26 U.S.C. § 6502(a) provides that a tax may be collected within ten years after the assessment of the tax. This limitation period is specific to the taxpayer against whom the assessment is made. *Bowers* supports this limitation. The court held that the assessment against the partnership extended the limitations period only to the partnership, not the partners. "The assessment of a tax against a partnership does not constitute an assessment against the individual partners."

Furthermore, *United States v. Riddell* (Young v. Riddell), 283 F.2d 909 (9th Cir. 1960), reinforces that a person cannot recover a refund if the assessment did not name them, implying that the assessment must be specific to the person seeking to enforce the right. "A person liable for taxes may not recover a refund of taxes he paid because of the fact the assessment did not name him."

Because the IRS failed to assess the individual partners, the ten-year period applicable to the Partnership did not toll the three-year period applicable to the partners. The IRS is now barred from collecting the debt from the individual partners.
