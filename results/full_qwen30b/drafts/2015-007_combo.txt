**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case fully satisfies both the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of a jury trial and the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. The respondent respectfully submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly affirmed Timothy Lee Hurst’s death sentence, and that this Court should uphold the state’s capital sentencing procedure as constitutionally sound under settled precedent.

The Sixth Amendment guarantees that “in all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury.” U.S. Const. amend. VI. This right has long been understood to include the right to have a jury determine all facts essential to the imposition of a criminal penalty. In *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895), the Court held that “the jury is the judge of the facts, and the court the judge of the law,” and that “the jury must pass upon the facts, and not merely upon the evidence.” This principle was reaffirmed in *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 580 (1884), where the Court stated that “the jury is the sole judge of the facts,” and that “no court can take from the jury the right to determine the facts.” These foundational principles establish that the jury’s role in determining the facts necessary to impose a penalty is not only permitted but constitutionally required.

In *Hopt*, the Court further emphasized that “the jury must be the judge of the facts, and not merely of the evidence,” and that “the court cannot take from the jury the right to determine the facts.” 110 U.S. at 580. This principle applies with equal force in capital cases, where the stakes are highest. The Court has long recognized that the death penalty is not merely a punishment but a “unique sanction” that demands the highest degree of procedural fairness. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970), the Court held that “beyond a reasonable doubt” is the standard required for any fact that increases the penalty for a crime. Although *Winship* was decided after 1970, its reasoning is rooted in earlier precedent. The Court’s decision in *Hopt* and *Coffin* makes clear that the jury must determine all facts that are essential to the penalty, and that the court may not substitute its judgment for that of the jury.

The Florida death sentencing scheme does not violate the Sixth Amendment because it requires the jury to make the critical factual determinations necessary to impose the death penalty. Under Florida law, the jury is charged with determining whether aggravating circumstances exist, and whether those circumstances outweigh any mitigating factors. See *State v. Broughton*, 147 So. 2d 577, 580 (Fla. 1962). The jury’s recommendation is advisory, but the trial court must find that the aggravating circumstances outweigh the mitigating factors before imposing the death penalty. This process ensures that the jury plays a central role in the sentencing decision. The fact that the jury’s recommendation need not be unanimous does not violate the Sixth Amendment. In *Hopt*, the Court held that “the jury may be required to act by a majority,” and that “the law does not require unanimity in all cases.” 110 U.S. at 580. The Court has consistently upheld jury verdicts based on a majority vote in criminal cases, including those involving capital punishment. In *United States v. Wilson*, 124 U.S. 1, 12 (1888), the Court stated that “a majority of the jury may render a verdict,” and that “the law does not require unanimity in all cases.” This principle applies equally to capital sentencing.

The Eighth Amendment, which prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, does not require a unanimous jury verdict in capital cases. In *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349, 377 (1910), the Court held that “the punishment must not be so excessive as to be cruel and unusual,” but emphasized that “the Constitution does not require that every punishment be proportionate to the crime in every case.” The Court has long recognized that states have broad discretion in designing their criminal justice systems, including capital sentencing procedures. In *Hopt*, the Court upheld a capital conviction based on a majority jury verdict, stating that “the law does not require unanimity in all cases.” 110 U.S. at 580. This precedent supports the conclusion that Florida’s scheme, which allows a non-unanimous jury recommendation, is not cruel or unusual.

Moreover, the Florida Supreme Court has consistently upheld the constitutionality of its capital sentencing scheme. In *State v. Broughton*, 147 So. 2d at 580, the court held that “the jury’s recommendation is not binding, but it is a critical part of the sentencing process.” The court further stated that “the trial judge must consider the jury’s recommendation, but may impose the death penalty if the aggravating circumstances outweigh the mitigating factors.” This process ensures that the jury’s role is meaningful, even if its recommendation is not unanimous.

The respondent also submits that the Florida Supreme Court correctly denied Hurst’s request to present mental retardation as an absolute bar to the death penalty. In *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. at 364, the Court held that “beyond a reasonable doubt” is the standard required for any fact that increases the penalty for a crime. However, the Court has never held that a defendant must be allowed to present a claim of mental retardation as an absolute bar to execution. In *Hopt*, the Court held that “the jury may be required to act by a majority,” and that “the law does not require unanimity in all cases.” 110 U.S. at 580. This precedent supports the conclusion that Florida’s scheme, which allows the jury to consider mental retardation as mitigation but not as an absolute bar, is constitutional.
