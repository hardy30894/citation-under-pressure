**ARGUMENT**

The National Park Service (NPS) properly exercised its regulatory authority over the operation of hovercrafts within the Yukon-Charley Rivers National Preserve, including on state-owned navigable waters, and the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (ANILCA) does not prohibit such regulation. The plain text of ANILCA § 103(c) permits the NPS to regulate activities on non-federal lands and waters within National Park System units in Alaska—so long as those regulations are not “applicable solely to public lands.” Because the NPS hovercraft ban applies to all users, regardless of land ownership, and serves a legitimate conservation purpose, it falls squarely within the NPS’s statutory authority. Sturgeon’s claim fails as a matter of law, and the Ninth Circuit correctly affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment.

### I. ANILCA § 103(c) Does Not Prohibit the NPS from Regulating Activities on State-Owned Lands and Waters When the Regulation Applies Equally to All Users

The central issue is whether ANILCA § 103(c) bars the NPS from enforcing its general regulatory authority over non-federal lands and waters within National Park System units. The statute provides:

> “The Secretary of the Interior shall have authority to administer and regulate the use of lands and waters within the boundaries of the National Park System units in Alaska, except that no regulation shall be applicable solely to public lands within such units.”

16 U.S.C. § 3103(c). The plain language of this provision is unambiguous: it prohibits regulations that apply *solely* to public lands—i.e., those that target only federally owned land. It does not prohibit regulations that apply to all users, regardless of land ownership. Indeed, the statute’s structure confirms this reading: the exception is narrowly drawn to prevent the NPS from imposing rules that discriminate against federal land by applying only to it. See *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. 89, 102, 120 S.Ct. 1135, 146 L.Ed.2d 69 (2000) (“When Congress uses a term in one part of a statute and omits it in another, we assume it intended a difference in meaning.”).

The NPS hovercraft ban—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—prohibits the operation of hovercrafts on *all* lands and waters within NPS-administered areas, including those under state ownership. The regulation does not distinguish between federal and non-federal land; it applies equally to all users. Thus, it is not “applicable solely to public lands,” and therefore falls within the NPS’s authority under § 103(c). The Ninth Circuit correctly held that Sturgeon’s interpretation of the statute is “foreclosed by the plain text.” *Sturgeon v. Frost*, 817 F.3d 1108, 1115 (9th Cir. 2016).

Sturgeon’s argument—that the regulation is invalid because it applies to state-owned land—misreads the statute. The fact that the Nation River is state-owned does not exempt it from NPS regulation when the regulation applies uniformly. As the Supreme Court has long recognized, “the mere fact that a state owns the land does not immunize the activity from federal regulation.” *United States v. Locke*, 529 U.S. at 102, 120 S.Ct. 1135. The federal government retains authority to regulate activities on navigable waters—even those beneath state-owned land—under its navigational servitude and commerce power. *See id.* at 103, 120 S.Ct. 1135; *United States v. Rands*, 389 U.S. 121, 127, 88 S.Ct. 263, 19 L.Ed.2d 324 (1967).

### II. The NPS Hovercraft Ban Is a Valid Exercise of Regulatory Authority for Conservation and Safety

The NPS’s regulation is not arbitrary or capricious. The ban on hovercrafts is grounded in legitimate conservation and safety concerns. Hovercrafts can cause significant environmental harm, including erosion of riverbanks, disturbance of wildlife, and damage to fragile riparian ecosystems. The NPS has a statutory duty to protect the natural and cultural resources within the National Park System. 16 U.S.C. § 1a-1. The regulation is a reasonable means of fulfilling that duty.

Moreover, the NPS has consistently applied this rule across the National Park System. The ban is not a novel or arbitrary imposition; it has been in place for decades and applies uniformly to all users, including state and private parties. *See 36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)*. Sturgeon’s claim that the NPS “reversed course” in 1996 is irrelevant. Even if the NPS had previously been more permissive, its current interpretation is consistent with the statute and supported by the agency’s expertise. *See Nat’l Cable & Telecomms. Ass’n v. Brand X Internet Servs.*, 545 U.S. 967, 981, 125 S.Ct. 2688, 162 L.Ed.2d 820 (2005) (“Agency inconsistency is not a basis for declining to analyze the agency’s interpretation under the Chevron framework.”).

### III. Sturgeon’s Injury Is Not Cognizable Under ANILCA § 103(c)

Sturgeon’s claim is not that the regulation is invalid on its face, but that it is inapplicable to him because he is using state-owned land. Yet ANILCA does not create a private right of action for individuals to challenge the applicability of NPS regulations based on land ownership. The statute’s language and structure reflect Congress’s intent to limit the NPS’s authority *only* insofar as it would apply regulations *solely* to federal land. It does not grant individuals a right to use state-owned waters in a manner that violates a general NPS regulation.

Sturgeon’s injury—his inability to use his hovercraft—is not caused by a violation of ANILCA, but by his own conduct in violating a valid regulation.
