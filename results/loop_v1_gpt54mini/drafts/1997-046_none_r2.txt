Ohio’s clemency process creates no protected life or liberty interest, and the optional interview does not violate the Fifth Amendment.

**I. Clemency is an act of grace, not a protected entitlement.**  
From the founding era forward, the Court has treated clemency as an executive prerogative, not a judicially enforceable right. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. (7 Pet.) 150, 160 (1833), explained that a pardon “is an act of grace.” *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 333, 380–81 (1866), likewise described the pardon power as entrusted to the Executive. And *Biddle v. Perovich*, 274 U.S. 480, 486–87 (1927), reiterated that clemency is a matter for executive judgment rather than prisoner entitlement. See also *In re Bonner*, 151 U.S. 242, 260–61 (1894). Because the decision whether to grant mercy is discretionary, an inmate has no vested life or liberty interest in clemency itself.

That principle defeats any due process claim based on the clemency process. Due process protects recognized legal interests, not hopes of favorable treatment. *Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 118 U.S. 356, 369–70 (1886); *Cafeteria & Restaurant Workers Union, Local 473 v. McElroy*, 367 U.S. 886, 895–99 (1961). The Court later confirmed that property and liberty interests arise only when law places substantive limits on official discretion. *Board of Regents v. Roth*, 408 U.S. 564, 569–71 (1972); *Perry v. Sindermann*, 408 U.S. 593, 599–603 (1972). Ohio’s clemency system imposes no such entitlement. The inmate may apply, but he cannot demand relief. That is the end of the due process inquiry. *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81; *Biddle*, 274 U.S. at 486–87.

**II. A voluntary clemency interview does not compel self-incrimination.**  
The Fifth Amendment prohibits compulsion. It does not bar a State from offering a prisoner the chance to speak in support of mercy. *Hale v. Henkel*, 201 U.S. 43, 67–68 (1906). Nor does it invalidate every decision in which silence may be strategically costly. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. 591, 598–605 (1896), and *Mann v. People of State of Illinois*, 292 U.S. 602, 604–05 (1934), both recognize that the privilege is violated only when testimony is compelled, not when a person elects to speak or withhold speech.

Ohio’s regulation is voluntary on its face. It permits, but does not require, a personal interview. It does not demand a waiver of the privilege against self-incrimination. A prisoner may decline the interview, may submit written materials instead, or may speak selectively. That is choice, not compulsion. The Court has consistently required actual legal coercion before finding a Fifth Amendment violation. *Counselman v. Hitchcock*, 142 U.S. 547, 562–64 (1892); *McCarthy v. Arndstein*, 266 U.S. 34, 40–41 (1924); *United States v. Monia*, 317 U.S. 424, 427 (1943).

The possibility that a prisoner’s voluntary statement might later be used somewhere does not change the analysis. The privilege does not forbid a State from listening when an inmate speaks freely. *Brown v. Walker*, 161 U.S. at 598–605. And the Court has made clear that adverse consequences flowing from a choice to speak or remain silent do not, without more, amount to unconstitutional compulsion. *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308, 318–20 (1976). The Constitution forbids forced testimony; it does not guarantee that a voluntary interview will be consequence-free.

**III. The decision below improperly constitutionalizes discretionary clemency.**  
The Sixth Circuit’s approach treats clemency as if it were an extension of trial or direct review. That cannot be squared with the Court’s repeated recognition that clemency is a matter of grace entrusted to the Executive. *United States v. Wilson*, 32 U.S. at 160; *Ex parte Garland*, 71 U.S. at 380–81; *Biddle*, 274 U.S. at 486–87. It also expands the Fifth Amendment beyond its text by converting a voluntary interview into compulsion. Yet the Court has never held that a prisoner must be insulated from all strategic choices simply because he is seeking discretionary mercy. *Hale*, 201 U.S. at 67–68; *McCarthy*, 266 U.S. at 40–41; *Mann*, 292 U.S. at 604–05.

The Constitution requires neither judicial supervision of clemency nor immunity from the ordinary consequences of voluntarily speaking. Ohio’s procedure offers an opportunity, not a deprivation. Because there is no protected liberty interest in clemency and no compelled self-incrimination in the interview option, the judgment below should be reversed.
