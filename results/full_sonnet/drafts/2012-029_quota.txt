**ARGUMENT**

**I. THE PATENT QUESTION EMBEDDED IN THIS LEGAL-MALPRACTICE ACTION IS NOT A "SUBSTANTIAL" FEDERAL ISSUE UNDER THIS COURT'S "ARISING UNDER" PRECEDENTS.**

Federal jurisdiction over patent-related claims arises under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a), construed coextensively with the general federal-question statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1331. *Christianson v. Colt Indus. Operating Corp.*, 486 U.S. 800, 808–09 (1988). Under *Christianson*, § 1338(a) jurisdiction attaches only when "the plaintiff's right to relief necessarily depends on resolution of a substantial question of federal patent law." *Id.* at 809. This Court has long cautioned that not every case touching a federal issue belongs in federal court; jurisdiction depends on "a selective process which picks the substantial causes out of the web and lays the other ones aside." *Gully v. First Nat'l Bank in Meridian*, 299 U.S. 109, 117–18 (1936). A suit does not arise under federal law unless it "really and substantially involves a dispute or controversy respecting the validity, construction, or effect of such a law, upon the determination of which the result depends." *Shulthis v. McDougal*, 225 U.S. 561, 569 (1912).

In *Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing*, 545 U.S. 308 (2005), this Court held that jurisdiction exists over state-law claims only where a federal issue is "actually disputed and substantial," and a federal forum "may entertain [it] without disturbing any congressionally approved balance of federal and state judicial responsibilities." *Id.* at 314. *Grable* found the embedded tax-notice question substantial because it was "the only legal or factual issue contested in the case" and its resolution controlled the government's capacity to enforce tax liens nationwide. *Id.* at 315.

No comparable stakes exist here. Minton's malpractice claim requires only a retrospective, hypothetical assessment of how the experimental-use exception would have fared in patent litigation concluded years ago. That case-within-a-case inquiry, familiar to every malpractice action, see *Schaeffer v. O'Brien*, 39 S.W.3d 719, 720 (Tex. App.—Eastland 2001, pet. denied) (malpractice plaintiffs must prove a "case within a case"), binds no one, invalidates no patent, and shapes no future controversy. It bears none of the generally applicable significance that rendered the federal issue substantial in *Grable*. As the Fifth Circuit recognized in construing an analogous embedded intellectual-property question, jurisdiction requires (1) that resolving the federal issue be necessary to the state claim, (2) that it be actually disputed, (3) that it be substantial, and (4) that federal adjudication not disturb the state-federal balance. *Singh v. Duane Morris LLP*, 538 F.3d 334, 338 (5th Cir. 2008). Elements one and two may be satisfied here, but elements three and four are not.

**II. EXERCISING EXCLUSIVE FEDERAL JURISDICTION WOULD DISTURB THE CONGRESSIONALLY APPROVED BALANCE BETWEEN STATE AND FEDERAL COURTS.**

*Grable*'s final requirement independently forecloses jurisdiction. Legal malpractice, like the regulation of the practice of law generally, is a field of traditional and predominant state concern. This Court has cautioned against federal-question jurisdiction over garden-variety state claims that merely incorporate a federal standard, recognizing that such jurisdiction would improperly shift entire categories of state litigation into federal court. *Merrell Dow Pharm. Inc. v. Thompson*, 478 U.S. 804, 811–12 (1986). State courts adjudicate malpractice suits daily and are fully competent to resolve embedded federal questions where necessary to determine causation and damages. Numerous courts confronting materially identical malpractice claims arising from underlying patent litigation agree. See *New Tek Mfg., Inc. v. Beehner*, 270 Neb. 264, 702 N.W.2d 336 (2005) ("[w]hen patent issues are merely implicated incidentally in a cause of action... federal courts do not have jurisdiction of the case pursuant to § 1338"); *E-Pass Tech., Inc. v. Moses & Singer LLP*, 189 Cal. App. 4th 1140, 1152 (2010) ("to the extent that the subject matter of patent law is relevant to the determination of the professional negligence claim, it does not present a question of patent law that is substantial").

The Federal Circuit's contrary approach in *Air Measurement Technologies, Inc. v. Akin Gump Strauss Hauer & Feld, L.L.P.*, 504 F.3d 1262 (Fed. Cir. 2007), would sweep every malpractice claim touching a patent case—however incidentally—into exclusive federal jurisdiction, displacing the States' traditional authority over attorney regulation and multiplying, rather than avoiding, friction between the state and federal systems. That result cannot be squared with *Grable*'s insistence on preserving "the sound division of labor between state and federal courts." 545 U.S. at 319.

Because the embedded patent question here is neither substantial in the *Grable* sense nor amenable to federal resolution without upsetting the state-federal balance, the Texas courts possessed subject-matter jurisdiction over Minton's malpractice claim. The judgment of the Supreme Court of Texas should be reversed.
