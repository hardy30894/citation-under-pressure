# ARGUMENT

## I. THE FDCPA'S TEXT DISTINGUISHES DEBT COLLECTORS FROM CREDITORS BASED ON WHOSE DEBT IS BEING COLLECTED, NOT WHEN THE DEBT WAS ACQUIRED

The FDCPA regulates "debt collectors," a term Congress defined with precision in 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). That provision identifies as a debt collector any person "who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed or due or asserted to be owed or due another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6). This Court has recognized that the statute's definition "includes a person 'who regularly collects or attempts to collect, directly or indirectly, debts owed [to] . . . another.'" *Heintz v. Jenkins*, 514 U.S. 291, 293 (1995) (alteration in original) (quoting § 1692a(6)). The critical word is "another"—a debt collector, under this operative clause, collects on behalf of someone else. A creditor collecting its own debt is not so covered.

Santander purchased petitioners' defaulted loans outright from CitiFinancial Auto. After that purchase, Santander was not collecting a debt "owed or due . . . another"; it was collecting a debt owed to itself. The debts, once purchased, were Santander's own receivables, held for its own account and collected for its own benefit. Nothing in the text of § 1692a(6) suggests that the default status of a debt at the time of acquisition transforms an owner of that debt into an agent collecting for someone else. The statute asks only whether, at the time of the challenged collection conduct, the person collecting was doing so for "another." Congress could easily have written the statute to turn on when the debt was acquired relative to default, as it did elsewhere in the statute's exclusions, see 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(6)(F)(iii) (addressing debt "not in default at the time it was obtained"). But that exclusionary provision is irrelevant unless a defendant first satisfies the threshold definition of "debt collector"—and a purchaser and owner of debt collecting for itself never satisfies that threshold in the first place.

## II. PETITIONERS' "DEFAULT AT ACQUISITION" RULE CANNOT BE DERIVED FROM THE STATUTORY EXCLUSIONS AND WOULD IMPROPERLY COLLAPSE THE STATUTE'S TWO-STEP STRUCTURE

Petitioners' theory rests entirely on exclusionary language never designed to define the category of persons covered by the Act in the first instance. Section 1692a(4) excludes from the definition of "creditor" a person who receives an assigned debt "solely for the purpose of facilitating collection of such debt for another." 15 U.S.C. § 1692a(4). Section 1692a(6)(F)(iii) separately excludes from "debt collector" a person collecting debt "owed or due . . . another" if that debt "was not in default at the time it was obtained." Petitioners would have this Court read these two exclusions together to produce an affirmative rule that any post-default purchaser is automatically a debt collector. But an exclusion applicable only where a person already collects debt "for another" cannot logically expand to cover a person who purchases debt outright and thereafter collects it solely for itself. As this Court has cautioned in analogous contexts of statutory construction, terms grouped in a series should be read consistently according to their ordinary and coordinated function, not stretched beyond their textual anchor. *Cf. Paroline v. United States*, 572 U.S. 434 (2014) (discussing coordinated construction of statutory language). The exclusions in § 1692a(6) presuppose that the threshold definition is independently satisfied; they cannot substitute for it.

Congress's purpose in the FDCPA confirms this reading. The Act's statement of purpose provides that "[i]t is the purpose of this subchapter to eliminate abusive debt collection practices by debt collectors." 15 U.S.C. § 1692(e); see *Jerman v. Carlisle, McNellie, Rini, Kramer & Ulrich, L.P.A.*, 559 U.S. 573, 577 (2010) (Congress enacted the FDCPA "to eliminate abusive debt collection practices"). Congress targeted the distinctive incentive problems posed by third-party collection agents who have no ongoing relationship with the debtor and profit only through aggressive collection tactics, not the incentives facing an entity that has purchased a loan and stands to profit from the entire lending relationship, including future repayment. A consumer finance company like Santander—which purchases loan portfolios as capital investments and thereafter collects for its own account—operates economically as a creditor, not as the type of hired collection specialist Congress sought to regulate through the FDCPA's more stringent obligations.

## III. THIS COURT'S DECISION IN HEINTZ CONFIRMS THAT THE RELEVANT INQUIRY IS THE CAPACITY IN WHICH THE DEFENDANT ACTED AT THE TIME OF THE CHALLENGED CONDUCT

In *Heintz*, this Court examined whether an attorney engaged in litigation to collect a consumer debt fell within the Act's definition because the attorney "regularly tri[ed] to obtain payment of consumer debts through legal proceedings" on behalf of a creditor-client. 514 U.S. at 294. The touchstone of that analysis was whether the defendant was, in the transaction at issue, collecting a debt "owed or due . . . another." *Id.* at 293. The same touchstone applies here, and it points toward affirmance: Santander was not collecting on behalf of CitiFinancial Auto or any other creditor when it engaged in the conduct petitioners challenge. It was collecting on debt it owned outright, having paid substantial consideration to acquire it as one part of a $3.55 billion portfolio of receivables.

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Fourth Circuit.
