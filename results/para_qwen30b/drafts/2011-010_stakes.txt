**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) benefits only to injuries occurring *on* the outer Continental Shelf—requires a careful reading of the statutory text, legislative history, and binding precedent. The Ninth Circuit’s decision, which rejects the long-standing “situs-of-injury” requirement, misreads the law and creates a dangerous precedent that undermines the geographic boundaries Congress explicitly established. For the reasons set forth below, this Court should reverse.

### I. The OCSLA’s Workers’ Compensation Provision Applies Only to Injuries Occurring on the Outer Continental Shelf

The plain language of 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) makes clear that LHWCA benefits are available only when an injury “occur[s] as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” (Emphasis added.) This is not a “but-for” test, nor is it a “substantial nexus” test. It is a *situs* requirement: the injury must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf. The phrase “occurring as the result of” does not eliminate the need for the injury to occur in the designated location. Rather, it establishes a causal link between the injury and the operations *on* the Shelf. The injury may be proximate to the operations, but it must occur *on* the Shelf.

This interpretation is consistent with the Supreme Court’s decision in *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), which, while not directly on point, provides critical guidance. In *Tallentire*, the Court considered whether a choice-of-law provision in OCSLA applied to a helicopter crash that occurred *above* the outer Continental Shelf. The Court held that the provision applied only to the “subsoil and seabed of the outer Continental Shelf, and artificial islands and fixed structures erected thereon,” and explicitly noted that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. The Court then added, in a footnote: “Only one provision of OCSLA superimposes a status requirement on the otherwise determinative OCSLA situs requirement; § 1333(b) makes compensation . . . payable under the LHWCA.” Id. at 219 n.2.

This footnote is not mere dicta. It is a *construing* statement by the Supreme Court, made in the context of interpreting OCSLA’s geographic scope. The Court recognized that while most of OCSLA is governed by *situs*, § 1333(b) adds a *status* requirement—namely, that the injured person be an “employee” engaged in operations on the Shelf. But the Court did not say that the injury could occur *anywhere*. It said that *situs* is the primary determinant of coverage, and that § 1333(b) is the *only* provision that adds a status element. This confirms that *situs* remains the central criterion for coverage under § 1333(b). If Congress had intended to eliminate the situs requirement, it would have said so. It did not.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc.*, 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), is misplaced. *Curtis* involved a car accident on land, en route to a helicopter bound for an offshore platform. The Third Circuit held that the injury was “as the result of” operations on the Shelf because the employee was traveling to perform work on the Shelf. But this reasoning ignores the statutory language: the injury must occur *as the result of* operations on the Shelf, not *in preparation for* them. The injury in *Curtis* occurred *before* the employee reached the Shelf, and the court’s “but-for” test effectively erased the geographic boundary. This approach is inconsistent with *Tallentire* and with the principle that statutory interpretation must respect the limits Congress has drawn.

### II. The Fifth Circuit’s “Situs-of-Injury” Test Is the Correct Interpretation

The Fifth Circuit’s decision in *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), correctly interprets § 1333(b). The court held that an injury must occur *on* the outer Continental Shelf—either on a platform, on the seabed, or on the waters above the Shelf—to qualify for OCSLA benefits. This is not a rigid rule; it is a principled application of the statute’s text and purpose. The outer Continental Shelf is a distinct legal and geographic zone, defined by 43 U.S.C. § 1301(a)(2) as “all submerged lands lying seaward and outside of the area of lands beneath navigable waters.” The boundary is not arbitrary—it reflects Congress’s intent to extend federal jurisdiction to a specific maritime zone, not to all activities related to offshore operations.

The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, by contrast, would allow OCSLA coverage for injuries occurring anywhere in the world, so long as the employee was working for a company engaged in offshore operations. This would eviscerate the geographic limits of OCSLA and transform it into a general workers’ compensation statute for offshore contractors. Such a result is inconsistent with the legislative history, which repeatedly emphasizes the *situs* of operations. See S. Rep. No. 95-1270, at 1 (1978) (“The Act applies to the outer Continental Shelf, which is defined as the submerged lands lying seaward of the three-mile limit.”).
