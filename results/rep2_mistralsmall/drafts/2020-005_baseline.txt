**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Montana Supreme Court correctly held that Ford is subject to specific personal jurisdiction in Montana because Gullett’s claims “arise out of or relate to” Ford’s in-state activities.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s forum activities. *See Helicopteros Nacionales de Colombia, S.A. v. Hall*, 466 U.S. 408, 414 (1984). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the “arise out of or relate to” standard is met when there is “a connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” *See Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985); *Keeton v. Hustler Magazine, Inc.*, 465 U.S. 770, 773 (1984). The Montana Supreme Court properly applied this standard in finding jurisdiction over Ford.

Ford’s own conduct in Montana—including its extensive advertising, parts distribution, and service network—provided the very channels through which Gullett purchased and maintained her Ford Explorer. *See Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 443 P.3d 407, 415 (Mont. 2019). This Court has recognized that a defendant’s forum activities need not be the *sole* cause of the plaintiff’s injury; they need only be a *sufficient* cause. *See Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277, 284 (2014). Here, Ford’s Montana-based marketing and parts distribution directly facilitated the sale and upkeep of the vehicle that caused Gullett’s fatal accident. The Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that this connection satisfies the “arise out of or relate to” requirement.

**II. Ford’s assertion that jurisdiction is improper because Gullett’s claims did not arise from its Montana contacts misreads the governing standard.**

Ford argues that jurisdiction is improper because Gullett’s claims did not arise from its Montana contacts. This argument conflates the “arise out of” and “relate to” prongs of the specific jurisdiction test. *See Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 137 S. Ct. 1773, 1780 (2017). The Court has expressly rejected the notion that a plaintiff’s claims must be *directly* caused by the defendant’s forum activities. *See Calder v. Jones*, 465 U.S. 783, 788 (1984) (finding jurisdiction where defendant’s forum-based conduct was “expressly aimed” at the forum, even though the injury occurred elsewhere). The Montana Supreme Court properly relied on this precedent in upholding jurisdiction.

**III. Ford’s jurisdictional challenge fails under the stream-of-commerce theory recognized in *J. McIntyre Mach., Ltd. v. Nicastro*.**

Ford contends that its mere placement of the vehicle into the stream of commerce is insufficient to establish jurisdiction. This argument misapprehends *J. McIntyre*. In *Nicastro*, the plurality held that a defendant’s “purposeful availment” of a forum’s market is required. 564 U.S. at 875 (plurality opinion). Here, Ford’s extensive advertising, parts distribution, and service network in Montana demonstrate its deliberate targeting of the state’s market. *See Ford*, 443 P.3d at 416. The Montana Supreme Court correctly found that Ford’s forum activities satisfied the *Nicastro* standard.

**IV. The exercise of jurisdiction over Ford does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.**

Even if jurisdiction were not otherwise proper, the Montana Supreme Court correctly concluded that the exercise of jurisdiction comports with due process. *See World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 292 (1980). The Court has identified several factors to assess fairness, including the burden on the defendant, the forum’s interest in adjudicating the dispute, and the plaintiff’s interest in obtaining convenient and effective relief. *See id.* at 292. Here, Montana has a strong interest in providing a forum for its residents injured by defective products. Gullett’s estate seeks to litigate in the state where the injury occurred, and Ford’s extensive Montana operations render the burden of litigation minimal. The Montana Supreme Court properly balanced these factors in upholding jurisdiction.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
