# ARGUMENT

## I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING JMOL BECAUSE FACEBOOK FAILED TO PROVE INVALIDITY BY CLEAR AND CONVINCING EVIDENCE

A patent enjoys a statutory presumption of validity, and a party seeking to invalidate it under § 102(b) bears the burden of proving invalidity by clear and convincing evidence. *Microsoft Corp. v. i4i Ltd. Partnership*, 564 U.S. 91, 95 (2011) (holding that 35 U.S.C. § 282's presumption "requires an invalidity defense to be proved by clear and convincing evidence"). This Court has repeatedly emphasized that this is a "heavy burden," particularly where, as here, the alleged invalidating activity is based on ambiguous internal documents and inconsistent trial testimony rather than clear documentary proof. *SRI Int'l, Inc. v. Matsushita Elec. Corp. of Am.*, 775 F.2d 1107, 1125 (Fed. Cir. 1985) (en banc).

Judgment as a matter of law is warranted where the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict, "permits only one reasonable conclusion" contrary to the jury's finding. *Perkin-Elmer Corp. v. Computervision Corp.*, 732 F.2d 888, 893 (Fed. Cir. 1984). Here, Facebook's invalidity theory required the jury to find that Leader's 1999-era Leader2Leader® product embodied every limitation of the asserted claims—including the requirement that metadata be "dynamically associated" with data on a "web-based computing platform" and that the system "track movement of the user" between environments while "dynamically updating" metadata accordingly. '761 patent, col.21 ll.38-58. The trial record, however, demonstrates that the pre-critical-date system lacked these architectural features as a matter of undisputed technical fact, notwithstanding McKibben's isolated and equivocal testimony that the patent covered the "underlying engine." J.A. 25585-86.

## II. THE COURT MUST STRICTLY CONSTRUE THE CLAIM LIMITATIONS BEFORE COMPARING THEM TO THE PRIOR PRODUCT

Invalidity analysis under § 102(b) requires a two-step inquiry: first, the court must construe the claims as a matter of law; second, the fact-finder must compare the properly construed claims to the allegedly invalidating prior art. *Markman v. Westview Instruments, Inc.*, 52 F.3d 967, 976 (Fed. Cir. 1995) (en banc), *aff'd*, 517 U.S. 370 (1996). Every limitation of the claim must be found, either expressly or inherently, in the single prior art reference for anticipation to apply. *Verdegaal Bros., Inc. v. Union Oil Co. of Cal.*, 814 F.2d 628, 631 (Fed. Cir. 1987) ("A claim is anticipated only if each and every element as set forth in the claim is found, either expressly or inherently described, in a single prior art reference."). This "identity of invention" standard is exacting and does not permit a party to invalidate a claim through generalized or aspirational statements that a product "covers" an invention. *Id.*

The district court erred by permitting Facebook to satisfy its burden through inferential leaps from marketing materials—such as the Wright Patterson white paper's boast that Leader had "discovered and fixed a plethora of serious shortcomings and flaws," J.A. 27202—rather than through rigorous claim-by-claim technical proof. Marketing puffery describing a product as "[f]ully developed," J.A. 27204, or "operational," J.A. 27207, is not competent evidence that the specific claimed architecture existed in the actual reduced-to-practice system. This Court has cautioned that promotional statements cannot substitute for technical analysis establishing that each claim limitation was embodied in the prior device. *Scaltech, Inc. v. Retec/Tetra, LLC*, 269 F.3d 1321, 1328 (Fed. Cir. 2001) (requiring that the prior art device actually practice each limitation of the claims, not merely relate generally to the same field).

## III. THE "ON-SALE" AND "PUBLIC USE" BARS REQUIRE THAT THE INVENTION BE READY FOR PATENTING AND EMBODY EVERY CLAIM LIMITATION

Under *Pfaff v. Wells Electronics, Inc.*, 525 U.S. 55, 67-68 (1998), the on-sale bar applies only when, before the critical date, (1) the product was the subject of a commercial offer for sale, and (2) the invention was ready for patenting. The second prong—readiness for patenting—may be satisfied by proof of reduction to practice or by drawings sufficiently specific to enable a person skilled in the art to practice the invention. *Id.* at 67. Critically, "ready for patenting" does not mean merely offered for sale in a preliminary or evolving form; it requires that the specific claimed invention, in its entirety, was complete. *Group One, Ltd. v. Hallmark Cards, Inc.*, 254 F.3d 1041, 1046 (Fed. Cir. 2001).

The evidence at trial showed, at most, that Leader was developing and iterating the Digital Leaderboard® engine during the relevant period, with Lamb testifying only generally that the project was completed in the "2002ish time frame." J.A. 24829. This imprecise timeline, standing alone, cannot establish clear and convincing proof that the specific claimed limitations—dynamic metadata updating tied to user movement between web-based environments—were reduced to practice or embodied in an offered product before the critical date of December 10, 2002. *See TransOcean Offshore Deepwater Drilling, Inc. v. Maersk Drilling USA, Inc.*, 617 F.3d 1296, 1309 (Fed. Cir. 2010) (invalidity proponent must show the offered product practiced every element of the claims).

## CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, the jury's verdict lacked the requisite clear and convincing evidentiary support, and the district court's denial of JMOL should be reversed.
