**Argument**

Section 523(a)(2)(A) excepts from discharge only debts “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by” fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). Congress chose a narrow phrase. The debt must be “for” the money or property actually obtained by fraud. In *Cohen v. de la Cruz*, 523 U.S. 213 (1998), however, this Court held that the exception reaches not only the value of the money or property obtained, but also treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs arising from the same fraudulent conduct. That interpretation is the better reading of the statute and the one most consistent with the Bankruptcy Code’s treatment of fraud debts.

The starting point is the text. In *Grogan v. Garner*, 498 U.S. 279, 287 (1991), the Court explained that § 523(a) embodies a judgment that the “honest but unfortunate debtor” should receive a fresh start, but not one who has committed fraud. Section 523(a)(2)(A) reflects that balance by excepting debts obtained by “false pretenses, a false representation, or actual fraud.” The statutory focus is on the debt’s origin: if the debt arises from fraud, it is not discharged. As the Court observed in *Cohen*, “any debt” for money obtained by fraud is excepted from discharge. 523 U.S. at 218. That breadth necessarily includes liability fixed by law as a consequence of the fraudulent acquisition itself.

The petitioners’ view—that only the amount originally transferred by the fraud is nondischargeable—cannot be squared with the statute’s words or structure. Congress did not say “the amount actually obtained” or “the amount equivalent to the value received.” It said “any debt” for money obtained by fraud. 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). Once a debtor’s fraudulent conduct gives rise to a legal obligation, that obligation is part of the fraud debt. The Court in *Cohen* relied on the ordinary meaning of “debt,” which includes “liability on a claim.” 523 U.S. at 220; see 11 U.S.C. § 101(12), (5). Treble damages and fee awards are liabilities on claims arising from fraud, and therefore fall within the exception.

This reading is reinforced by the Court’s treatment of other fraud exceptions. In *Field v. Mans*, 516 U.S. 59, 69-70 (1995), the Court held that § 523(a)(2)(A) incorporates common-law elements of fraud, signaling that Congress legislated against an established backdrop in which fraud liability includes consequential remedies. Likewise, in *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 287-88, the Court recognized that discharge exceptions prevent a debtor from using bankruptcy to escape responsibility for wrongful conduct. If a fraud debtor could discharge statutory multipliers and fee awards, the debtor would retain the benefit of fraud while forcing victims to absorb the full remedial scheme chosen by Congress and the States.

The contrary rule would produce arbitrary and irrational results. A debtor who commits fraud under a statute providing only compensatory damages would be nondischargeable as to the entire award, while a debtor who commits the same fraud under a statute providing treble damages or attorney’s fees would discharge the portion above the compensatory amount. Nothing in § 523(a)(2)(A) suggests Congress intended such disparity. In *Cohen*, the Court correctly rejected that distinction, explaining that the “debt” is the entire liability arising from the fraud, not merely the initial transfer. 523 U.S. at 223.

Nor does the Bankruptcy Code’s fresh-start policy help petitioners. *Local Loan Co. v. Hunt*, 292 U.S. 234, 244 (1934), describes bankruptcy as intended to relieve the “honest but unfortunate debtor.” But this Court has repeatedly limited that policy where Congress has specified exceptions. *Grogan*, 498 U.S. at 286-87. Fraud is one of those exceptions. Allowing discharge of enhanced damages and fee awards would undermine deterrence and dilute the remedial effect of fraud statutes. See *Cohen*, 523 U.S. at 222-23.

This case is straightforward under *Cohen*. Cohen’s fraudulent rent overcharges created liability under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act. The treble damages, attorney’s fees, and costs all arose from that same fraud and were therefore part of the nondischargeable debt. 523 U.S. at 223. The judgment below should be affirmed.
