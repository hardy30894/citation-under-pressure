ARGUMENT

The settlement agreement created enforceable contractual obligations that Wells Fargo was bound to perform before foreclosure. Under settled principles, a promise made for the benefit of a third party may be enforced by that party. Lawrence v. Fox, 20 N.Y. 268, 274 (1859) (“the person for whose benefit the promise was made may maintain an action upon it”). Foley, as a member of Settlement Class B, was entitled to the sequential consideration for modification spelled out in the agreement. Breach of that duty states a claim. See also Seaver v. Ransom, 224 N.Y. 233, 238 (1918) (enforcing third-party rights arising from contract).

The district court further erred in holding that federal law preempts Foley’s state-law claims. Federal authority does not displace state remedies absent a clear expression of congressional intent to occupy the field. McCulloch v. Maryland, 17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316, 426 (1819) (states retain “the power of taxing the people and their property” except where the Constitution withholds it). Nothing in the record demonstrates such displacement of Massachusetts contract or foreclosure-related statutes.

Finally, the complaint adequately alleges that Wells Fargo’s servicing failures violated duties imposed by the agreement and by statute. A party may not invoke its own nonperformance to defeat another’s rights. See United States v. Peck, 102 U.S. 64, 65 (1880) (“he who prevents a thing from being done shall not avail himself of the nonperformance”). The judgment of dismissal should be reversed.
