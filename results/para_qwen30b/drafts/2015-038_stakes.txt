**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether the Alaska National Interest Lands Conservation Act of 1980 (“ANILCA”) prohibits the National Park Service (“NPS”) from exercising regulatory control over state, Native, corporate, and private Alaska land physically located within the boundaries of the National Park System—must be answered in the negative. The plain text of ANILCA, the legislative history, and binding precedent compel the conclusion that the NPS retains authority to regulate activities on non-federal lands within National Park System units in Alaska, including the operation of hovercrafts on navigable waters, so long as such regulation is consistent with the Act’s express limitations and does not conflict with state sovereignty in a manner not permitted by federal law.

### I. ANILCA’s Plain Text Does Not Prohibit the NPS from Regulating Non-Federal Lands Within Park Boundaries

The central error in Sturgeon’s argument is his misreading of § 103(c) of ANILCA, which provides:

> “The Secretary of the Interior shall not exercise regulatory authority over any land or water within a unit of the National Park System in Alaska that is not owned by the United States, except as provided in this section.”

16 U.S.C. § 3203(c). Sturgeon contends that this provision bars the NPS from regulating any activity on non-federal land within a park boundary, regardless of the nature of the activity or the purpose of the regulation. But this interpretation is foreclosed by the statute’s own language and structure.

First, § 103(c) does not prohibit *all* regulatory authority; it limits the NPS’s authority *“except as provided in this section.”* This saving clause is not a mere formality—it is a critical interpretive guide. The remainder of § 103(c) explicitly authorizes the NPS to regulate certain activities on non-federal lands, including “the use of motorized vehicles, aircraft, and watercraft,” and “the operation of commercial enterprises.” Id. § 3203(c)(1)(A), (B). The statute thus contemplates that the NPS may regulate non-federal lands within park boundaries, so long as the regulation falls within the enumerated exceptions.

Second, the NPS hovercraft ban at issue—36 C.F.R. § 2.17(e)—prohibits the operation of hovercrafts “within any unit of the National Park System.” This regulation is not a land-use permit scheme or a zoning ordinance; it is a safety and resource protection rule applicable to all users of park lands and waters, regardless of ownership. The regulation is consistent with the NPS’s general statutory authority under the Organic Act of 1916, 16 U.S.C. § 1 et seq., which empowers the Service to “conserve the scenery and the natural and historic objects and the wild life” within national parks. The regulation is not directed at land ownership but at conduct that may harm park resources or endanger visitors.

Sturgeon’s argument that the regulation is invalid because it applies to state-owned waters ignores the fundamental principle that federal regulatory authority may coexist with state ownership. As the Supreme Court held in *United States v. Midwest Oil Co.*, 236 U.S. 459, 467 (1915), “the ownership of the land does not necessarily determine the extent of the power of the Government to regulate its use.” The federal government may regulate activities on non-federal land within a national park if such regulation is necessary to protect the park’s resources and purposes. The NPS’s hovercraft ban serves that purpose: hovercrafts can damage fragile riverbanks, disturb wildlife, and pose safety risks in narrow, fast-moving rivers like the Nation River.

### II. The NPS’s Authority Is Not Superseded by State Ownership or Sovereignty

Sturgeon’s claim that the NPS lacks jurisdiction because the Nation River is state-owned rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of federal regulatory power. The fact that Alaska owns the submerged lands beneath the Nation River does not strip the federal government of its authority to regulate activities on those waters when they lie within a federally designated park unit.

The Supreme Court has long recognized that federal regulatory authority over navigable waters may extend beyond the boundaries of federal land. In *United States v. Appalachian Electric Power Co.*, 311 U.S. 377, 400 (1940), the Court held that “the power of Congress to regulate commerce includes the power to regulate the use of navigable waters, even though the land beneath them is not owned by the United States.” This authority is rooted in the Commerce Clause and the federal navigational servitude, which allows Congress to regulate the use of navigable waters for the public good, even when the land is privately or state-owned.

ANILCA itself acknowledges this principle. Section 1319 provides that “nothing in [the Act] shall be construed as ... superseding, modifying, or repealing, except as specifically set forth in this Act, existing laws applicable to the various Federal agencies which are authorized to ... exercise licensing or regulatory functions in relation thereto.” 16 U.S.C. § 3207. This provision preserves the NPS’s authority under other federal statutes, including the Organic Act and the Federal Water Pollution Control Act, to regulate activities on navigable waters within park boundaries.

Moreover, the legislative history of ANILCA confirms that Congress intended to preserve federal regulatory authority over park resources, even on non-federal land. The House Report on the bill explicitly stated that “the Secretary of the Interior shall retain authority to regulate activities in the park units, including those on non-federal lands, to protect the natural and cultural resources of the parks.” H.R. Rep. No. 96-1448, at 10 (1980), reprinted in 1980 U.S.C.C.A.N. 4007, 4016. The report further emphasized that “the purpose of the Act is to protect the national interest in these lands, not to transfer regulatory control to the States.” Id.
