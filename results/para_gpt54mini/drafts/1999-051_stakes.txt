**ARGUMENT**

A state prisoner may not use a procedurally defaulted ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim as “cause” to excuse the procedural default of another federal habeas claim unless the ineffective-assistance claim itself was properly exhausted and preserved in state court. This Court’s cases make that conclusion inescapable.

In *Murray v. Carrier*, 477 U.S. 478 (1986), the Court held that attorney error rising to constitutionally ineffective assistance can constitute cause for a procedural default. But the Court also made clear that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause for a procedural default.” *Id.* at 488-89. That rule follows from the fundamental premise that “the state courts should have the first opportunity to review this claim and provide any necessary relief.” *Id.* at 489. *Carrier* therefore forecloses petitioner’s attempt to bootstrap an unexhausted, defaulted ineffectiveness claim into cause for another defaulted claim.

The Court reaffirmed that principle in *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446 (2000). There, the Court squarely held that “an ineffective-assistance-of-counsel claim asserted as cause for the procedural default of another claim can itself be procedurally defaulted,” and that if it is, the petitioner must satisfy the ordinary cause-and-prejudice standard for that default as well. *Id.* at 452-53. The Court reasoned that “[t]he procedural default doctrine and its attendant ‘cause and prejudice’ standard are ‘grounded in concerns of comity and federalism,’” *id.* at 451 (quoting *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722, 730 (1991)), and that allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to excuse another default “would allow the exception to swallow the rule.” *Edwards*, 529 U.S. at 453. In other words, a habeas court cannot rely on a procedurally barred ineffectiveness claim unless that claim itself is not barred, or the petitioner separately shows cause and prejudice for the ineffectiveness default. *Id.* at 451-53.

That result is also compelled by *Coleman v. Thompson*, 501 U.S. 722 (1991). *Coleman* held that where a state prisoner has defaulted federal claims in state court under an independent and adequate state procedural rule, federal habeas review is barred unless the prisoner demonstrates cause and actual prejudice or a fundamental miscarriage of justice. *Id.* at 750. The Court explained that there is no constitutional right to counsel in state postconviction proceedings, so attorney error in those proceedings ordinarily cannot constitute cause. *Id.* at 752-54. That principle matters here because the cause inquiry cannot be detached from the procedural posture of the predicate ineffectiveness claim. If that claim was itself defaulted in state court, federal review is barred unless the petitioner independently satisfies *Coleman’s* cause-and-prejudice requirement. *Edwards*, 529 U.S. at 452-53.

Petitioner’s contrary rule would also undermine *Wainwright v. Sykes*, 433 U.S. 72 (1977), which rejected federal habeas review of claims bypassed in state court absent cause and prejudice. *Id.* at 87. The Court later emphasized in *Engle v. Isaac*, 456 U.S. 107 (1982), that the default doctrine is designed to require prisoners to present their claims first to the state courts, thereby respecting state procedural rules and conserving federal judicial resources. *Id.* at 129. Allowing a defaulted ineffectiveness claim to serve as cause without itself being properly preserved would permit the petitioner to evade those state procedures and obtain federal merits review by indirection.

Nor does *Murray v. Carrier* support petitioner. To be sure, *Carrier* recognized that “the exhaustion doctrine . . . generally requires that a claim of ineffective assistance be presented to the state courts as an independent claim before it may be used to establish cause.” 477 U.S. at 489. Petitioner seeks to invert that rule by using the barred ineffectiveness claim first and asking whether it can revive a different defaulted claim. *Carrier* and *Edwards* reject that sequence. The defaulted ineffectiveness claim cannot do work it was never properly presented to do.

The Sixth Circuit’s rule below is therefore wrong. The court held that a procedurally defaulted ineffectiveness claim could serve as cause “regardless of whether” that claim had itself been defaulted. That holding cannot be reconciled with *Edwards v. Carpenter*, 529 U.S. 446 (2000), which expressly requires that the ineffective-assistance claim be free of default, or else independently excused. *Id.* at 452-53. Because petitioner did not properly preserve the ineffectiveness claim in state court, and because he has shown neither separate cause and prejudice nor actual innocence, federal habeas review of the underlying claim remains barred. *Coleman*, 501 U.S. at 750; *Schlup v. Delo*, 513 U.S. 298, 314-15 (1995).

For those reasons, the judgment below should be reversed.
