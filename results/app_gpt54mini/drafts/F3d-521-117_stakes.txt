**Argument**

The district court erred in denying suppression because the frisk of Soares was unsupported by the individualized reasonable suspicion that the Fourth Amendment requires. Although police may order occupants out of a lawfully stopped car, *Pennsylvania v. Mimms*, 434 U.S. 106, 111 n.6 (1977), and may frisk a person only when they can “point to specific and articulable facts” creating a reasonable belief that the person is armed and dangerous, *Terry v. Ohio*, 392 U.S. 1, 21, 27 (1968), the record here does not satisfy that standard as to Soares.

A frisk is not justified by generalized caution or by a hunch. The officer must have “a reasonable belief that his safety or that of others was in danger,” based on the “specific reasonable inferences which he is entitled to draw from the facts in light of his experience.” *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27. This Court has likewise held that a pat-frisk requires “reasonable suspicion” supported by articulable facts, and the inquiry is objective, not a post hoc ratification of officer unease. *United States v. Tiru-Plaza*, 766 F.3d 111, 116-17 (1st Cir. 2014); *United States v. Ruidíaz*, 529 F.3d 25, 28-29 (1st Cir. 2008). On this record, the officers lacked such suspicion particularized to Soares.

The Government will rely on the late hour, the high-crime location, and the occupants’ movement in the car. Those facts, even combined, do not themselves establish that Soares was armed. The Supreme Court has cautioned that presence in a high-crime area is relevant but not enough by itself. *Illinois v. Wardlow*, 528 U.S. 119, 124 (2000). This Court has repeatedly recognized that nervousness, agitation, and equivocal movement during a traffic stop are common human reactions and do not alone create reasonable suspicion of danger. *United States v. McKoy*, 428 F.3d 38, 40-42 (1st Cir. 2005); *United States v. Guerrero*, 667 F.3d 130, 136-37 (1st Cir. 2012). Here, the officers observed movements before the stop ended, but nothing linked those movements to a weapon, and no officer testified to seeing a bulge, weapon-like outline, or any specific act suggesting Soares was reaching for a firearm. That is precisely the sort of gap *Terry* forbids the Government from filling with speculation. *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21-22.

Nor did Soares’s verbal protest and agitation supply the missing justification. The Fourth Amendment does not permit a frisk simply because a suspect is verbally abusive, argumentative, or disrespectful. *United States v. Hensley*, 469 U.S. 221, 235 (1985); *United States v. Jones*, 187 F.3d 210, 218 (1st Cir. 1999). Soares’s objection that he was being stopped for “driving while Black,” while harshly expressed, was speech; it was not evidence that he was armed. The Constitution does not convert protected or merely obnoxious expression into reasonable suspicion.

The court below also erred in relying on the officers’ subjective unease. Officer Reid testified that he “got a little uneasy” and asked backup to remove Soares because he “felt really uneasy about ... the way [Soares] was behaving.” But the Supreme Court has made clear that the question is whether the facts, viewed objectively, support reasonable suspicion, not whether an officer felt uneasy in the moment. *Whren v. United States*, 517 U.S. 806, 813 (1996); *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 21-22. This Court has similarly held that “inchoate and unparticularized suspicion or ‘hunch’” cannot justify a frisk. *United States v. Brown*, 500 F.3d 48, 54 (1st Cir. 2007) (quoting *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27). The record here shows exactly that: a hunch elevated into a search.

The Government may point to the fact that Lee, the driver, consented to a frisk and that additional officers had arrived. But reasonable suspicion to frisk one passenger cannot be bootstrapped from another person’s consent or from the mere presence of police backup. *Ybarra v. Illinois*, 444 U.S. 85, 91 (1979), is directly controlling: a search of one person cannot be justified by the search of others absent individualized suspicion. Soares’s rights were personal, and the police needed specific facts about Soares himself. *Ybarra*, 444 U.S. at 91. They did not have them.

This case is also unlike *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032, 1049-50 (1983), which allows a protective search of a vehicle when officers reasonably believe an occupant is dangerous and may gain immediate control of weapons. Here the police did not search the car for a weapon; they frisked Soares’s person after removing him. That required a separate and individualized basis under *Terry* and *Mimms*. *Mimms*, 434 U.S. at 111 n.6; *Terry*, 392 U.S. at 27.

Because the frisk violated the Fourth Amendment, the gun should have been suppressed as the fruit of the unlawful search. *Mapp v. Ohio*, 367 U.S. 643, 655 (1961); *Wong Sun v. United States*, 371 U.S. 471, 484-85 (1963). Soares’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) rests on evidence that should never have been admitted. The judgment should be reversed.
