**Argument**

I. **Milford Did Not Violate the Good News Club’s Free Speech Rights**

Milford’s community-use policy created, at most, a limited public forum. When the government opens such a forum, it may restrict access only if its rules are reasonable and viewpoint neutral. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 46 (1983); *Lamb’s Chapel v. Ctr. Moriches Union Free Sch. Dist.*, 508 U.S. 384, 390 (1993). Milford’s exclusion of the Club was reasonable because the policy barred use “for religious purposes,” and the Club sought to use the school for meetings centered on prayer, Bible instruction, and evangelistic invitation. Those activities fit the policy’s prohibition. They were not denied because Milford disfavored Christianity, but because Milford concluded the proposed use was religious worship and instruction. The Court has recognized that a school may enforce access rules tied to the subject matter of the forum. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 49; *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985).

The Second Circuit erred by treating the Club’s exclusion as permissible subject-matter discrimination rather than viewpoint discrimination. This Court’s precedents make clear that the Free Speech Clause does not allow a public school to exclude speech from an open forum merely because the school dislikes the perspective from which a permitted subject is addressed. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, a school district violated the First Amendment when it excluded a church’s film series on child rearing and family values because the series was presented “from a religious perspective.” 508 U.S. at 393-94. The Court rejected the district’s effort to characterize the exclusion as a content-based restriction, holding that the policy “permit[ted] school property to be used for the presentation of all views about family issues and child rearing except those dealing with the subject matter from a religious viewpoint.” *Id.* at 393. Likewise, in *Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of the Univ. of Va.*, 515 U.S. 819, 831 (1995), the Court held that when the government permits discussion of a subject, it may not “discriminate[] against speech because of its message.” The same principle applies here.

Milford opened its facilities to civic, social, recreational, and welfare-related uses. Having done so, it could not deny the Club access simply because the Club wished to discuss morals and character from a Christian standpoint. The Court has repeatedly rejected attempts to carve religious viewpoints out of otherwise available forums. *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 393-94; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 831-32. The Club’s meetings, as described in the record, involved a moral lesson, songs, games, and a Bible story. But the fact that religious expression may accompany speech does not transform viewpoint discrimination into permissible subject-matter discrimination. See *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (“singing hymns, reading scripture, and teaching biblical principles” remain forms of speech). Milford therefore violated the Free Speech Clause.

II. **The Establishment Clause Did Not Justify Excluding the Club**

Milford’s principal defense is that allowing the Club to meet would violate the Establishment Clause. That contention fails under *Widmar*, *Lamb’s Chapel*, and *Rosenberger*. In *Widmar*, the Court held that a university’s open-forum policy did not violate the Establishment Clause when it permitted a student religious group to use facilities available to other student groups. 454 U.S. at 270-75. The Court explained that equal access, accompanied by the university’s neutral administration, did not have the effect of advancing religion in a constitutionally forbidden way. *Id.* at 274-75. In *Lamb’s Chapel*, the Court rejected the same Establishment Clause defense advanced by a school district, concluding that permitting a church to show films on school property after hours would not be perceived as school endorsement. 508 U.S. at 395.

*Rosenberger* confirms that the Constitution does not require the government to suppress religious viewpoints in order to avoid an Establishment Clause concern. The Court held that equal access for a religious publication in a student-activity funding program did not constitute endorsement of religion. 515 U.S. at 845-46. The Establishment Clause is not offended when the government treats religious speech on the same terms as comparable secular speech in a neutral forum. *Id.* at 844-46; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395.

This case is even easier for the Club than *Widmar* and *Lamb’s Chapel*. Milford’s policy already opened school facilities to outside groups after hours. Any risk of endorsement would be minimized by the school’s neutral access policy, the after-hours setting, and the fact that the Club would use the premises as one of many permitted community groups. The Establishment Clause does not require a school district to discriminate against religious speech in order to avoid the mistaken impression that it is sponsoring that speech. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 274; *Lamb’s Chapel*, 508 U.S. at 395; *Rosenberger*, 515 U.S. at 841-46.

III. **The Judgment of the Court of Appeals Should Be Reversed**

Under this Court’s cases, Milford excluded the Club because of the religious viewpoint from which it sought to address morals and character. That is unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. And because equal access would not have violated the Establishment Clause, Milford’s asserted justification fails. The judgment below should be reversed.
