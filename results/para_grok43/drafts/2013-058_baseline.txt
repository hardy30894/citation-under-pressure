ARGUMENT

Section 522(b)(3)(C) and (d)(12) exempts only “retirement funds” held in specified tax-deferred accounts. The phrase is undefined, yet its ordinary meaning requires that the funds be “set aside for retirement.” An inherited IRA fails this test because its mandatory distributions must begin within one year of the original owner’s death and cannot be deferred until the beneficiary’s own retirement. 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C).

Statutory exemptions are construed according to their text, not their supposed purpose. “No legislation pursues its purposes at all costs.” Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525–26 (1987). The Code’s other exemptions expressly tie protection to the debtor’s use or ownership at filing. See, e.g., § 522(b)(3)(A) (homestead occupied by debtor for 730 days). Section 522(b)(3)(C) contains no such temporal limit only because new contributions by the debtor remain exempt; it does not preserve the character of funds that ceased to be anyone’s retirement savings upon the original owner’s death.

The Fifth Circuit’s contrary view—that funds retain exempt status if they were ever anyone’s retirement funds, In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486 (5th Cir. 2012)—would convert a time-limited tax deferral into a permanent creditor shield. That result finds no support in the statute and would allow debtors to shelter assets usable for current consumption, contrary to the limited purpose of the retirement-fund exemption.

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
