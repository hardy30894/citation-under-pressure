**ARGUMENT**

**I. The district court plainly erred in admitting Agent Jones’s expert testimony, which invaded the jury’s exclusive province to determine credibility and lacked any reliable scientific basis.**

Because Mr. Hill did not contemporaneously object, this Court reviews for plain error. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Plain error requires (1) error, (2) that is plain, (3) that affects substantial rights, and (4) that seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* at 732–37. All four prongs are satisfied here.

**A. The district court erred by admitting Agent Jones’s testimony under Rule 702 and Daubert.**

Rule 702 requires that expert testimony be “the product of reliable principles and methods” and that the witness “reliably appl[y]” those principles to the facts. Fed. R. Evid. 702. The district court has a “gatekeeping” obligation to ensure that expert testimony is both relevant and reliable. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharms., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 589 (1993). Agent Jones’s testimony failed that standard.

Jones claimed training in “special tactics and ways to identify deception,” but the government offered no evidence that his methods were scientifically valid, testable, or generally accepted. Courts have repeatedly rejected such “deception detection” testimony as unreliable. *See United States v. Adams*, 271 F.3d 1236, 1245 (10th Cir. 2001) (holding that expert testimony on credibility is inadmissible because it is not “scientific, technical, or other specialized knowledge” that will assist the jury). Jones’s opinions—that Mr. Hill’s answers “did not make sense,” that his behavior was “common among the criminal element,” and that invoking faith is a sign of deception—were nothing more than subjective impressions dressed in an FBI agent’s credentials. They were not derived from reliable methodology, and the district court plainly erred in admitting them.

**B. The testimony impermissibly opined on Mr. Hill’s credibility and criminal propensity, usurping the jury’s role.**

Expert testimony that directly comments on a defendant’s credibility is inadmissible because it invades the jury’s exclusive province to assess witness truthfulness. *Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1245. Agent Jones did precisely that. He testified that “many of [Mr. Hill’s] answers were not worthy of credence,” that he had “never” seen an innocent person make the statements Mr. Hill made, and that Mr. Hill’s behavior was “common among the criminal element.” These statements were not general observations; they were direct opinions that Mr. Hill was lying and that he was a criminal. Such testimony “does not aid the jury in making a decision; rather, it tells the jury what result to reach.” *United States v. Rodriguez*, 581 F.3d 775, 784 (9th Cir. 2009) (quoting *United States v. Lockett*, 919 F.2d 585, 590 (9th Cir. 1990)). The Tenth Circuit has consistently condemned this type of evidence. *See Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1245; *United States v. Charley*, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267 (10th Cir. 1999) (expert may not “vouch” for a witness’s credibility).

The error is plain. The law in this circuit was well-settled at the time of trial: expert testimony on a defendant’s credibility is inadmissible. *Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1245. The district court’s failure to exclude the testimony sua sponte was an obvious deviation from that rule.

**C. The error affected Mr. Hill’s substantial rights and the fairness of the proceedings.**

To establish that substantial rights were affected, Mr. Hill must show a reasonable probability that the error influenced the jury’s verdict. *United States v. Benally*, 541 F.3d 990, 998 (10th Cir. 2008). That standard is met here.

The government’s case against Mr. Hill was circumstantial. He was not identified as one of the masked men who entered the bank. The government’s theory was that he served as the getaway driver, but no witness placed him in the vehicle or at the scene of the robbery. The GPS tracking device led officers to the East Pine residence, where Mr. Hill was found hours later, but he was not seen leaving or entering the home during the relevant period. The first trial ended in a mistrial on the charges against Mr. Hill, demonstrating that the evidence was not overwhelming.

Agent Jones’s testimony was the centerpiece of the government’s effort to transform Mr. Hill’s post-arrest behavior—including his initial use of a false name and his emotional statements—into proof of guilt. Jones told the jury that Mr. Hill’s answers were “not worthy of credence,” that his behavior was “common among the criminal element,” and that his invocation of God was itself a marker of deception. This was not merely cumulative; it was a direct attack on Mr. Hill’s credibility from a purported expert, and it likely tipped the scales. *See Rodriguez*, 581 F.3d at 784 (expert testimony on deception “is highly prejudicial” because it carries an “aura of scientific reliability”).

Finally, the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, and public reputation of the judicial proceedings. Allowing a government expert to tell a jury that a defendant is lying and that his behavior is typical of criminals undermines the fundamental presumption of innocence and the jury’s role as the sole arbiter of credibility. *See Adams*, 271 F.3d at 1245. This is precisely the kind of error that warrants correction on appeal.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Mr. Hill respectfully requests that this Court reverse his conviction and remand for a new trial.
