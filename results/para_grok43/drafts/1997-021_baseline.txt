**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board's "good-faith reasonable doubt" standard for employer polling is irrational and inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

An incumbent union enjoys a rebuttable presumption of majority support. *Fall River Dyeing & Finishing Corp. v. NLRB*, 482 U.S. 27, 37-41 (1987). An employer may overcome that presumption with "objective indications sufficient to raise a reasonable doubt about the union's majority status." *NLRB v. Curtin Matheson Scientific, Inc.*, 494 U.S. 775, 778 (1990). The Board permits polling only when the employer already possesses evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition. This equivalence renders polling "marginally useful" at best and "potentially disruptive and unsettling." *Texas Petrochemicals Corp.*, 296 N.L.R.B. 1057, 1061-62 (1989).

The Fifth, Sixth, and Ninth Circuits correctly rejected this approach. They held that an employer may poll if it possesses "substantial, objective evidence of a loss of union support," even if that evidence would not justify withdrawal. *A.W. Thompson, Inc. v. NLRB*, 651 F.2d 1141, 1145 (5th Cir. 1981); *Thomas Indus., Inc. v. NLRB*, 687 F.2d 863, 867 (6th Cir. 1982); *Mingtree Restaurant, Inc. v. NLRB*, 736 F.2d 1295, 1299 (9th Cir. 1984). The Board's insistence on identical standards for polling and withdrawal ignores that polling supplies "more certain, precise information" before an employer acts. *Texas Petrochemicals*, 296 N.L.R.B. at 1063. Because the Act nowhere mandates the Board's restrictive rule, and because the Board has failed to explain why a less disruptive, more accurate mechanism should be available only when it is already superfluous, the standard is arbitrary. *Beth Israel Hosp. v. NLRB*, 437 U.S. 483, 500-01 (1978).

**II. The Board's finding that Allentown Mack lacked objective reasonable doubt is not supported by substantial evidence.**

On January 25, 1991, Allentown Mack possessed statements from seven unit employees repudiating the union, plus uncontradicted reports that the entire night shift and additional employees opposed continued representation. The Board refused to credit statements by Ridgick and Marsh solely because they were made during job interviews in which the company indicated it would be non-union. Board precedent treating such statements as inherently insincere does not constitute substantial evidence when the record contains no affirmative proof of coercion. *Middleboro Fire Apparatus, Inc.*, 234 N.L.R.B. 888, 894 (1978).

The Board likewise discounted Bloch's and Mohr's reports of unit-wide opposition because the speakers did not testify. Yet the Act imposes no requirement that an employer obtain sworn testimony before forming a reasonable doubt; it requires only objective indications. *Curtin Matheson*, 494 U.S. at 778. The ALJ's and Board's refusal to consider these statements left the company with no practical means of testing majority support short of an unfair-labor-practice finding. That result is unsupported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951).

The petition for review should be granted and the bargaining order set aside.
