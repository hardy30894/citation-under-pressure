**ARGUMENT**

**I. Bond Lacks Standing to Challenge Her Conviction on Tenth Amendment Grounds**

Article III limits federal judicial power to litigants who can show that their own legally protected interests, not those of some absent sovereign, have been invaded. The Tenth Amendment protects the States' retained sovereignty; it confers no personal right on private citizens that they may assert to escape a validly imposed federal criminal penalty.

This Court settled the point directly in *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, private utilities argued that a federal agency's competition with them violated the Tenth Amendment. The Court held that the private litigants, "absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. The logic is straightforward: because the Tenth Amendment "is not intended to be a limitation upon the power delegated to the Federal Government," *id.* at 143, but rather a guarantee running to the States as sovereign entities, only the States—or someone asserting an interest derivative of the States' own—may invoke it.

The same principle undergirds this Court's broader standing jurisprudence. In *Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), the Court refused to entertain a constitutional challenge because the plaintiff had not shown "that he has sustained or is immediately in danger of sustaining some direct injury as the result of [the statute's] enforcement, and not merely that he suffers in some indefinite way in common with people generally." *Id.* at 488. Bond's asserted injury—prosecution under a statute she says intrudes on a domain reserved to the States—is, at bottom, an institutional grievance belonging to the States themselves, not a personal deprivation of a right that the Tenth Amendment assigns to her.

*Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes*, 302 U.S. 464 (1938), confirms that a litigant may not premise standing on the theory that government action exceeds constitutional bounds unless the litigant can point to a legal right of her own that the action invades; competitive or generalized injury does not suffice. Likewise, *Coleman v. Miller*, 307 U.S. 433 (1939), recognized that certain structural constitutional claims belong only to those possessing the sovereign or official interest the Constitution protects—there, state legislators asserting the State's own prerogative—not to private parties asserting the same structural principle for their own benefit.

Finally, *United Public Workers v. Mitchell*, 330 U.S. 75 (1947), reaffirmed that federal courts exercise the power to adjudicate constitutional questions only when the litigant's own interests require that protection; the judicial power is not a vehicle for vindicating interests that belong to another sovereign. Bond's position as a criminal defendant does not transform the States' Tenth Amendment interest into her own. If the States believe that § 229 impermissibly displaces their police power, they may say so—through their Attorneys General, through amicus participation, or through the "local political processes" available to sovereign States. Bond, a private citizen, may not commandeer that argument to void an otherwise applicable federal criminal statute.

**II. In Any Event, § 229 Is a Valid Exercise of Congress's Power Under the Necessary and Proper Clause to Implement the Treaty Power, and the Tenth Amendment Supplies No Independent Bar**

Even were standing no obstacle, Bond's substantive claim fails. "Under our federal system[,] the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of [its] delegated powers, has created offenses against the United States." *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91, 109 (1945). Section 229 falls squarely within delegated power because it implements a valid treaty—the Chemical Weapons Convention—through legislation authorized by the Necessary and Proper Clause.

This Court's decision in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), controls. There Justice Holmes explained that "there may be matters of the sharpest exigency for the national well being that an act of Congress could not deal with but that a treaty followed by such an act could." *Id.* at 433. Once a treaty is validly made under Article II, implementing legislation enacted pursuant to it draws its authority from Article I, § 8's grant of power to Congress to make laws "necessary and proper for carrying into Execution" the treaty power. The absence of an independent Commerce Clause hook, or of a jurisdictional element tying § 229 to interstate commerce, is therefore immaterial; the statute's authority flows from the Treaty Power and the Necessary and Proper Clause working together, exactly as in *Holland*.

Nor does the Tenth Amendment cut against this conclusion. As this Court held in *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100 (1941), the Tenth Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *Id.* at 124. Because the People, through Article II, surrendered to the National Government the power to make treaties, and through Article I the power to enact laws necessary to execute that power, no reserved state power remains to be violated when Congress legislates within that domain. The Chemical Weapons Convention required the United States to "prohibit natural and legal persons anywhere on its territory" from producing, possessing, or using chemical weapons, 32 I.L.M. 800, 810 (1993)—precisely what § 229 does. That the statute reaches conduct also punishable under state law, such as Bond's poisoning of Ms. Haynes, does not diminish Congress's delegated authority to punish it as well.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
