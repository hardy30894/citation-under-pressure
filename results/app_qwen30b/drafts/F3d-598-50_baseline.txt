**ARGUMENT**

The District Court’s erroneous admission of highly prejudicial and legally impermissible testimony violated Appellants’ constitutional right to a fair trial under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. The jury was permitted to hear from three government officials—two Assistant District Attorneys (“ADAs”) and a police lieutenant—whose testimony directly vouched for the credibility of Officers Ramos and Rivera, offered legal conclusions about probable cause, and interpreted evidence that the jury was fully capable of assessing on its own. These errors were not harmless, as they injected the government’s imprimatur onto the two most contested issues in the case: the officers’ credibility and the existence of probable cause. Under well-settled principles of evidence law and constitutional due process, such testimony is inadmissible and irredeemably prejudicial. The judgment must therefore be reversed and a new trial ordered.

**I. The District Court Erred in Admitting Government Witnesses’ Testimony That Vouched for the Officers’ Credibility and Offered Legal Conclusions**

The admission of testimony from ADAs and a police lieutenant that directly assessed the credibility of the arresting officers and the legal sufficiency of probable cause violated fundamental rules of evidence and constitutional due process. *See United States v. Hines, 558 F.3d 142, 150 (2d Cir. 2009)* (“Testimony that vouches for the credibility of a witness is impermissible because it improperly elevates the witness’s status and undermines the jury’s role as the sole arbiter of credibility.”). Here, the ADAs and the lieutenant repeatedly testified that they believed Ramos and Rivera were credible, that their accounts were “consistent,” and that the officers “had probable cause” to arrest Cameron and Higgenbottom. These statements were not merely opinion; they were legal conclusions masquerading as fact.

For example, one ADA stated: “Based on what I’ve heard, I believe the officers were telling the truth.” Another said: “The evidence supports that they had probable cause.” The police lieutenant testified: “I believe the officers acted appropriately.” Such testimony is precisely what *United States v. Gaudin*, 515 U.S. 506, 510 (1995), condemned: “The jury is the sole judge of the credibility of witnesses,” and “no witness may testify as to the ultimate issue of guilt or innocence.” The government’s own agents were permitted to testify as to the ultimate issue—whether the officers were credible and whether probable cause existed—thereby effectively instructing the jury on the law and undermining the jury’s independence.

Moreover, the admission of such testimony violated the rule against “vouching,” which prohibits government witnesses from implying that they possess special knowledge or authority that confirms the truth of another witness’s account. *See United States v. Mendoza, 552 F.3d 228, 235 (2d Cir. 2009)* (“Vouching occurs when a witness, particularly a government agent, implies that the government has special knowledge or access to evidence that supports the credibility of a witness.”). The ADAs and the lieutenant were not neutral observers; they were participants in the prosecution of Cameron and had a direct interest in the outcome. Their testimony that “the officers had probable cause” or that “their story was consistent” amounted to an official endorsement of the officers’ version of events—exactly the kind of impermissible vouching that *Gaudin* and *Hines* prohibit.

**II. The Errors Were Not Harmless and Prejudiced Appellants’ Right to a Fair Trial**

The admission of this testimony was not harmless error. *See United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 734 (1993)* (“Harmless error analysis requires a determination of whether the error had substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury’s verdict.”). The jury was instructed to determine whether Ramos and Rivera had probable cause and whether they were credible—two issues that were central to the false arrest and malicious prosecution claims. Yet the government’s own agents were allowed to testify that the officers were credible and had probable cause, effectively resolving the very issues the jury was tasked with deciding.

The prejudice was compounded by the fact that the ADAs and the lieutenant were perceived as authoritative figures. Their testimony carried significant weight with the jury, especially when delivered in the context of a criminal prosecution. *See United States v. Candelaria, 532 F.3d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 2008)* (“The presence of a government agent as a witness who vouches for another witness’s credibility can be particularly prejudicial because the jury may perceive the government as endorsing the witness’s truthfulness.”). The jury was thus deprived of its constitutional role as the sole arbiter of credibility and the ultimate factfinder on the existence of probable cause.

**III. The District Court Should Have Granted Judgment as a Matter of Law on the Basis of Contradictory Photographs**

Appellants also contend that the District Court erred in denying their motion for judgment as a matter of law, based on a series of security camera photographs that directly contradict Appellees’ version of events. The photographs, taken at the scene, show Cameron standing at the passenger-side window of the squad car, gesturing with her right hand, but never reaching into the vehicle or touching Officer Ramos. The images are unimpeached and uncontroverted. *See Hynes v. Coughlin, 79 F.3d 285, 291 (2d Cir. 1996)* (“When the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable jury could find for the nonmoving party, judgment as a matter of law is appropriate.”).

Appellees’ account—that Cameron grabbed Ramos’s shirt and was disorderly—cannot be reconciled with the photographic record. The images show no such contact. While the photographs do not definitively prove Appellants’ version of events, they are sufficient to render Appellees’ version “so implausible as to be legally untenable.” *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 251 (1986)* (“A party opposing summary judgment must present evidence from which a reasonable jury could find in its favor.”). Here, the evidence is so one-sided that no reasonable jury could accept Appellees’ account. The District Court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law was therefore error.
