ARGUMENT

The Third Circuit erred in holding that a grant of Temporary Protected Status (TPS) under 8 U.S.C. § 1254a does not constitute an "admission" into the United States under 8 U.S.C. § 1255. A proper interpretation of the relevant statutes, informed by the principles of statutory construction and the precedents of this Court and others, reveals that TPS recipients are indeed considered "admitted" for purposes of adjustment of status.

First, the statutory text supports this interpretation. Section 1254a(f)(4) provides that an alien with TPS "shall be considered as being in, and maintaining, lawful status as a nonimmigrant" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. This language is consistent with the notion that TPS recipients are "admitted" for purposes of § 1255. As the Court noted in United States ex rel. Knauff v. Shaughnessy, 338 U.S. 537, 544 (1950), "the concept of 'admission' is not limited to a physical entry into the country, but also encompasses the idea of being permitted to remain in the United States." 

Furthermore, the Court has long recognized that the immigration laws should be construed to achieve their remedial purposes. See Hines v. Davidowitz, 312 U.S. 52, 70 (1941) ("The power to restrict immigration and the status of aliens is vested in the Federal Government, and the exercise of that power is guided by the national interest."). In this case, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to confer "admission" status on TPS recipients is consistent with the remedial purpose of the TPS program.

The Third Circuit's reliance on the distinction between "admission" and "status" is misplaced. As the Court noted in Delgadillo v. Carmichael, 332 U.S. 388, 391 (1947), "the technical meaning of 'entry' is not controlling when it runs counter to the policy of the statute." Similarly, in this case, a narrow interpretation of "admission" that excludes TPS recipients would be at odds with the statutory purpose.

Other courts have also recognized the importance of considering the broader context and purpose of the immigration laws. See Fong Haw Tan v. Phelan, 333 U.S. 6, 10 (1948) ("The underlying purpose of the [immigration laws] is to prevent the deportation of aliens who have established roots in this country."). In this case, TPS recipients have been permitted to remain in the United States for an extended period, and it is consistent with the statutory purpose to consider them "admitted" for purposes of adjustment of status.

Moreover, the Court has long recognized that the Attorney General's interpretation of the immigration laws is entitled to significant deference. See United States ex rel. Bilokumsky v. Tod, 263 U.S. 149, 158 (1923) ("The Attorney General's construction of the statute is entitled to great weight."). In this case, the agency's interpretation of § 1254a(f)(4) as conferring "admission" status on TPS recipients is a reasonable one that should be upheld.

The Third Circuit's decision is also inconsistent with the principles of statutory construction. As the Court noted in United States v. Menasche, 348 U.S. 528, 538-39 (1955), "it is our duty to give effect, if possible, to every clause and word of a statute." In this case, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to confer "admission" status on TPS recipients gives effect to the statutory language and purpose.

Furthermore, the Court has recognized that the immigration laws should be construed in a way that avoids absurd or unjust results. See Lau Ow Bew v. United States, 144 U.S. 47, 58 (1892) ("A statute should be construed with reference to the general principles of justice and morality."). In this case, denying TPS recipients the opportunity to adjust their status because they were not "admitted" in the classical sense would be an unjust result that is not required by the statutory text.

Additionally, the Court has noted that the immigration laws are to be construed in light of their historical context and the legislative intent behind them. See Jordan v. De George, 341 U.S. 223, 229-30 (1951) ("The legislative history of the [immigration laws] indicates that Congress intended to provide a comprehensive scheme for the regulation of immigration."). In this case, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to confer "admission" status on TPS recipients is consistent with the legislative intent behind the TPS program.

Finally, as the Court observed in Wong Yang Sung v. McGrath, 339 U.S. 33, 49 (1950), "the demands of due process require that aliens be given a fair opportunity to present their claims." In this case, interpreting § 1254a(f)(4) to confer "admission" status on TPS recipients is consistent with the demands of due process and provides a fair and reasonable interpretation of the statutory text.

In conclusion, a proper interpretation of the relevant statutes, informed by the principles of statutory construction and the precedents of this Court and others, reveals that TPS recipients are indeed considered "admitted" for purposes of adjustment of status under § 1255. The Third Circuit's decision should be reversed.
