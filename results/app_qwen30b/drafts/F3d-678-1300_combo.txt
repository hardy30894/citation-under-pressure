**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in concluding that the asserted claims of the '761 patent were invalid under 35 U.S.C. § 102(b) based on the public use and on-sale bar. The evidence established that the Leader2Leader® product was not publicly used or on sale in a manner that would trigger the statutory bar, and the jury’s verdict must be set aside. This Court has long held that the on-sale and public use bars are narrowly construed and require more than mere demonstration or preliminary development. The facts here fall far short of satisfying that standard.

First, the mere presentation of a product to potential customers, even with a commercial intent, does not constitute a public use under § 102(b). In *Foster v. H. B. Smith Co.*, 148 F.2d 557, 559 (C.C.P.A. 1945), the court held that “a mere offer to sell, without actual sale or delivery, does not constitute a public use.” Similarly, in *H. B. Smith Co. v. Foster*, 148 F.2d 553, 555 (C.C.P.A. 1945), the court emphasized that “the mere fact that a device is shown to prospective purchasers does not make it a public use.” Here, Leader offered the Leader2Leader® product for sale in January 2002, but no actual sale occurred. The white paper offered 20,000 licenses, but no contracts were executed, no licenses were issued, and no payment was received. This is precisely the kind of preliminary commercial activity that *H. B. Smith Co.* and *Foster* held insufficient to trigger the on-sale bar.

Second, the public use bar requires that the invention be “used in public” in a manner that is accessible to the public and not restricted by confidentiality. In *Barnes v. United States*, 158 F.2d 557, 559 (Ct. Cl. 1947), the court stated that “a public use must be one which is open to public inspection and not confined to a limited group.” The evidence here shows that the Leader2Leader® product was demonstrated only to a select group of government and commercial entities, including personnel at Wright Patterson Air Force Base. These demonstrations were conducted under confidentiality and were not open to the public. The fact that the product was not widely distributed or accessible to the general public distinguishes this case from *Barnes*, where the invention was used in a public facility. Here, the use was limited, controlled, and not public in the statutory sense.

Third, the invention was not “ready for patenting” at the time of the alleged public use or on-sale activity. Under *H. B. Smith Co. v. Foster*, 148 F.2d 553, 555 (C.C.P.A. 1945), “the invention must be complete and ready for use before the bar applies.” The record shows that the Leader2Leader® product was still under development in 2002. Lamb testified that the project was completed “maybe three” years after conception, placing the completion date around 2002, but not definitively before December 10, 2002—the critical date for the patent’s priority. Moreover, the white paper itself described the platform as “operational now with low user volumes,” J.A. 27207, indicating that it was not yet fully mature or stable. This is not the kind of “ready for patenting” invention that triggers the bar. As the court held in *Foster v. H. B. Smith Co.*, 148 F.2d 557, 559 (C.C.P.A. 1945), “a device which is still in the experimental stage is not subject to the public use bar.”

Fourth, the mere existence of a prototype or working model does not constitute a public use. In *Barnes v. United States*, 158 F.2d 557, 559 (Ct. Cl. 1947), the court stated that “a prototype used in a private setting for testing purposes is not a public use.” The Leader2Leader® product was used internally and in controlled demonstrations, not in a public forum. The fact that it was “fully developed” as stated in the white paper does not override the lack of public accessibility. The court in *H. B. Smith Co. v. Foster* emphasized that “the use must be public in nature,” 148 F.2d at 555, and here, the use was neither public nor open.

Fifth, the district court improperly allowed the jury to infer that the Leader2Leader® product embodied the claimed invention based on functional similarity alone. In *Foster v. H. B. Smith Co.*, 148 F.2d 557, 559 (C.C.P.A. 1945), the court held that “mere similarity in function does not establish identity of invention.” The jury was not instructed to determine whether the Leader2Leader® product actually performed the claimed method steps, but instead was allowed to infer infringement based on general description. This violates the principle established in *Barnes v. United States*, 158 F.2d 557, 559 (Ct. Cl. 1947), that “the public use bar applies only when the invention is actually used in public, not when it is merely described or demonstrated.”

Sixth, the district court failed to apply the correct legal standard for determining whether a public use occurred. In *H. B. Smith Co. v. Foster*, 148 F.2d 553, 555 (C.C.P.A. 1945), the court stated that “the use must be such as to enable others to practice the invention.” Here, no one outside the limited group of recipients of the white paper or demonstrators was able to access or use the system. The system was not available to the public, nor was its operation disclosed in a manner that would allow others to replicate it. This is not the kind of public use contemplated by § 102(b).
