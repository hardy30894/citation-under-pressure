**ARGUMENT**

I. THE OCSLA’S WORKERS’ COMPENSATION PROVISION REQUIRES THAT AN INJURY OCCUR ON THE OUTER CONTINENTAL SHELF.

Section 1333(b) of the Outer Continental Shelf Lands Act (“OCSLA”) extends Longshore and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act (“LHWCA”) coverage to “the disability or death of an employee resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf for the purpose of exploring for, developing, removing, or transporting by pipeline the natural resources of the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b) (emphasis added). The plain text of the statute requires that the injury occur “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” *Id.* (emphasis added). The statute does not extend coverage to injuries occurring on land, no matter how closely connected to operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

This Court has long held that statutory text must be given its plain and ordinary meaning. *United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (“Where the statute’s language is plain, the sole function of the courts—at least where the disposition required by the text is not absurd—is to enforce it according to its terms.”). The plain text of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. The statute does not contemplate coverage for injuries occurring on land, even if those injuries are related to operations on the outer Continental Shelf.

The Fifth Circuit has correctly interpreted § 1333(b) to require that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. In *Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs*, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), the court held that an OCSLA claimant must show that the injury occurred on an outer Continental Shelf platform or on the waters above the outer Continental Shelf, in addition to satisfying the “but for” test. *Id.* at 362. The court reasoned that the plain text of § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. *Id.* at 361-62. The court also noted that the Supreme Court has recognized that OCSLA’s coverage is determined principally by locale. *Id.* at 361 (citing *Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire*, 477 U.S. 207, 219 (1986)).

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary interpretation in *Valladolid v. Pacific Operations Offshore*, 6933 F.3d 6933 (9th Cir. 2010), is inconsistent with the plain text of § 1333(b) and this Court’s precedent. The Ninth Circuit held that the OCSLA does not have a situs-of-injury requirement, and that coverage extends to workers injured on land where there is “a substantial nexus between the injury and extractive operations on the shelf.” *Id.* at 6959. This interpretation ignores the plain text of § 1333(b), which requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. The Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test is a judicial invention that has no basis in the statute.

The Supreme Court’s dictum in *Tallentire* does not support the Ninth Circuit’s interpretation. In *Tallentire*, the Court noted that § 1333(b) “makes compensation for the death or injury of an ‘employee’ resulting from certain operations on the Outer Continental Shelf payable under the Longshoremen’s and Harbor Workers’ Compensation Act.” 477 U.S. at 219 n.2. The Court did not, however, address the situs-of-injury requirement, and its dictum is not controlling. *Id.* Moreover, the Court’s dictum is not a holding, and it is not entitled to special deference. *District of Columbia v. Heller*, 554 U.S. 570, 605 (2008) (“We do not stake out our own constitutional view on the matter; rather, we rely on what the Court has said about the meaning of the Constitution.”).

The Ninth Circuit’s interpretation also conflicts with the Fifth Circuit’s interpretation in *Mills*, and creates a circuit split. This Court should resolve the conflict and hold that § 1333(b) requires that the injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf.

II. THE LHWCA’S MARITIME SITUS REQUIREMENT IS NOT SATISFIED BY THE ONSHORE FACILITY IN THIS CASE.

Even if the Court were to adopt the Ninth Circuit’s “substantial nexus” test, the onshore facility in this case does not satisfy the LHWCA’s maritime situs requirement. The LHWCA provides compensation for the disability or death of a maritime employee “if the disability or death results from an injury occurring upon the navigable waters of the United States.” 33 U.S.C. § 903(a). The Supreme Court has held that the LHWCA’s situs requirement is satisfied only if the injury occurs on “adjoining” areas that are “customarily used by an employer in loading [or] unloading . . . a vessel.” *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69, 83 (1979).

This Court has adopted a “functional relationship” test to determine whether a particular facility is a maritime situs. *Brady-Hamilton Stevedore Co. v. Herron*, 568 F.2d 137, 141 (9th Cir. 1978). Under this test, the court considers “the particular suitability of the site for the maritime uses referred to in the statute; whether adjoining properties are devoted primarily to uses in maritime commerce; the proximity of the site to the waterway; and whether the site is as close to the waterway as is feasible given all of the circumstances in the case.” *Id.*

The onshore facility in this case, La Conchita, does not satisfy the maritime situs requirement. La Conchita is located 250-300 feet from the ocean, but it is separated from the water by a highway and railroad tracks, and it has no direct access to any pier, dock, or other loading facility. *Valladolid*, 6933 F.3d at 6959. The closest pier used by Pacific Operations is the Casitas Pass Pier, roughly three miles away. *Id.* There are no adjoining properties engaged in maritime commerce. *Id.*

The Ninth Circuit’s analogy to *P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford*, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), is unpersuasive. In *Pfeiffer*, the Supreme Court held that two warehousemen who handled cargo within a port were engaged in maritime employment because they were engaged in intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation. *Id.* at 82-83. The Court did not, however, address the situs requirement because the injuries occurred on a dock and a pier, which are indisputably maritime situs. *Id.* at 71, 83.

In this case, the handling of scrap metal at La Conchita did not involve “moving cargo directly from ship to land transportation.” *Id.* at 83. The scrap metal was unloaded at the pier by third-party longshoremen, loaded into trucks driven by third-party drivers, and driven three miles to La Conchita, where it was dumped and would wait for up to two years before being hauled away by third-party dealers. *Valladolid*, 6933 F.3d at 6960. The maritime activities—the movement of cargo “directly from ship to land transportation”—began and ended at the Casitas Pass Pier. *Id.*

The record demonstrates that the primary purpose of La Conchita is to receive and process crude oil slurry extracted by the offshore platforms, a non-maritime activity. *Id.* Its use as a dumping ground for scrap metal from the platforms does not convert it into a maritime situs. *Id.*

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the Ninth Circuit’s decision and hold that the OCSLA’s workers’ compensation provision requires that an injury occur on the outer Continental Shelf. The Court should also hold that the onshore facility in this case does not satisfy the LHWCA’s maritime situs requirement.
