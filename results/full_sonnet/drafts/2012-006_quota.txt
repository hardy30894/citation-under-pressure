# ARGUMENT

## I. The Due Process Clause Requires the Government to Prove Beyond a Reasonable Doubt That a Defendant Remained a Member of the Conspiracy During the Limitations Period.

The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause "protects the accused against conviction except upon proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every fact necessary to constitute the crime with which he is charged." *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). Membership in a conspiracy during the period the indictment charges is not a peripheral detail—it is the conduct that the statute of limitations makes criminal. Where, as here, petitioners came forward with evidence that the alleged conspiracy's architects, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, had split and gone their separate ways before the limitations window began, the timing of continued participation became squarely "necessary to constitute the crime." *Id.* Once that evidence entered the case, due process forbade the government from resting on an inference of continuity; it had to prove, beyond a reasonable doubt, that petitioners' membership persisted into the charged period.

This Court's decision in *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684 (1975), confirms that a State (or the federal government) may not shift to the defendant the burden of disproving a fact that bears on the very culpability the law punishes. *Mullaney* held that the Due Process Clause requires the prosecution to prove beyond a reasonable doubt every fact necessary to establish the degree of criminal liability charged, and that a burden-shifting device cannot circumvent that requirement merely by labeling the contested fact a "defense." *Id.* at 698–701. Whether a defendant remained in a conspiracy during the limitations period is functionally indistinguishable from the facts at issue in *Mullaney*: it determines whether the government may prosecute at all.

## II. *Patterson v. New York* Does Not Permit the Government to Recast an Element as an Affirmative Defense.

The government's reliance on ordinary affirmative-defense doctrine, exemplified by *Patterson v. New York*, 432 U.S. 197 (1977), is misplaced. *Patterson* permits a legislature to place the burden of proving an affirmative defense on a defendant only where the defense "does not serve to negative any facts of the crime which the State is to prove in order to convict." *Id.* at 207. Withdrawal prior to the limitations period does precisely what *Patterson* says a true affirmative defense does not do: it negates the fact—continued membership—that the government must otherwise establish to bring the prosecution within the limitations period at all. Because timely membership is an element rather than a mitigating circumstance, *Patterson*'s allowance for defense-side burdens is inapplicable, and *Mullaney*'s rule controls.

Nor can the government evade this rule by invoking the general principle that statutes of limitation are not "elements" in the ordinary sense. This Court has long instructed that criminal limitations statutes are "to be liberally interpreted in favor of repose," *Toussie v. United States*, 397 U.S. 488, 493 (1970), precisely because they embody a legislative judgment that stale accusations should not proceed absent proof that the crime persisted into the protected window. That policy of repose is honored, not undermined, by requiring the government—not the accused—to shoulder the burden of proving that the conspiracy did not end before the limitations period began.

## III. This Court's Conspiracy Precedents Confirm That Continued Membership, Not Withdrawal, Is What the Government Must Prove.

The conspiracy offense is inherently temporal: it exists only so long as the agreement persists. As this Court explained in *Hyde v. United States*, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912), a conspiracy "continues until abandoned or until the objects of it have been accomplished." It follows that a defendant's abandonment—his withdrawal—terminates his membership as a factual matter; the government's proof of the "conspiracy," as applied to that defendant, must therefore stop at the point of withdrawal unless the government proves continued participation thereafter.

This Court has also cautioned against permitting prosecutors to lean on presumptions of continuity to paper over gaps in proof. In *Krulewitch v. United States*, 336 U.S. 440, 442–44 (1949), the Court rejected the notion that participation in a conspiracy could be presumed to continue indefinitely for evidentiary purposes, emphasizing that such presumptions dangerously expand liability beyond what the government has actually proved. Similarly, in *Grunewald v. United States*, 353 U.S. 391, 396–97 (1957), the Court held that the statute of limitations for conspiracy runs from the last provable act in furtherance of the scheme, and refused to allow the government to extend a conspiracy's life through inference or acts of concealment that the government had not proven were part of the original agreement. And in *United States v. Jimenez Recio*, 537 U.S. 270, 274 (2003), this Court reaffirmed that a conspiracy ends "when the conspirators abandon the conspiracy," underscoring that continued membership—not mere historical association—is what criminal liability requires at every stage of the prosecution, including for limitations purposes.

Taken together, *Hyde*, *Krulewitch*, *Grunewald*, and *Jimenez Recio* establish that once a defendant introduces evidence of separation from the conspiracy's leadership before the limitations period, the presumption of continued membership on which the government's case depends evaporates. At that point, *Winship* and *Mullaney* require the government—not the defendant—to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant's membership persisted into the charged period. The courts below erred in holding otherwise, and this Court should reverse.
