**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred in designating Mr. Furlow as an armed career criminal under the Armed Career Criminal Act (“ACCA”) and as a career offender under U.S.S.G. § 4B1.1. Neither his South Carolina conviction for distribution of crack cocaine nor his Georgia conviction for first-degree arson qualifies as a predicate offense under the applicable definitions.

**A. The South Carolina crack cocaine distribution conviction is not a “serious drug offense” under the ACCA or a “controlled substance offense” under the Guidelines.**

The ACCA defines a “serious drug offense” as a state-law offense “involving manufacturing, distributing, or possessing with intent to manufacture or distribute, a controlled substance” for which “a maximum term of imprisonment of ten years or more is prescribed by law.” 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(A)(ii). The Guidelines define a “controlled substance offense” as an offense “punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” that prohibits, *inter alia*, the “distribution” of a controlled substance. U.S.S.G. § 4B1.2(b). Both definitions require a court to apply the categorical approach, examining the elements of the state statute rather than the facts of the prior conviction. *Descamps v. United States*, 570 U.S. 254, 260–61 (2013).

South Carolina’s distribution-of-crack-cocaine statute, S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-375(B), is divisible by drug type and quantity, with different penalty ranges. For crack cocaine, the maximum penalty varies: distribution of less than one gram carries a maximum of 15 years, but distribution of one gram or more carries a maximum of 30 years. *See* S.C. Code Ann. § 44-53-375(B)(1)–(2). The record does not establish which subpart of the statute formed the basis of Mr. Furlow’s conviction. Under the modified categorical approach, a court may consult only “the charging document, written plea agreement, transcript of plea colloquy, and any explicit factual finding by the trial judge to which the defendant assented.” *Shepard v. United States*, 544 U.S. 13, 16 (2005). The government’s information alleged only a “felony distribution of crack cocaine” in 2016, without specifying the quantity. The district court therefore lacked the necessary Shepard-approved documents to determine whether the conviction carried a maximum penalty of ten years or more—a prerequisite for an ACCA predicate. *See United States v. Jones*, 914 F.3d 893, 901 (4th Cir. 2019) (holding that the government bears the burden to prove the predicate conviction qualifies). Similarly, for the Guidelines, the court could not ascertain whether the offense was “punishable by imprisonment for a term exceeding one year” without knowing the specific subpart. Because the record is silent, the conviction cannot serve as a predicate under either regime.

**B. The Georgia first-degree arson conviction is not a “violent felony” under the ACCA or a “crime of violence” under the Guidelines.**

Both the ACCA and the Guidelines list “arson” as a qualifying predicate. 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(ii); U.S.S.G. § 4B1.2(a)(2). This Court has held that “arson” in these provisions refers to “generic arson”—the malicious burning of a building or structure. *United States v. Vail-Ballou*, 868 F.3d 228, 236 (4th Cir. 2017) (citing *Taylor v. United States*, 495 U.S. 575, 598 (1990)). Georgia’s first-degree arson statute, Ga. Code Ann. § 16-7-61, criminalizes the knowing and malicious burning of “any building or structure” or “any property, real or personal, of another.” The statute’s inclusion of “personal property” sweeps more broadly than generic arson, which is limited to buildings. *See United States v. White*, 571 F.3d 365, 369 (4th Cir. 2009) (holding that a state arson statute covering personal property is overbroad). Because the Georgia statute is indivisible—it sets out a single set of elements—the categorical approach requires the court to compare the full range of conduct covered by the statute to generic arson. *Mathis v. United States*, 136 S. Ct. 2243, 2251 (2016). Since the statute criminalizes burning personal property, it is categorically overbroad. *See Vail-Ballou*, 868 F.3d at 236–37 (finding that a state arson statute covering “any property” is not a categorical match). The district court therefore erred in counting this conviction as a predicate.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, Mr. Furlow lacks the three prior convictions necessary for an ACCA enhancement and the two prior convictions necessary for a career-offender enhancement. The sentence must be vacated and the case remanded for resentencing without those designations.
