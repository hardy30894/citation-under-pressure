**ARGUMENT**

**A reasonable attorney's fee has always been measured, in part, by the quality of representation and the results the representation produced, and nothing in the text or purpose of federal fee-shifting statutes forecloses an enhancement on that basis in the rare case where the lodestar fails to capture what counsel actually achieved.**

**I. The equitable roots of fee-shifting doctrine have long treated the caliber of counsel's work and the results obtained as legitimate components of a "reasonable" fee.**

Long before Congress enacted modern fee-shifting statutes, this Court recognized that a federal court sitting in equity may award compensation to an attorney whose efforts created or preserved a benefit for others, and that the amount of that compensation must be tied to the value of what was actually accomplished. *Trustees v. Greenough*, 105 U.S. 527 (1881). In fixing that value, the Court did not confine itself to a mechanical hours-times-rate calculation; it looked to the service rendered and the benefit secured by that service. *Id.*

This Court elaborated on the relevant considerations in *Central Railroad & Banking Co. v. Pettus*, 113 U.S. 116 (1885), explaining that the propriety of an allowance for counsel depends on the character of the litigation, the difficulty of the questions presented, and the results secured for those on whose behalf the fund was created. The same emphasis on outcome and quality of service appears in *Newton v. Consolidated Gas Co. of New York*, 259 U.S. 101 (1922), where the Court examined not only the labor expended by counsel but the benefit that labor conferred, and in *United States v. Equitable Trust Co.*, 283 U.S. 738 (1931), where the reasonableness of a fee award was assessed in light of what the litigation actually accomplished for the parties who benefited from it.

These principles were not limited to the common-fund context. In *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co.*, 312 U.S. 262 (1941), this Court reviewed a fee award to counsel in a corporate reorganization and made clear that the "correlation between the value of the services and the amount of the reward" is the touchstone of reasonableness, one that requires courts to weigh the skill exercised and the results obtained, not merely the time consumed. And in *Sprague v. Ticonic National Bank*, 307 U.S. 161 (1939), the Court emphasized that there is no rigid formula for fee determinations; the trial court must exercise judgment suited to the particular circumstances of the case before it.

This unbroken line of authority establishes that, since at least 1881, the federal courts have understood a "reasonable fee" to be one that reflects the actual value of counsel's work, including its quality and its results, and have entrusted the assessment of those factors to the sound discretion of the judge who presided over the litigation.

**II. Congress legislated fee-shifting statutes against that settled backdrop, and the purposes those statutes serve confirm that quality and results remain relevant in the appropriate case.**

Federal fee-shifting statutes displace the American Rule for particular categories of litigation precisely because Congress determined that private enforcement of certain rights required fee awards sufficient to attract able counsel. *Fleischmann Distilling Corp. v. Maier Brewing Co.*, 386 U.S. 714 (1967). This Court has likewise recognized that fee-shifting provisions in civil rights legislation are designed to enable plaintiffs to secure counsel who will vindicate rights that Congress considered of high priority, casting successful plaintiffs' counsel as "private attorney[s] general" who advance the public interest through their litigation. *Newman v. Piggie Park Enterprises, Inc.*, 390 U.S. 400, 402 (1968) (per curiam).

That remedial purpose is not served by a rule that flattens every fee award to an hours-times-rate product regardless of what the litigation achieved. Where, as the equitable authorities above demonstrate, courts have always looked to the value actually delivered by counsel's efforts, a "reasonable fee" under a modern fee-shifting statute should likewise be capable, in the rare and extraordinary case, of reflecting that a lodestar built from ordinary market rates simply failed to capture the extraordinary value counsel provided. Nothing in *Fleischmann* or *Piggie Park* suggests Congress meant to abandon, for federal fee-shifting statutes, the traditional equitable understanding of a reasonable fee that this Court had already articulated in *Greenough*, *Pettus*, *Newton*, *Equitable Trust*, *Sprague*, and *Woods*.

**III. Deference to the trial judge's firsthand assessment of counsel's performance and the results obtained is likewise consistent with this historical practice.**

The trial judge who presides over litigation from filing to judgment occupies a unique vantage point to evaluate whether counsel's performance and the result achieved were ordinary or exceptional. This Court's equitable fee jurisprudence has consistently entrusted that evaluation to the district court's discretion rather than confining it to a rigid formula. *Sprague*, 307 U.S. at 161; *Woods*, 312 U.S. at 262. That deference does not evaporate merely because the fee is now awarded pursuant to statute rather than pursuant to a court's inherent equitable power over a common fund; the same institutional competence that justified deference in *Sprague* and *Woods* justifies deference here.

**Conclusion**

The historical understanding of a reasonable attorney's fee, as reflected in this Court's decisions from *Trustees v. Greenough* through *Woods v. City National Bank & Trust Co.*, has never treated the quality of counsel's performance or the results obtained as irrelevant to the fee calculation. Federal fee-shifting statutes were enacted against that backdrop and should be construed consistently with it. The district court's enhancement, supported by its own detailed findings regarding the exceptional quality of representation and the extraordinary results achieved for the plaintiff class, was a proper exercise of the discretion these authorities confirm.
