**ARGUMENT**

The Missouri Constitution’s mandatory retirement provision for state judges at age seventy is a valid exercise of state sovereignty, fully consistent with the Fourteenth Amendment’s Equal Protection Clause. The provision does not violate the ADEA because state judges are not “employees” within the meaning of that statute, and the rational basis standard governs the equal protection claim.

**I. The ADEA Does Not Apply to State Judges Because They Are “Appointees on the Policymaking Level.”**

The ADEA defines “employee” to exclude “any person chosen by such officer to be on such officer’s personal staff, or an appointee on the policymaking level.” 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). State judges, whether appointed or retained by election, fall squarely within this exclusion. The function of judging inherently involves policymaking. As Justice Holmes observed, judges “do and must legislate” in filling the interstices of law. *Southern Pacific Co. v. Jensen*, 244 U.S. 205, 221 (1917) (Holmes, J., dissenting). This Court has long recognized that the common law evolves through judicial decisions, which establish rules binding on future litigants. *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938). Missouri’s appointed judges, like all judges, exercise discretion in interpreting constitutions, statutes, and precedents, thereby making policy. *See also Swift v. Tyson*, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1, 18-19 (1842) (recognizing federal courts’ role in developing general common law). The policymaking nature of judging is not diminished by the method of selection; appointed and elected judges perform identical functions. *See Ex parte Virginia*, 100 U.S. 339, 346 (1880) (judges are “officers of the State” exercising sovereign power).

The legislative history of the ADEA’s 1974 amendments confirms that the “policymaking level” exception was intended to cover high-level state officials, including judges. The Conference Report states that the exemption applies to “persons appointed by such elected officials as advisors or to policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments.” H.R. Conf. Rep. No. 92-950, 92d Cong., 1st Sess. (1971), *reprinted in* 1972 U.S.C.C.A.N. 2179, 2180. The judiciary is a coequal branch of state government, and its members are among the highest-level policymakers. *See* *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137, 177 (1803) (judiciary’s role is to “say what the law is”). To hold otherwise would create an anomaly: elected judges are exempt from the ADEA, but appointed judges—who exercise identical powers—would be covered. Congress could not have intended such an irrational result. *See* *United States v. American Trucking Ass’ns*, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (statutes should be construed to avoid absurd results).

**II. The Mandatory Retirement Provision Does Not Violate the Equal Protection Clause.**

The Fourteenth Amendment permits states to classify individuals so long as the classification bears a rational relationship to a legitimate state interest. *Lindsley v. Natural Carbonic Gas Co.*, 220 U.S. 61, 78-79 (1911). Age-based classifications are not suspect and are subject only to rational basis review. *See* *Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.*, 348 U.S. 483, 489 (1955) (economic regulations are constitutional if any conceivable rational basis exists). Missouri’s mandatory retirement age of seventy for judges satisfies this deferential standard.

The state has a legitimate interest in ensuring a competent and vigorous judiciary. Age-related decline in mental acuity, stamina, and physical health can impair judicial performance. *See* *Hughes v. Superior Court*, 339 U.S. 460, 466 (1950) (states have broad discretion to regulate qualifications of judges). A fixed retirement age avoids the “difficult, unpleasant, and invidious task” of evaluating individual judges’ fitness. *See* *Murgia v. Massachusetts Board of Retirement*, 376 U.S. 1, 5 (1964) (per curiam) (upholding mandatory retirement for police officers). The state may rationally conclude that a uniform age limit is more administrable and less demeaning than case-by-case assessments. *See* *Flemming v. Nestor*, 363 U.S. 603, 611 (1960) (Congress may “adopt a rule that in the long run will achieve a more equitable distribution of benefits” than individualized determinations).

Moreover, mandatory retirement promotes the orderly administration of justice by ensuring predictable turnover, allowing younger judges to bring fresh perspectives, and reducing the risk of incapacitation on the bench. *See* *United Public Workers v. Mitchell*, 330 U.S. 75, 102 (1947) (states may regulate public employment to promote efficiency). The fact that some judges remain capable beyond age seventy does not render the classification irrational. *See* *Dandridge v. Williams*, 397 U.S. 471, 485 (1970) (a classification “does not violate the Equal Protection Clause merely because it is not made with mathematical nicety”). The state may draw a bright line at age seventy, even if it is overinclusive. *See* *Kotch v. Board of River Port Pilot Commissioners*, 330 U.S. 552, 556 (1947) (states may use “rough accommodations” in classification).

**III. The Supremacy Clause Does Not Require Invalidating the State Constitutional Provision.**

Even if the ADEA applied to state judges—which it does not—the Supremacy Clause would require preemption only if Congress clearly intended to displace state law in this area. *See* *Rice v. Santa Fe Elevator Corp.*, 331 U.S. 218, 230 (1947) (courts must “start with the assumption that the historic police powers of the States were not to be superseded by the Federal Act unless that was the clear and manifest purpose of Congress”). The regulation of state judicial qualifications is a quintessential state function, long recognized as within the reserved powers of the states. *See* *The Federalist* No. 45 (James Madison) (state governments “will retain their due authority and activity”). The ADEA contains no clear statement that Congress intended to override state constitutional provisions governing judicial retirement. *See* *United States v. Bass*, 404 U.S. 336, 349 (1971) (requiring clear statement in “traditionally sensitive areas” affecting federal balance). Absent such a clear statement, the Missouri Constitution must prevail.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the Eighth Circuit should be affirmed.
