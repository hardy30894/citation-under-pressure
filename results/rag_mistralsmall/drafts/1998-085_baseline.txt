**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Text and Structure of § 1981a Do Not Require a Finding of “Egregious” or “Outrageous” Conduct Independent of the Employer’s Mental State.**

The plain text of 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1) authorizes punitive damages where an employer acts “with malice or with reckless indifference to the federally protected rights of an aggrieved individual.” Congress did not add any additional textual requirement—such as “egregious” or “outrageous” conduct—beyond the specified mental states. This Court has repeatedly cautioned against importing judicially created limitations into statutes where Congress has spoken clearly. *See, e.g., Connecticut Nat. Bank v. Germain*, 503 U.S. 249, 253–54 (1992) (“Courts must presume that a legislature says in a statute what it means and means in a statute what it says there.”).

The legislative history cited by petitioner is not to the contrary. The Senate Report’s reference to “extraordinarily egregious cases” was part of a discussion of the cap on punitive damages, not a requirement for liability. *See* S. Rep. No. 102-210, at 23 (1991) (“[T]he Committee intends that punitive damages be allowed in cases of intentional discrimination in which the employer’s conduct is extraordinary, outrageous, or egregious.”). This Court has recognized that such generalized statements do not override the statute’s clear text. *See, e.g., United States v. Ron Pair Enters., Inc.*, 489 U.S. 235, 241 (1989) (legislative history cannot “overcome the clearly expressed intent of Congress”).

Moreover, the structure of § 1981a distinguishes between liability and damages. Liability is established by proof of intentional discrimination, while punitive damages require an additional showing of either malice or reckless indifference. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(a), (b)(1). This Court has long held that punitive damages are not awarded as of right, even where liability is clear. *Smith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30, 35 (1983) (“Punitive damages are never awarded as of right, no matter how egregious the defendant’s conduct.”). Thus, the jury’s discretion to award punitive damages is cabined by the statutorily defined mental states, not by an amorphous “egregiousness” standard.

**II. This Court’s Precedent Establishes That Punitive Damages Under Title VII Require Only a Showing of the Statutorily Prescribed Mental State.**

In *Smith v. Wade*, this Court held that punitive damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983 may be awarded where a defendant acts with reckless indifference to the plaintiff’s rights. 461 U.S. at 56. The Court rejected the argument that punitive damages required a showing of “actual malicious intent,” id. at 50, and instead held that the jury may consider whether the defendant’s conduct was “so reprehensible as to justify the imposition of further sanctions to achieve punishment and deterrence,” id. at 56. The same standard applies under § 1981a, which uses identical language to authorize punitive damages. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1).

Petitioner’s reliance on *Kolstad v. American Dental Ass’n*, 139 F.3d 958 (D.C. Cir. 1998), aff’d in part and rev’d in part, 527 U.S. 526 (1999), is misplaced. The *Kolstad* panel’s discussion of “egregiousness” was dicta, and this Court explicitly rejected the argument that punitive damages require a showing of conduct independent of the statutorily prescribed mental states. *Kolstad v. Am. Dental Ass’n*, 527 U.S. 526, 536–37 (1999) (“The terms ‘malice’ and ‘reckless indifference’ in § 1981a(b)(1) focus on the actor’s state of mind.”).

**III. The Jury Instructions in This Case Properly Informed the Jury of the Statutory Standard.**

The jury was instructed that punitive damages could be awarded only if it found that the American Dental Association acted with “malice” or “reckless indifference” to Carole Kolstad’s federally protected rights. This instruction tracked the language of § 1981a(b)(1) and comported with this Court’s precedent. *SeeSmith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. at 56. Petitioner’s contention that the instructions were insufficient because they did not include the word “egregious” is without merit. The absence of that term does not render the instructions erroneous, as the statutory standard focuses on the employer’s mental state, not the degree of its conduct.

**IV. The Evidence Did Not Support an Award of Punitive Damages Under § 1981a.**

Even assuming, arguendo, that “egregiousness” were a relevant consideration, the evidence in this case did not support an award of punitive damages. The record shows that the ADA’s decisionmakers acted on the basis of legitimate, nondiscriminatory reasons—Kolstad’s lack of recent legislative experience and perceived value in her current role—and not with malice or reckless indifference to her rights. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 1981a(b)(1). This Court has held that punitive damages are not warranted where the employer’s actions, while discriminatory, were not motivated by the requisite mental state. *See generallySmith v. Wade*, 461 U.S. 30.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed. The text and structure of § 1981a, this Court’s precedent, and the jury instructions in this case all confirm that punitive damages under Title VII require only a showing that the employer acted with malice or reckless indifference to the plaintiff’s federally protected rights. The evidence in this case did not support an award of punitive damages under that standard.
