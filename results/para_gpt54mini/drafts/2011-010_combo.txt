Respondent should urge affirmance because § 1333(b) extends LHWCA coverage only where the injury itself occurs on the outer Continental Shelf. The statute provides compensation for disability or death “resulting from any injury occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). Congress chose an injury-based formulation tied to the Shelf itself, not a general “but for” employment nexus. The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule rewrites the text and disregards the ordinary presumption that Congress means what it says. Caminetti v. United States, 242 U.S. 470, 485 (1917).

This Court has long required fidelity to statutory geography when Congress defines coverage by situs. In Radzanower v. Touche Ross & Co., 426 U.S. 148, 155 (1976), the Court later reiterated the canon that a specific statutory venue rule controls over a general one; the same principle applies here. Earlier cases likewise enforced Congress’s geographic line-drawing. Panama R. Co. v. Johnson, 264 U.S. 375, 386-87 (1924), sustained the Jones Act because Congress expressly chose the maritime setting; the decision turned on the statute’s territorial scope. See also Hartford Accident & Indemnity Co. v. Illinois Central R. Co., 298 U.S. 648, 655-56 (1936) (Congress may define coverage by the place of injury). OCSLA similarly hinges on where the injury occurs, not merely where antecedent operations were conducted.

The Supreme Court’s pre-1970 decisions also confirm that compensation statutes are ordinarily situs-bound unless Congress clearly provides otherwise. A. H. Phillips, Inc. v. Walling, 324 U.S. 490, 493-94 (1945), emphasized that remedial coverage cannot be expanded beyond the statute’s terms. And in NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel Corp., 301 U.S. 1, 30-31 (1937), the Court held that statutory reach depends on the language Congress enacted, not on broad policy preferences. Section 1333(b) contains no hint that Congress intended to cover every worker whose job bore some relation to offshore production.

Petitioner’s “substantial nexus” test is also inconsistent with pre-1970 authorities distinguishing status from situs. In NLRB v. Fainblatt, 306 U.S. 601, 606-07 (1939), the Court refused to infer coverage beyond the statutory criteria. Likewise, in Addison v. Holly Hill Fruit Products, Inc., 322 U.S. 607, 617-18 (1944), the Court declined to enlarge a statutory exemption by resort to policy. These cases foreclose judicial supplementation of the Shelf statute with an amorphous nexus standard.

The structure of OCSLA confirms this reading. Congress elsewhere used broad incorporation language when it wanted a provision to travel beyond the Shelf’s physical boundaries. See, e.g., 43 U.S.C. § 1333(a)(2)(A). Section 1333(b) does not. Courts must give effect to the difference. Commissioner v. National Carbide Corp., 167 F.2d 304, 306 (2d Cir. 1948), aff’d, 336 U.S. 422 (1949), recognized that divergent statutory wording reflects divergent congressional intent; the same rule applies here.

Finally, the respondent’s position is consistent with the Supreme Court’s insistence that workers’ compensation regimes are creatures of exact legislative definition. Crowell v. Benson, 285 U.S. 22, 41-42 (1932), upheld administrative compensation only within the limits fixed by Congress. If the Court allows coverage to extend to any land-based accident with a downstream offshore connection, it will replace the Shelf situs Congress chose with an open-ended causation test Congress never enacted. That should not be done. United States v. Wiltberger, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95-96 (1820).

Accordingly, the judgment below should be affirmed because § 1333(b) does not cover injuries occurring off the outer Continental Shelf.
