**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts statute as a content-neutral time, place, and manner regulation consistent with the First Amendment.

Public sidewalks are traditional public fora, and speech restrictions there must be content neutral, narrowly tailored to serve a significant governmental interest, and leave open ample alternative channels of communication. *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 45-46 (1983); *Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989). Massachusetts’s law satisfies that standard. The Commonwealth enacted a 35-foot buffer zone around reproductive-health-care facilities to protect patient access, safety, and tranquility at clinic entrances, exits, and driveways. The First Circuit properly recognized that the statute regulates where speech may occur, not what viewpoint a speaker may express. *McCullen v. Coakley*, 571 F.3d 167, 176-81 (1st Cir. 2009); *McCullen v. Coakley*, 573 U.S. 464, 480-81 (2014).

This Court has repeatedly upheld buffer-zone and clinic-access regulations against First Amendment challenge. In *Madsen v. Women’s Health Ctr., Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 768-70 (1994), the Court sustained an injunction establishing clinic buffer zones because the restriction burdened no more speech than necessary to serve significant governmental interests. In *Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York*, 519 U.S. 357, 381-82 (1997), the Court upheld fixed buffer zones around clinic entrances. And in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703, 719-30 (2000), the Court sustained a statute limiting approaching and leafletting within 100 feet of a health-care facility entrance. Massachusetts’s law is narrower than the statute upheld in *Hill* and is supported by the same core interests recognized in *Madsen*, *Schenck*, and *Hill*: protecting access and ensuring that patients can enter medical facilities without harassment or obstruction. *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 767-68; *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 376-77; *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715.

Petitioners argue that the law fails narrow tailoring because less restrictive alternatives exist. But narrow tailoring does not require the least restrictive means. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 798-99. A regulation is valid so long as it does not burden substantially more speech than necessary to further the government’s legitimate interests. *Id.* at 799. The First Circuit correctly concluded that the statute leaves open meaningful alternatives: petitioners may speak, hold signs, pray, distribute literature, and counsel outside the buffer zones and at other places and times. That is enough under *Ward* and *Hill*. *Ward*, 491 U.S. at 802; *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 726-30.

Petitioners also invoke *Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 576 U.S. 155 (2015), but that case does not alter the analysis here. Even after *Reed*, this Court has continued to distinguish laws that regulate speech by place in a content-neutral manner from laws that single out particular messages for differential treatment. See *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 480-81. Massachusetts’s law applies to all persons within a defined zone, regardless of message. *Id.* at 479-80. The First Circuit therefore did not err.

II. If *Hill v. Colorado* is implicated, it should be followed; if reconsidered, the Court should not disturb the judgment below.

Petitioners ask the Court to overrule or limit *Hill*. Stare decisis weighs heavily against that request. *Dickerson v. United States*, 530 U.S. 428, 443-44 (2000); *Payne v. Tennessee*, 501 U.S. 808, 827-28 (1991). *Hill* has been on the books for more than two decades and has been relied upon in subsequent clinic-access decisions. *Schenck*, 519 U.S. at 381-82; *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 480-81. Overruling it would unsettle a settled body of doctrine governing sensitive access rights around medical facilities.

Nor is *Hill* irreconcilable with the Court’s later First Amendment cases. The Court in *Hill* emphasized that the statute was content neutral because it regulated the approach of speakers in a public forum rather than targeting speech based on message. *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 719-25. Later cases such as *Citizens United v. FEC*, 558 U.S. 310, 340-41 (2010), and *Sorrell v. IMS Health Inc.*, 564 U.S. 552, 565-66 (2011), condemned content- and speaker-based discrimination; they did not reject ordinary time, place, and manner regulation in public fora. The First Circuit faithfully applied that distinction. *McCullen*, 571 F.3d at 176-81; *McCullen*, 573 U.S. at 480-86.

At minimum, petitioners offer no basis for invalidating Massachusetts’s law as applied. The Commonwealth need not wait for obstruction, confrontation, or intimidation before acting to protect access to clinics. *Madsen*, 512 U.S. at 769; *Hill*, 530 U.S. at 715. The judgment should be affirmed.
