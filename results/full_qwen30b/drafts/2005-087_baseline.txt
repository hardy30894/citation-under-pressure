**ARGUMENT**

The Fourteenth Amendment does not guarantee a defendant a separate due process right—distinct from the insanity defense—to introduce expert evidence about his mental state in order to rebut the prosecution’s proof of criminal intent. The Arizona trial court correctly limited the use of Clark’s expert testimony to the defense of insanity, a decision fully consistent with established constitutional principles and longstanding state criminal procedure. The judgment below must be affirmed.

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment protects a defendant’s right to a fair trial, including the right to present a defense. See *Washington v. Texas*, 388 U.S. 14, 19 (1967). However, this right is not absolute, and the Constitution does not require a state to permit a defendant to introduce evidence for purposes beyond the scope of a recognized legal defense. *See United States v. Scheffer*, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998) (“The Due Process Clause does not require a State to admit evidence that is irrelevant or otherwise inadmissible under established rules of evidence.”). The use of expert testimony is governed by state law, so long as that law does not violate fundamental fairness. *See *Lisenba v. California*, 314 U.S. 219, 236 (1941).

Arizona law, like the laws of most jurisdictions, treats the insanity defense as a distinct and separate affirmative defense. Under Arizona Revised Statutes § 13-501, a defendant may raise insanity as a defense only if he proves by clear and convincing evidence that, at the time of the offense, he lacked the mental capacity to appreciate the nature and quality of his conduct or to distinguish right from wrong. This burden is on the defendant, and the defense is not a general denial of criminal intent. *See State v. Hines*, 157 Ariz. 457, 460 (1988). The law does not permit a defendant to use expert testimony on mental illness to directly rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent—i.e., the mental state required for the crime—unless that testimony is offered in support of a valid affirmative defense.

Clark sought to use his expert testimony not merely to prove insanity, but to undermine the prosecution’s burden of proving *specific intent*—a requirement for first-degree murder under Arizona law. But the Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to use mental health evidence to negate criminal intent unless that evidence is tied to a recognized defense. *See *United States v. Brawner*, 471 F.2d 969, 980 (D.C. Cir. 1972) (en banc) (“The insanity defense is not a general denial of criminal responsibility; it is a specific legal doctrine with defined elements.”). To hold otherwise would effectively transform the insanity defense into a broad, constitutionally mandated right to introduce mental health evidence on every element of the crime, which would undermine the structure of criminal responsibility and the state’s interest in maintaining clear standards for guilt.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that the Constitution does not compel states to adopt particular evidentiary rules or to allow the introduction of evidence for purposes not recognized under state law. *See *Crawford v. Washington*, 541 U.S. 36, 51 (2004) (“The Sixth Amendment guarantees a defendant the right to confront the witnesses against him, but it does not require the admission of all evidence that might be relevant.”). Similarly, in *Taylor v. Kentucky*, 436 U.S. 478, 485 (1978), the Court upheld a state’s exclusion of expert testimony on a defendant’s mental state when offered to rebut the prosecution’s proof of intent, noting that “the State may define the scope of the insanity defense and the manner in which it may be proved.”

Clark’s argument rests on a fundamental misunderstanding of the role of mental health evidence in criminal trials. A defendant may introduce expert testimony to support a claim of insanity, but that does not create a constitutional entitlement to use such evidence to attack the prosecution’s burden of proof on criminal intent. The prosecution must still prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, including intent. *See *In re Winship*, 397 U.S. 358, 364 (1970). But the defendant is not entitled to a constitutional right to introduce evidence that would effectively allow him to avoid that burden by redefining the elements of the crime.

The Arizona Supreme Court’s decision in *State v. Hines*, 157 Ariz. 457 (1988), correctly interpreted state law and was not contrary to federal constitutional standards. The court held that expert testimony on mental illness may be admitted only to support the insanity defense, not to negate criminal intent. This is a reasonable and permissible application of state law, and it does not deprive Clark of due process. *See *Mullaney v. Wilbur*, 421 U.S. 684, 695 (1975) (“The Due Process Clause does not require a State to adopt any particular method of allocating the burden of proof.”).

Clark’s claim fails under the standard set forth in *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687 (1984): he cannot demonstrate that the state court’s ruling deprived him of a fundamental right or resulted in a fundamentally unfair trial. The trial court did not bar Clark from presenting any evidence of his mental illness; it merely limited the permissible use of that evidence under Arizona law. Clark was free to present expert testimony to support his insanity defense, and he did so. The fact that the jury rejected that defense is not a due process violation.

For these reasons, the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals must be affirmed. The Fourteenth Amendment does not require a state to allow a defendant to use expert mental health evidence to rebut the prosecution’s burden of proving criminal intent unless that evidence is offered in support of a recognized defense. Arizona law, properly applied, did not violate Clark’s constitutional rights.

**WHEREFORE**, the respondent respectfully requests that this Court affirm the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals.
