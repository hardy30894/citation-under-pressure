The withdrawal of a conspirator from the unlawful agreement terminates his liability for subsequent acts of his former associates. Hyde v. United States, 225 U.S. 347, 369 (1912). Once that withdrawal occurs before the limitations period, the government must prove the defendant's continued membership in the conspiracy during the period for which prosecution is timely. United States v. Kissel, 218 U.S. 601, 607 (1910). The prosecution therefore bears the burden of persuasion on that element beyond a reasonable doubt. Coffin v. United States, 156 U.S. 432, 453 (1895).

A statute of limitations in a criminal case is not a mere procedural formality; it is a substantive limitation upon the power to prosecute. Bridges v. United States, 346 U.S. 209, 218 (1953). The government cannot shift to the defendant the obligation to establish his own non-membership after withdrawal. Davis v. United States, 160 U.S. 469, 487 (1895). The presumption of innocence remains with the accused throughout the trial and requires the prosecution to prove every essential element of the offense charged. Id. at 488.

Proof that a conspiracy existed at an earlier time does not, without more, establish its continuation into the limitations period. Grunewald v. United States, 353 U.S. 391, 399 (1957). Acts and declarations of co-conspirators after a defendant's withdrawal are inadmissible against him. Krulewitch v. United States, 336 U.S. 440, 442 (1949). The prosecution must therefore adduce independent evidence linking the defendant to the conspiracy during the actionable period. Lutwak v. United States, 344 U.S. 604, 617 (1953).

The same principle governs vicarious liability. A conspirator is responsible only for acts committed in furtherance of the conspiracy while he remains a member. Pinkerton v. United States, 328 U.S. 640, 645 (1946). Once withdrawal is shown, the government must prove re-entry or continued participation to hold the defendant accountable for later offenses. Dennis v. United States, 341 U.S. 494, 499 (1951). Failure to carry that burden requires acquittal on any count resting solely on post-withdrawal conduct. Yates v. United States, 354 U.S. 298, 312 (1957).

These settled rules protect the accused against stale prosecutions and ensure that criminal liability rests on proof, not presumption. The court of appeals erred in relieving the government of its constitutional obligation.
