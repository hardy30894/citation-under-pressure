ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Plainly Erred by Admitting Agent Jones’s Expert Testimony on Stanley’s Credibility and Guilt.

Expert witnesses may not opine on the credibility of other witnesses. United States v. Samara, 643 F.2d 701, 705 (10th Cir. 1981) (“The credibility of witnesses is exclusively for the jury.”). Nor may they “usurp the exclusive function of the jury to weigh the evidence and determine credibility.” United States v. Charley, 189 F.3d 1251, 1267 (10th Cir. 1999) (quoting United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 142 (2d Cir. 1988)).

Agent Jones testified that Stanley’s answers “did not make sense,” displayed “evasive behaviors common among the criminal element,” and that “Never in my career have I seen that with an innocent person.” He further opined that invoking faith to validate statements constitutes deception. This testimony directly assessed Stanley’s truthfulness and guilt, exceeding the bounds of proper expert opinion under Rule 702. See Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc., 509 U.S. 579, 589-92 (1993).

Because Stanley did not object, review is for plain error. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725, 732-34 (1993). The error is plain under Samara and Charley. It affected Stanley’s substantial rights by bolstering the government’s case on the central issue of his involvement and credibility. See United States v. Hill, 749 F.3d 1250, 1263-64 (10th Cir. 2014). The testimony seriously affected the fairness and integrity of the proceedings by placing the imprimatur of an FBI expert on the ultimate question of guilt. Reversal is therefore required.
