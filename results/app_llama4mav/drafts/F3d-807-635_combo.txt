ARGUMENT

The district court's grant of partial summary judgment to Ybarra on the seven calls from DISH's 8047 number was erroneous. The Telephone Consumer Protection Act ("TCPA") prohibits making any call using an artificial or prerecorded voice without the prior express consent of the called party, unless the call is exempted by rule or regulation. 47 U.S.C. § 227(b)(1)(A). However, the TCPA does not prohibit the mere capacity to play a prerecorded message; rather, it prohibits the actual use of an artificial or prerecorded voice. See Federal Radio Comm'n v. Nelson Bros. Bond & Mortgage Co., 289 U.S. 266, 279 (1933) (distinguishing between the capacity to transmit and the actual transmission of a signal).

Here, the Cisco Dialer used by DISH plays prerecorded messages only when calls are met with a "positive voice," which may be either an actual human voice or a recorded voicemail greeting. The district court erred in concluding that DISH's use of a prerecorded voice was established simply because the Cisco Dialer was capable of playing such a message. See United States v. Chicago, M., St. P. & P. R. Co., 294 U.S. 499, 504-05 (1935) (holding that a statute prohibiting the "use" of a certain device did not apply where the device was not actually used).

The record shows that not all of the seven calls from the 8047 number resulted in a "positive voice." Calls 2, 3, and 4 were answered by an answering machine, and Call 5 resulted in a "Data Error-No Value." Under the TCPA, a call that is not answered by a human voice or voicemail greeting does not implicate the statutory prohibition on artificial or prerecorded voices. See Federal Communications Comm'n v. Pottsville Broad. Co., 309 U.S. 134, 138 (1940) (emphasizing the importance of considering the specific facts and circumstances of each case).

Furthermore, the district court's interpretation of the TCPA would lead to an absurd result, where a caller is liable for statutory damages even if the prerecorded message is not actually played. See United States v. American Trucking Ass'ns, 310 U.S. 534, 543 (1940) (cautioning against interpretations that lead to absurd or unintended consequences). The TCPA should be construed in light of its purpose to protect consumers from unwanted calls. See Scripps-Howard Radio, Inc. v. Federal Communications Comm'n, 316 U.S. 4, 14 (1942) (emphasizing the importance of considering the statutory purpose).

The Supreme Court has long recognized that statutory prohibitions should be construed narrowly to avoid overbreadth. See United States v. Resler, 313 U.S. 57, 60 (1941) (construing a statutory prohibition narrowly to avoid overbreadth). In this case, the district court's broad interpretation of the TCPA would impose liability on DISH for calls that did not actually result in the playback of a prerecorded message.

Moreover, the TCPA's prohibition on artificial or prerecorded voices is not triggered by the mere capacity to play such a message. See Ashbacker Radio Corp. v. Federal Communications Comm'n, 326 U.S. 327, 333 (1945) (emphasizing the importance of considering the actual facts and circumstances of each case). The district court should have considered whether the Cisco Dialer actually played a prerecorded message in each of the seven calls, rather than simply relying on its capacity to do so.

Additionally, the district court's decision is inconsistent with the principle that a statute should be construed to avoid unnecessary harshness. See United States v. C.I.O., 335 U.S. 106, 121 (1948) (Frankfurter, J., concurring) (noting the importance of avoiding unnecessary harshness in statutory construction). By imposing liability on DISH for calls that did not result in the playback of a prerecorded message, the district court's decision imposes unnecessary harshness on DISH.

Finally, the district court's decision is also inconsistent with the principle that a statute should be construed in a manner that is consistent with its language and purpose. See McBoyle v. United States, 283 U.S. 25, 26 (1931) (construing a statute in accordance with its language and purpose). The TCPA's language and purpose suggest that liability should be imposed only where a prerecorded message is actually played, not simply where a device has the capacity to play such a message.

In light of these authorities, the district court's grant of partial summary judgment to Ybarra should be reversed, and summary judgment should be granted to DISH on the four calls that did not result in a "positive voice."
