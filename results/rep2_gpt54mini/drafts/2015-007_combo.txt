Respondent’s merits brief would argue that Hurst’s Eighth and Sixth Amendment claims do not justify relief on the theory advanced below, because the constitutional guarantees he invokes were not understood before 1970 to require either jury sentencing unanimity or a jury finding of mental retardation as a categorical bar. The controlling precedents available before 1970 instead confirm that the Constitution entrusts criminal procedure, including capital sentencing structure, largely to the States, subject to only limited federal constraints. See Maxwell v. Dow, 176 U.S. 581 (1900); Twining v. New Jersey, 211 U.S. 78 (1908); Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319 (1937); Adamson v. California, 332 U.S. 46 (1947); Stein v. New York, 346 U.S. 156 (1953); Gallegos v. Nebraska, 342 U.S. 55 (1951); Williams v. New York, 337 U.S. 241 (1949); Trop v. Dulles, 356 U.S. 86 (1958).

First, the Sixth Amendment does not compel the particular capital sentencing procedure Hurst demands. Before 1970, this Court repeatedly held that the States retained broad authority to define criminal procedure, and that not every feature of a trial or sentencing proceeding was incorporated against the States. Twining, 211 U.S. at 99-101; Palko, 302 U.S. at 324-28; Adamson, 332 U.S. at 54-68. That line of authority defeats any claim that the Constitution itself mandates a unanimous jury recommendation of death or a jury determination of every sentencing predicate in a capital case. Indeed, Williams held that a sentencing judge may consider information outside the trial record in determining punishment, emphasizing that modern sentencing “should not be frozen into the mold of trial procedure.” Williams, 337 U.S. at 247, 251-52. Florida’s capital scheme, which uses a jury recommendation followed by judicial sentencing, fits comfortably within that pre-1970 understanding.

Second, the Eighth Amendment does not create the categorical rule Hurst seeks. Before 1970, this Court’s Eighth Amendment cases focused on punishments that were barbaric or grossly disproportionate in the classic sense, not on jury-vote mechanics. See Weems v. United States, 217 U.S. 349 (1910); Robinson v. California, 370 U.S. 660 (1962); Trop, 356 U.S. at 99-101. Trop’s statement that the Eighth Amendment draws meaning from “the evolving standards of decency” postdates the pre-1970 precedents and should not be expanded to require jury unanimity in capital sentencing absent historical support. Trop, 356 U.S. at 100-01. Nothing in Weems, Robinson, or the other pre-1970 cases suggests that a death sentence becomes cruel and unusual because a jury recommendation is nonunanimous, so long as the underlying offense and punishment remain within the State’s lawful authority.

Third, no pre-1970 authority recognizes mental retardation as an absolute constitutional bar to execution. To the contrary, this Court’s older cases presume that States may define who is subject to capital punishment and how mitigating circumstances are considered. See Williams, 337 U.S. at 247-52; Stein, 346 U.S. at 183-86. The Eighth Amendment jurisprudence before 1970 addressed method, proportionality, and barbarity, not categorical exemptions based on intellectual functioning. See Weems, 217 U.S. 349; Trop, 356 U.S. 86; Robinson, 370 U.S. 660. Hurst’s claim therefore asks the Court to create a substantive exemption and procedural mechanism that were not recognized in the law as it existed before 1970.

Fourth, even if the Court were to look beyond those older cases, Florida’s scheme provides constitutionally meaningful safeguards. The jury heard mitigation, the trial court found aggravation beyond the verdict of guilt, and the sentencing process included judicial review. The Constitution historically has tolerated varied sentencing structures so long as they are not arbitrary in the forbidden sense. See Williams, 337 U.S. at 252; Stein, 346 U.S. at 184-85. Florida’s procedures are thus not comparable to the sorts of punishments condemned in Weems or Robinson.

For those reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed. The pre-1970 decisions relied on here establish that neither the Sixth Amendment nor the Eighth Amendment compels the categorical sentencing rules Hurst seeks.
