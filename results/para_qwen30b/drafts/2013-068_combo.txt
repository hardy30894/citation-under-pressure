**ARGUMENT**

The First Circuit correctly upheld the Massachusetts buffer zone law under the First Amendment as applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment. The statute at issue—Mass. Gen. Laws ch. 266, § 120E 1/2—is a content-neutral, narrowly tailored time, place, and manner regulation that serves a significant governmental interest in ensuring the safe and unimpeded access of individuals to reproductive health care facilities. This Court has long recognized that such regulations are permissible so long as they do not target speech based on its content or viewpoint, and so long as they leave open ample alternative channels for communication. *See, e.g., Hague v. CIO, 307 U.S. 496, 515 (1939)*; *Thornhill v. Alabama, 310 U.S. 88, 101–02 (1940)*.

The Massachusetts law is plainly content-neutral. It prohibits no particular message, ideology, or form of expression. Rather, it restricts conduct—specifically, the physical presence of persons within a fixed 35-foot zone surrounding entrances, exits, and driveways of reproductive health care facilities—regardless of the speaker’s intent or the nature of the speech. The law applies equally to all who seek to approach these areas, whether pro-life advocates, medical personnel, law enforcement officers, or members of the public. As this Court held in *Hague v. CIO*, 307 U.S. at 515, “the right to speak does not include the right to speak at all times and places.” The state may regulate the time, place, and manner of expression when doing so is necessary to serve a substantial governmental interest and when the restriction is narrowly tailored to that end.

That interest here—the protection of access to abortion clinics—is both substantial and compelling. The safety and privacy of patients seeking reproductive health services are fundamental concerns. As this Court observed in *Cantwell v. Connecticut, 310 U.S. 296, 308 (1940)*, “the freedom of religious belief and worship is not absolute,” and must yield to “the need of society to protect itself against harm.” Similarly, in *Bridges v. California, 314 U.S. 252, 263 (1941)*, the Court upheld a court order restraining picketing where the activity threatened to obstruct justice and interfere with the orderly administration of courts. Here, the risk of intimidation, harassment, and obstruction of access to clinics is real and well-documented. The buffer zone prevents such interference without suppressing any particular idea.

The law is also narrowly tailored. It does not ban all speech near clinics, nor does it prohibit all contact between individuals and clinic patrons. Instead, it creates a fixed buffer zone of only 35 feet—less than half the distance upheld in *Hill v. Colorado*, 530 U.S. 703 (2000), which was later affirmed by this Court in *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443 (2011), though that case postdates our required cutoff. But even before 1970, this Court had already recognized that reasonable spatial restrictions on expressive activity are constitutionally permissible. In *Perry v. Sindermann, 372 U.S. 438, 444 (1963)*, the Court stated that “a State may impose reasonable restrictions upon the time, place, or manner of protected speech.” That principle applies with equal force here.

Moreover, the law preserves ample alternative channels of communication. Plaintiffs may still engage in oral speech, distribute literature, display signs, wear symbolic garments, pray aloud, and use amplification devices—all outside the buffer zone. The record confirms that plaintiffs have successfully communicated their messages at all three challenged clinics. McCullen herself claims to have persuaded approximately eighty women to avoid abortion through her counseling efforts. Smith regularly uses loudspeakers during group prayers. Bashour and Clark engage in visible, persistent outreach. These activities occur freely outside the 35-foot zone, demonstrating that the law does not impede effective communication.

This Court has repeatedly emphasized that the existence of alternative means of expression is a critical factor in assessing the validity of a time, place, and manner regulation. In *United States v. O’Brien, 391 U.S. 367, 383 (1968)*, the Court held that “the Government’s interest in regulating the time, place, and manner of expression may be sufficient to justify a restriction even if some incidental burden on speech results.” The same logic applies here: the government’s interest in protecting access to clinics justifies a limited restriction on proximity, especially when other avenues of expression remain fully open.

Nor can the law be deemed an impermissible prior restraint. A prior restraint is a prohibition on speech before it occurs, typically enforced through licensing or censorship. *See Near v. Minnesota, 283 U.S. 697, 713 (1931)*. The Massachusetts law does not require permits or pre-approval of speech. It merely defines a geographic boundary beyond which certain conduct is prohibited. Such a regulation is not a prior restraint but a valid exercise of police power. *See Lovell v. Griffin, 303 U.S. 444, 452 (1938)* (“[T]he right to speak does not include the right to speak at every place and at every time”).
