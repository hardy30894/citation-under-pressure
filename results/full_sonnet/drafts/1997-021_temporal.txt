**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board's "Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt" Standard for Polling Cannot Be Reconciled With the Rights This Court Has Recognized Under the Act.**

The National Labor Relations Act protects both an employer's right to insist that any bargaining representative in fact enjoy majority support and the employees' right to select or reject representation free of coercion. See NLRB v. Gissel Packing Co., 395 U.S. 575, 596–600 (1969). This Court in Gissel recognized that an employer confronted with disputed evidence of union support may test that claim through means less disruptive than outright withdrawal of recognition, including resort to the Board's election machinery, precisely because forcing an employer to choose only between full recognition and unlawful withdrawal disserves the Act's core purpose of ensuring that bargaining representatives actually represent employee sentiment. Id. at 594.

A poll conducted under the procedural safeguards long approved by the Board itself serves that same protective function. See Struksnes Construction Co., 165 N.L.R.B. 1062, 1063 (1967) (approving employer polling where conducted by secret ballot, with assurances against reprisal, and for the sole purpose of determining truth of a union's claim of majority). Yet the Board's rule condemns an employer for taking the very step — a secret, professionally supervised poll — that most reliably protects employees from the coercion the Act forbids, unless the employer already possesses evidence sufficient to justify the more drastic step of withdrawing recognition outright. That standard is irrational: it treats the more moderate and more protective course as if it required the same showing as the more extreme one.

This Court has consistently required that an administrative agency's rule be a reasoned one, connected to the policies the agency is charged with enforcing. See NLRB v. Metropolitan Life Ins. Co., 380 U.S. 438, 443–44 (1965) (Board must articulate a rational basis for the distinctions it draws under the Act); SEC v. Chenery Corp., 332 U.S. 194, 196–97 (1947) (an agency's order must be sustainable on the reasoning the agency itself provides). The Board's polling standard fails that test. It cannot be squared with the presumption, recognized in Brooks v. NLRB, 348 U.S. 96, 103–04 (1954), that a certified union's majority status, while entitled to a presumption of continuity, is a *rebuttable* presumption — one that yields once the employer produces evidence casting genuine doubt on continuing majority support. See also Franks Bros. Co. v. NLRB, 321 U.S. 702, 705 (1944) (recognizing that the presumption of majority support exists to promote stability, not to insulate a union that may no longer represent the employees). A rule that permits an employer to act on such doubt only if the doubt is strong enough to justify abandoning the presumption altogether collapses the distinction between rebutting the presumption for purposes of inquiry and rebutting it for purposes of final action. The Board's own precedent under Struksnes contemplated polling as a measured, intermediate response to uncertainty — not as a redundant formality available only to employers who no longer need to ask the question.

**II. The Board's Finding That Petitioner Lacked a Good-Faith Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence in the Record as a Whole.**

Even accepting the Board's standard, its finding cannot stand because it is not supported by substantial evidence. This Court has long held that judicial review of Board fact-finding requires more than a search for isolated support; the reviewing court must examine "the record in its entirety, including the body of evidence opposed to the Board's view." Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). Substantial evidence is evidence a reasonable mind would accept as adequate to support the conclusion reached, not merely evidence consistent with it. See Consolidated Edison Co. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938) (substantial evidence means "such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion"); NLRB v. Columbian Enameling & Stamping Co., 306 U.S. 292, 300 (1939) (reviewing court must set aside Board findings unsupported by the record as a whole).

Here, the record contained direct statements from multiple bargaining-unit employees disclaiming continued support for Local 724, together with a supervisor's and a steward's reports reflecting broader dissatisfaction among coworkers. The ALJ and the Board discounted several of these statements not because they were incredible, but by applying categorical rules of exclusion — refusing to consider statements made during job interviews, statements of dissatisfaction with dues, and secondhand reports of coworker sentiment, regardless of their individual reliability. That approach does not weigh the evidence "in its entirety," Universal Camera, 340 U.S. at 488; it discards relevant evidence by rule rather than by reasoned assessment of its probative weight. When the improperly excluded evidence is restored to the balance, the record does not permit the conclusion that petitioner's doubt about the union's majority status was anything but genuine and reasonably grounded.

**Conclusion**

For these reasons, the Board's polling standard cannot be reconciled with the Act's protection of employer inquiry into disputed union support, and, in any event, the Board's finding that petitioner lacked a good-faith reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole.
