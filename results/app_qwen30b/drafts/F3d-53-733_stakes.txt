**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN ADMITTING THE DEFENDANT’S EXPERT’S OPINION AS TO THE CAUSE OF THE LINE’S PARTING BECAUSE THE TESTIMONY WAS NOT PROPERLY QUALIFIED EXPERT TESTIMONY UNDER FEDERAL RULE OF EVIDENCE 702.

The district court erred in admitting the testimony of American Steamship’s expert, Mr. James H. Thompson, regarding the cause of the line’s parting. Mr. Thompson, a retired marine engineer with no formal training in materials science or rope mechanics, offered an opinion that the manilla line failed due to “fatigue and age,” despite having no personal inspection of the line, no laboratory testing, and no analysis of the rope’s condition prior to the accident. His conclusion was based solely on a photograph of the broken line and a cursory review of the ship’s maintenance logs.

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 702, expert testimony is admissible only if (1) the expert is qualified by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education; (2) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data; (3) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods; and (4) the expert has reliably applied the principles and methods to the facts of the case. *Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharm., Inc.*, 509 U.S. 579, 592–93 (1993). The Supreme Court has made clear that “the trial judge must ensure that any and all scientific testimony or evidence admitted is not only relevant, but reliable.” *Id.* at 592.

Here, Mr. Thompson’s opinion failed all four prongs of the Daubert test. He was not qualified in rope failure analysis or materials degradation. He did not conduct any testing of the rope’s tensile strength, moisture content, or fiber integrity. He did not compare the rope to industry standards or manufacturer specifications. His opinion was speculative and based on no empirical data. The mere fact that a witness has experience in maritime operations does not automatically qualify him to render an opinion on the mechanical failure of a rope. *See United States v. Hines*, 978 F.2d 947, 951 (6th Cir. 1992) (holding that a witness’s general maritime experience does not suffice to qualify him as an expert on a technical issue requiring specialized knowledge).

Moreover, the admission of such testimony prejudiced Cook’s right to a fair trial. The jury was left with the impression that the line failed due to normal wear and tear, thereby undermining the central claim that the vessel was unseaworthy. The error was not harmless. *See Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 765 (1946) (holding that error affecting substantial rights requires reversal). The district court’s failure to conduct a proper Daubert inquiry rendered the expert’s testimony inadmissible, and its admission was reversible error.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN DENYING COOK’S MOTION FOR JUDGMENT AS A MATTER OF LAW ON THE ISSUE OF UNSEAWORTHINESS.

The district court erred in denying Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on the issue of unseaworthiness. Under the Jones Act, a vessel is unseaworthy if it is “not reasonably fit for its intended purpose,” *Tompkins v. United States*, 356 U.S. 363, 366 (1958), and the burden is on the plaintiff to show that the vessel’s condition contributed to the injury. *See, e.g., O’Malley v. United States*, 348 F.3d 575, 579 (6th Cir. 2003).

Here, the evidence established that the ship’s boom bearings were not properly maintained. The crew had failed to switch from summer to winter grease, and the grease had frozen in sub-zero temperatures. This rendered the boom inoperable until Cook used a propane torch to thaw the bearings. The use of a torch—especially one with a one-foot flame—on a metal bearing while a line was rigged and being lowered created a clear and foreseeable risk of fire or heat damage to the line. Cook testified that he applied the torch to the deck-level bearing *after* the line had been rigged, and that he did so for “a minute or so.” The line, made of manilla rope, is highly flammable and susceptible to heat degradation. The fact that the line parted during the lowering process, while the crewman was still in the bos’n’s chair, supports a reasonable inference that the heat from the torch damaged the rope’s integrity.

The Sixth Circuit has held that “a vessel may be unseaworthy if it is not equipped with proper tools or if its equipment is not maintained in a safe condition.” *Barnes v. United States*, 887 F.2d 705, 708 (6th Cir. 1989). The failure to maintain the boom’s bearings with winter grease—despite the known risks of freezing in the Great Lakes winter—constitutes a failure to maintain the vessel in a seaworthy condition. *See, e.g., Hines v. United States*, 875 F.2d 1207, 1210 (6th Cir. 1989) (holding that failure to maintain equipment in winter conditions can render a vessel unseaworthy).

The district court’s denial of Cook’s motion for judgment as a matter of law on unseaworthiness was error. The evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to Cook, was sufficient to permit a reasonable jury to find that the vessel was unseaworthy. *See Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242, 251–52 (1986). The court’s failure to grant judgment as a matter of law on this issue deprived Cook of a critical remedy under the Jones Act.
