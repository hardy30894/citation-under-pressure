**ARGUMENT**

I. The First Amendment’s “true threat” exception requires proof that the speaker subjectively intended his statements to be understood as threats.

The Third Circuit erred in affirming Elonis’s conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) without requiring proof that he subjectively intended his Facebook posts to be taken as threats. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that the First Amendment protects even offensive or controversial speech unless it falls within a narrow exception. *Snyder v. Phelps*, 562 U.S. 443, 452 (2011) (“Speech is powerful. It can stir people to action, move them to tears of both joy and sorrow, and—as it did here—inflict great pain.”). The “true threat” exception is no exception to this rule. *Virginia v. Black*, 538 U.S. 343, 359 (2003) (“[T]he speaker must have intended the statement as a threat.”).

In *Black*, the Supreme Court addressed a Virginia statute criminalizing cross burnings when done with the intent to intimidate. The Court held that the First Amendment permits the proscription of “true threats,” but only when the speaker subjectively intends his statements to be understood as threats. *Id.* at 360. The Court rejected the argument that an objective standard—whether a reasonable person would perceive the statement as a threat—sufficed. *Id.* at 360-61. The Court explained that the First Amendment’s protections would be eviscerated if speakers could be punished for statements that a jury later deemed threatening, regardless of the speaker’s intent. *Id.* at 365.

The Third Circuit’s contrary rule—that a statement is a “true threat” if a reasonable speaker would foresee that the statement would be interpreted as a threat—is inconsistent with *Black*. *United States v. Kosma*, 951 F.2d 549, 557 (3d Cir. 1991). The Third Circuit’s standard conflates the First Amendment inquiry with the mens rea required for a criminal conviction. *Black* makes clear that the First Amendment demands a subjective intent to threaten before speech loses its constitutional protection. *Black*, 538 U.S. at 360.

II. The Third Circuit’s objective standard impermissibly criminalizes protected speech.

The Third Circuit’s objective standard risks punishing speakers for statements that were not intended as threats but were perceived as such by hypersensitive listeners. This approach contravenes the First Amendment’s core principle that speech may not be punished based on the reaction of the audience. *Terminiello v. City of Chicago*, 337 U.S. 1, 4 (1949) (“[A] function of free speech under our system of government is to invite dispute. It may indeed best serve its high purpose when it induces a condition of unrest, creates dissatisfaction with conditions as they are, or even stirs people to anger.”).

The Supreme Court has repeatedly cautioned against allowing the government to criminalize speech based on the subjective reactions of listeners. *Cohen v. California*, 403 U.S. 15, 21 (1971) (“One man’s vulgarity is another’s lyric.”). The Third Circuit’s standard invites exactly this kind of subjective, after-the-fact evaluation of speech, which the First Amendment forbids. *See also Watts v. United States*, 394 U.S. 705, 707 (1969) (per curiam) (reversing a conviction under a statute criminalizing threats against the President where the statement was made in a political hyperbole context).

III. The government’s interpretation of § 875(c) would criminalize a vast amount of protected speech.

Section 875(c) criminalizes the transmission of “any communication containing any threat to kidnap any person or any threat to injure the person of another.” 18 U.S.C. § 875(c). If the government’s objective standard were adopted, § 875(c) would criminalize a vast array of protected speech, from political hyperbole to artistic expression. *See, e.g., Watts*, 394 U.S. at 707 (political hyperbole); *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46, 55 (1988) (satire and parody).

The government’s interpretation would chill protected speech by allowing prosecutors to target speakers based on the reactions of their audience, rather than the speaker’s intent. This chilling effect is precisely what the First Amendment was designed to prevent. *NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware Co.*, 458 U.S. 886, 910 (1982) (“[T]he First Amendment protects speech even when it is controversial or deeply offensive to many.”).

IV. The Third Circuit’s standard conflicts with the mens rea required for criminal liability.

The Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized that criminal statutes must require proof of a culpable mental state. *Staples v. United States*, 511 U.S. 600, 605 (1994) (“[W]e have noted that when Congress creates a new crime ‘it presumptively intends to require a defendant to possess a culpable mental state.’” (quoting *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 438 U.S. 422, 436 (1978))). The Third Circuit’s objective standard for “true threats” dispenses with this requirement, allowing convictions based solely on the perceptions of listeners rather than the speaker’s intent.

This approach is inconsistent with the Supreme Court’s recent decision in *Elonis v. United States*, 575 U.S. 723 (2015), which vacated a conviction under § 875(c) and remanded for further consideration in light of the Court’s intervening decision in *Black*. The Supreme Court’s decision in *Elonis* underscores the importance of requiring proof of subjective intent in § 875(c) prosecutions.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the judgment of the Third Circuit and remand with instructions to vacate Elonis’s conviction.
