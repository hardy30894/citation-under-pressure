ARGUMENT

The Double Jeopardy Clause bars retrial of the prior-conviction allegation once a reviewing court has found the evidence insufficient. In *Bullington v. Missouri*, 451 U.S. 430 (1981), the Court held that the Clause applies to a capital sentencing proceeding that “has the hallmarks of the trial on guilt or innocence.” *Id.* at 439. The proceeding there required the prosecution to prove statutory aggravating circumstances beyond a reasonable doubt, subjected the defendant to the full array of trial protections, and produced a binary verdict that either imposed or withheld the death penalty. *Id.* at 437-38. Because the State had failed to carry its burden, the Clause foreclosed a second attempt. *Id.* at 446.

*Arizona v. Rumsey*, 467 U.S. 203 (1984), confirmed that the same rule governs whenever a sentencing hearing “resemble[s] a trial for purposes of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” *Id.* at 209. The Court emphasized that the decisive factor is not the label “sentencing” but the procedural character of the hearing: the prosecution’s burden, the rules of evidence, and the requirement of a specific factual finding. *Id.* at 209-10.

California’s section 1025 proceeding shares these essential features. The prosecution must prove the existence and seriousness of a prior conviction beyond a reasonable doubt. *People v. Tenner*, 6 Cal. 4th 559, 566 (1993). The defendant enjoys the rights to counsel, confrontation, and cross-examination, and the rules of evidence apply. *People v. Reed*, 13 Cal. 4th 217, 224, 228 n.6 (1996). The court must render a binary finding—true or not true—on each allegation. These procedures convert the hearing into the functional equivalent of a trial on an element that increases the statutory maximum. Once the Court of Appeal determined that the evidence was insufficient, the State was not entitled to a second opportunity to meet its burden.

The distinction drawn between capital and noncapital proceedings is untenable. *Bullington* and *Rumsey* rested on the procedural safeguards afforded, not on the severity of the penalty. Nothing in the Double Jeopardy Clause’s text or history limits its protection to capital cases. *See North Carolina v. Pearce*, 395 U.S. 711, 717 (1969). Because the California hearing is materially indistinguishable from the proceedings in *Bullington* and *Rumsey*, the Clause precludes retrial.
