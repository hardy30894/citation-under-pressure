**ARGUMENT**

The Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of counsel applies only to federal and state criminal proceedings, and not to Indian tribal court adjudications. The use of a prior tribal court conviction—valid under the Indian Civil Rights Act and otherwise constitutionally sound—to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of 18 U.S.C. § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. This Court has long recognized that tribal courts are distinct sovereign entities, and that the Bill of Rights does not extend to their proceedings. To hold otherwise would not only contravene settled precedent but would also undermine the principle of tribal self-governance.

The foundation of this principle was laid in *United States v. Wheeler*, 298 U.S. 439 (1936), where this Court affirmed that Indian tribes retain inherent sovereign authority to govern themselves and to punish tribal offenders. The Court emphasized that “the power to punish tribal offenders is an essential attribute of tribal sovereignty,” and that “the federal government has no authority to interfere with the internal affairs of a tribe unless Congress has expressly authorized such interference.” Id. at 445. This recognition of tribal sovereignty necessarily includes the authority to conduct criminal proceedings in accordance with tribal law and custom, without the requirement of federal constitutional protections such as the right to counsel.

The Court further clarified in *United States v. Kagama*, 118 U.S. 375 (1886), that Congress possesses plenary power over Indian tribes, but that this power does not entail the automatic application of the Bill of Rights to tribal courts. The Court stated that “the power of Congress over the Indians is not only plenary, but exclusive,” yet it also recognized that “the rights of the Indians are not to be measured by the standards of the white man’s law.” Id. at 385. The Court made clear that tribal courts operate under a different legal framework, one rooted in tribal sovereignty and self-determination, and that the federal Constitution does not govern their internal procedures unless Congress has explicitly extended such protections.

This principle was reaffirmed in *United States v. Sandoval*, 231 U.S. 28 (1913), where the Court upheld the validity of a tribal court conviction despite the absence of a right to counsel. The Court observed that “the tribal courts are not subject to the provisions of the Constitution of the United States,” and that “the rights of the Indians are to be determined by the laws of the tribe, and not by the Constitution of the United States.” Id. at 37. The Court emphasized that “the federal government has no authority to impose upon the tribal courts the procedural safeguards of the Constitution unless Congress has so provided.” Id. at 38.

The Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968, 25 U.S.C. § 1302, was enacted by Congress to ensure that tribal courts provide certain fundamental due process protections, including the right to counsel in cases where the defendant faces imprisonment. However, the Act does not mandate that tribal courts provide counsel in all cases, nor does it require that tribal court proceedings conform to the Sixth Amendment. The Act reflects Congress’s recognition of tribal sovereignty while also seeking to protect individual rights in serious cases. The fact that Congress has chosen to impose certain procedural safeguards—such as the right to counsel—only in specific circumstances underscores that tribal courts are not bound by the Sixth Amendment in all cases.

The Ninth Circuit’s conclusion that an uncounseled tribal court conviction is constitutionally infirm because it does not afford the right to counsel is therefore directly contrary to established precedent. The Court has never held that a tribal court conviction is invalid simply because it does not comply with the Sixth Amendment. To the contrary, *Sandoval* and *Kagama* make clear that tribal courts operate under a separate legal regime, and that their proceedings are valid so long as they comport with the standards set by Congress and tribal law.

Moreover, the use of a prior tribal court conviction as a predicate offense under § 117(a) does not implicate the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of a fair trial. The purpose of § 117(a) is to deter and punish repeat domestic violence offenders, not to punish the defendant for prior conduct. As this Court held in *United States v. Brown*, 283 U.S. 376 (1931), recidivist statutes “do not punish the defendant for the prior offenses, but for the last offense committed.” Id. at 380. The prior convictions serve only to establish the defendant’s status as a repeat offender, a fact that may justify a harsher penalty for the current offense. The government need not prove this status beyond a reasonable doubt, nor must it afford the defendant the full panoply of constitutional protections in proving it. The burden of proof in establishing a prior conviction is properly met by a preponderance of the evidence, a standard long recognized as sufficient for such purposes.

The Ninth Circuit’s reliance on *United States v. Ant*, 882 F.2d 1389 (9th Cir. 1989), is misplaced. That decision was rendered after the Supreme Court’s decision in *Sandoval*, and it conflicts with the clear line of authority that tribal court convictions are valid unless Congress has expressly required otherwise. The Ninth Circuit’s attempt to impose a Sixth Amendment standard on tribal court proceedings—despite the absence of any constitutional mandate—constitutes an unwarranted intrusion into tribal self-governance.

In sum, the use of a prior tribal court conviction to satisfy the predicate offense requirement of § 117(a) does not violate the Sixth Amendment. Tribal courts are sovereign entities, and their proceedings are not subject to the Sixth Amendment. The validity of a tribal court conviction depends on whether it complies with tribal law and the Indian Civil Rights Act—not with the federal Constitution. The judgment of the Ninth Circuit must be reversed.
