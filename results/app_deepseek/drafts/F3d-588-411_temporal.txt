**ARGUMENT**

**A. The District Court’s Supplemental Instruction on “Shielding” Was Erroneous**

The district court defined “shielding” for the jury as “any conduct that tends to substantially increase the difficulty of their detection by the government.” That instruction invaded the jury’s fact-finding role and diluted the government’s burden of proof. The term “shields from detection” in 8 U.S.C. § 1324(a)(1)(A)(iii) is a statutory word of art that must be construed strictly. *United States v. Wiltberger*, 18 U.S. (5 Wheat.) 76, 95 (1820) (“Penal laws are to be construed strictly.”); *United States v. Lacher*, 134 U.S. 624, 628 (1890) (same). A vague, non-statutory gloss—especially one that focuses only on “difficulty” of detection—impermissibly broadens the offense.

The critical element of the crime is not merely making detection harder; it is conduct specifically intended to *prevent* or *evade* detection by government officials. The court’s definition omitted any requirement of intent to thwart official inquiry. Consequently, the instruction allowed the jury to convict based on ordinary business practices (e.g., paying cash, not keeping time cards) that might incidentally make detection more difficult, even if no purpose to hide from authorities existed. *Cf. United States v. Noveck*, 271 U.S. 201, 203 (1926) (jury instruction must not mislead as to essential elements of offense).

Ye objected to any definition being given, but the district court erred by supplying a supplemental instruction that was both overbroad and insufficiently tethered to the statutory language. When a statutory term is clear and commonplace, a court should not “invade the province of the jury” by supplying an expansive, judge-made definition. *Sparf v. United States*, 156 U.S. 51, 74 (1895). Here, “shields from detection” is not so ambiguous that a jury needed a supplemental explanation; the district court's instruction effectively created a new, more lenient standard for conviction. *See Connally v. General Constr. Co.*, 269 U.S. 385, 391–92 (1926) (a penal statute must “give fair warning” of the prohibited conduct; an overbroad instruction fails that standard). The instruction thus violated Ye’s right to a fair trial under the Due Process Clause.

**B. The Evidence Was Insufficient to Prove Intent to Shield from Detection**

Even if the instruction were proper, the evidence at trial failed to establish that Ye acted with the specific intent to “shield” his employees “from detection” by the government. The government’s case showed that Ye knowingly hired illegal aliens, failed to complete I-9 forms, paid cash, and provided housing. But none of that conduct, standing alone, demonstrates an intention to prevent the government from discovering the illegal workers. The test for sufficiency is whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, a rational jury could find every element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Glasser v. United States*, 315 U.S. 60, 80 (1942). The government must prove “affirmative conduct calculated to evade detection.” *United States v. Manton*, 107 F.2d 834, 839 (2d Cir. 1938) (interpreting analogous statute).

Here, Ye did not hide his employees. He did not instruct them to lie or conceal their status. Government agents easily observed the workers on multiple occasions. Ye admitted their illegal status and even met with investigators, acknowledging the lack of I-9 forms. Such openness is inconsistent with an intent to shield. The mere failure to maintain paperwork, while evidence of the hiring misdemeanor, does not alone prove the specific intent required for the felony of shielding. *Holland v. United States*, 348 U.S. 121, 139–40 (1954) (evidence must exclude every reasonable hypothesis other than guilt). To allow a conviction on these facts would collapse the distinction between the two counts of the indictment and eliminate the mens rea element of “shielding from detection.” The verdict on Count One must be reversed.
