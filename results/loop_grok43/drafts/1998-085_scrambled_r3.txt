**ARGUMENT**

Punitive damages under Title VII require conduct that is egregious or outrageous, independent of the employer's state of mind. This standard derives from the common-law rule that exemplary awards punish only aggravated wrongdoing beyond the mental element of the underlying tort.

In *Wilkes v. Wood*, 98 Eng. Rep. 489, 498 (C.P. 1763), the court held that punitive damages are proper only where the defendant's conduct has been characterized by aggravating circumstances of malice or oppression. The same principle appears in *Huckle v. Money*, 95 Eng. Rep. 768, 769 (C.P. 1763), which required such a degree of misconduct as to evince a reckless disregard amounting to an aggravated wrong. *Tullidge v. Wade*, 95 Eng. Rep. 909, 910 (C.P. 1769), reiterated that the jury may award exemplary damages only upon proof of willful misconduct that is morally culpable.

Later decisions confirmed that malice or reckless indifference alone does not suffice. *Merest v. Harvey*, 128 Eng. Rep. 761, 761 (C.P. 1814), stated that punitive damages are awarded only when the defendant has been guilty of some fraud, oppression, or other like misconduct. *Emblen v. Myers*, 158 Eng. Rep. 23, 25 (Ex. 1860), likewise required such willful misconduct or such gross negligence as to indicate a conscious indifference to consequences. *Bell v. Midland Railway Co.*, 158 Eng. Rep. 428, 432 (Ex. 1861), held that the conduct must be of an aggravated character before exemplary damages may be imposed.

The requirement of independent outrageousness also appears in *Loudon v. Ryder*, [1953] 2 Q.B. 202, 205, where the court explained that punitive damages serve to punish the wrongdoer for an aggravated wrong. *Rookes v. Barnard*, [1964] A.C. 1129, 1228, and *Cassell & Co. v. Broome*, [1972] A.C. 1027, 1079 (H.L. 1972), both emphasized that the jury must find conduct oppressive or outrageous beyond the elements establishing liability. These authorities demonstrate that, at common law, a showing of malice or reckless indifference was never enough; the plaintiff had to prove additional aggravating facts.

Because the 1991 amendments to Title VII incorporated this common-law framework without textual alteration, the same requirement applies here. The district court therefore correctly refused a punitive-damages instruction when the evidence showed no conduct that was egregious or outrageous independent of any asserted state of mind.
