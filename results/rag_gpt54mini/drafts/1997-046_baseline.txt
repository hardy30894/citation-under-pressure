**Argument**

I. **Woodard had no protected liberty or life interest in the clemency process itself**

The Due Process Clause protects interests recognized by the Constitution or created by law, but clemency is not one of them. In *Connecticut Board of Pardons v. Dumschat*, 452 U.S. 458 (1981), this Court held that a prisoner had no protected liberty interest in commutation where the Board could grant or deny relief in its discretion. The Court emphasized that “pardon and commutation decisions” have not “traditionally been the business of courts” and are “rarely, if ever, appropriate subjects for judicial review.” *Id.* at 464. That principle controls here.

Ohio’s clemency scheme is no less discretionary than the Connecticut scheme in *Dumschat*. Ohio’s governor retains the constitutional power to grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons, and the parole authority’s role is only investigative and advisory. As in *Jago v. Van Curen*, 454 U.S. 14 (1981), where Ohio parole remained wholly discretionary and created no protected liberty interest, *id.* at 20-21, Woodard cannot convert a discretionary clemency procedure into a constitutionally protected entitlement simply because the State has chosen to structure the process.

Nor does *Greenholtz v. Inmates of the Nebraska Penal & Correctional Complex*, 442 U.S. 1 (1979), help Woodard. *Greenholtz* found a liberty interest in parole because Nebraska’s statute used mandatory language and created an expectancy of release when specified criteria were met. *Id.* at 11-12. Ohio’s clemency process contains nothing comparable. It is a request for mercy, not a statutory entitlement. The same is true of *Board of Pardons v. Allen*, 482 U.S. 369 (1987), which again turned on mandatory parole-release language. *Id.* at 377-81. Clemency is different in kind: it is an act of grace, not a legally compelled release mechanism.

Woodard’s attempt to recast clemency as part of the “adjudicatory process” also fails. This Court has recognized due process protections in state-created procedures when they are integral to criminal adjudication, as in *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), and *Evitts v. Lucey*, 469 U.S. 387 (1985). But those cases involved direct review of criminal judgments, not executive mercy after judgment has become final. Clemency is not a stage of litigation; it is a separate executive prerogative. *Dumschat*, 452 U.S. at 464. The Constitution therefore does not impose substantive due process requirements on the choice whether to grant clemency or on the procedures the State uses to consider it.

II. **The voluntary interview option does not violate the Fifth Amendment**

Woodard also alleges that Ohio’s offer of a voluntary clemency interview compels self-incrimination. It does not. The Fifth Amendment forbids compelled testimonial self-incrimination; it does not prohibit the State from offering a prisoner an opportunity to speak in support of mercy. *United States v. Washington*, 431 U.S. 181 (1977), confirms that the privilege is concerned with compulsion, not every setting in which a person may choose to speak. There, the Court analyzed the adequacy of warnings and the voluntariness of grand-jury testimony; the inquiry turned on whether the witness was forced to forgo the privilege. *Id.* at 187-89. Here, by contrast, Ohio’s regulation makes the interview optional and does not require the prisoner to answer any question.

That is dispositive under *Baxter v. Palmigiano*, 425 U.S. 308 (1976). The Court held that the Constitution is not violated where a person is given a choice whether to testify, even if silence may carry practical consequences. *Id.* at 317-20. Likewise, in *Morrissey v. Brewer*, 408 U.S. 471 (1972), the Court recognized that due process in a discretionary post-conviction setting may be flexible, but it did not suggest that offering a hearing or interview itself creates unconstitutional compulsion. Ohio’s clemency interview is even less coercive than the settings in *Baxter* and *Washington* because the prisoner may decline the interview entirely or participate selectively.

Woodard’s “Hobson’s choice” theory therefore rests on speculation, not compulsion. A theoretical possibility that a prisoner’s voluntary remarks might someday be used in some future proceeding is too remote to establish a present Fifth Amendment violation. The Constitution does not require the State to withhold a voluntary opportunity for mercy merely because a prisoner fears that candor may be strategically unwise. As Judge Nelson correctly observed below, the regulation “does not require a waiver” and leaves the prisoner “free to say as much or as little” as he wishes.

III. **The Sixth Circuit’s remand should be reversed**

Because Woodard has no protected liberty interest in clemency and because Ohio’s voluntary interview procedure does not compel self-incrimination, the Sixth Circuit erred in remanding for further due process proceedings. *Dumschat*, *Jago*, and *Baxter* foreclose that result. The judgment should be reversed.
