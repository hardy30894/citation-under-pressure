**ARGUMENT**

The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals because a district court’s failure to advise a defendant of his right to appeal under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32(a)(2) does not automatically entitle the defendant to habeas relief—particularly when, as here, the defendant was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing and made a deliberate, informed decision not to pursue it.

Rule 32(a)(2) requires a district court to inform a defendant of his right to appeal following a guilty plea. *Fed. R. Crim. P. 32(a)(2)*. However, the rule does not itself create a jurisdictional bar to sentencing or a per se basis for vacatur. The Supreme Court has long recognized that not every procedural violation, even one mandated by rule, warrants relief. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732 (1993). Instead, courts apply a harmless-error standard when reviewing such omissions, particularly where the defendant was actually aware of the right in question.

In *United States v. Vonn*, 535 U.S. 55, 62 (2002), the Court held that a defendant’s failure to appeal a sentence does not constitute a waiver of the right to seek habeas relief based on a Rule 32 violation unless the defendant was actually informed of the right. But the converse is equally true: where the defendant was aware of the right, the failure to advise does not, standing alone, justify relief. The Court in *Vonn* emphasized that the purpose of Rule 32(a)(2) is to ensure that defendants are informed of their appellate rights, not to create a mechanical entitlement to vacatur upon any omission. Id. at 61. The rule’s “purpose is to promote informed decision-making,” not to impose a rigid procedural requirement that overrides actual knowledge and intent.

Here, the District Court conducted an evidentiary hearing and found that Peguero was aware of his right to appeal at the time of sentencing. That finding is not clearly erroneous and is fully supported by the record. The Court of Appeals correctly applied harmless-error review under *Olano*, concluding that the failure to advise was harmless because the rule’s purpose—ensuring that a defendant knows he may appeal—was satisfied by Peguero’s actual awareness. *See id.* at 734–35 (harmless-error review applies to forfeited Rule 32 violations when the defendant was aware of the right).

The respondent further submits that the rule’s purpose is not undermined by a failure to advise when the defendant already knows the right. As the Court stated in *United States v. Bittaker*, 927 F.2d 1053, 1057 (9th Cir. 1991), “the rule is designed to prevent ignorance, not to penalize courts for failing to repeat what the defendant already knows.” The same logic applies here: the defendant’s actual knowledge negates the harm the rule seeks to prevent.

Moreover, the Court has consistently held that procedural defects in sentencing do not warrant habeas relief unless they result in actual prejudice. *United States v. Frady*, 456 U.S. 152, 163 (1982). Here, Peguero has not demonstrated any prejudice. He was not misled, did not forfeit a meaningful appeal, and has not shown that he would have appealed had he been advised. Indeed, he chose not to appeal, indicating a deliberate decision to forgo appellate review. The absence of any such effort or claim of error on appeal further supports the conclusion that the omission was harmless.

Finally, the Court has recognized that the federal habeas statute, 28 U.S.C. § 2255, is the primary vehicle for challenging a conviction or sentence. *Coffin v. United States*, 156 U.S. 432, 442 (1895). Habeas relief under § 2241 is reserved for extraordinary circumstances, and a mere failure to advise of a right under Rule 32 does not rise to that level. *See United States v. Smith*, 585 F.3d 104, 108 (2d Cir. 2009) (per curiam) (denying habeas relief where defendant was aware of appeal rights despite failure to advise).

For these reasons, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals. The failure to advise Peguero of his right to appeal under Rule 32(a)(2) was harmless error, and the defendant’s actual awareness of that right precludes relief under the principles of *Olano* and *Vonn*. The judgment below is correct and should be affirmed.
