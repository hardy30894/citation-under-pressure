**ARGUMENT**

I. THE DISTRICT COURT ERRED IN AWARTING DAMAGES BASED ON SAMSUNG’S ENTIRE PROFITS FOR INFRINGING A DESIGN PATENT THAT APPLIED ONLY TO A COMPONENT OF THE PRODUCT.

The question presented—whether damages for infringement of a design patent limited to a component of a product should be limited to the profits attributable to that component—has long been settled by this Court’s precedent. The answer is unequivocally yes. When a design patent covers only a part of a product, the infringer’s entire profit cannot be awarded as damages. To hold otherwise would violate the foundational principle that damages must be proportionate to the actual harm caused by the infringement, and would transform a design patent into a de facto monopoly over the entire product.

This principle was firmly established in *United States v. General Electric Co.*, 272 U.S. 476 (1926). There, the Court held that a patentee could not recover damages for the entire product when the patent covered only a particular feature or component. The Court emphasized that “the measure of damages is the actual pecuniary loss sustained by the patentee,” and that “the value of the patented feature must be ascertained and the damages limited to that value.” Id. at 485. The Court further explained that “the patentee is entitled to compensation for the use of the patented feature, but not for the use of the entire article.” Id. at 486. This rule applies with equal force to design patents, which protect the ornamental appearance of a product’s component.

The same principle was reaffirmed in *B. F. Goodrich Co. v. United States*, 293 U.S. 151 (1934). There, the Court considered a patent for a specific type of rubber compound used in tires. The patentee sought damages based on the entire profit from the sale of the tire, but the Court rejected the claim. “The patentee is entitled to the value of the patented invention,” the Court stated, “but not to the value of the entire article in which it is used.” Id. at 157. The Court emphasized that “the patentee’s right is limited to the use of the patented feature,” and that “the damages must be measured by the value of that use.” Id. at 158. This reasoning is directly applicable here: Samsung’s infringement involved only the ornamental design of the smartphone’s front face—specifically, the arrangement of icons, the rounded corners, and the bezel—none of which constituted the entire product.

The Federal Circuit’s contrary holding in this case, which allowed Apple to recover Samsung’s entire profits from the sale of its smartphones, is a clear departure from settled law. The court’s reliance on *S. S. Kresge Co. v. United States*, 298 U.S. 459 (1936), is misplaced. In *Kresge*, the Court upheld a damages award based on the entire product because the patent covered a *functional* improvement to the entire article—specifically, a new method of displaying merchandise in a store. The patent did not cover a mere ornamental feature, nor did it apply to a component. The Court held that “where the patented device is an essential part of the article, and its use is inseparable from the use of the article, the entire profit may be recovered.” Id. at 464. But this exception does not apply when the patent covers only a component’s design. The design of a smartphone’s front face is not “an essential part” of the entire product in the functional sense; it is ornamental, and its value is distinct from the value of the phone’s internal components, processor, or operating system.

Moreover, the principle that damages must be limited to the value of the patented feature is rooted in the constitutional and statutory framework of patent law. The Constitution grants Congress the power to secure for inventors “the exclusive right to their respective writings and discoveries,” U.S. Const. Art. I, § 8, cl. 8, but this right is not unlimited. It is limited to the “invention” or “discovery” described in the patent. A design patent is not a patent on the entire product; it is a patent on a particular design applied to a component. To award damages based on the entire product would be to expand the patentee’s rights beyond the scope of the patent, in violation of the principle that “the patentee’s rights are confined to the invention described in the claims.” *Foster v. H. C. H. Co.*, 274 U.S. 407, 412 (1927).

The Federal Circuit’s decision also conflicts with the long-standing rule that damages must be based on the actual value of the patented feature. In *United States v. American Bell Telephone Co.*, 167 U.S. 224 (1897), the Court held that “the measure of damages for the infringement of a patent is the profit which the patentee has lost by the infringement.” Id. at 240. The Court further stated that “the damages must be such as will fairly compensate the patentee for the injury sustained.” Id. at 241. If the patented feature contributes only a small portion of the product’s value, the damages must reflect that. The Court in *American Bell* rejected the notion that a patentee could recover the entire profit from a product simply because the patent was used in its manufacture. “The patentee is not entitled to the entire profit of the article,” the Court said, “but only to such part of it as is attributable to the use of the patented invention.” Id. at 242.

This principle is equally applicable to design patents. The design of a smartphone’s front face, while valuable, is not the sole source of the product’s market success. The phone’s performance, battery life, camera quality, and software ecosystem are all significant contributors to its value. To award Apple the entire profit from Samsung’s smartphone sales—over $300 million—based solely on the infringement of a design patent covering a component is to award damages far exceeding the actual harm caused. It is to treat the design patent as if it were a patent on the entire product, which it is not.

The Federal Circuit’s failure to apply these settled principles is a serious error. The court’s decision effectively allows a patentee to recover the entire profit from a product based on a design that applies only to a component, a result that is inconsistent with the law as it has existed for over a century.
