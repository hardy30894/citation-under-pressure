**ARGUMENT**

**I. GUAM’S SECTION 107 CLAIM IS TIMELY AND SHOULD NOT BE BARRED BY THE DISTRICT COURT’S MISAPPLICATION OF CERCLA’S CONTRIBUTION PROVISIONS.**

The District of Columbia Circuit erred in concluding that Guam’s 2004 Consent Decree with the EPA triggered § 113(f)(3)(B) of the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA), 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(3)(B), thereby barring Guam’s § 107 cost-recovery action. The court’s interpretation conflates the distinct remedial schemes of § 107 and § 113, contrary to the Supreme Court’s guidance in *Cooper Industries, Inc. v. Aviall Services, Inc.*, 543 U.S. 157 (2004), and this Court’s own precedent.

CERCLA’s text and structure distinguish between cost-recovery and contribution actions. Section 107(a) imposes strict liability on potentially responsible parties (PRPs) for response costs incurred by the government or private parties. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9607(a). Section 113(f), by contrast, provides a mechanism for PRPs to seek contribution from other liable parties *after* they have resolved their liability through settlement or judgment. *See id.* § 9613(f)(1), (3)(B). As the Supreme Court has explained, § 113(f)(3)(B) “provides a right to contribution for a party that has resolved its liability to the United States or a State in an administrative or judicially approved settlement.” *Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167 (emphasis added). The critical question is whether the settlement “resolves” the settling party’s liability for response costs—i.e., whether it discharges the settling party’s obligation to pay for cleanup.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision misreads the 2004 Consent Decree. The decree explicitly states that it is “based on the pleadings, before taking testimony or adjudicating any issue of fact or law, and without any finding or admission of liability against or by the Government of Guam.” Consent Decree at 3, *United States v. Guam*, No. 02-00022 (D. Guam 2004). This disclaimer of liability is fatal to the government’s argument that the decree “resolved” Guam’s liability under § 113(f)(3)(B). *See Florida Power Corp. v. FirstEnergy Corp.*, 810 F.3d 996, 1002 (6th Cir. 2015) (finding no resolution of liability where the settling party refused to concede liability). Other circuits have similarly held that a disclaimer of liability precludes a finding that a settlement resolves liability for § 113(f)(3)(B) purposes. *See, e.g., Asarco LLC v. Atlantic Richfield Co.*, 866 F.3d 1113, 1123 (9th Cir. 2017); *Refined Metals Corp. v. Doe Run Resources Corp.*, 937 F.3d 923, 931 (8th Cir. 2019).

Moreover, the Consent Decree did not impose liability on Guam for response costs; it merely required Guam to close the Ordot Dump and install a cover system to comply with the Clean Water Act. *See* CWA Compl. ¶ 29. CERCLA’s definition of “response action” includes “remedial action,” which encompasses “the collection of leachate and runoff.” 42 U.S.C. § 9601(24). However, the decree did not allocate responsibility for the underlying contamination or require Guam to pay for remediation of hazardous substances. *See id.* § 9607(a)(4)(A) (imposing liability for “all costs of removal or remedial action incurred by the United States Government or a State”). Thus, the decree did not resolve Guam’s liability for response costs under CERCLA, and § 113(f)(3)(B) is inapplicable.

**II. THE DISTRICT COURT CORRECTLY CONCLUDED THAT GUAM’S § 107 CLAIM IS NOT TIME-BARRED.**

The government argues that even if the Consent Decree did not trigger § 113(f)(3)(B), Guam’s § 107 claim is time-barred because it accrued when the EPA first ordered Guam to remediate the Ordot Dump in 1986. This argument misapprehends the accrual rule for § 107 claims. CERCLA’s statute of limitations for § 107 actions provides that a claim accrues “on the date of discovery of the release or threatened release of a hazardous substance which gives rise to the action.” 42 U.S.C. § 9613(g)(2)(B). The Supreme Court has held that this provision “tolls the limitations period until the plaintiff discovers, or reasonably should have discovered, the injury giving rise to the action.” *United States v. Lone Pine Mining Co.*, 519 F.3d 215, 222 (3d Cir. 2008) (citing *Rotella v. Wood*, 528 U.S. 549, 556 (2000)).

Here, Guam did not discover the full extent of the contamination or the Navy’s role in causing it until after the EPA’s 2002 lawsuit and the 2004 Consent Decree. *See* Am. Compl. ¶¶ 11–15. The government’s argument that Guam should have known of its liability in 1986 ignores the fact that the Ordot Dump’s hazards were not fully understood until later, and that the EPA did not designate the Navy as a PRP until 1988. *See id.* ¶ 13. Thus, Guam’s § 107 claim accrued no earlier than 2002, and its 2017 lawsuit is timely under § 9613(g)(2)(B).

**III. THE DISTRICT COURT PROPERLY REJECTED THE GOVERNMENT’S ATTEMPT TO CONVERT GUAM’S § 107 CLAIM INTO A § 113 CONTRIBUTION CLAIM.**

The government’s argument that Guam must proceed under § 113 because it incurred response costs pursuant to the Consent Decree conflates the two provisions. Section 113(f)(1) allows a PRP to seek contribution from other liable parties *during or following* a § 107 action. *See* 42 U.S.C. § 9613(f)(1). It does not, however, bar a PRP from pursuing a § 107 claim against a non-settling PRP. *See Cooper*, 543 U.S. at 167–68. The government cites no authority for the proposition that a PRP that incurs response costs under a consent decree is precluded from bringing a § 107 claim against another PRP. To the contrary, the Supreme Court has recognized that § 107 and § 113 serve distinct purposes and are not mutually exclusive. *Id.* at 168.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, the Court should reverse the D.C. Circuit’s decision and hold that Guam’s § 107 claim is timely and not barred by § 113(f)(3)(B). The judgment of the court below should be affirmed.
