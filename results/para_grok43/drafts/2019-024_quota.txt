The three-year limitations period in 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2) bars respondent’s claims. That provision requires an ERISA action under 29 U.S.C. § 1104 to be commenced no later than “three years after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” The Ninth Circuit’s contrary construction—that actual knowledge demands subjective awareness that disclosed acts were imprudent—contradicts the statute’s text and this Court’s precedents.

Section 1113(2) measures the limitations period from actual knowledge of the underlying facts, not from knowledge that those facts constitute a legal violation. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held that trustees “had actual knowledge of the transaction at the time it took place” because they “actually made the decision,” and rejected the claim that knowledge of illegality was required. Id. at 991-92. Meagher v. International Ass’n of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418 (9th Cir. 1988), applied the same rule, stating that the statute “is triggered by [a claimant’s] knowledge of the transaction that constituted the alleged violation, not by [his] knowledge of the law.” Id. at 1423. Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548 (9th Cir. 1990), confirmed that actual knowledge arises once the plaintiff learns the material facts of the challenged conduct. Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Restaurant Employees Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509 (9th Cir. 1991), likewise measured the period from “the ‘earliest date’ on which [the plaintiff] knew of the breach.” Id. at 520.

These holdings align with the fiduciary standards set forth in Lockheed Corp. v. Spink, 517 U.S. 882 (1996), which recognized that ERISA imposes duties of prudence and loyalty measured by objective conduct, not by a fiduciary’s subjective legal conclusions. Id. at 887. Tibble v. Edison International, 135 S. Ct. 1823 (2015), reiterated that the limitations inquiry focuses on when the plaintiff acquired knowledge of the relevant facts. Id. at 1827. Firestone Tire & Rubber Co. v. Bruch, 489 U.S. 101 (1989), and Central States, Southeast & Southwest Areas Pension Fund v. Central Transport, Inc., 472 U.S. 559 (1985), further establish that ERISA participants are charged with knowledge of information the plan has properly disclosed. Varity Corp. v. Howe, 516 U.S. 489 (1996), confirms that fiduciary obligations are enforced through disclosure of material facts, not through a requirement that participants subjectively appreciate every legal implication.

Petitioners’ disclosures—Fund Fact Sheets, Summary Plan Descriptions, and website notices—conveyed the precise facts respondent now challenges: the allocation to alternative investments, the strategy behind those allocations, and their performance characteristics. Once those documents were furnished, respondent possessed actual knowledge of the transactions. The Ninth Circuit’s insistence on additional subjective awareness of imprudence would revive the constructive-knowledge provision Congress repealed in 1987 and would render § 1113(2) a nullity whenever a participant elects not to read plan materials. Because respondent filed suit more than three years after receiving the disclosures, the action is time-barred.
