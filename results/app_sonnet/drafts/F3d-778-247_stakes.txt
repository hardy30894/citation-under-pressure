# ARGUMENT

## I. The Warrant Application Failed to Establish a Nexus Between the Alleged Crimes and Joubert's Parents' Home

The Fourth Amendment requires that a warrant application establish "a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place." *Illinois v. Gates*, 462 U.S. 213, 238 (1983). This requires more than probable cause that the target committed a crime; it requires a nexus between the crime and the specific place to be searched. *United States v. Feliz*, 182 F.3d 82, 86 (1st Cir. 1999).

Here, the affidavit established that SJ witnessed Joubert *destroy* his computer tower and discard its components before the search—the opposite of evidence that incriminating material would still be found at the residence. The affiant's conclusory assertion that persons who commit these offenses "often maintain possession and/or control of physical or electronic documents pertaining to their victims" is precisely the kind of generalized, boilerplate assertion this Court has treated with skepticism when it substitutes for particularized facts connecting the alleged evidence to the place searched. *See United States v. Zayas-Diaz*, 95 F.3d 105, 111 (1st Cir. 1996) (nexus cannot rest on rank speculation). Because SJ's own account demonstrated that Joubert was actively disposing of the very category of evidence sought, the affidavit failed to supply the required probability that such evidence remained at the Manchester residence weeks later.

Nor does *United States v. Leon*, 468 U.S. 897 (1984), save the search. Leon's good-faith exception does not apply where, as here, the affidavit is "so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render official belief in its existence entirely unreasonable." *Id.* at 923. Given the affiant's own averment that Joubert dismantled and discarded his hard drive, no reasonable officer could believe the residence still housed the sought-after digital evidence.

## II. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Admitting Uncharged Molestation Testimony

Evidence of uncharged misconduct is inadmissible to show propensity, though it may be admitted for other purposes if it survives Rule 403 balancing. *Huddleston v. United States*, 485 U.S. 681, 691–92 (1988); Fed. R. Evid. 404(b). Even where a permissible purpose is identified, the district court must weigh probative value against unfair prejudice, and "[t]he term 'unfair prejudice' … speaks to the capacity of some concededly relevant evidence to lure the factfinder into declaring guilt on a ground different from proof specific to the offense charged." *Old Chief v. United States*, 519 U.S. 172, 180 (1997).

The testimony regarding uncharged molestation of other children did precisely that. This Court has cautioned that admission of such evidence demands "a searching inquiry" into whether its probative value is substantially outweighed by the risk that the jury will convict based on the defendant's bad character rather than the charged conduct. *United States v. Varoudakis*, 233 F.3d 113, 122 (1st Cir. 2000). Here, the volume and vividness of testimony about unrelated victims risked precisely that outcome: the jury was invited to conclude that Joubert was a pedophile and therefore must have committed the charged acts against KC, rather than to weigh the specific evidence tied to the indicted counts. The district court's failure to meaningfully constrain the scope of this testimony, or to give a limiting instruction sufficiently tailored to cabin its use, was an abuse of discretion warranting reversal.

## III. The Commerce Clause Does Not Support Application of §§ 2251(a) and 2252A(a)(5)(B) to Joubert's Purely Local Conduct

Congress's Commerce Clause power does not extend to purely local, noneconomic activity merely because it involves objects that once moved in interstate commerce, when doing so would obliterate the distinction between what is truly national and what is local. *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549, 567 (1995); *United States v. Morrison*, 529 U.S. 598, 617–18 (2000). While *Gonzales v. Raich*, 545 U.S. 1, 22 (2005), permits regulation of purely local activity when it is part of a broader regulatory scheme addressing economic activity substantially affecting interstate commerce, the production of child pornography for entirely personal, noncommercial purposes—as here, where Joubert recorded material for his own use and never distributed or sold it—is not economic activity in the sense contemplated by *Lopez* and *Morrison*.

This Court's decision in *United States v. Robinson*, 137 F.3d 652 (1st Cir. 1998), upheld § 2251 against a Commerce Clause challenge based on the jurisdictional hook that the camera or videotape had traveled in interstate commerce. Joubert preserves this argument for further review because that jurisdictional hook, divorced from any actual economic activity by the defendant, does not satisfy the substantial-effects test properly applied, and the statute as applied to his wholly local, noncommercial conduct exceeds the outer boundary of the commerce power.

## IV. The 480-Month Sentence Was Substantively Unreasonable

A sentence must be reasonable in light of the factors set forth in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), and appellate review asks whether the sentencing court struck a defensible balance among them. *Gall v. United States*, 552 U.S. 38, 51 (2007). This Court reviews for abuse of discretion, but must still ensure the sentence is not "greater than necessary" to serve the statutory purposes. *United States v. Martin*, 520 F.3d 87, 92 (1st Cir. 2008). Given Joubert's age, lack of prior convictions, and the mitigating circumstances presented at sentencing, the 480-month term—effectively a life sentence—was substantively unreasonable and should be vacated and remanded for resentencing.
