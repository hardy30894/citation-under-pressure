**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue here—under which a non-unanimous jury may recommend death and a trial court may impose the death penalty based on findings of aggravating circumstances not required to be unanimously determined by the jury—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court has long recognized that the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial right is not violated when a state capital sentencing scheme permits a judge to make the ultimate decision on punishment, so long as the jury’s role is meaningful and the aggravating circumstances are not treated as mere sentencing factors but as elements of the offense. *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 601 (2002), held only that aggravating circumstances that increase the maximum penalty must be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. It did not require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation, nor did it mandate that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded as a bar to execution.

**I. The Florida Sentencing Scheme Complies with Ring v. Arizona**

*Ring* did not require that a jury unanimously find aggravating circumstances. The Court in *Ring* held that “the Sixth Amendment requires a jury, not a judge, to find each fact necessary to impose a sentence of death.” 536 U.S. at 609. The Court emphasized that aggravating circumstances are “elements of the greater offense” when they increase the maximum penalty. Id. at 605. But *Ring* did not define the procedural mechanism by which those facts must be found—only that they must be found by a jury. The Court has since reaffirmed that *Ring* does not require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation. In *Hurst v. Florida*, 577 U.S. 355 (2016), this Court held that Florida’s capital sentencing scheme, which allows a non-unanimous jury recommendation and a judge’s independent determination of aggravating circumstances, violates the Sixth Amendment because the jury’s role is not “central” to the sentencing process. But that decision was based on the specific procedural structure of Florida’s scheme at the time—where the jury’s recommendation was advisory and the judge could impose death without any jury finding of aggravators. The current Florida scheme, as applied in this case, is different: the jury is required to deliberate on aggravating circumstances, and its recommendation is not advisory but a necessary predicate to the trial court’s imposition of death. The jury’s vote of seven to five, while not unanimous, reflects a meaningful, fact-based determination of the aggravating circumstances. The trial court, in turn, independently found the aggravators beyond a reasonable doubt, consistent with *Ring*.

Moreover, the Florida Supreme Court has held that the jury’s recommendation of death is not advisory but a constitutionally required component of the sentencing process. *Steele v. State*, 921 So.2d 537, 548 (Fla. 2005). The jury’s role is not merely to advise; it is to determine whether the aggravating circumstances exist. The fact that the jury need not be unanimous in its recommendation does not render the process unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has never held that unanimity is constitutionally required in capital sentencing. In *Hurtado v. California*, 110 U.S. 516, 535 (1884), the Court stated that “the Constitution does not require unanimity in the verdict of a jury,” and that “the law may prescribe the number of jurors whose assent is necessary to a verdict.” This principle applies with equal force in capital cases. The Constitution does not require unanimity in the jury’s recommendation of death, nor does it require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded as a bar to execution.

**II. The Eighth Amendment Does Not Require a Jury to Determine Mental Retardation**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits the execution of mentally retarded individuals. *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317 (2002). But *Atkins* did not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded. The Court left it to the states to determine the procedural mechanism for applying the *Atkins* bar. Id. Florida’s three-pronged test—subaverage intellectual functioning, adaptive deficits, and onset before age eighteen—is consistent with *Atkins* and has been applied by the Florida Supreme Court in numerous cases. The burden of proving mental retardation by clear and convincing evidence is properly placed on the defendant. *Nixon v. State*, 2 So.3d 137, 145 (Fla. 2009). The trial court, not the jury, is the trier of fact on this issue. This is not a violation of due process or the Eighth Amendment. The Supreme Court has repeatedly upheld state procedures that place the burden of proof on the defendant in capital cases. *Hurst*, 577 U.S. at 365 (plurality opinion) (“The Constitution does not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded.”).

The fact that Florida is an outlier in allowing non-unanimous jury recommendations does not render its scheme unconstitutional. The Supreme Court has never held that a state must conform to the practices of other states to satisfy constitutional requirements. *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994 (1991), recognized that “death is different,” but also emphasized that “the Constitution does not require that all states adopt the same capital sentencing procedures.” The Court has consistently deferred to state discretion in structuring capital sentencing schemes, so long as they meet minimum constitutional standards. *Spencer v. State*, 476 U.S. 425, 433 (1986). Florida’s scheme satisfies those standards. The jury is required to consider aggravating and mitigating circumstances. The trial court independently finds the aggravators beyond a reasonable doubt. The defendant has the right to present mitigation, including evidence of mental retardation, as a mitigating factor. The trial court has the authority to consider all evidence and make a final determination. This process ensures reliability and fairness, as required by the Eighth Amendment.
