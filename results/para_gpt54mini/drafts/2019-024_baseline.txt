**Argument**

ERISA’s three-year limitations period begins only “after the earliest date on which the plaintiff had actual knowledge of the breach or violation.” 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2). That text requires knowledge, not mere access to information. The Court should affirm because Congress used “actual knowledge” to impose a heightened trigger, and a participant does not acquire actual knowledge simply because a fiduciary posts disclosures the participant never reads or cannot recall reading. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. 768, 774–75 (2020); Merck & Co. v. Reynolds, 559 U.S. 633, 644–48 (2010).

I. **“Actual knowledge” means awareness of the relevant facts, not constructive notice from disclosure.**

This Court has long distinguished actual from constructive knowledge. In Merck, interpreting similarly worded limitations language, the Court held that “actual knowledge” requires more than “what a reasonably diligent plaintiff would have discovered” and rejected a rule that would start the clock upon disclosure alone. 559 U.S. at 643–45. ERISA’s text is even more demanding because Congress intentionally retained “actual knowledge” while repealing the former constructive-knowledge provision. Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 775–76; Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1987, Pub. L. No. 100-203, § 9342(b), 101 Stat. 1330.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary rule—under which availability of disclosures could suffice even if the participant did not read them—conflicts with the statutory text and this Court’s ordinary meaning cases. See Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 776–77. As this Court explained, “to have ‘actual knowledge’ of a piece of information, one must in fact be aware of it.” Id. at 777. That conclusion follows from common usage and from ERISA’s structure. Id. at 777–78.

II. **Disclosed information does not establish actual knowledge unless the plaintiff actually read or otherwise became aware of it.**

Intel’s rule would convert ERISA’s three-year limitations period into a constructive-notice regime. But Congress already provided a six-year period for fiduciary-breach claims, and shortened that period only where the plaintiff had actual knowledge. 29 U.S.C. § 1113; Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 775. If posting plan documents were enough, “actual knowledge” would do no work. Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 777–78; Phillips v. Alaska Hotel & Rest. Emps. Pension Fund, 944 F.2d 509, 520–21 (9th Cir. 1991).

That is why the Ninth Circuit’s own cases require awareness of the transaction or breach itself, not merely the existence of documents describing it. In Blanton v. Anzalone, 760 F.2d 989, 991–92 (9th Cir. 1985), the court held the fiduciaries had actual knowledge because they were “parties to the transaction” and “actually made the decision to undertake the transaction.” In Ziegler v. Connecticut General Life Insurance Co., 916 F.2d 548, 552 (9th Cir. 1990), the plaintiffs had actual knowledge only after the insurer informed them of the consequences of the election. And in Meagher v. International Association of Machinists & Aerospace Workers Pension Plan, 856 F.2d 1418, 1423 (9th Cir. 1988), the plaintiff had knowledge of the transaction because he received reduced checks; the court emphasized that knowledge of illegality was unnecessary, not that notice alone sufficed.

Intel’s position would collapse these cases into constructive notice. But this Court rejected that move. Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 778–79. The Court explained that “the statute requires more than that the plaintiff have notice of the information contained in the relevant plan documents.” Id. at 779.

III. **A plaintiff who does not read or recall reading the disclosures lacks actual knowledge as a matter of law.**

The record here is straightforward. Sulyma testified that he did not recall seeing the disclosures Intel relies on and was unaware during employment that the plans were heavily invested in hedge funds and private equity. Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 772–73. That is fatal to Intel’s limitations defense because actual knowledge turns on subjective awareness, not the mere possibility of access. Id. at 777–80.

This Court should reject Intel’s effort to recast “actual knowledge” as “readable knowledge.” ERISA’s language is narrower, and Congress could have chosen a constructive-knowledge rule if that were its aim. It did not. 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2); Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. at 775–76.

**Conclusion**

The judgment should be reversed. Under 29 U.S.C. § 1113(2), the three-year period does not begin until the plaintiff actually knows of the breach, and disclosure alone is not enough. Intel Corp. Inv. Policy Comm. v. Sulyma, 140 S. Ct. 768 (2020).
