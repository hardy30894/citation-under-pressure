# ARGUMENT

**Carol Anne Bond Lacks Standing to Challenge the Constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 229 on Tenth Amendment Grounds**

The Tenth Amendment provides that "[t]he powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." U.S. Const. amend. X. This Court has long recognized that the Amendment "states but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." *United States v. Darby*, 312 U.S. 100, 124 (1941). It is a structural guarantee running to the States as sovereign entities, not a personal right that an individual criminal defendant may invoke to escape a federal prosecution.

This principle is settled by *Tennessee Electric Power Co. v. Tennessee Valley Authority*, 306 U.S. 118 (1939). There, private utility companies argued that the federal government's sale of electricity violated the Tenth Amendment by intruding on matters reserved to the States. This Court rejected the claim on standing grounds, holding that "the appellants, absent the states or their officers, have no standing in this suit to raise any question under the [Tenth] Amendment." *Id.* at 144. The logic of *Tennessee Electric* controls here: the reserved powers protected by the Tenth Amendment belong to the States, and a private litigant—whether a public utility or a criminal defendant—may not assert the State's prerogative as her own.

*Massachusetts v. Mellon*, 262 U.S. 447 (1923), confirms the same rule from the other direction. There this Court held that a State itself lacked standing to challenge a federal spending statute as an invasion of the reserved powers of the States, because the State's interest in the sovereignty question was "not a case appropriate for judicial determination" when brought merely to vindicate an abstract federalism interest rather than a concrete injury. *Id.* at 484-85. If a sovereign State cannot manufacture a justiciable Tenth Amendment claim absent a genuine controversy over its own prerogatives, a fortiori a private citizen—who possesses no sovereign prerogative to begin with—cannot do so. *Alabama Power Co. v. Ickes*, 302 U.S. 464 (1938), reinforces this conclusion, denying standing to private parties who sought to challenge federal action as encroaching upon matters of state concern.

This rule reflects the broader doctrine that a litigant ordinarily may not rest a claim on the legal rights of a third party. In *Tileston v. Ullman*, 318 U.S. 44 (1943) (per curiam), this Court held that a physician lacked standing to challenge a state statute on the ground that it violated his patients' constitutional rights, because the physician "is not the true party at interest" and could not litigate rights personal to another. *Id.* at 46. Likewise, in *Yazoo & Mississippi Valley Railroad Co. v. Jackson Vinegar Co.*, 226 U.S. 217 (1912), this Court refused to entertain a challenge to a statute predicated on hypothetical applications that might infringe the rights of others not before the Court. *Id.* at 219-20. The same logic forecloses Bond's claim: the sovereign interest she invokes belongs to the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania, which has neither joined this litigation nor asserted any objection to the exercise of federal authority over her conduct.

Nothing about Bond's status as a criminal defendant, rather than a civil litigant, alters this analysis. The concern animating the standing doctrine—that the Judiciary should not adjudicate abstract questions of sovereign power at the instance of a party who has suffered no injury cognizable under the Amendment—applies with equal force in the criminal context. As this Court explained in *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), "[u]nder our federal system the administration of criminal justice rests with the States except as Congress, acting within the scope of the powers granted to it, has created offenses against the United States." *Id.* at 109. That statement describes the constitutional allocation of authority between sovereigns; it does not suggest that a defendant convicted under a validly enacted federal criminal statute may vindicate the States' sovereign interests as a personal defense to prosecution. If Pennsylvania believed that § 229's application to Bond's conduct trenched upon matters properly left to state law, it was free to say so. It has not.

Permitting Bond to raise this claim would, moreover, produce an anomalous result: a criminal defendant who has personally violated a validly enacted federal statute would obtain a windfall dismissal based on a generalized grievance about federal-state relations that the affected sovereign itself has not raised. Nothing in *Missouri v. Holland*, 252 U.S. 416 (1920), suggests that such generalized grievances are judicially cognizable when raised by a private party rather than by the State whose reserved powers are supposedly at stake. To the contrary, *Holland* addressed the merits of federalism concerns only in a suit brought by the State of Missouri itself, seeking to enjoin enforcement of a federal statute implementing a treaty. *Id.* at 430-31. Missouri, not a private individual, was the appropriate party to raise the claim.

Because the Tenth Amendment protects the reserved sovereignty of the States, and because Pennsylvania has not challenged § 229 as an intrusion upon its authority, Bond—like the private utilities in *Tennessee Electric*—lacks standing to raise this claim. The judgment of the Court of Appeals should be affirmed.
