ARGUMENT

I. The District Court Erred in Granting Summary Judgment Because Burnett Presented Sufficient Evidence to Create Genuine Issues of Material Fact Regarding His Notice to Habitat of a Serious Health Condition.

Summary judgment is a drastic remedy that should be granted only when the moving party is entitled to judgment as a matter of law and no genuine issue of material fact exists. *Sartor v. Arkansas Natural Gas Corp.*, 321 U.S. 620, 627 (1944). On a motion for summary judgment, the court must view all evidence and draw all reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the non-moving party. *United States v. Diebold, Inc.*, 369 U.S. 654, 655 (1962) (per curiam). The district court failed to apply this standard, instead improperly weighing Burnett’s statements and resolving disputed inferences against him.

The court concluded that Burnett did not provide adequate notice of a serious health condition under the FMLA or the ADA. But Burnett’s multiple communications to Habitat—from October 2003 through January 2004—were sufficient to place a reasonable employer on notice that he suffered from a serious medical condition. In October 2003, Burnett told his supervisor, Sergio Polo, that he had a “weak bladder” and was seeing a doctor. In December 2003, he gave Polo a doctor’s order for blood tests and explained that he had high PSA and cholesterol levels. He further stated that he was “sick” during his week-long absence, and he explicitly compared his condition to his brother-in-law’s prostate cancer. In January 2004, Burnett provided a supervisor with a document detailing his upcoming prostate biopsy, which stated that the procedure was indicated by “an abnormal rectal exam or an elevation of prostate cancer screening blood test (PSA).” These repeated statements, taken together, made Habitat aware that Burnett was dealing with a potentially serious prostate condition.

Notice does not require the employee to use magic words or to provide a formal diagnosis. The question is whether the employer received enough information to know that the employee’s absence or medical condition might be serious. *Carpenter v. United States*, 316 U.S. 253, 259 (1942) (holding that notice is sufficient if it “fairly apprises” the recipient of the essential facts). A jury could reasonably find that Burnett’s statements—including the reference to prostate cancer, the high PSA level, and the biopsy document—constituted adequate notice. *Lavender v. Kurn*, 327 U.S. 645, 653 (1946) (explaining that a jury may draw reasonable inferences from circumstantial evidence); *Tennant v. Peoria & Pekin Union Ry. Co.*, 321 U.S. 29, 35 (1944) (stating that “it is the function of the jury to weigh the evidence and resolve conflicts”).

Moreover, Polo’s own conduct demonstrates that he understood Burnett’s condition to be serious. On December 16, 2003, Polo sent an email to other supervisors directing that Burnett be granted sick leave for future doctor’s appointments. This internal acknowledgment of Burnett’s need for medical leave undercuts any claim that Habitat lacked notice. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting System, Inc.*, 368 U.S. 464, 473 (1962) (summary judgment improper when the non-movant’s evidence is “more than a scintilla” and “tends to show” the existence of a material fact).

The district court also erred by discounting the document Burnett gave to Supervisor Mitch Hehr. Even if Polo did not personally receive it, Hehr was a supervisor who, under Habitat’s standard policy, should have forwarded it to Polo. Knowledge acquired by an agent within the scope of employment is imputed to the principal. *American Surety Co. v. Pauly*, 170 U.S. 133, 143 (1898). Thus, Hehr’s receipt of the biopsy document is attributable to Habitat.

Finally, the district court failed to consider that Burnett’s notice obligations under the FMLA and ADA are not identical, but both require only that the employer be aware of the employee’s serious health condition. Burnett’s consistent and escalating communications provided that awareness. The record contains genuine disputes—for example, whether Polo received the biopsy document, whether Burnett’s statements were sufficient to describe a serious condition, and whether Habitat acted on that knowledge. These disputes must be resolved by a jury, not by summary judgment. *Sartor*, 321 U.S. at 628.

Because the evidence, viewed in Burnett’s favor, raises a genuine issue of material fact as to whether he gave adequate notice, the district court’s grant of summary judgment should be reversed.
