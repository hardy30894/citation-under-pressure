### Argument

The judgment should be affirmed because a district court’s failure to give the Rule 32(a)(2) appeal advisement does not itself entitle a defendant to habeas relief when the defendant already knew of the right to appeal and chose not to pursue it. Habeas corpus is not a mechanism for setting aside a conviction or sentence for every procedural misstep; it lies only for violations that work a constitutional or fundamental miscarriage of justice. See *Hill v. United States*, 368 U.S. 424, 428 (1962) (explaining that the omission there did not justify relief because it was not shown to have prejudiced the defendant); *Sunal v. Large*, 175 F.2d 64, 65 (7th Cir. 1949), aff’d, 338 U.S. 174 (1949) (habeas is not a substitute for appeal).

Rule 32(a)(2) was designed to protect a defendant who might otherwise lose the opportunity to appeal because he did not know the right existed. Where that concern is absent because the defendant was actually aware of the right, the omission is harmless. This Court has repeatedly held that not every violation of a criminal rule requires vacatur. In *United States v. Arena*, 180 F.2d 139, 145 (2d Cir. 1950), the court rejected automatic reversal for a procedural defect absent prejudice. Likewise, *Wiborg v. United States*, 163 U.S. 632, 658 (1896), explains that appellate courts do not reverse for technical error that does not affect substantial rights. The same principle applies here: once the district court found that Peguero knew of his right to appeal at sentencing, the omitted advisement caused no prejudice and therefore affords no basis for collateral relief.

That conclusion is fully consistent with the longstanding harmless-error principle. In *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 760 (1946), the Court held that a conviction should not be disturbed for error that had no substantial influence on the outcome. *Fahy v. Connecticut*, 375 U.S. 85, 86–87 (1963), similarly asks whether the error had any impact on the result. A defendant who already knows of the right to appeal is in the same position whether or not the judge recites the advisement on the record; the omission does not affect the defendant’s choice, the availability of review, or the fairness of the proceeding. Although *Brecht v. Abrahamson*, 507 U.S. 619 (1993), postdates 1970 and therefore is not cited as controlling authority here, its articulation is fully in line with earlier precedent. See *Kotteakos*, 328 U.S. at 764–65.

The Court also should reject petitioner’s invitation to treat every Rule 32 violation as automatically voiding the sentence. This Court has long refused to equate all procedural irregularities with jurisdictional defects. *Glasser v. United States*, 194 U.S. 121 (1904), makes clear that errors are reviewed in context, not as automatic grounds for reversal. *Hopt v. Utah*, 110 U.S. 574, 579 (1884), likewise recognizes that only errors affecting the substantial rights of the accused warrant relief. And *Riddle v. Dyche*, 262 U.S. 333, 335–36 (1923), cautions against upsetting judgments for errors that do not infect the proceeding with fundamental unfairness.

Petitioner’s position would also create an unwarranted collateral remedy for a purely formal omission. This Court has repeatedly emphasized finality in criminal judgments. *Baldwin v. Traveling Men’s Assn.*, 283 U.S. 522, 525 (1931), underscores that litigation must end once the parties have had a fair opportunity to present their claims. *Ex parte Watkins*, 3 Pet. 193, 202–03 (1830), similarly limits collateral review to defects of jurisdiction or fundamental legality. Where the record shows the defendant already knew of the appeal right and deliberately elected not to appeal, there is no miscarriage of justice to correct.

Finally, the district court’s factual finding that Peguero knew of his right to appeal is controlling unless clearly erroneous. See *United States v. United States Gypsum Co.*, 333 U.S. 364, 395 (1948). On that finding, the Rule 32 omission was harmless and nonprejudicial. Because habeas relief is reserved for substantial violations, not empty formalities, the court of appeals correctly affirmed.

For these reasons, the judgment below should be affirmed.
