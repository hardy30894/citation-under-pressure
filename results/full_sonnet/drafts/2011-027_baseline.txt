**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Equal-Footing Doctrine Confirms Montana's Title to the Riverbeds Because the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers Were Navigable Waterways of Commerce at Statehood.**

Under the equal-footing doctrine, "the shores of navigable waters, and the soils under them" pass to a new State upon its admission to the Union "as an incident of sovereignty." *Pollard v. Hagan*, 44 U.S. (3 How.) 212, 230 (1845). This Court's test for navigability-for-title purposes asks whether the waters were "used, or susceptible of being used, in their ordinary condition, as highways for commerce, over which trade and travel are or may be conducted." *The Daniel Ball*, 77 U.S. (10 Wall.) 557, 563 (1871). The inquiry examines the river's capacity to sustain commercial use, not whether every mile of the channel is free of obstruction. As this Court explained in *Economy Light & Power Co. v. United States*, 256 U.S. 113, 122 (1921), the "capacity of a river to serve as a highway of commerce" is not destroyed by "occasional difficulties in navigation," including portages around rapids or falls. So too in *United States v. Utah*, 283 U.S. 64, 82 (1931), the Court held that "the extent to which the river was actually used" and its "susceptibility to use" must be assessed river-by-river, but that interruptions requiring portage do not sever a navigable river into non-navigable segments where the watercourse as a whole functioned as a highway of trade.

Montana's courts faithfully applied this standard. The trial court and Montana Supreme Court examined extensive historical evidence—expedition journals, territorial commerce records, and contemporaneous newspaper accounts—establishing that the Missouri, Madison, and Clark Fork Rivers were used for the transport of furs, mining equipment, and other goods in the era surrounding statehood in 1889. That the Great Falls reach of the Missouri required brief portage does not defeat navigability any more than the rapids at issue in *Economy Light* defeated the navigability of the Fox River. This Court has never required, and PPL's proposed rule would wrongly demand, that a State prove commercial traffic traversed every rod of a riverbed without interruption before title vests. The relevant unit of analysis is the river as a highway of commerce, not an artificially fragmented segment isolated at the site of a later-built dam.

**II. The Historical Record Properly Considered by the Montana Courts Was Directly Probative of Navigability at Statehood.**

PPL errs in suggesting that only evidence contemporaneous with the precise moment of statehood may be considered. This Court has long recognized that evidence both before and after the date of admission is competent to establish a river's natural and ordinary condition, because rivers' physical characteristics are typically stable over time. In *Utah v. United States*, 403 U.S. 9, 11-12 (1971), the Court relied on evidence of navigation both preceding and following Utah's 1896 admission to determine whether the Great Salt Lake and connected waters satisfied the navigability-for-title test, explaining that such evidence sheds light on "the natural and ordinary condition" of the water body. Likewise in *Utah*, 283 U.S. at 75, the Court considered testimony and records spanning decades to determine the "ordinary and natural condition" of the Colorado River. The Montana courts did precisely this: they credited historical records documenting use of the rivers for freight and travel in the years bracketing 1889, evidence that was, contrary to PPL's characterization, properly authenticated and admissible under Montana's evidentiary rules governing ancient documents and public records. See M. R. Evid. 901(8), 902(4)-(8), 803(8).

**III. Montana's Ownership of the Riverbeds Is Consistent With, Not Preempted by, the Federal Navigational Servitude and the Federal Power Act.**

PPL's effort to recast the navigability-for-title inquiry cannot be divorced from the underlying purpose of the equal-footing doctrine: vesting in the States, "as an incident of sovereignty," ownership of the beds of navigable waters within their borders. *Pollard*, 44 U.S. at 230; see also *Montana v. United States*, 450 U.S. 544, 551 (1981) (title to the riverbed underlying navigable waters "passes to the States as an incident of statehood" and is distinct from the federal navigational servitude, which governs the paramount power of Congress over interstate commerce). Because the federal navigational servitude and state title are analytically distinct, the Federal Power Act's licensing scheme for hydroelectric projects does not deprive Montana of the property interest it obtained at statehood. See *First Iowa Hydro-Electric Coop. v. Federal Power Comm'n*, 328 U.S. 152, 175-76 (1946) (FPA preserves state law governing "beds and banks" of watercourses within the dual federal-state regulatory system). PPL's predecessors built dams atop these state-owned riverbeds without ever securing title or paying compensation; the HRA merely requires PPL to compensate the State's school trust for the ongoing use of state property, precisely the kind of "proprietary right" the FPA leaves undisturbed. *First Iowa*, 328 U.S. at 175-76.

**CONCLUSION**

The Montana Supreme Court properly applied the constitutional navigability-for-title test, correctly recognized that occasional portages do not defeat a river's status as a highway of commerce, and properly relied on historical evidence probative of the rivers' condition at and around 1889. The judgment below should be affirmed.
