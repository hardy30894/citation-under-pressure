**ARGUMENT**

**I. MILFORD’S COMMUNITY-USE POLICY IS A NEUTRAL, REASONABLE REGULATION OF A LIMITED PUBLIC FORUM AND DOES NOT DISCRIMINATE ON THE BASIS OF VIEWPOINT.**

The Second Circuit correctly held that Milford’s policy excluding religious worship from its limited public forum is viewpoint-neutral and constitutional. *Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Defense & Educ. Fund*, 473 U.S. 788, 806 (1985) (a regulation is viewpoint-neutral if it "draws distinctions in an effort to reserve [the forum] for certain messages"); *Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n*, 460 U.S. 37, 49 (1983) ("Control over access to a nonpublic forum can be based on subject matter and speaker identity so long as the distinctions drawn are reasonable"). Milford’s policy excludes only religious worship, not religious *discussion*. *Widmar v. Vincent*, 454 U.S. 263, 269 n.6 (1981) (distinguishing between religious *worship* and religious *discussion*). The Club’s activities—singing hymns, reciting scripture, and praying—constitute worship, not mere moral instruction. *Id.* at 267-68 (holding that religious *worship* is not protected in a university’s open forum). Thus, Milford’s policy is viewpoint-neutral and does not violate the First Amendment.

**II. MILFORD’S POLICY IS JUSTIFIED BY THE ESTABLISHMENT CLAUSE BECAUSE IT PREVENTS THE APPEARANCE OF STATE-SPONSORED RELIGION.**

The Establishment Clause permits Milford to exclude religious worship from its facilities to avoid the perception of government endorsement of religion. *Abington Sch. Dist. v. Schempp*, 374 U.S. 203, 225 (1963) ("The place of religion in our society is an exalted one, but in the relationship between man and religion, the State is firmly committed to a position of neutrality"). Milford’s policy ensures that its facilities are not used in a way that could be perceived as state-sponsored religious activity. *Epperson v. Arkansas*, 393 U.S. 97, 106 (1968) ("The State’s secular objectives may be accomplished only by means which have no significant risk of fostering a seditious effect"). Excluding religious worship from school facilities avoids the risk of impermissible entanglement between church and state. *Lemon v. Kurtzman*, 403 U.S. 602, 612-13 (1971) (establishing the three-part test for Establishment Clause violations, including the requirement that government action have a secular purpose and avoid excessive entanglement).

**III. THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES CONSTITUTE RELIGIOUS WORSHIP, NOT MORAL INSTRUCTION, AND THEREFORE FALL OUTSIDE THE PROTECTIONS OF THE FIRST AMENDMENT.**

The Club’s activities—singing hymns, reciting scripture, and praying—are quintessentially religious worship, not moral instruction. *Widmar*, 454 U.S. at 267-68 (holding that religious *worship* is not protected in a university’s open forum). The Club’s own materials demonstrate that its primary purpose is to proselytize and engage in religious devotion. *Abington*, 374 U.S. at 223-24 (holding that mandatory Bible readings in public schools violate the Establishment Clause). Because the Club’s activities constitute religious worship, Milford’s exclusion does not violate the First Amendment.

**IV. EVEN IF THE CLUB’S ACTIVITIES WERE VIEWED AS MORAL INSTRUCTION, MILFORD’S POLICY IS REASONABLE AND NEUTRAL.**

If the Club’s activities were deemed moral instruction, Milford’s policy remains reasonable and viewpoint-neutral. *Cornelius*, 473 U.S. at 806 (a regulation is viewpoint-neutral if it "draws distinctions in an effort to reserve [the forum] for certain messages"). Milford allows other groups to use its facilities for moral instruction, but excludes religious worship. *Perry*, 460 U.S. at 49 (control over access to a nonpublic forum can be based on subject matter and speaker identity so long as the distinctions drawn are reasonable). Thus, Milford’s policy does not discriminate on the basis of viewpoint and is constitutional.

**CONCLUSION**

For the foregoing reasons, Milford’s exclusion of the Good News Club from its facilities does not violate the First Amendment. The judgment of the Second Circuit should be affirmed.
