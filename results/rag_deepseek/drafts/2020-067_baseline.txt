**ARGUMENT**

A federal appellate court reviewing a conviction for plain error may consult the entire trial record—including evidence not submitted to the jury—to determine whether an error affected the defendant’s substantial rights or the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. The Eleventh Circuit correctly followed this Court’s settled precedent.

**I. Plain-Error Review Permits Consideration of the Whole Record**

Under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b), an appellate court may correct a forfeited error only if the error is plain, affects substantial rights, and seriously impairs the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725, 732-736 (1993). This Court has never limited that inquiry to the evidence actually presented to the jury. To the contrary, this Court consistently examines the entire record when conducting plain-error review.

In *Johnson v. United States*, 520 U.S. 461, 468-470 (1997), the Court held that a plain error affecting substantial rights requires a showing that the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings.” The Court evaluated that prejudice prong by looking at the “record as a whole,” including evidence that was not part of the jury’s consideration. *Id.* at 470. Similarly, in *Neder v. United States*, 527 U.S. 1, 19-20 (1999), the Court conducted harmless-error review by examining “the entire record” to assess whether a jury would have found an omitted element beyond a reasonable doubt. *Neder* made clear that reviewing courts may look beyond the evidence actually admitted to determine whether an error was prejudicial.

The Eleventh Circuit’s approach here is consistent with *Olano*, *Johnson*, and *Neder*. The court properly surveyed not only the trial transcript but also the undisputed facts establishing Greer’s status as a convicted felon. Greer stipulated in his plea agreement that he had five prior convictions for aggravated burglary, and he never contested that status at trial. The court thus permissibly concluded that any *Rehaif* error—the failure to instruct the jury on Greer’s knowledge of his felon status—did not affect his substantial rights because the record conclusively demonstrated that Greer knew he was a felon.

**II. The Prejudice Inquiry Is Not Confined to the Jury’s Evidentiary Horizon**

Petitioner argues that prejudice must be assessed solely through the lens of what the jury considered. That argument misunderstands the nature of plain-error review. The substantial-rights prong asks whether the error “affected the outcome of the district court proceedings,” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 734, not whether the jury specifically found every element. When a defendant cannot demonstrate that the error made a difference, relief is unwarranted.

This Court has repeatedly held that reviewing courts may consider reliable, uncontested facts outside the trial record when evaluating prejudice. In *Lockhart v. Nelson*, 488 U.S. 33, 40-42 (1988), the Court held that an appellate court may consider “the entire record” to determine whether the evidence was legally sufficient to support a conviction, even if some evidence was later found inadmissible. And in *United States v. Wells*, 519 U.S. 482, 494-496 (1997), the Court examined the full record to assess whether a jury instruction error was harmless.

The same principle applies here. Greer’s admissions—including his plea agreement’s acknowledgment that he was “punishable as an Armed Career Criminal” based on five prior convictions—demonstrate beyond any reasonable doubt that he knew he was a felon. See *Rehaif v. United States*, 139 S. Ct. 2191, 2200 (2019) (noting that a defendant’s prior convictions alone may suffice to show knowledge of status). The Eleventh Circuit did not err by relying on those admissions.

**III. The Fairness, Integrity, and Public Reputation of the Proceedings Are Not Impaired**

The fourth prong of plain-error review is “permissive, not mandatory.” *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 735. An appellate court may correct a plain error only if the error “seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.” *Id.* at 736. Here, the error—failing to instruct on an element that no rational jury could have disputed—did not undermine the fundamental fairness of Greer’s trial. See *United States v. Cotton*, 535 U.S. 625, 633-634 (2002) (declining to reverse where the evidence of the omitted element was “overwhelming” and “essentially uncontroverted”).

Greer received a full and fair trial. He was convicted by a jury that found every contested element beyond a reasonable doubt. The only omission was a *mens rea* element that the undisputed facts establish. Reversing a conviction under these circumstances would make a mockery of the justice system, not preserve its integrity.

**CONCLUSION**

The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
