**ARGUMENT**

The court below erred in holding that the government may rest on a presumption of continued membership in a conspiracy, rather than bearing the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was a member during the relevant statute-of-limitations period. The Constitution requires the government to prove every element of the charged offense beyond a reasonable doubt. *Howard v. Fleming*, 191 U.S. 126, 136 (1903) (holding that “the guilt of the accused must be shown beyond a reasonable doubt”). The existence of a conspiracy during the limitations period is an essential element of the offense, and the government cannot escape this burden merely because the defendant has presented evidence of withdrawal.

**I. The Government Must Prove Every Element of the Conspiracy Beyond a Reasonable Doubt, Including Its Continuation Into the Limitations Period.**

A criminal conspiracy is an agreement in fact, and the government must prove the agreement existed within the limitations period. *See Hyde v. Shine*, 199 U.S. 62, 76-77 (1905) (discussing the need for an overt act within the jurisdiction to establish conspiracy). The burden of proof on this element does not shift even when the defendant introduces evidence of withdrawal. This principle flows from the fundamental requirement that the government bears the risk of nonpersuasion on all elements. *Howard*, 191 U.S. at 136. Where, as here, the defendant proffers evidence that the conspiracy’s leaders split before the limitations period, the government must still satisfy the jury—beyond a reasonable doubt—that the conspiracy continued into that period. *See Emich Motors Corp. v. General Motors Corp.*, 340 U.S. 558, 568 (1951) (recognizing that the “prima facie evidence” from a criminal conviction does not relieve the government of proving the conspiracy’s existence in a civil context; a fortiori, the burden is heavier in the criminal case itself).

The lower court’s ruling effectively shifts the burden to the defendant to disprove his membership during the limitations period. That is impermissible. A defendant’s withdrawal—like any affirmative evidence that the conspiracy had ended—merely creates a factual issue. The government cannot rest on the indictment alone. *Cf. Price v. Henkel*, 216 U.S. 488, 493 (1910) (explaining that certified copies of indictments are “competent evidence” of probable cause but do not conclusively prove the charge). Instead, the government must come forward with evidence that the conspiracy persisted. *See First Nat. Bank of Ariz. v. Cities Service Co.*, 391 U.S. 253, 271 (1968) (holding that the party opposing summary judgment bears no burden unless the movant first meets its burden; analogously, the government must first prove its case). A bare indictment, without more, cannot satisfy the reasonable-doubt standard. *Price*, 216 U.S. at 493.

**II. Withdrawal Is a Factual Defense That Does Not Alter the Government’s Burden of Persuasion on the Limitations Element.**

The lower court treated withdrawal as an affirmative defense that the defendant must prove. That misapprehends the nature of conspiracy. Withdrawal is not a separate element; it is evidence that the conspiracy had ended before the limitations period. *McDaniel v. Traylor*, 212 U.S. 428, 430 (1909), described the “essence” of a conspiracy as a “combination” to achieve a common objective. If the combination dissolves before the limitations period, no conspiracy exists within that period. The government must prove the combination’s continuity. *Id.* at 431 (noting that the alleged conspirators “by combination and conspiracy” procured the fraudulent allowance of claims, but requiring proof of the continuing agreement).

Requiring the defendant to prove withdrawal would pervert this elemental burden. In *Hyde v. Shine*, this Court recognized that the government must establish the conspiracy’s existence within the charging period. 199 U.S. at 77. The defendant does not need to prove that he “withdrew”; he need only raise a reasonable doubt about the conspiracy’s duration. *See Howard*, 191 U.S. at 136. Once the defendant presents evidence that the conspiracy ended—such as the leaders’ split—the government must prove continuation beyond a reasonable doubt. The lower court’s contrary holding would permit convictions on speculation, undermining the presumption of innocence.

**III. The Government’s Reliance on a Presumption of Continuity Violates Due Process.**

Some lower courts have allowed a presumption that a conspiracy continues unless the defendant affirmatively proves withdrawal. But such a presumption cannot stand where the government has the burden on each element. *Partmar Corp. v. Paramount Pictures Theatres Corp.*, 347 U.S. 89, 95 (1954), held that “prima facie evidence” under the Clayton Act does not shift the ultimate burden of persuasion. Similarly, in a criminal case, evidence of an earlier conspiracy cannot, by itself, prove its continuation. The government must adduce specific facts showing the conspiracy’s activity within the limitations period. *See In re Belt*, 159 U.S. 95, 97 (1895) (discussing the importance of a jury’s role in finding facts based on evidence, not presumptions). Without such facts, the conviction cannot stand.

**IV. The Lower Court’s Error Requires Reversal.**

The record shows that the leaders of the alleged conspiracy, Rodney Moore and Kevin Gray, split before the limitations period. The government offered no evidence that the conspiracy continued thereafter. The jury thus lacked a sufficient basis to find beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendants remained members during the limitations period. *Emich Motors*, 340 U.S. at 568-69 (stressing that a general verdict of guilty on a conspiracy charge does not reveal which acts the jury found; here, the jury could not have rationally found participation within the limitations period). The court below therefore erred in denying the motions for new trial.

For these reasons, the judgment should be reversed, and the case remanded for proceedings consistent with the government’s burden of proof.
