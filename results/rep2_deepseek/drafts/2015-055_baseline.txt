**Argument**

Petitioner Gregory Welch’s sentence under the Armed Career Criminal Act’s (ACCA) residual clause is unconstitutional. The rule announced in *Johnson v. United States*, 576 U.S. ___ (2015)—that the residual clause is void for vagueness—applies retroactively to cases on collateral review. This Court should reverse the Eleventh Circuit’s judgment.

**I. The Rule in *Johnson* Is Substantive and Therefore Retroactive Under *Teague*.**

Under *Teague v. Lane*, 489 U.S. 288 (1989), new constitutional rules of criminal procedure do not apply retroactively to cases on collateral review, but new substantive rules do. *Schriro v. Summerlin*, 542 U.S. 348, 351–52 (2004). A rule is substantive if it “alters the range of conduct or the class of persons that the law punishes.” *Id.* at 353. *Johnson* announced a substantive rule because it narrowed the definition of “violent felony” under ACCA, thereby limiting the class of offenders subject to the 15-year mandatory minimum. The residual clause required courts to assess “the hypothetical risk posed by an abstract generic version” of a prior offense, *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at ___, making its application arbitrary and unpredictable. By holding that clause unconstitutionally vague, *Johnson* “prohibit[s] a certain category of punishment,” *Montgomery v. Louisiana*, 577 U.S. ___, ___ (2016), and thus “places a class of individuals beyond the State’s power to punish by statute.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311 (plurality opinion). Welch’s prior conviction for Florida strong-arm robbery can no longer qualify as a residual-clause predicate, and he is therefore not subject to the ACCA enhancement. Because *Johnson* “renders a sentencing range substantively illegal,” it is a substantive rule that must be applied retroactively. *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at ___.

**II. The Eleventh Circuit Erred in Denying Retroactive Application.**

The Eleventh Circuit, in its opinion below, did not address retroactivity because it denied Welch’s certificate of appealability before *Johnson* was decided. But the court’s underlying rationale—that Florida robbery categorically satisfied the residual clause—cannot survive *Johnson*. The residual clause’s vagueness infects any determination that a prior conviction “presents a serious potential risk of physical injury to another.” *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at ___. Because the Eleventh Circuit relied on that clause to uphold Welch’s sentence, the sentence is now invalid. *Johnson* necessarily applies retroactively because it “annulled a part of the statute that authorized the punishment.” *Montgomery*, 577 U.S. at ___. This Court has consistently applied such substantive rules retroactively. *See, e.g., Bouie v. City of Columbia*, 378 U.S. 347, 362–63 (1964) (retroactive application of judicial narrowing of criminal statute). The Constitution requires the same result here.

**III. Even If *Johnson* Were Procedural, It Would Satisfy the “Watershed” Exception.**

*Johnson* is a substantive rule, but it also satisfies the narrow exception to *Teague* for “watershed rules of criminal procedure” that are “implicit in the concept of ordered liberty.” *Teague*, 489 U.S. at 311 (plurality opinion). The void-for-vagueness doctrine protects due process by ensuring that statutes give fair notice and prevent arbitrary enforcement. *Johnson*, 576 U.S. at ___. The residual clause failed both prongs, making its application “so standardless that it invites arbitrary enforcement.” *Id.* at ___. A rule that eliminates such fundamental unfairness from sentencing proceedings is the “rare” exception to nonretroactivity. *See Schriro*, 542 U.S. at 352. The Court need not resolve this question, however, because *Johnson* is substantive.

**Conclusion**

Because *Johnson* announced a substantive rule that decriminalizes the conduct that supported Welch’s ACCA sentence, it applies retroactively. The Eleventh Circuit’s judgment should be reversed, and Welch should be resentenced without the residual clause.
