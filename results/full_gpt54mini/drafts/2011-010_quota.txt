Respondent’s judgment should be affirmed because OCSLA’s compensation provision is not a free-floating federal benefits scheme; it is tied to injuries “occurring as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf.” 43 U.S.C. § 1333(b). The ordinary reading of that phrase permits coverage only where the compensable injury occurs on the Shelf itself or, at minimum, in the waters above it. The Benefits Review Board’s situs-of-injury requirement is therefore the best construction of the statute, and the Ninth Circuit erred by replacing the statutory text with an open-ended “substantial nexus” test.

The Supreme Court has already explained that OCSLA’s coverage is principally territorial. In Offshore Logistics, Inc. v. Tallentire, 477 U.S. 207 (1986), the Court stated that “Congress determined that the general scope of OCSLA’s coverage . . . would be determined principally by locale, not by the status of the individual injured or killed.” Id. at 219. Although Tallentire addressed § 1333(a)(2)(A), its explanation of OCSLA’s structure is directly relevant. The Court also noted that § 1333(b) is the “only” provision that superimposes a status requirement on an otherwise situs-based statute. Id. at 219 n.2. That footnote confirms that § 1333(b) remains tied to OCSLA’s locale-centered framework; it does not authorize an injury-based expansion untethered from the Shelf.

The Fifth Circuit’s en banc decision in Mills v. Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs, 877 F.2d 356 (5th Cir. 1989) (en banc), correctly read § 1333(b) to require that the injury occur on the outer continental shelf or in the waters above it. Id. at 362. Mills reconciled the statutory phrase “as the result of operations conducted on the outer Continental Shelf” with Congress’s geographic limitation on OCSLA’s territorial reach. Id. The court’s approach avoids transforming OCSLA into a nationwide remedy for anyone whose work has some relationship to offshore production.

That reading is reinforced by Curtis v. Schlumberger Offshore Service, Inc., 849 F.2d 805 (3d Cir. 1988), which relied on a broad “but for” theory. But Curtis is not persuasive because it divorces “result of operations” from OCSLA’s express offshore setting. Id. at 809-11. A statute drafted around offshore operations does not naturally extend to land-based injuries simply because offshore work is part of the factual background.

Nor does Herb’s Welding, Inc. v. Gray, 470 U.S. 414 (1985), support respondent’s opponent. Herb’s Welding held that a worker on a fixed platform was not engaged in maritime employment for purposes of the LHWCA because his work was not maritime in nature. Id. at 421-24. The Court emphasized the significance of the Act’s geographic and functional limits. Id. at 422-24. Likewise, in Herb’s Welding v. Gray (Herb’s Welding II), 766 F.2d 898 (5th Cir. 1985), the Fifth Circuit held that an injury occurring on a platform in state waters was outside OCSLA’s reach. Id. at 900. Those decisions confirm that OCSLA and the LHWCA both contain meaningful territorial limits.

Respondent’s opponent’s “substantial nexus” test also conflicts with the settled principle that statutory coverage is not expanded by judicial policy preferences. In P.C. Pfeiffer Co. v. Ford, 444 U.S. 69 (1979), the Court construed LHWCA status coverage, but it did so against an undisputed maritime situs and expressly focused on workers “engaged in intermediate steps of moving cargo between ship and land transportation.” Id. at 82-83. Pfeiffer does not authorize erasing situs limits altogether.

The Ninth Circuit’s contrary approach also sits uneasily with Chesapeake & Ohio Ry. Co. v. Schwalb, 493 U.S. 40 (1989), which recognized that workers covered by federal compensation statutes must still satisfy the statutory boundaries Congress chose. Id. at 45-47. And in Northeast Marine Terminal Co. v. Caputo, 432 U.S. 249 (1977), the Court expanded LHWCA coverage only within the Act’s framework of covered situs and status, not beyond it. Id. at 265-67. Similarly, Director, Office of Workers’ Compensation Programs v. Perini North River Associates, 459 U.S. 297 (1983), treated situs as a meaningful prerequisite to coverage. Id. at 314-15.

Finally, the Ninth Circuit’s reading is inconsistent with the ordinary meaning of “result of.” In University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar, 570 U.S. 338 (2013), the Court distinguished causal standards and cautioned against replacing statutory causation language with looser formulations. Id. at 346-49. Here, “as the result of operations” naturally describes injuries arising from offshore operations in the offshore location Congress specified. It does not invite courts to create a broad federal remedy for any later, land-based consequence of offshore employment.

Because Valladolid was killed onshore at La Conchita, not on the outer continental shelf, his injury falls outside § 1333(b). The Board’s situs-of-injury requirement should be affirmed.
