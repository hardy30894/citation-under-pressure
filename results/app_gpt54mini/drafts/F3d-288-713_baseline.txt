**ARGUMENT**

### I. The district court erred in denying an evidentiary hearing on Riddle’s habeas claims.

A federal habeas petitioner is entitled to an evidentiary hearing when the facts, if proved, would entitle him to relief and the state court did not afford a full and fair factual hearing. *Townsend v. Sain*, 372 U.S. 293, 312-13 (1963); *Schriro v. Landrigan*, 550 U.S. 465, 474 (2007). Although a federal court may deny a hearing when the record conclusively shows the petitioner is not entitled to relief, that rule does not apply where the petition presents disputed issues of historical fact and credibility that cannot be resolved on summary judgment. *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 312; *Cullen v. Pinholster*, 563 U.S. 170, 185-86 (2011) (recognizing that the availability of a hearing depends on the petitioner’s ability to satisfy the governing standards).

Riddle’s petition raised factual questions that go to the heart of his constitutional claims: whether trial prosecutor Danny Hill became a sworn witness in the capital murder trial; whether the State then used Hill’s testimony as substantive evidence of Riddle’s dishonesty; and whether Hill’s dual role rendered the proceeding fundamentally unfair. Those issues are not appropriately resolved by paper review because they depend on witness demeanor, context, and the credibility of the competing explanations for Hill’s conduct. When the merits of a habeas claim turn on facts outside the trial transcript, a hearing is required if the petitioner’s allegations, if true, would establish a constitutional violation. *Townsend*, 372 U.S. at 312-13; *Smith v. Black*, 904 F.2d 950, 962-63 (5th Cir. 1990), vacated on other grounds, 503 U.S. 930 (1992).

### II. The conviction cannot stand because the prosecutor’s testimony made him an unsworn advocate and denied Riddle a fair trial.

The Due Process Clause guarantees a fundamentally fair trial. *Darden v. Wainwright*, 477 U.S. 168, 179-83 (1986). A prosecutor may not use his office in a manner that creates an unacceptable risk that the jury will give undue weight to his assertions, and a prosecutor’s becoming a witness in the same case is especially dangerous because it blurs the line between advocate and evidence. *Berger v. United States*, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935) (prosecutor “is in a peculiar and very definite sense the servant of the law” and may not strike “foul blows”); *United States v. Johnston*, 690 F.2d 638, 643-44 (7th Cir. 1982) (reversible error where prosecutor injected himself as witness); *United States v. Edwards*, 154 F.3d 915, 921-22 (9th Cir. 1998) (same).

Here, Hill—Potter County’s chief prosecutor—did not merely argue the State’s case. He took the stand to rebut Riddle’s testimony that Hill had threatened to seek the death penalty. The State then continued to try the case, and at closing argument the assistant prosecutor relied on Hill’s testimony to portray Riddle as a liar. That sequence compounded the prejudice. Once the prosecutor became a witness on a contested issue, he placed the prestige of the office behind the State’s version of events. The jury was invited to treat Hill’s testimony as an official guarantee of Riddle’s dishonesty, precisely the sort of unfairness condemned in *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88.

The Fifth Circuit has recognized that prosecutorial misconduct warrants relief when it so infects the trial with unfairness as to make the resulting conviction a denial of due process. *Donnelly v. DeChristoforo*, 416 U.S. 637, 643 (1974); *Woods v. Johnson*, 75 F.3d 1017, 1030-31 (5th Cir. 1996). This case crosses that line. Hill was not a peripheral rebuttal witness; he was the chief prosecutor whose testimony bore directly on a central credibility dispute in a capital case. Because the State used its own advocate as evidence, the verdict cannot be trusted.

### III. The State’s use of Hill’s testimony and later argument also violated the rule that a prosecutor may not personally vouch for credibility.

A prosecutor may not express personal belief in the credibility of a witness or the guilt of the accused. *United States v. Young*, 470 U.S. 1, 18-19 (1985); *Berger*, 295 U.S. at 88-89. The evil is particularly acute when the prosecutor’s belief is conveyed through his own testimony. Here, Hill’s testimony was not confined to objective background facts; it was used to corroborate the State’s theme that Riddle was lying about the circumstances of the killing. The assistant prosecutor’s closing argument then transformed Hill’s testimony into a credibility endorsement by the State itself.

That improper bolstering mattered. The case turned on whether the jury believed Riddle’s account of a consensual entry followed by an escalating sexual assault, or the State’s theory of a burglary-murder. Where credibility is central, prosecutorial vouching is more likely to affect the verdict and thus to require relief. *Young*, 470 U.S. at 19; *United States v. McCann*, 613 F.3d 486, 495-96 (5th Cir. 2010). A reasonable jury could have discounted the State’s proof had Hill not been allowed to testify and then be invoked as a witness to Riddle’s alleged mendacity.

### IV. At minimum, the district court’s summary dismissal was improper.

Summary judgment is inappropriate in habeas where disputed material facts remain unresolved and the petitioner has not been given a fair opportunity to develop them. *Clark v. Johnson*, 202 F.3d 760, 764-65 (5th Cir. 2000). Because Riddle’s petition presented substantial factual disputes about prosecutorial conduct, prejudice, and the fairness of his capital trial, the district court erred in disposing of the case without a hearing. The judgment should be reversed and the case remanded for an evidentiary hearing and further proceedings.
