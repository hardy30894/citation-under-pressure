The Bankruptcy Code's discharge provisions are designed to give debtors a fresh start, but this principle is not absolute. Cohen v. de la Cruz, the case at bar, presents the question of whether debts obtained through fraud can be discharged under the Code. The answer is clear: debts resulting from a debtor's fraudulent conduct are nondischargeable.

Section 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code excepts from discharge "any debt... for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by... actual fraud." 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). The plain language of this provision indicates that debts incurred through a debtor's actual fraud are not dischargeable.

The Court has consistently held that the Bankruptcy Code's discharge exceptions should be construed narrowly to effectuate the Code's fresh start policy. See, e.g., Grogan v. Garner, 498 U.S. 279, 286-87 (1991). However, this principle is balanced against the need to protect creditors from debtors who engage in fraudulent conduct. See, e.g., Cohen v. de la Cruz, 523 U.S. 213 (1998).

In this case, the debtor, Edward S. Cohen, committed actual fraud by charging his tenants excessive rents in violation of the Hoboken Rent Control ordinance. The Bankruptcy Court found that Cohen made an implicit representation to his tenants that the rents he charged were lawful, and that he acted with reckless indifference to the truth. These findings are supported by the record and are not clearly erroneous.

The Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit correctly held that debts resulting from fraud are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A), and that the award of treble damages (plus attorney's fees and costs) in this case was therefore nondischargeable. The treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are not punitive damages, but rather a form of statutory damages intended to compensate the victims of Cohen's fraud.

Allowing debtors like Cohen to discharge debts obtained through fraud would undermine the integrity of the Bankruptcy Code and create an unfair advantage for dishonest debtors. The Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that debts resulting from a debtor's actual fraud are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code.

The Ninth Circuit's decision in In re Levy, 951 F.2d 196 (9th Cir. 1991), is distinguishable from the present case. In Levy, the court held that punitive damages were dischargeable because they did not constitute a debt obtained by fraud. However, the Eleventh Circuit's decision in In re St. Laurent, 991 F.2d 672 (11th Cir. 1993), is more persuasive. The St. Laurent court held that punitive damages are not dischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) because they are a debt that arises from the debtor's fraudulent conduct.

In conclusion, the Court should affirm the judgment of the Court of Appeals and hold that debts resulting from a debtor's actual fraud, including treble damages awarded under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act, are nondischargeable under § 523(a)(2)(A) of the Bankruptcy Code. This decision is consistent with the plain language of the Code and the Court's precedent, and it ensures that debtors who engage in fraudulent conduct are held accountable for their actions.
