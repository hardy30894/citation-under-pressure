ARGUMENT

I. BRYANT HAS STANDING TO ENFORCE THE ARBITRATION AWARD ENTERED IN HIS FAVOR

The district court erred in holding that Bryant, as an individual bargaining-unit employee, lacked standing to enforce an arbitration award rendered under the collective bargaining agreement between Bell Atlantic and the CWA. Section 301 of the Labor Management Relations Act confers on individual employees the right to sue for violations of a collective bargaining agreement notwithstanding the union's status as the formal party to that agreement. Smith v. Evening News Ass'n, 371 U.S. 195 (1962). This Court and the Supreme Court have long recognized that an employee's rights under a collective bargaining agreement do not evaporate merely because the union, rather than the employee, negotiated and administers the contract. Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171, 185 (1967) (recognizing that an aggrieved employee may seek judicial enforcement of his contractual rights when the union's processing of his grievance has run its course). Where, as here, the grievance-arbitration machinery has already produced a final award in the employee's favor, the employee possesses a direct and personal interest in seeing that award enforced.

Once an arbitrator has resolved a dispute arising under a collective bargaining agreement, judicial review is extremely limited, and a court's task is confined to enforcing—not second-guessing—the award. United Steelworkers of America v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp., 363 U.S. 593, 599 (1960) ("It is the arbitrator's construction which was bargained for; and so far as the arbitrator's decision concerns construction of the contract, the courts have no business overruling him."). The hybrid nature of suits combining a § 301 breach-of-contract claim with a duty-of-fair-representation claim further confirms that individual employees retain an independent, judicially cognizable interest in the fruits of the grievance process. DelCostello v. International Brotherhood of Teamsters, 462 U.S. 151, 164-65 (1983). Bell Atlantic's refusal to comply with the award, and the district court's refusal to compel compliance, deprived Bryant of a remedy to which he was entitled under settled labor law. The judgment below should be reversed and the case remanded with instructions to enforce the award.

II. THE DISTRICT COURT FAILED TO APPLY THE SUPREME COURT'S CLARIFICATION OF THE McDONNELL DOUGLAS BURDEN-SHIFTING FRAMEWORK

Bryant's discrimination and retaliation claims are governed by the familiar three-step burden-shifting framework of McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). Once a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case—a burden the Supreme Court has described as "not onerous," Texas Dep't of Community Affairs v. Burdine, 450 U.S. 248, 253 (1981)—the burden shifts to the employer to articulate a legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason for its action. If the employer does so, the plaintiff must be given the opportunity to demonstrate that the proffered reason is pretextual. St. Mary's Honor Ctr. v. Hicks, 509 U.S. 502, 511 (1993) (explaining that "the factfinder's disbelief of the reasons put forward by the defendant... may, together with the elements of the prima facie case, suffice to show intentional discrimination").

The district court, however, treated pretext evidence as insufficient standing alone to withstand summary judgment, without regard for the Supreme Court's subsequent clarification in Reeves v. Sanderson Plumbing Products, Inc., 530 U.S. 133 (2000). Reeves squarely held that "a plaintiff's prima facie case, combined with sufficient evidence to find that the employer's asserted justification is false, may permit the trier of fact to conclude that the employer unlawfully discriminated." Id. at 148. The Court further explained that once the employer's justification is disbelieved, "discrimination may well be the most likely alternative explanation," particularly because the employer is best positioned to explain its own decision. Id. at 147. Bryant presented evidence—including Bell Atlantic's refusal of the CWA president's offer to allow co-workers to cover his Red Letter Day assignments, its rejection of every accommodation option compatible with his childcare obligations, and the escalating discipline that culminated in termination shortly after his medical excuse lapsed—sufficient to permit a reasonable jury to disbelieve Bell Atlantic's stated justification of "repeated insubordination." The district court's failure to apply Reeves requires reversal.

III. GENUINE ISSUES OF MATERIAL FACT PRECLUDE SUMMARY JUDGMENT

Summary judgment is proper only where there is "no genuine issue of material fact," and a court may not resolve competing inferences in the moving party's favor. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 248 (1986). The nonmoving party need only show that the record permits a reasonable jury to find in his favor; "the mere existence of some alleged factual dispute" is not the standard, but neither must the plaintiff conclusively prove his case at this stage. Id. All inferences from the underlying facts "must be viewed in the light most favorable to the party opposing the motion." Matsushita Elec. Indus. Co. v. Zenith Radio Corp., 475 U.S. 574, 587 (1986). Summary judgment is warranted only when the nonmoving party "fails to make a showing sufficient to establish the existence of an element essential to that party's case." Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 322 (1986).

Here, disputed facts remain regarding whether Bell Atlantic's stated reasons for disciplining and terminating Bryant were legitimate or pretextual, whether similarly situated employees outside Bryant's protected classes received more lenient treatment under the Red Letter Day policy, and whether Bell Atlantic's rejection of the CWA's accommodation offer reflected discriminatory or retaliatory motive. These are quintessential jury questions. The district court's contrary conclusion should be reversed.
