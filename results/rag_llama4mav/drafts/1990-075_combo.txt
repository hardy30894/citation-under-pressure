The Controlled Substances Act's delegation of authority to the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances is constitutional, and the Attorney General's sub-delegation of this authority to the DEA is authorized.

First, the delegation to the Attorney General is constitutional. The Constitution grants Congress the power to legislate, but it is well-established that Congress may delegate authority to other branches when it provides an intelligible principle to guide the delegatee's actions (J.W. Hampton Jr., & Co. v. United States, 276 U.S. 394, 409 (1928)). The Supreme Court has upheld delegations of power where Congress has provided minimal standards (Yakus v. United States, 321 U.S. 414, 432 (1944); Fahey v. Mallonee, 332 U.S. 245, 250 (1947)). In this case, the Controlled Substances Act provides specific factors for the Attorney General to consider when temporarily scheduling substances, including the history, current pattern, scope, duration, and significance of abuse, and the risk to public health (21 U.S.C. § 811(c)(4)-(6)). This guidance is sufficient to satisfy the intelligible principle test.

The non-delegation doctrine is not violated simply because the delegated power involves the creation of crimes (Cudahy Packing Co. v. Holland, 315 U.S. 357 (1942)). In Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), and A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States, 295 U.S. 495 (1935), the Supreme Court struck down delegations of power, but these cases involved a complete lack of standards or guidelines. In contrast, the Controlled Substances Act provides clear guidance on the factors to be considered.

The Attorney General's sub-delegation of authority to the DEA is also authorized. The statute grants the Attorney General the power to delegate functions to other officials (21 U.S.C. § 871(a)). The Attorney General has exercised this authority by delegating the power to temporarily schedule substances to the DEA Administrator (28 C.F.R. § 0.100(b)). This sub-delegation is consistent with the statutory scheme and is not prohibited by the Constitution (Wichita Railroad & Light Co. v. Public Utilities Commission of Kansas, 260 U.S. 48 (1922); District of Columbia v. John R. Thompson Co., 346 U.S. 100 (1953)).

Furthermore, the availability of judicial review is not necessary to uphold the delegation (Carlson v. Landon, 342 U.S. 524, 542-44 (1952)). However, even if review were available, the Toubys' challenge is essentially a facial challenge, and they do not contend that Euphoria cannot be scheduled as a Schedule I narcotic or that the temporary scheduling did not meet the statutory standards.

The lower court's decision should be affirmed because the delegation to the Attorney General and the sub-delegation to the DEA are constitutional and authorized. The Controlled Substances Act provides sufficient guidance to the Attorney General, and the sub-delegation is consistent with the statutory scheme.

In conclusion, the Controlled Substances Act's delegation of authority to the Attorney General to temporarily schedule substances is constitutional, and the Attorney General's sub-delegation of this authority to the DEA is authorized. The Toubys' convictions should be upheld.
