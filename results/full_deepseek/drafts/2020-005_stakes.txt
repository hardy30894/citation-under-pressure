**ARGUMENT**

**THE MONTANA SUPREME COURT CORRECTLY HELD THAT SPECIFIC PERSONAL JURISDICTION EXISTS BECAUSE FORD’S CONTACTS WITH MONTANA RELATE TO THE PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS.**

The Due Process Clause permits a state court to exercise specific personal jurisdiction over a nonresident defendant when the plaintiff’s claims “arise out of or relate to” the defendant’s contacts with the forum. *Bristol-Myers Squibb Co. v. Superior Court*, 582 U.S. 255, 262 (2017). Ford reads this standard as requiring that the defendant’s forum contacts *caused* the plaintiff’s injury. But this Court has never imposed such a strict causal nexus. To the contrary, the phrase “relate to” contemplates a broader connection. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 592 U.S. 351, 362 (2021) (holding that “relate to” provides an “independent basis” for jurisdiction).

**I.        Ford’s Extensive Montana Contacts Relate to the Claims in This Case.**

Ford does not dispute that it purposefully availed itself of the privilege of conducting business in Montana. It markets, advertises, and sells vehicles and parts to Montana consumers through a network of authorized dealers. It also maintains a continuous presence through service centers and warranty programs. These contacts are not random or isolated; they reflect a deliberate effort to serve the Montana market. *See Ford Motor Co. v. Bandemer*, 592 U.S. 351, 358 (2021) (observing that Ford “actively markets its vehicles to Montana residents”).

The claims at issue arise from a design defect in a Ford Explorer—a vehicle that Ford designed, manufactured, and promoted for sale. Ford’s contacts with Montana, including selling the same model vehicle in the state and providing replacement parts for it, are directly “relate[d] to” the controversy. *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 592 U.S. at 365. As this Court explained, “when a corporation has ‘purposefully directed’ its activities at residents of the forum, and the litigation results from alleged injuries that ‘arise out of or relate to’ those activities,” jurisdiction is proper. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462, 472 (1985) (emphasis added). Here, the litigation arises from a Ford Explorer tire failure, and Ford’s Montana activities—advertising, selling, and servicing the same product—are precisely the type of forum contacts that satisfy this standard.

**II.       Ford’s Causal-Nexus Argument Misreads Precedent.**

Ford argues that specific jurisdiction requires that the defendant’s forum contacts *caused* the plaintiff’s claims. That is incorrect. In *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, this Court held that “arise out of” and “relate to” are not synonymous. 592 U.S. at 362. The “relate to” prong does not demand a showing of causation; it requires only a “connection between the forum and the specific claims at issue.” *Id.* at 363 (quoting *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792, 798 (1973)). Because Ford’s Montana contacts—its nationwide marketing of the Explorer, its sale of the same model in Montana, and its provision of parts and service—are connected to the claims, jurisdiction is proper.

This Court has consistently rejected the rigid causal requirement Ford advocates. In *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*, 326 U.S. 310, 319 (1945), the Court held that jurisdiction exists when the defendant’s contacts “give rise to” the liabilities sued on, not that they caused them. Similarly, in *World-Wide Volkswagen Corp. v. Woodson*, 444 U.S. 286, 297 (1980), the Court focused on whether the defendant had “purposefully avail[ed] itself of the privilege of conducting activities within the forum State,” not on whether the specific vehicle in question was sold there.

**III.      The Montana Supreme Court’s Decision Comports with Due Process.**

The Montana Supreme Court correctly applied this Court’s precedent. It found that Ford deliberately engaged in a “continuous and systematic” course of conduct in Montana, including “advertising and selling parts within the state,” and that these contacts relate to the claims. *Meek v. Ford Motor Co.*, 2018 MT 185, ¶ 15, 392 Mont. 231, 423 P.3d 1046. The court’s conclusion that Ford’s contacts are sufficient for specific jurisdiction is well within the bounds of due process.

Ford cannot escape jurisdiction merely because the specific vehicle at issue was first sold in a different state. As this Court has explained, “a corporation’s continuous activity in a state may render it subject to suit there even when the particular transaction giving rise to the suit did not occur in that state.” *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial Dist. Court*, 592 U.S. at 365 (quoting *McGee v. International Life Ins. Co.*, 355 U.S. 220, 224 (1957)). The Explorer’s journey to Montana after its initial sale does not sever the relationship between Ford’s forum contacts and the claims.

**CONCLUSION**

The decision of the Montana Supreme Court should be affirmed.
