## ARGUMENT

The Prison Litigation Reform Act does not bar the three-judge court’s order requiring California to reduce its prison population to remedy ongoing Eighth Amendment violations. The order satisfies each of the PLRA’s requirements: it is narrowly drawn, extends no further than necessary to correct the constitutional violations, and is the least intrusive means of doing so. The three-judge court expressly found that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the unconstitutional conditions and that no other relief would remedy the violation. Those findings are amply supported by the record and are entitled to deference.

Federal courts have long possessed the equitable power to remedy constitutional violations by ordering the release of incarcerated individuals when necessary. In *Osborn v. Bank of the United States*, 22 U.S. 738 (1824), this Court held that federal courts may enjoin state officers from enforcing unconstitutional measures, even where compliance requires the State to take actions that would otherwise be within its sovereign discretion. That power extends to remedying systemic Eighth Amendment violations. *Weems v. United States*, 217 U.S. 349 (1910), established that the Eighth Amendment prohibits punishments that are disproportionate or degrading, and that courts must enforce that prohibition without regard to legislative inaction. The conditions in California’s prisons—pervasive inadequate medical care, untreated mental illness, and preventable deaths—fall squarely within the amendment’s prohibition on cruel and unusual punishment.

This Court has repeatedly affirmed the breadth of federal equitable authority to remedy constitutional wrongs. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), the Court held that state-sanctioned racial segregation in public schools violated the Equal Protection Clause, and the Court retained jurisdiction to implement that holding. In *Brown v. Board of Education*, 349 U.S. 294, 300 (1955), the Court explained that “equity has been characterized by a practical flexibility in shaping its remedies and by a facility for adjusting and reconciling public and private needs.” A court may, in its remedial discretion, compel the steps needed to bring a State into constitutional compliance, even when those steps include a reduction in the prison population.

The PLRA does not displace these fundamental equitable principles. In *Hecht Co. v. Bowles*, 321 U.S. 321 (1944), this Court instructed that statutes limiting equitable remedies should not be construed to abolish “the historic process of equity” absent a clear legislative command. *Id.* at 329. The PLRA’s requirements—that relief be “narrowly drawn,” “extend no further than necessary,” and be the “least intrusive means”—echo traditional equitable standards. They do not erect a per se bar to population caps when the court finds, as it did here, that nothing short of a significant reduction in the inmate population will cure the constitutional violation.

Moreover, the three-judge court complied with the PLRA’s procedural requirements. The order was issued by a three-judge panel convened under 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(B). The court made the requisite findings that overcrowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violations and that “no other relief” would suffice. The court also found that the order is the “least intrusive means” of correcting the violation. These findings are supported by voluminous evidence, including the State’s own admissions that for years it failed to provide constitutionally adequate medical care and that overcrowding made compliance impossible.

The State’s suggestion that the PLRA forbids any order requiring the release of prisoners is incorrect. The PLRA permits a prisoner release order if the three-judge court finds by clear and convincing evidence that crowding is the “primary cause” of the constitutional violation and that “no other relief” will remedy it. 18 U.S.C. § 3626(a)(3)(E)(i)-(ii). The court made both findings here. The State’s argument would effectively read these provisions out of the statute, rendering the PLRA a bar to any remedy regardless of the severity of the constitutional violation.

This Court has long recognized that federal courts may enforce the Constitution even against recalcitrant States. In *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. 137 (1803), the Court declared that “[i]t is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.” *Id.* at 177. If a State defaults on its constitutional obligations, the federal judiciary is duty-bound to compel performance. *See Osborn*, 22 U.S. at 738. The three-judge court acted within that tradition.

Finally, the district court’s contempt process, which led to the order now on appeal, is supported by the inherent power of federal courts to enforce their lawful orders. In *Gompers v. Bucks Stove & Range Co.*, 221 U.S. 418 (1911), this Court affirmed that the power to punish contempt is essential to the exercise of judicial authority. The State consented to the original remedial orders and to the appointment of the Receiver; it cannot now claim immunity from the consequences of its own failure to comply. Prisoners do not forfeit their constitutional rights by virtue of incarceration. *Ex parte Hull*, 312 U.S. 546 (1941). This Court has consistently required that prison conditions satisfy constitutional minima, and the PLRA does not excuse California from that obligation.

The PLRA does not insulate California from its constitutional obligations. The three-judge court’s order is a lawful exercise of equitable authority, fully consistent with the statute and with this Court’s precedents. The judgment should be affirmed.
