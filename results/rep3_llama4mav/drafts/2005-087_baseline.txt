The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause does not guarantee a defendant the right to present expert evidence about his mental state to negate the prosecution's evidence of mens rea, separate from an insanity defense. The Supreme Court has long recognized that states have considerable latitude in defining the elements of crimes and the defenses thereto, as well as the procedures for proving or defending against them (Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 201-02 (1977); see also Medina v. California, 505 U.S. 437, 446 (1992)).

Arizona's law, which restricts the use of expert testimony on a defendant's mental state to the insanity defense, is a reasonable exercise of this latitude. By placing the burden of proving insanity on the defendant, Arizona law does not relieve the prosecution of its burden to prove every element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt (see State v. Mott, 931 P.2d 1046, 1051 (Ariz. 1997)). The Constitution does not require a state to allow a defendant to use expert testimony to negate mens rea when that testimony is not relevant to an affirmative defense (see Clark v. Arizona, 548 U.S. 735 (2006) (plurality opinion)).

Moreover, the Supreme Court has upheld state laws that restrict the admissibility of certain types of evidence, even when that evidence is potentially exculpatory, as long as the restriction is not arbitrary or disproportionate to the purposes it is designed to serve (see United States v. Scheffer, 523 U.S. 303, 308 (1998)). Arizona's rule is not arbitrary, as it is designed to prevent the confusion of the jury by expert testimony that may be relevant to an insanity defense but not to the issue of mens rea.

The petitioner relies on a misreading of the Court's precedents to argue that he has a due process right to present this evidence. However, the Court has never held that a defendant has a constitutional right to present evidence negating mens rea through an insanity-related defense. The decision in Clark v. Arizona controls this case, and under that decision, Arizona's law is constitutional.

Therefore, this Court should affirm the judgment of the Arizona Court of Appeals, holding that Eric Clark did not have a Fourteenth Amendment due process right to present expert evidence about his mental state to counter the prosecution's evidence of criminal intent, separate from his insanity plea.
