**ARGUMENT**

**I. The Board’s “Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt” Standard for Polling Is Irrational and Inconsistent with the National Labor Relations Act.**

The Board requires an employer to possess objective evidence sufficient to justify withdrawal of recognition before it may poll employees concerning union support. This standard renders polling a useless exercise. An employer who already possesses evidence justifying withdrawal has no need to poll; it may simply withdraw recognition. The Board’s rule thus forces employers into an all-or-nothing choice: either withdraw recognition based on existing evidence or refrain from any inquiry. That result conflicts with the Act’s fundamental purpose of promoting the peaceful settlement of industrial disputes through collective bargaining. *La Crosse Telephone Corp. v. Wisconsin Employment Relations Board*, 336 U.S. 18, 24 (1949).

The Act does not require such a rigid standard. The Board’s own earlier precedent recognized that polling serves a legitimate function when an employer has a good-faith doubt about union support, even if that doubt does not rise to the level justifying withdrawal. The Board’s departure from that approach is arbitrary. The Board’s rationale—that polling is “potentially disruptive”—cannot justify a standard that effectively prohibits the very tool it purports to regulate. The Board’s interpretation must be rational and consistent with the Act, and here it is not. *Marine Engineers Beneficial Ass’n v. Interlake Steamship Co.*, 370 U.S. 173, 181 (1962) (holding that the Board’s interpretation must be rational and consistent with the Act).

Moreover, the Board’s standard creates an anomaly: an employer may withdraw recognition—a drastic step that terminates the bargaining relationship—on the same quantum of evidence required merely to poll. The Act favors stability of collective-bargaining relationships. *United Mine Workers of America v. Arkansas Oak Flooring Co.*, 351 U.S. 62, 72 (1956) (recognizing the stability of collective-bargaining relationships as a central objective of the Act). A standard that encourages precipitous withdrawal rather than measured inquiry undermines that stability. The Board’s rule is therefore arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. *Boire v. Greyhound Corp.*, 376 U.S. 473, 481 (1964) (applying the Administrative Procedure Act standard of review to Board determinations).

**II. The Board’s Factual Determination That Allentown Mack Lacked a Good-Faith Reasonable Doubt Is Not Supported by Substantial Evidence.**

Even under the Board’s flawed standard, its conclusion that Allentown Mack lacked a reasonable doubt is unsupported by substantial evidence on the record as a whole. *Universal Camera Corp. v. NLRB*, 340 U.S. 474, 488 (1951). The Board discounted or ignored overwhelming evidence of employee disaffection.

Seven employees directly repudiated the union. The Board improperly excluded three employees—McArthur, Wehr, and Zoltack—because they were not in the unit on January 25, 1991. But the company’s doubt arose from statements made *before* that date, and the Board’s rigid cutoff ignores the dynamic nature of employee sentiment. The Board also discounted statements by Ridgick and Marsh, finding them equivocal. Yet Ridgick’s statement that “there was no need for a union” and Marsh’s complaint about dues are unambiguous expressions of opposition. The Board’s refusal to credit them conflicts with its duty to consider the evidence in its entirety. *NLRB v. Pittsburgh Steamship Co.*, 340 U.S. 498, 502 (1951) (holding that the Board must consider all relevant evidence).

The Board further rejected hearsay reports from Bloch and Mohr that the night shift and service department opposed the union. The Board’s categorical exclusion of such evidence is inconsistent with the Act’s policy of allowing employers to rely on objective indications of loss of support. *Arkansas Oak Flooring*, 351 U.S. at 72. The Board’s own precedent permits reliance on employee statements even when not directly from the employee. *See, e.g., Vaca v. Sipes*, 386 U.S. 171, 190 (1967) (recognizing that union’s duty of fair representation requires consideration of all available information). Here, the company had multiple, consistent reports from different sources indicating that a majority of the 32 employees no longer supported the union.

The Board’s finding that only 7 of 32 employees opposed the union is contradicted by the poll itself, which showed 19 votes against the union. While the poll cannot retroactively justify the decision to poll, it confirms that the company’s doubt was objectively reasonable. The Board’s refusal to consider this evidence is arbitrary. *Smith v. Evening News Assn.*, 371 U.S. 195, 200 (1962) (the Board must consider all relevant facts).

In sum, the Board’s standard is irrational, and its factual finding is unsupported by substantial evidence. This Court should reverse.
