**ARGUMENT**

The district court erred by instructing the jury to disregard testimony about post-accident design changes that Morbark’s own counsel had invited. This Court should reverse and remand for a new trial.

**I. The District Court Abused Its Discretion by Reversing Its Prior Ruling and Striking Admissible Impeachment Evidence**

Rule 407 of the Federal Rules of Evidence excludes evidence of subsequent remedial measures when offered to prove negligence or culpable conduct, but it does not bar such evidence when offered for another purpose, such as impeachment. *See* Fed. R. Evid. 407. Once Morbark’s counsel opened the door by suggesting that the wood chipper’s design had not changed, Wood was entitled to rebut that implication with evidence of the lengthened chute. *Cf. Brown v. United States*, 356 U.S. 148, 154-55 (1958) (a party who opens a subject on direct examination cannot complain when the opponent explores it on cross-examination).

The trial court initially recognized this principle, overruling Morbark’s objection and permitting the testimony. R4-141-51. The court then explicitly warned Morbark’s counsel that questioning about continued use of the machine “opened the door to the fair rebuttal that was offered.” R4-141-56. Yet, after the jury heard the testimony, the court reversed course and instructed the jury to disregard it. This was an abuse of discretion.

When a party introduces evidence or argument that creates a misleading impression, the opposing party must be allowed to correct that impression through otherwise inadmissible evidence. *See, e.g., Tigner v. National Sur. Co.*, 31 F.2d 447, 449 (5th Cir. 1929) (rule of completeness permits a party to introduce the remainder of a conversation when the opponent has presented a misleading portion). Here, Morbark’s opening statement and cross-examination implied the chipper remained unchanged. Wood’s rebuttal evidence—that a longer chute had been added—was the only means to correct that false implication.

The district court’s belated exclusion of this evidence cannot be squared with the well-settled principle that a trial court cannot “take back” admissible evidence once a party has relied upon it. *See, e.g., Hickory v. United States*, 160 U.S. 408, 421 (1896) (a party who elicits testimony on a subject cannot later complain of its admission). Moreover, the court’s instruction to disregard effectively told the jury to ignore the truth and accept Morbark’s unrebuked implication.

**II. The Error Was Prejudicial Because It Deprived Wood of a Fair Trial**

The erroneous instruction was not harmless. The central issue at trial was whether the seventeen-inch infeed chute was unreasonably dangerous. Morbark’s inference that the city’s continued use of the machine indicated its safety was powerful and prejudicial. *See New York Cent. R.R. v. Johnson*, 279 U.S. 310, 317 (1929) (evidence that a party continued to use a device after an accident may be unfairly prejudicial if allowed without context). To counter that inference, Wood needed the jury to understand that the city had in fact modified the machine—lengthening the chute to address the very defect alleged. *See, e.g., Standard Oil Co. v. Brown*, 218 U.S. 78, 86-87 (1910) (evidence of changes after an accident admissible to show feasibility when the opponent argues no change was needed).

Without this rebuttal, the jury was left with Morbark’s misleading claim that the design had not changed—and the court’s instruction to disregard any contrary evidence likely compounded the prejudice. *See, e.g., Union Pac. Ry. Co. v. Field*, 80 F.2d 240, 243 (10th Cir. 1935) (instruction to disregard evidence that was properly admitted constitutes reversible error where the evidence was critical to the case).

**III. The District Court’s Erroneous Application of Rule 407 Does Not Bar This Evidence**

This Court has never decided whether Rule 407 applies in strict products liability cases. But even assuming it does, the rule does not exclude evidence offered for impeachment. *See, e.g., The Fair v. Kohler Die & Specialty Co.*, 228 U.S. 22, 25 (1913) (subsequent improvements admissible to rebut claim of no change in practice). Here, Wood offered the evidence not to prove negligence or defect, but to impeach Morbark’s misleading portrayal of the chipper as unchanged and therefore safe. The district court itself recognized this purpose when it overruled Morbark’s objection. R4-141-56.

**CONCLUSION**

The district court abused its discretion by striking admissible impeachment evidence after allowing the jury to hear it. The error was prejudicial. This Court should reverse and remand for a new trial.
