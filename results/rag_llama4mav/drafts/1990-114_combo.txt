The Missouri Constitution's mandatory retirement, age seventy, requirement for state court judges does not violate the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA) or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. The ADEA excludes from its coverage "appointee[s] on the policymaking level." 29 U.S.C. § 630(f). Missouri's appointed judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" because they exercise significant discretion in decisionmaking and create law through their decisions. See EEOC v. Massachusetts, 858 F.2d 52, 55 (1st Cir.1988) (citing EEOC v. Massachusetts, 680 F.Supp. 455, 462 (D.Mass.1988)).

The authority cited by the court of appeals in this case, including EEOC v. Massachusetts, demonstrates that judges perform important policymaking functions. As the First Circuit noted, "policymaking is indisputably a part of the function of judging to the extent that judging involves lawmaking to fill the interstices of authority found in constitutions, statutes, and precedents." 858 F.2d at 55. This understanding of the judicial role is consistent with long-standing Supreme Court precedent. See Duncan v. Missouri, 152 U.S. 377, 382 (1894) (noting that state courts have the power to interpret state constitutions and statutes).

Furthermore, the Supreme Court has recognized that judges are not simply ministerial officers, but rather exercise significant discretion in their decisionmaking. See Dreyer v. Illinois, 187 U.S. 71, 84 (1902) (noting that judges are not simply "ministerial officers" but rather exercise "judicial discretion"). This discretion is a hallmark of policymaking, and it is therefore reasonable to conclude that Missouri's appointed judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" within the meaning of the ADEA.

The legislative history of the ADEA exemptions supports this interpretation. Although not dispositive, the Joint Explanatory Statement of Managers at the Conference on H.R. 1746 indicates that the exemptions are intended to apply to high-level appointees, including those in "policymaking positions at the highest levels of the departments or agencies of State or local governments." 1972 U.S. Code Cong. & Admin. News 2179-2180. The state judiciary can be considered one of the more powerful "agencies" of state government, and therefore falls within the scope of this exemption.

Moreover, the mandatory retirement provision does not violate the Equal Protection Clause. The Supreme Court has held that a state need only show a rational basis for its actions to withstand an equal protection challenge. See Joseph S. Finch & Co. v. McKittrick, 305 U.S. 395, 401 (1939) (noting that a state statute will be upheld if it is "rationally related to a legitimate state interest"). Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to the state's interest in ensuring that its judges are capable of performing their duties. See Trafelet v. Thompson, 594 F.2d 623, 627 (7th Cir. 1979) (noting that mandatory retirement "reduces delays in the administration of justice caused by death or disabling illness of sitting judges").

The Court has long recognized that states have significant latitude in regulating their judiciary. See Layton v. Missouri, 187 U.S. 356, 361 (1902) (noting that a state's power to regulate its judiciary is "subject only to the limitations imposed by the Federal Constitution"). In Ohio Ex Rel. Bryant v. Akron Metropolitan Park District, 281 U.S. 74, 79-80 (1930), the Court upheld a state constitutional provision against a challenge based on the Fourteenth Amendment, noting that the provision was not "arbitrary or capricious." Similarly, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is not arbitrary or capricious, and therefore does not violate the Equal Protection Clause.

The Court has also recognized that age is not a suspect classification, and therefore state actions that distinguish between individuals based on age are subject only to rational basis review. See Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton Railroad, 295 U.S. 330, 349-350 (1935) (noting that age is not a suspect classification). As the Court noted in Booth v. United States, 291 U.S. 339, 352-353 (1934), a mandatory retirement provision for judges is not unconstitutional simply because it distinguishes between judges based on age.

In Gorman v. Washington University, 316 U.S. 98, 105-106 (1942), the Court noted that a state's power to regulate its own affairs is significant, and that federal courts should be cautious in intervening in state matters. Similarly, in Hannum v. United States, 226 U.S. 436, 447 (1913), the Court recognized that Congress has significant latitude in regulating the pay and retirement of government officials. These cases demonstrate that Missouri's mandatory retirement provision is a legitimate exercise of state power.

In Dodge v. Board of Ed. of Chicago, 302 U.S. 74, 79 (1937), the Court upheld a state statute that required the retirement of teachers at a certain age, noting that the statute was not arbitrary or capricious. Similarly, Missouri's mandatory retirement provision for judges is not arbitrary or capricious, and is therefore constitutional.

In conclusion, Missouri's mandatory retirement requirement for state court judges does not violate the ADEA or the Fourteenth Amendment's Equal Protection Clause. The state's appointed judges are "appointee[s] on the policymaking level" within the meaning of the ADEA, and the mandatory retirement provision is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. The judgment of the district court should be affirmed.
