**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether states may alter the qualifications for the U.S. Congress specifically enumerated in the Constitution—is governed by a settled principle of constitutional law: the qualifications for federal office are exclusive and non-delegable. The Framers of the Constitution deliberately placed the qualifications for members of Congress in Article I, Section 2 and Section 3, and the Constitution’s structure reflects a deliberate choice to reserve these qualifications to the national government. The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment, by imposing additional, state-imposed qualifications on candidates for the U.S. House of Representatives and U.S. Senate, violates this constitutional design.

**I. The Constitution’s Qualifications Clauses Are Exclusive and Cannot Be Altered by the States**

The Constitution explicitly sets forth the qualifications for members of Congress. Article I, Section 2, Clause 2 provides that a Representative must be “at least twenty-five Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for seven Years, and [who] shall have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for.” Article I, Section 3, Clause 1 provides that a Senator must be “at least thirty Years of Age, and a Citizen of the United States for nine Years, and [who] shall have been an Inhabitant of the State he shall be chosen for.” These qualifications are not merely guidelines; they are the sole standards by which Congress may be constituted. The Framers did not leave room for state legislatures or voters to add to or subtract from them.

This Court has long held that the qualifications clauses are not subject to state modification. In *U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton*, 514 U.S. 779, 800 (1995), the Court affirmed that “the Constitution’s qualifications for federal office are exclusive.” The Court emphasized that “the Framers of the Constitution intended to establish a uniform national standard for eligibility to federal office,” and that “the States may not impose additional qualifications.” The Court further noted that “the Constitution’s structure reflects a deliberate choice to reserve the qualifications for federal office to the national government.” Id. at 801.

The Arkansas Amendment directly contravenes this principle. By barring individuals who have served three or more terms in the House or two or more terms in the Senate from appearing on the ballot, Arkansas imposes a new, state-created qualification: term limits. This is not a mere procedural rule about ballot access; it is a substantive disqualification based on prior service. The Court has made clear that “a state may not impose a qualification that is not in the Constitution.” *Id.* at 802. The fact that the Amendment is styled as a “ballot access” restriction does not alter its substance. As the Court observed, “the distinction between outright bar and ballot-access is too fine a point for the average voter to grasp.” *Id.* at 803. The effect is the same: a candidate is excluded from the ballot based on a criterion not found in the Constitution.

**II. The Arkansas Amendment Violates the Supremacy Clause and the Principle of National Uniformity**

The Constitution’s design ensures that federal officeholders are subject to uniform standards across all states. The Framers rejected proposals to allow states to impose additional qualifications, recognizing that a patchwork of state rules would undermine the national character of Congress. As Justice Scalia wrote in *Thornton*, “the Constitution’s structure reflects a deliberate choice to reserve the qualifications for federal office to the national government.” 514 U.S. at 801. Allowing states to impose term limits would create a system where eligibility for Congress depends on the whims of state voters, not the Constitution.

The Arkansas Amendment would permit a candidate to be eligible in one state but ineligible in another based solely on the number of terms served. This would result in a “hodge-podge of qualifications,” a scenario the Framers explicitly sought to avoid. *Id.* at 802. The Court has consistently rejected such fragmentation. In *United States v. Cruikshank*, 92 U.S. 542, 553 (1875), the Court held that “the Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land,” and that “no state law can be in conflict with it.” The Arkansas Amendment is in direct conflict with the Constitution’s express qualifications for federal office.

**III. The Severability Clause Does Not Save the Amendment**

The Arkansas Supreme Court held that Section 3 was severable from Sections 1 and 2, but concluded that the entire amendment must fall because the two provisions were “systemically fused” and presented as an “all or nothing” package. This conclusion is sound and consistent with precedent.

In *Combs v. Glenn Falls Ins. Co.*, 237 Ark. 745, 750, 375 S.W.2d 809, 811 (1964), this Court held that a severability clause is effective only if the legislature or people “would have enacted the remaining provisions even without the invalid ones.” Here, the record shows that the voters were not presented with a choice between state term limits and federal term limits. The ballot title and preamble referred to “elected officials” in the singular, and the amendment was marketed as a single, unified reform. The sponsors of the amendment knew that Section 3 was constitutionally dubious, yet they bundled it with state term limits to increase its appeal. This is not a case of a minor flaw that can be excised; it is a deliberate effort to use a severability clause as a shield for a constitutionally invalid provision.

The Court has recognized that “a state may not use a severability clause to rescue a constitutionally invalid provision by coupling it with a valid one.” *Thornton*, 514 U.S. at 804. The Arkansas Supreme Court correctly concluded that the “dichotomous issue was not explained to the voters in a meaningful way,” and that the “two groups were not only inextricably linked—they were systemically fused.” The amendment’s structure was designed to ensure that voters could not approve one part without approving the other. Under these circumstances, the severability clause cannot save the amendment.

**Conclusion**

The Arkansas Term Limitation Amendment imposes a new, state-created qualification on candidates for Congress—term limits—that is not found in the Constitution. This violates the exclusive nature of the qualifications clauses in Article I and undermines the principle of national uniformity. The amendment is not merely a procedural rule; it is a substantive disqualification.
