**Argument**

Rule 23(b)(3) permits class certification only if common questions “predominate” over individual ones. Fed. R. Civ. P. 23(b)(3). That requirement is not satisfied unless the plaintiff adduces competent evidence showing that the alleged injury and the resulting damages can be proved with classwide proof. In *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, the Court held that a class-certification inquiry must be “rigorous,” and that a trial court may consider merits issues only to the extent necessary to determine whether Rule 23 is met. 564 U.S. 338, 351-52 (2011). Likewise, *General Telephone Co. of the Southwest v. Falcon* requires courts to conduct a “rigorous analysis” of the Rule 23 prerequisites. 457 U.S. 147, 161 (1982). A district court therefore abuses its discretion when it certifies a class on the basis of speculation rather than admissible proof. *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. 27, 33-34 (2013).

Here, the class was certified without adequate admissible evidence that damages could be measured on a classwide basis. That was error. Rule 23 does not authorize a damages model untethered to the theory of liability. As this Court held in *Comcast*, “Questions of individual damage calculations do not preclude class certification,” but only if “the model is consistent with the liability case.” 569 U.S. at 35. The model must measure only those damages “resulting from the particular antitrust injury” that the plaintiffs’ theory of liability seeks to redress. *Id.* at 35-36. A district court may not accept a damages methodology that “cannot bridge the gap between” the alleged wrongful conduct and the damages actually measured. *Id.* at 34.

The Third Circuit’s decision below conflicts with that rule. The court affirmed certification even though the district court had expressly narrowed the case to a single theory of antitrust impact: that Comcast’s clustering deterred overbuilder entry. *Behrend v. Comcast Corp.*, 655 F.3d 182, 186-87 (3d Cir. 2011). Yet the damages model credited by the district court bundled multiple theories of harm together and did not isolate damages attributable to the only theory left in the case. *Id.* at 188-89. That is precisely what *Comcast* forbids. 569 U.S. at 35-36.

The court below also relied on evidence that was not adequate to satisfy Rule 23’s demanding standard. In *In re Hydrogen Peroxide Antitrust Litigation*, the Third Circuit itself held that a district court “must resolve all factual or legal disputes relevant to class certification,” and that the plaintiff must prove compliance with Rule 23 by a preponderance of the evidence. 552 F.3d 305, 307, 320-21 (3d Cir. 2008). The court explained that certification cannot rest on “assumptions” or “unsupported speculation.” *Id.* at 320. A plaintiff must come forward with evidence that is “susceptible to class-wide proof.” *Id.* at 311-12. The district court here did the opposite: it accepted a damages methodology despite the absence of admissible evidence showing that the model could measure damages for all class members attributable solely to the certified theory of injury.

This Court’s antitrust precedent confirms the point. In *Zenith Radio Corp. v. Hazeltine Research, Inc.*, the Court held that antitrust damages must be shown with reasonable estimation, but the estimate must be grounded in the injury actually caused by the antitrust violation. 395 U.S. 100, 123-25 (1969). And in *Blue Chip Stamps v. Manor Drug Stores*, the Court rejected procedures that would permit liability based on conjecture rather than proof. 421 U.S. 723, 740-41 (1975). Rule 23 cannot be used to relax the plaintiff’s burden to present a reliable, case-specific damages showing. *Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Dukes*, 564 U.S. at 351-52; *Comcast Corp. v. Behrend*, 569 U.S. at 34-36.

Nor can certification be sustained on the theory that damages issues can simply be sorted out later. The class-certification decision must be made on the record then before the court, and that record must establish that common proof will actually carry the case. *Eisen v. Carlisle & Jacquelin*, 417 U.S. 156, 177-78 (1974); *Hydrogen Peroxide*, 552 F.3d at 321-22. Where the record does not support a classwide damages methodology tied to the certified theory of liability, certification is improper. *Comcast*, 569 U.S. at 35-36.

Because the district court certified this class without adequate admissible evidence that damages may be measured and quantified on a classwide basis, the judgment should be reversed.
