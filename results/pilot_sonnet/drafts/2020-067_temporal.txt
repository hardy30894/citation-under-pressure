# ARGUMENT

Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(b) permits a court of appeals to correct an unpreserved error that is plain and that "affect[s] substantial rights," and even then only if the error "seriously affect[s] the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." That standard did not spring from nothing; it codified appellate practice this Court had already articulated, and that practice has always required courts to examine the record as a whole—not merely the evidence placed before the jury—when assessing prejudice and the fairness of the proceeding. Petitioner's rule, confining review to the four corners of what the jury heard, is foreign to that practice and should be rejected.

## I. This Court's Plain-Error Precedents Direct Courts to Look Beyond the Jury's Evidence to the Whole Record.

The plain-error doctrine originates in United States v. Atkinson, 297 U.S. 157 (1936), where this Court held that appellate courts may notice unpreserved errors "if the errors are obvious, or if they otherwise seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings." Id. at 160. That formulation does not ask whether the jury's own evidentiary universe was tainted; it asks whether the proceeding, viewed as a whole, was fair. A standard framed in terms of the "public reputation of judicial proceedings," id., necessarily invites consideration of matters of public record—including the defendant's own prior adjudications—that inform whether the proceeding's outcome was in fact unjust. This Court reaffirmed that appellate courts retain discretion "in the exercise of a sound discretion" to notice errors affecting the integrity of the proceeding in Johnson v. United States, 318 U.S. 189, 200 (1943). Neither Atkinson nor Johnson confined that inquiry to the trial transcript.

This Court's harmless- and plain-error jurisprudence confirms the point. In Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), the Court explained that the touchstone of prejudice is not whether error occurred in isolation but "the impact of the thing done wrong on the minds of other men, not on one's own, in the total setting." Id. at 764. The Court instructed that a reviewing court must ask, "after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole," whether the outcome was substantially swayed. Id. at 764–65. "All that happened" is not limited to what twelve jurors heard; it includes the entire record made in the case. Two decades later, this Court applied the same whole-record approach to constitutional error, holding that a court must determine harmlessness by reference to the entire record of the proceeding. Chapman v. California, 386 U.S. 18, 23–24 (1967) (requiring courts to determine, from "the entire record," whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt). If a court may canvass the whole record to determine whether a constitutional trial error is harmless, it may do the same, a fortiori, in the more deferential plain-error setting, where the defendant bears the burden of establishing prejudice rather than the government bearing the burden of disproving it. Kotteakos and Chapman thus confirm that the "substantial rights" inquiry has never been artificially confined to the jury's own evidentiary diet.

This Court's practice of reviewing the "record as a whole" to assess prejudice from trial error is likewise reflected in Glasser v. United States, 315 U.S. 60 (1942), and Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78 (1935), both of which examined the full proceedings—not simply the isolated moment of error—to determine whether a defendant's rights were impaired. That method is equally sound whether the error concerns a conflict of interest, prosecutorial misconduct, or, as here, an omitted element.

## II. Undisputed Matters of Record Bearing on Knowledge Are Properly Considered in Assessing Prejudice From an Omitted Mens Rea Element.

This principle is especially apt where, as here, the error consists of a failure to require jury findings on a knowledge element. This Court has long recognized that guilty knowledge is a fact ordinarily proved by circumstantial evidence, including a defendant's own history and conduct. In Screws v. United States, 325 U.S. 91 (1945), the plurality recognized that willfulness may be inferred from the totality of a defendant's circumstances rather than from any single piece of evidence presented at trial. And in Morissette v. United States, 342 U.S. 246 (1952), this Court explained that criminal intent is "a state of mind" ordinarily "ascertained by taking into account all the circumstances," id. at 276, not merely those circumstances a jury happened to be shown. Where the record—including matters such as a defendant's own multiple, lengthy prior felony convictions and terms of imprisonment—makes overwhelmingly clear that he knew of his disabling status, an appellate court applying Kotteakos's "total setting" test, 328 U.S. at 764, cannot sensibly avert its eyes from that record simply because the point was never disputed before the jury.

## CONCLUSION

Because Atkinson, Johnson, Kotteakos, Chapman, Glasser, Screws, and Morissette all direct appellate courts to assess prejudice and fairness by reference to the record as a whole, the Eleventh Circuit correctly considered the full record, including Greer's undisputed history of prior convictions, in concluding that the Rehaif error here did not affect his substantial rights or the fairness of his proceeding.
