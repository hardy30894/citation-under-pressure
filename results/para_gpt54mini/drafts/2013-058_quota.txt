## Argument

The Bankruptcy Code does not exempt an inherited IRA from a debtor’s estate. Section 522(b)(3)(C) and § 522(d)(12) exempt only “retirement funds” held in an account exempt from taxation under specified provisions of the Internal Revenue Code. 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(12). An inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” in the hands of the debtor because, under the tax rules governing inherited accounts, the beneficiary may not add contributions, may not roll over the account, and must begin distributions promptly rather than preserve the funds for retirement. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B); 26 U.S.C. § 402(c)(11)(A). As the Seventh Circuit correctly held, inherited IRAs are “a time-limited tax-deferral vehicle, but not a place to hold wealth for use after the new owner’s retirement.” In re Clark, 714 F.3d 559, 561 (7th Cir. 2013).

Text and structure control. Congress used the phrase “retirement funds,” not “funds that once were retirement funds” or “funds held in an account labeled IRA.” 11 U.S.C. § 522(b)(3)(C); 11 U.S.C. § 522(d)(12). This Court has repeatedly held that statutory interpretation begins with the enacted text and its ordinary meaning. Lamie v. United States Trustee, 540 U.S. 526, 534 (2004); Hartford Underwriters Ins. Co. v. Union Planters Bank, N.A., 530 U.S. 1, 6 (2000). When Congress includes a term in one provision and omits it from another, courts do not rewrite the statute. Russello v. United States, 464 U.S. 16, 23 (1983).

An inherited IRA fails the ordinary understanding of a retirement fund because it is not preserved for the beneficiary’s retirement. The beneficiary must take withdrawals long before retirement, and the account cannot be augmented or consolidated into a new retirement vehicle. 26 U.S.C. § 408(d)(3)(C); 26 U.S.C. § 401(a)(9)(B). The Seventh Circuit correctly reasoned that the funds “became no one’s retirement funds” when the original owner died. In re Clark, 714 F.3d at 563. That conclusion accords with the Fifth Circuit’s contrary decision in In re Chilton, 674 F.3d 486, 489-90 (5th Cir. 2012), which recognized the statutory phrase “retirement funds” but erred by divorcing that term from the present function of the account.

Petitioners’ reading also conflicts with this Court’s treatment of exemptions as status-based at the time of bankruptcy, not as relics of the asset’s prior history. In Schwab v. Reilly, 560 U.S. 770, 774 (2010), the Court emphasized that exemption claims turn on the property interest the debtor actually holds. Similarly, in Rousey v. Jacoway, 544 U.S. 320, 325-26 (2005), IRA funds were exempt because they served the retirement purpose Congress identified. But Rousey involved the debtor’s own IRA, not an inherited account subject to mandatory early distribution. Id. at 326-27. The same distinction matters here.

The better analogy is to homestead and other possession-based exemptions. In White v. Stump, 266 U.S. 310, 313-14 (1924), the Court held exemption rights are fixed when bankruptcy begins. In Owen v. Owen, 500 U.S. 305, 308 (1991), the Court explained that exemptions depend on the debtor’s interest as defined by applicable law, not by a hoped-for future change in use. An inherited IRA, like property that used to be exempt in someone else’s hands, does not become exempt simply because it once served an exempt purpose.

Finally, the rule of construction urged by petitioners cannot overcome the statute’s text. While exemptions are construed to effectuate congressional choice, courts may not enlarge them beyond their terms. Hanover Nat’l Bank v. Moyses, 186 U.S. 181, 192 (1902); Butner v. United States, 440 U.S. 48, 54-55 (1979). Nor may courts assume Congress meant to protect every asset with a retirement pedigree; legislative purpose does not displace enacted language. Rodriguez v. United States, 480 U.S. 522, 525-26 (1987).

Because an inherited IRA is not “retirement funds” in the debtor’s hands, it is not exempt under § 522(b)(3)(C) or § 522(d)(12). The judgment of the court of appeals should be affirmed.
