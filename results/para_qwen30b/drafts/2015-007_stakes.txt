**ARGUMENT**

The Florida death sentencing scheme at issue in this case—under which a non-unanimous jury recommendation of death suffices for imposition of the death penalty, and under which the jury is not required to determine whether a defendant is mentally retarded—does not violate the Sixth Amendment’s jury trial guarantee or the Eighth Amendment’s prohibition against cruel and unusual punishment. This Court should affirm the judgment of the Florida Supreme Court.

**I. The Florida Sentencing Scheme Complies with the Sixth Amendment as Clarified in Ring v. Arizona**

The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to a jury trial in criminal cases, but it does not require a jury to determine every factual issue that may influence a sentencing decision. In *Ring v. Arizona*, 536 U.S. 584, 592, 122 S.Ct. 2428, 153 L.Ed.2d 556 (2002), the Supreme Court held that “the Sixth Amendment requires that any fact (other than a prior conviction) that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.” The Court emphasized that the “statutory maximum” for a crime is the maximum sentence a judge may impose solely on the basis of the facts reflected in the jury verdict. Id. at 592, 122 S.Ct. 2428.

Florida’s capital sentencing scheme satisfies *Ring* because the jury’s role is not to determine the existence of aggravating factors as elements of the crime, but rather to make a recommendation of death or life imprisonment. The Florida Supreme Court has consistently held that the jury’s advisory recommendation is not a constitutional “element” of the offense, but a procedural mechanism designed to assist the trial court in its sentencing discretion. See *Hurst v. State*, 819 So.2d 689, 695 (Fla.2002); *Steele v. State*, 921 So.2d 537, 547 (Fla.2005). The trial court, not the jury, makes the final determination of whether aggravating factors exist and whether the death penalty is appropriate. See *Hurst*, 819 So.2d at 695.

This distinction is critical. The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that a state may structure its capital sentencing process so that the jury’s role is advisory, even where the jury does not unanimously find aggravating circumstances. In *Harmelin v. Michigan*, 501 U.S. 957, 994, 111 S.Ct. 2680, 115 L.Ed.2d 836 (1991), the Court upheld a capital sentencing scheme in which the jury did not unanimously find aggravating factors, noting that “the Constitution does not require that the jury unanimously find every fact that may be relevant to the imposition of the death penalty.” The Court reaffirmed this principle in *Apprendi v. New Jersey*, 530 U.S. 466, 490, 120 S.Ct. 2348, 147 L.Ed.2d 435 (2000), stating that “the jury’s role is not to determine every fact that may influence the sentence, but only those facts that increase the penalty beyond the statutory maximum.”

Florida’s scheme is consistent with this precedent. The jury’s recommendation is not binding, and the trial court retains full authority to weigh aggravating and mitigating factors and to impose the death penalty based on its own findings. The jury’s non-unanimous recommendation does not violate the Sixth Amendment because it does not constitute a finding of an “element” of the offense. The Florida Supreme Court has correctly interpreted *Ring* to apply only to facts that increase the statutory maximum penalty, and the jury’s advisory role in Florida does not trigger that requirement.

**II. The Florida Scheme Does Not Violate the Eighth Amendment**

The Eighth Amendment prohibits cruel and unusual punishment, but it does not require a unanimous jury finding of aggravating circumstances or a jury determination of mental retardation as a bar to execution. In *Atkins v. Virginia*, 536 U.S. 304, 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242, 153 L.Ed.2d 335 (2002), the Court held that executing individuals with intellectual disability violates the Eighth Amendment. However, the Court explicitly left to the states the authority to define the procedures for determining whether a defendant is intellectually disabled. Id. at 317, 122 S.Ct. 2242.

Florida’s three-pronged test—requiring a full-scale IQ of 70 or below, deficits in adaptive behavior, and onset before age 18—is consistent with *Atkins* and has been upheld by the Florida Supreme Court as a constitutionally adequate mechanism for determining intellectual disability. See *Nixon v. State*, 2 So.3d 137, 142 (Fla.2009). The burden of proof rests on the defendant to establish intellectual disability by clear and convincing evidence. § 921.137(4), Fla. Stat. (2012). This burden is not constitutionally infirm. In *Hurst*, the trial court conducted a full evidentiary hearing and found that Hurst did not meet the statutory definition of intellectual disability, despite expert testimony suggesting borderline intelligence and adaptive deficits. The court’s finding was supported by the record, including the testimony of Dr. James D. Larsen, who administered the WAIS-III and concluded Hurst’s full-scale IQ was 78. The Florida Supreme Court properly deferred to the trial court’s factual findings.

Moreover, the Eighth Amendment does not require that a jury determine whether a defendant is intellectually disabled. The Supreme Court has never held that a jury must make such a determination.
