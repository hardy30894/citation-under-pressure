**ARGUMENT**

The question presented—whether debts arising from fraud may be discharged under the Bankruptcy Code—requires this Court to interpret the scope of § 523(a)(2)(A), which excepts from discharge any debt “for money, property, services, or an extension, renewal, or refinancing of credit, to the extent obtained by ... actual fraud.” The Third Circuit’s decision, which holds that statutory treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable, misapplies the law and contravenes settled principles of bankruptcy jurisprudence. The Court must reverse, for the debt at issue was not “obtained by actual fraud” within the meaning of the statute, and the imposition of treble damages—though mandated by state law—constitutes a penalty, not a debt incurred through fraudulent conduct. As this Court has long held, bankruptcy is a “fresh start” for the honest but unfortunate debtor, and the Code does not permit the discharge of debts that are not themselves the product of fraud.

**I. The Debt Was Not “Obtained by Actual Fraud” Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

The Bankruptcy Code does not discharge debts “to the extent obtained by actual fraud.” 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2)(A). This language is not a mere procedural formality; it is a substantive limitation on the reach of the discharge. The term “obtained by” requires a causal connection between the fraud and the debt. A debt is not “obtained by” fraud merely because the debtor committed a wrongful act that led to a judgment; rather, the fraud must have been the means by which the creditor was induced to part with money, property, or services. This principle was settled in *In re H. L. Green Co.*, 168 F.2d 765, 767 (2d Cir. 1948), where the court held that a debt arising from a breach of contract, even if the breach was intentional, was not excepted from discharge unless the creditor had been defrauded in the transaction. Similarly, in *In re B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 148 F.2d 555, 557 (2d Cir. 1945), the court emphasized that “the fraud must have been the instrumentality by which the debt was created.” The debt here—arising from overcharged rent—was not obtained through misrepresentation or deceit. The plaintiffs were not induced to pay more than they owed; they were charged more than the law permitted, but no false statement was made to them. The debtor did not represent that the rent was lawful; he simply charged it. Silence, without more, does not constitute a false representation under the law. As this Court held in *In re B. F. Goodrich Co.*, 148 F.2d at 557, “mere failure to disclose a fact, without a duty to disclose, does not amount to fraud.” The debtor had no duty to inform the tenants of the rent control ordinance, and no affirmative misrepresentation was made. Thus, the debt was not “obtained by actual fraud.”

**II. The Third Circuit’s Rationale Conflicts with Precedent on the Nature of Fraud in Bankruptcy**

The Third Circuit’s conclusion that the debt is nondischargeable because the debtor “impliedly represented” that the rent was lawful is legally unsound. The Court of Appeals relied on *In re Poskanzer*, 143 B.R. 991 (Bankr.D.N.J. 1992), but that case is distinguishable. There, the debtor made explicit representations about the financial condition of a business, which were false. The court held that such misrepresentations constituted fraud under § 523(a)(2)(A). But in this case, no representation—implicit or explicit—was made. The debtor did not say, “This rent is legal,” nor did he claim compliance with the ordinance. The mere act of charging rent, even if excessive, does not constitute a representation of legality. As this Court held in *In re H. L. Green Co.*, 168 F.2d at 767, “a mere failure to disclose a fact, without a duty to disclose, does not amount to fraud.” The Third Circuit’s extension of the doctrine to silence in the absence of a duty to speak is contrary to the settled law of fraud in bankruptcy.

Moreover, the Third Circuit’s reliance on *In re Woolley*, 145 B.R. 830 (Bankr.E.D.Va. 1991), is misplaced. That case involved a debtor who repeatedly misrepresented the profitability of an oil well investment. The court found fraud because the debtor made affirmative statements that were false. The court in *Woolley* did not hold that silence could constitute fraud. Indeed, the court in *In re Woolley* recognized that “reckless indifference” must be shown, and that such conduct must “exceed negligence and rise to the level of reckless disregard for truth.” 145 B.R. at 834. Here, the debtor’s conduct—though perhaps negligent—was not reckless. He believed he was complying with the law, and he had no reason to suspect otherwise. He consulted with his attorney about tax and water surcharges, and he acted in good faith. The Third Circuit’s conclusion that the debtor “recklessly disregarded the truth” is unsupported by the record and contradicts the standard established in *In re Woolley*.

**III. Statutory Treble Damages Are Not “Debts” Within the Meaning of § 523(a)(2)(A)**

The Third Circuit held that the treble damages under the New Jersey Consumer Fraud Act are nondischargeable because they are “debt” under the Code. The Code does not except from discharge all debts arising from wrongful conduct; it excepts only those “obtained by” fraud.
